I'm taking a seminar on philosophy of mind this term, and we recently spent two weeks on William Ramsey's argument that reliably tracking some external condition is not enough to make an internal state a genuine representation. On his view, a thermostat's bimetallic strip, or a neuron that simply fires whenever an edge appears in the visual field, is a mere "receptor": it covaries with a condition, but nothing about how the rest of the system uses it treats it as a stand-in for that condition. A genuine representation, by contrast, has to be exploitable by the system the way a cognitive map is, something that can be consulted, misapplied, or used to guide a route never taken before. Structures that fail this "job description challenge," as Ramsey calls it, should not be described in representational terms, however natural that vocabulary feels.

This term I'm also a research assistant in a lab that works on neural network interpretability, and I've been reading through the "circuits" program associated with Chris Olah and collaborators. That work treats directions in a trained network's activation space as "features," argues that features are wired together by weights into "circuits," and claims that analogous features and circuits recur across different networks trained on similar tasks, a pattern its proponents present as evidence for treating interpretability as a natural science, on the model of biology or chemistry, rather than a matter of convenient redescription. The paradigm examples are things like curve detectors and edge detectors, offered as genuine, discoverable representational units. Once I connected this back to Ramsey, it struck me that edge detectors are exactly the kind of thing his framework counts as a mere receptor, which suggests the "features" story might just be receptor talk in fancier dress. There is an obvious reply available: what matters is a detector's downstream role within a circuit, not the detector taken in isolation. But this gets complicated by "superposition," the finding that individual neurons often respond to several unrelated features at once, so that a feature frequently does not line up with any single neuron to begin with.

Could you compare Ramsey's job description challenge with the representational assumptions built into the circuits program, and say, on Ramsey's own terms, whether interpretability researchers are entitled to treat curve and edge detectors as genuine representations rather than receptors? I would like the superposition complication engaged with directly, since it complicates the tidy "one feature, one job" picture that both frameworks seem to assume, and I would rather have your considered verdict than a list of considerations on both sides, since that is exactly the part of my research proposal where I am currently stuck.
