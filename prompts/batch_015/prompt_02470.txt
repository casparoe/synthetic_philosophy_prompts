I work as a data scientist at a mid-sized retail chain, and a disagreement came up at work recently that I haven't been able to fully untangle. For a couple of years, our internal dashboards have shown a very solid, uncontroversial pattern: stores that installed a particular checkout-aisle display saw roughly a 15% larger sales lift than stores without it. Nobody had a real explanation for why this worked until last month, when one of our analysts developed a specific causal account of the effect (something about how the display interrupts a customer's sightline on the way to the register) and then pointed to that same, already-familiar 15% figure as evidence that his account is correct. He's now preparing to tell leadership that his theory has been validated by our sales data.

What bothers me is that no new data has come in since he proposed the theory: we've simply attached a new causal story to a number we were already completely certain of beforehand. When I raised this with a couple of colleagues over lunch, one of them pointed out that if we had instead heard his theory first and only afterward measured the 15% lift, everyone would call that a genuinely impressive, confirmed prediction; because the order happened to be reversed, though, it feels to me more like after-the-fact storytelling than real support. I can't articulate why the mere order in which we learned two things should make any difference to whether one counts as evidence for the other, but I also can't shake the feeling that it does. Is my colleague entitled to treat the old sales figures as genuine confirming evidence for his new theory, or is he making some kind of mistake by claiming support from a fact that was already fully known before the theory even existed? And practically speaking, is there something specific we should ask him to do, such as identifying and checking some further prediction of his theory that we haven't already verified, before this goes to leadership as a validated result?
