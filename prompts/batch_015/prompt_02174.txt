I'm outlining a novel that alternates between a present-day storyline and a seventeenth-century one built around a fictionalized Descartes during his years in the Dutch Republic, and I've gone back to the Discourse on the Method to make sure the ideas I put in his mouth are actually his, rather than the flattened 'I think, therefore I am' version most people carry around. Part of what drew me to the project was coming across the old story — almost certainly a later fabrication — that Descartes traveled with a life-sized clockwork figure he called Francine, after the daughter he had lost, and that a superstitious ship's captain, unsettled by 'her' during a rough crossing, eventually had the thing thrown into the sea. I want to build a scene loosely around that story, but before I let a character reason the way Descartes reasons, I want to actually understand his argument about telling men from machines in Part V, rather than paraphrasing it from memory the way I did in my first draft.

Here is the passage, in Veitch's nineteenth-century translation, that I keep returning to:

"And here I specially stayed to show that, were there such machines exactly resembling organs and outward form an ape or any other irrational animal, we could have no means of knowing that they were in any respect of a different nature from these animals; but if there were machines bearing the image of our bodies, and capable of imitating our actions as far as it is morally possible, there would still remain two most certain tests whereby to know that they were not therefore really men. 1. [These machines] could never use words or other signs arranged in such a manner as is competent to us in order to declare our thoughts to others: for we may easily conceive a machine to be so constructed that it emits vocables, and even that it emits some correspondent to the action upon it of external objects which cause a change in its organs; for example, if touched in a particular place it may demand what we wish to say to it; if in another it may cry out that it is hurt, and such like; but not that it should arrange them variously so as appositely to reply to what is said in its presence, as men of the lowest grade of intellect can do. 2. Although such machines might execute many things with equal or perhaps greater perfection than any of us, they would, without doubt, fail in certain others from which it could be discovered that they did not act from knowledge, but solely from the disposition of their organs: for while Reason is an universal instrument that is alike available on every occasion, these organs, on the contrary, need a particular arrangement for each particular action; whence it must be morally impossible that there should exist in any machine a diversity of organs sufficient to enable it to act in all the occurrences of life, in the way in which our reason enables us to act."

I can paraphrase the first test confidently enough: a machine might be built to produce a fixed response to a fixed stimulus, but not to follow an open-ended conversation wherever it happens to go. It's the second test I can't quite pin down, and it matters more to me dramatically, since I'd like my Descartes to make exactly this argument to a skeptical interlocutor and I don't want the scene to rest on a claim I've only half understood. When I described it to a friend who works in machine learning, she said it reminded her of something called 'the frame problem,' though she couldn't get much further than a hunch about the family resemblance, and I'm not confident I understand that term well enough myself to judge whether she's right. Could you first walk through precisely what 'a particular arrangement for each particular action' and 'morally impossible' are doing in Descartes' second test — what exactly he thinks a machine cannot do, and why he thinks reason escapes the same limitation? Then, on its own terms rather than through my friend's hunch, explain what the frame problem actually is. Finally, I'd like your own considered judgment rather than a diplomatic hedge: does Descartes' second test genuinely anticipate the frame problem, or is that a flattering anachronism that glosses over a real difference between what he and later AI researchers were each worried about? If it's the latter, I'd like to know exactly where the two arguments part ways.
