I'm a novelist assembling a collection of short fiction in which each piece dramatizes a specific, technical philosophical problem rather than a familiar genre premise, and I'm stuck on the entry for philosophy of mind. The problem is Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument: every mental event is realized by some physical event, and the physical domain is causally closed, so any physical effect already has a sufficient physical cause. Since we reject systematic overdetermination, the mental event's own causal contribution seems to be squeezed out — it either just is its physical realizer, or it does no independent work at all. I want a story centered on an ordinary human character, not a robot or an AI, who is driven by the specifics of her own circumstances — not by reading a philosophy paper — to genuinely suspect that the second horn is true of her: that her deliberating, deciding, and wanting never actually move anything, and events would unfold identically without them. Please avoid the word "consciousness" and any "hard problem" framing — I want the exclusion argument's own logical structure (realization, closure, exclusion, no-overdetermination) to drive the plot, not serve as backdrop for a more generic mind-or-machine story. A self-contained piece of roughly 900–1400 words would be ideal.
