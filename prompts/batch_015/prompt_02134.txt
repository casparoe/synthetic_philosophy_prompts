This term I am co-teaching a graduate seminar on rational choice and the foundations of cooperation, cross-listed with our department's logic group, and the session that produced the most disagreement concerned the literature on "program equilibrium." The idea, introduced in Moshe Tennenholtz's 2004 paper of that name, is to change what a player submits to a game: instead of choosing an action, or even a mixed strategy, each player submits a computer program, and the programs are given mutual read-access to one another's source code before play. Tennenholtz showed that in the one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, two players who each submit what the later literature calls a "CliqueBot" — a program that cooperates if and only if the opponent's source code is syntactically identical to its own, and defects otherwise — are in Nash equilibrium, since deviating to any other program merely triggers mutual defection instead of mutual cooperation. He also proved a folk-theorem-style result: the set of payoffs achievable in some program equilibrium of a game coincides with the entire set of feasible, individually rational payoffs, so permanent mutual defection and lopsided outcomes that favor one player at the other's expense are just as supportable in equilibrium as fair cooperation is. A 2014 paper by Barasz, Christiano, Fallenstein, Herreshoff, LaVictoire, and Yudkowsky pushed the idea further with "FairBot," an agent that runs a bounded proof search over a shared formal system and cooperates with anything it can prove cooperates with it; by an application of Löb's theorem, two copies of FairBot can be shown to provably cooperate with each other without needing identical source code, which is what makes the result "robust" rather than a mere syntactic trick. (A further refinement, "PrudentBot," still cooperates with FairBot and with itself, but defects against unconditional cooperators, so as not to leave value on the table.)

Half the seminar wants to treat this as a genuine result in the philosophy of rational cooperation: a demonstration that self-interested agents who cannot communicate, rely on repeated play, or appeal to a third-party enforcer can nonetheless rationally reach the cooperative outcome in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, provided only that they can verify facts about each other's dispositions — a condition they take to be increasingly realistic for AI systems or smart contracts that can inspect one another's code before transacting. The other half thinks the whole apparatus is a shell game: since the folk theorem shows that almost any outcome, cooperative or otherwise, can be propped up as some program equilibrium once conditioning on the opponent's program is allowed, nothing in the mathematics itself explains why rational agents would converge on the fair, transparent, cooperative program rather than on one of the indefinitely many alternatives. On this view, program equilibrium does not solve the classical problem of how rational self-interest could underwrite cooperation; it merely re-describes that problem as a prior, unmodeled choice of which program to submit.

I would like a written verdict I can bring back to the seminar. Please write an essay that lays out the strongest version of each side, engages specifically with (a) the folk-theorem multiplicity result and (b) the contrast between the brittle, syntax-based CliqueBot solution and the provability-based, Löbian FairBot/PrudentBot solutions, and then reaches a definite conclusion about whether program equilibrium is a genuine solution to the problem of rational cooperation or merely a relocation of it. A conditional verdict is fine — for example, "it is a genuine solution given common knowledge of mutual transparency, and merely a relocation of the problem otherwise" — but it needs to be that specific, rather than settling for the conclusion that reasonable people can see it either way.
