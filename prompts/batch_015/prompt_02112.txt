Ok so I'm slowly building a collection of short historical fiction about thinkers who couldn't quite live by the logic of their own arguments, and the one I keep circling back to (and chickening out of) is Jeremy Bentham. Around 1785 he apparently wrote a manuscript essay -- something like 60 pages, part of what's sometimes catalogued under "Offences Against One's Self," though the bit everyone actually talks about is just referred to as "paederasty" (his word, not ours) -- where he runs consensual sex between men straight through his own felicific calculus and basically concludes there's no real pain in it anywhere, just what he elsewhere calls the "principle of sympathy and antipathy" dressed up as morality, so hanging men for it (the actual legal penalty in England at the time) was monstrous and indefensible on his own utilitarian terms. And then he just sat on the thing. Never published a word of it in his life, presumably because he figured it'd wreck his credibility as a law reformer and drag the rest of utilitiarism down with it -- it just sat among his papers until a scholar named Louis Crompton finally edited and published it properly in the late 1970s. I mentioned all this to my writing group as a possible next piece and got mostly blank stares, but I can't let it go.

What hooked me is the contrast with his actual personal life -- a romance in his twenties that his father put a stop to, then decades later a rejected marriage proposal mailed (never said in person) to a woman named Caroline Fox, and then, if the anecdote is to be believed, another awkward attempt to reach out to her when he was genuinely old -- all while he spent the last forty years of his life rattling around a big half-empty house in Westminster he liked to nickname "the Hermitage." I want a short story, maybe 1000-1500 words, set in that house late in his life, on some night when he's pulled the old manuscript back out of a drawer to reread or revise it and still can't make himself send it to a publisher. Feel free to invent a copyist, housekeeper, or visitor for him to play off of if that helps the scene breathe, and don't be precious about matching every biographical detail I just dumped on you -- but I really do want the philosophical spine of his actual argument (real pleasure and pain vs. mere inherited disgust) to come through properly, rather than just gestured at as "repressed old philosopher has a rough night."
