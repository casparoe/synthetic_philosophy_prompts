I'm a software engineer with an amateur interest in AI safety, and I keep running into so-called 'coherence arguments': the claim that any sufficiently competent agent that avoids dominated strategies — for instance, being money-pumped through a cycle of trades that leaves it strictly worse off — must behave as if it were maximizing expected utility with respect to some fixed utility function, and that this has real consequences for how we should expect advanced AI systems to behave. I've since learned that this conclusion is contested: some argue the underlying representation theorems are close to vacuous, since almost any behavior can be rationalized as maximizing the expectation of some utility function, while others argue that agents with genuinely incomplete preferences can resist money-pumps without ever becoming fully coherent. Write a dialogue, set during the Q&A after a conference talk on AI risk, between a researcher defending the strong version of the coherence argument and one who thinks it doesn't deliver on its promise, in which each is forced to directly answer the other's sharpest objection rather than simply restating their own position.
