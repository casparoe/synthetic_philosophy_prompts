In section 6 of "Computing Machinery and Intelligence," Turing considers nine objections to the claim that machines could think, and pushes back on all of them except the last. That one, the "Argument from Extra-Sensory Perception," gets different treatment: Turing accepts the empirical premise, stating that the statistical evidence for telepathy is "overwhelming" and calling the argument "quite a strong one." Rather than rebutting it, he patches his test, proposing that the competitors be seated in a "telepathy-proof room" to close off every channel of information except the typed questions and answers. This research tradition did not hold up: card-guessing studies of the kind Turing had in mind were later shown to be methodologically flawed at best, and in the well-documented case of S. G. Soal, one of the era's most prominent parapsychologists, deliberately manipulated at worst.
