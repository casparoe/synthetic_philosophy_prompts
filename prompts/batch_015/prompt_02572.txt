The passage below (translated from the German) is from Ludwig Boltzmann's 1897 reply to Ernst Zermelo, part of their dispute over whether the kinetic theory of gases can be reconciled with the second law of thermodynamics (Annalen der Physik 60 (1897), pp. 392–398). Trying to explain why entropy appears to increase even though the underlying mechanics is time-reversible, Boltzmann writes:

"Somewhere within a universe that, taken as a whole, rests in thermal equilibrium and is therefore dead, there must from time to time arise, here and there, comparatively small regions — on the order of the size of a single galaxy, which we may call 'worlds' — that depart markedly from equilibrium for a span of time that, cosmically speaking, is brief: a matter of eons. Among all such worlds, the probability of the state increases just as often as it decreases. Viewed from the standpoint of the universe as a whole, the two directions of time carry no distinguishing mark whatsoever, exactly as space itself has no privileged 'up' or 'down.' Yet just as, at some particular spot on the earth's surface, we are content to call 'down' whatever direction points toward the planet's centre, so too a living being who happens to occupy one of these worlds at some moment in its history can call 'past' whatever direction leads from less probable states to more probable ones, and 'future' the reverse of this; and having adopted that convention, such a being will inevitably find that its own small region, considered in isolation from the rest of the universe, looks — at the earliest moment accessible to it — like a highly improbable state."
