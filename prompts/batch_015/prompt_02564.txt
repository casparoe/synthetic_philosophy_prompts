So I'm about two-thirds through a historical novel set in a minor Italian city-state in the 1360s, and I've hit a scene I really want to get right. My protagonist, Costanza, is the daughter of the family that used to run the place before the current podestà -- who was only ever supposed to serve a one-year term -- simply never left, backed by his own hired swords, and has spent the years since squeezing the city half to death with taxes and reprisals. She goes to her confessor, a Dominican named Fra Silvestro, to ask, essentially, whether God would hold it against her if she helped get rid of him. I wanted Fra Silvestro to reason like an actual 14th-century Dominican who'd studied the Summa, not just spout generic "tyrants are bad, kill 'em" stuff, so I took a crack at his speech myself, working from what I remember of Aquinas on tyrannicide -- which, fair warning, is secondhand and a few years stale. Here's what I've got so far:

"My daughter, you ask if the man may be killed as one kills a wolf. Consider first how he came to rule us: not by the consent of the commune, nor by any right of succession, but by armed men in the piazza at night. A prince who is given his office by the people and only afterward turns cruel is owed a different reckoning than one who never held any office at all. For the first, the remedy belongs to those who made him -- the commune itself, or whatever authority stands above it -- and no private man may set himself up as its avenger, however just his grievance, for today it is a tyrant he strikes down and tomorrow it may be some lawful lord a hothead judges tyrannical besides. But the second sort of man is no prince at all in the eyes of God, only a robber grown large, and to fell him is no more a sin than felling any thief who waylays you on the road."

Could you look this over for accuracy and tell me how well it actually tracks Aquinas, rather then just the watered-down version of him that's floated around secondhand forever? Specifically: is the distinction Fra Silvestro's drawing -- usurper vs. legitimate-ruler-gone-bad -- really Aquinas's own, or am I unconsciously smushing him together with some later scholastic's gloss on him? And is he right that Aquinas wants the usurper case routed through something other than pure private judgment (a "tacit mandate," or however you'd phrase that for a friar who isn't talking like a law journal), or have I made that whole wrinkle up too? I'd rather catch this kind of thing now than get quietly side-eyed by some reader with a philosophy PhD in three years -- so any other spots where you think a real Thomist would wince are fair game as well.
