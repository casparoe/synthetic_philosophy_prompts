I'm part of a reading group on Confucian role ethics, and two members got into a running disagreement over Analects 13.18 -- where the Duke of She tells Confucius about "Upright Gong," who reported his own father to the authorities for stealing a sheep, and Confucius replies that the truly upright do the opposite: fathers and sons cover for each other, and uprightness (zhi) lies precisely in that concealment.

(1) Elena said this is a real problem for role ethics, not an awkward passage to explain away: Confucius isn't excusing a lapse, he's redefining "uprightness" so that shielding your father from the law counts as the upright act itself. A theory where the rightness of reporting a crime depends on your relation to the criminal has swapped impartial ethics for tribal loyalty, however "relational personhood" dresses it up.

(2) Marcus said she was conflating two acts: Confucius never says the son should lie or hide the sheep, only that not volunteering testimony against your own father isn't a vice -- and most legal systems grant close kin a similar testimonial privilege.

(3) Elena answered that the effect is the same either way: the victim gets no remedy, and the text doesn't call concealment a regrettable cost of loyalty -- it calls concealment itself the content of uprightness. That's not a carve-out from an independent standard; it's letting kinship fix what the standard is.

(4) Marcus replied that this is only damning if role ethics owes one uniform standard indifferent to who the parties are -- which is exactly what it denies. The tradition isn't silent on the theft either: elsewhere (4.18) a son must remonstrate with an erring parent, gently but persistently, so correction happens inside the relationship, not through the magistrate.

(5) Elena said role ethics then owes a principled stopping point, since "keep it in the family" is fine for a sheep and terrible for a murder. With no way to say when the duty must yield, this isn't an account of uprightness at all, just loyalty with better branding.

(6) Marcus answered that the tradition stages exactly that case: asked what Shun should do if his own father killed someone, Mencius says Shun's minister of justice would simply arrest him, while Shun himself would resign the throne and flee with his father into hiding rather than use his power to quash the charge -- a real limiting principle, even if one might draw the loyalty/law line elsewhere.

Go through this move by move: say who has the better of it at each point and why, not just who wins overall, then close with a short paragraph on what the whole exchange misses about Confucian role ethics.
