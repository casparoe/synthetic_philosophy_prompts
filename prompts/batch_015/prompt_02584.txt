I'm a backend engineer, and over the past year or so I've gotten fairly absorbed in AI safety writing, especially the more technical material connected to the Machine Intelligence Research Institute. One result that comes up again and again in that literature is the so-called "Löbian obstacle": because of Löb's theorem, a formal system generally cannot prove, from within itself, the schema "if my proof system can prove X, then X is true" for any X it hasn't already established directly — trying to license that kind of blanket self-trust either just fails to go through, or, in the limiting case, forces the system to certify its own inconsistency. I brought this up recently with two colleagues who work on formal verification of our distributed consensus protocols, and we all came away with the same reading: that this is essentially a hard mathematical proof that a reasoning system can never trust a future or successor version of itself in any load-bearing way, which would mean a self-modifying AI built on formal proof, rather than on heuristics we simply hope will generalize, can never safely authorize an upgrade to its own reasoning, since it would always be handing control to a process whose soundness it cannot verify in advance.

Given that Löb's theorem seems to settle this so cleanly, I'm puzzled about why MIRI-adjacent researchers keep publishing new frameworks aimed at exactly this problem — tiling agents built on descending hierarchies of successively weaker theories, probabilistic reflection, logical induction, and so on — rather than treating the matter as closed. It feels a bit like continuing to propose new general algorithms for the halting problem after Turing's proof: once you have an honest impossibility result for the goal itself, further cleverness in how you approach it shouldn't matter. So why does this remain an active research area instead of a settled dead end, and is "provably safe self-modifying AI" simply off the table as a coherent goal?
