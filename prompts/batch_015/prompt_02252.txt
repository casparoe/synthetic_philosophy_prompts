Retired biochemist here, spending my golden years falling down philosophy rabbit holes instead of running gels. Lately I've been chewing on Avicenna's 'flying man' thought experiment - the fella who's conjured fully grown, floating in a void, limbs spread apart so they can't even brush each other, deaf, blind, no smell or taste, nothing. Avicenna says this guy would still affirm 'I exist' with total certainty even though he cant affirm he has a body at all, and from that he concludes the self is known directly, seperate from the body and senses entirely - basically an argument for the soul being its own immaterial substance.

Thing is, that sits real awkward next to what people like Bud Craig and Anil Seth have dug up about interoception - the idea that our whole sense of being a self is stitched together out of internal body signals (heartbeat, gut, breathing, temperature) constantly being predicted by the brain. Seth's 'beast machine' theory basically says selfhood just IS that regulatory process, not something hovering free of it. Avicenna's setup never says anything about switching off the flying man's pulse or breathing, so is there a loophole there where interoceptive signals sneak in and give the game away? Or does his system actually have the resources to handle this (he's got separate vegetative/animal/rational souls, after all)?

Work through, on Avicenna's own terms, how you think he'd respond to this. Skip the generic 'medieval guy didn't know neuroscience' answer - actually dig into the logical structure of the argument and tell me whether there's a real gap or a way round it.
