I'm a PhD student in philosophy currently writing a seminar paper on Pascal's political thought for a graduate course on early modern conceptions of law and sovereignty, and I've gotten stuck on how to reconcile two fragments from the Pensées that our instructor placed side by side. The first is the well-known fragment on "Justice, force" (Lafuma 103 / Brunschvicg 298), where Pascal argues that justice without force is powerless and force without justice is tyrannical, and concludes that, because justice is subject to endless dispute while force is immediately recognizable, we have never managed to make what is just strong, but have instead brought it about that what is strong is just. The second is a nearby fragment (Lafuma 60 / Brunschvicg 294) that Pascal's own bundle classification places under "Misère" rather than under "Raisons des effets," where the first fragment sits; it contains the claim that custom "creates the whole of equity" simply because it is accepted, plus the famous observation that a few degrees of latitude can overturn an entire jurisprudence, so that there is truth on one side of the Pyrenees and error on the other.

Two of my classmates left that session with flatly opposed views of what these fragments amount to. One reads Pascal as essentially anticipating a Hobbesian, even error-theoretic, view of justice: there is no independent standard against which force and custom answer, "justice" simply names whatever entrenched power has settled on, and the Pyrenees remark is meant perfectly straight, as a first-order claim about law. The other insists that this misses the apologetic architecture of the whole project: these fragments are doing work within Pascal's larger case for the wretchedness of man without God, so the moral is not that justice is a fiction but that unaided reason cannot reach the one real justice belonging to what Pascal elsewhere calls the radically incommensurable "order of charity" — and that the word "tyrannical" only makes sense at all if some such standard is already smuggled in.

Could you write me a dialogue between these two readers — call them the Conventionalist and the Augustinian — in which they actually engage each other rather than talk past one another? I'd like them to be equally sharp, responding to each other's strongest moves point by point, and getting into specifics: how each handles the normatively loaded word "tyrannical," whether the bundle a fragment was filed under should carry any interpretive weight given that the Pensées were unfinished at Pascal's death, and whether Pascal's fideism even leaves room for there to be a single determinately correct answer here. They don't need to reach agreement; I'd just like the exchange to leave me clearer on what would have to be true of Pascal's larger system for one side rather than the other to be right.
