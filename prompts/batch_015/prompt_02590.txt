I write about science and ideas for a living, and while reporting a piece on confidence coaching in college sports I watched an exchange between a basketball coach and one of his players that's been stuck in my head ever since.

The coach has his players write down and say out loud every morning "I am a great shooter," even the ones stuck in shooting slumps under 30% from the field, because he says the belief itself changes your mechanics, and thats backed by real research on confidence and performance. The player, we'll call her J, pushed back: she's not going to lie to herself, and if she walks around already believing she's great, won't she stop grinding on the actual flaws in her form? He said it's not lying — she can still review film and fix mechanics in practice and walk into the game believing she's great, the affirmation is just for headspace in the moment of the shot. She said fine, but then why word it as "I am a great shooter" instead of something like "I'm going to make this shot" — if the exact phrasing matters that much, doesn't that prove whatever's doing the work has nothing to do with the sentence being true? He admitted the phrasing's deliberate — identity-level statements change behavior more than process ones do, same reason placebo pills work even without good evidence the pill does anything. Her comeback: a placebo works partly because the patient doesn't know it's a placebo, whereas she knows exactly why she's saying this sentence, so how's she even supposed to pull off believing it instead of just reciting it?

Neither one budged. Can you go through it move by move, say who's actually right at each step, and whether there's something bigger going on here that both of them are missing?
