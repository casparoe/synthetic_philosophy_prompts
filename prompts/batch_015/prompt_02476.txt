Quick one from a rabbit hole I fell into last week while researching a piece on paradoxes that still bite philosophers even once they think they understand them. I'd always known Fitch's "paradox of knowability" in the vague pop-philosophy form -- something like "if every truth were knowable, every truth would be known, gotcha verificationists" -- but then I actually sat down with the SEP entry and part of Salerno's "New Essays" collection, and it turns out to be way weirder, and also apparently not even originally Fitch's: the proof was handed to him anonymously in 1945 by a referee rejecting one of his papers, and nobody figured out the referee was Alonzo Church until 2005. Wild bit of trivia, though that's besides the point.

What I actually want help with is the part of the debate that comes after that. The move that keeps showing up as the anti-realist's best comeback is to reject classical logic in favor of intuitionistic logic, since the last step of Fitch's proof (getting from "there's no unknown truth" to "every truth is known") needs double-negation elimination, which intuitionists don't get for free -- so instead they're stuck with the much less embarassing "every truth is not-not known," and they redescribe our ordinary sense that "not everyone knows everything" as a sentence that's only classically (not intuitionistically) equivalent to the one Fitch's proof actually rules out. Except then I read that Percival built a parallel argument using "undecided" statements instead of unknown ones that boxes the intuitionist into basically the same corner a second time, forcing the same reinterpretation trick again -- and on top of that, the view seems committed to saying that if nobody ever finds out p, that alone is enough to make p false, for perfectly ordinary empirical claims. At some point it starts to look like the anti-realist just keeps re-translating plain English into weaker and weaker cousins every time the paradox comes back in a new costume, rather than actually solving anything.

So: could you write me a dialogue -- real back and forth, not two people talking past each other -- between two equally sharp philosophers arguing exactly this out? One thinks going intuitionist is a principled, independently-motivated way for the anti-realist to survive Fitch's paradox; the other thinks it's an ad hoc rescue that has to be rebuilt from scratch every time a new version of the argument shows up (Percival's included), and that swallowing "unknown truths are false" is a real cost rather than a footnote. I want them actually fighting over the logic -- what's doing the work at the double-negation step, whether the redescription move is principled or just question-begging, and whether the empirical-discourse consequence sinks the view -- rather than staying at the level of vibes. Neither one should be there just to lose; make them both land real punches on each other.
