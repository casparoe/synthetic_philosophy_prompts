I'm choosing a research question for my IB Extended Essay in Philosophy, and I keep coming back to a puzzle in the Second Essay of Nietzsche's *On the Genealogy of Morality*. Early on, Nietzsche describes the "sovereign individual"—the ripened endpoint of the long, often brutal process by which the morality of custom bred a calculable, promise-keeping animal out of prehistoric humanity—in strikingly admiring terms, as an autonomous being who has broken free of custom and finally earned the right to his own independent will. Read in isolation, this sounds like Nietzsche's positive ideal of freedom and self-mastery. But it sits oddly next to the rest of the essay, which treats guilt, bad conscience, and the ascetic ideal as forms of internalized cruelty that Nietzsche views with suspicion rather than admiration, even though the sovereign individual is itself a product of that very disciplinary history. Nietzsche scholars are genuinely split on this: some take the sovereign individual to be Nietzsche's own picture of real freedom, while others argue that this reading gets the passage backwards, and that the figure remains enmeshed in, rather than liberated from, the moral psychology the genealogy sets out to diagnose.

Write an essay addressing this question directly: is the sovereign individual best read as Nietzsche's positive ideal, or as a target of his critique? Weigh the strongest textual and philosophical considerations on each side, and defend a definite conclusion rather than a split-the-difference answer, since I need an actual thesis for the essay, not just a survey of both readings.
