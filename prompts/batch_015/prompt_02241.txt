So we just finished a unit on Epicurus in my philosophy elective and it kicked off this huge argument between two people in my class, Nate and Sofia, that spilled into the hallway after the bell rang. Nate said Epicurus is basically right: death can't be bad for you because there's no "you" left to be harmed once it happens ("where death is, I am not; where I am, death is not"), so being scared of it doesn't actually make sense. Sofia said that only works if harm always requires someone around experiencing it at the time, and brought up the Nagel reading -- if your friends trash talk you behind your back and you never find out, you're still harmed even though you never feel it, so death works the same way: it's bad because it deprives you of the life you would've had, not because it hurts. Nate came back with: ok, but then when exactly is that deprivation bad for you? not while you're alive (death hasn't happened yet), and not after (you don't exist to have anything be bad for you, including a deprivation). Sofia said it doesn't need one single moment -- the badness attaches to the specific years you're missing out on, and those years still have a spot on the timeline even if you're not around anymore to occupy it. Nate said that felt like a dodge but granted it anyway, then brought up Lucretius: if losing future time is what makes death bad, we should feel just as bad about all the time before we were born that we also "missed," and since basicaly nobody does, somethings inconsistent. Sofia's comeback was that there's a real asymmetry there, not just a double standard -- most people think it's rational to care more about future suffering than past suffering (like, given a choice between a bad hour of pain that already happened yesterday or a shorter bad hour tomorrow, most people would rather it already be over), so caring more about future non-existence than past non-existence fits a bigger pattern instead of being some special exception just to justify fearing death.
