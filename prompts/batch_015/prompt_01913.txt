Random rabbit hole from prepping a phil of bio seminar: Darwin was genuinely tormented by the peacock's tail, theres that great line in his 1860 letter to Asa Gray about how the sight of a peacock feather "makes me sick," because it seemed to cut against natural selection. His eventual fix, sexual selection via a female "taste for the beautiful," is basically an aesthetic story. Wallace hated it and insisted instead that showy traits had to be honest cues to vigor and vitality, not mere prettiness -- they had a real falling out over it, including one legendary weekend in 1868 spent arguing that settled nothing. Zahavi's handicap principle, a century later, kind of vindicates Wallace: its the wastefulness of the signal that makes it a reliable indicator of quality. So, if you sat Darwin down and walked him through Zahavi (plus the fact that it eventually got a rigorous game-theoretic backing), does he concede the point to Wallace, or does he dig in on the aesthetic-preference story, which honestly is closer to what Fisher's later runaway model formalizes? Actually work through the reasoning he'd have had to go through given what he was already committed to in the Descent of Man -- don't just hand me vibes.
