I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, but I've spent some evenings this month reading about the mathematical foundations of preference aggregation after a debate at work about how to combine reward models trained on different annotator pools. That led me to Harsanyi's aggregation theorem, and I got excited enough to draft a short post connecting it to RLHF. Before I put this anywhere public, I'd like your comments on it — specifically on whether I've represented the theorem accurately, whether the central analogy actually holds up structurally, and on any well-known objections to this style of argument that I seem to be missing. Here's the draft:

Harsanyi's Theorem and the Reward Model We Already Have

In 1955, John Harsanyi proved something that sounds almost too clean to be true. Suppose a group of individuals each have preferences over risky outcomes that satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, so each one is representable as maximizing an expected utility. Suppose "society," or some observer standing in for it, also has preferences over the same outcomes, satisfying those same axioms. Add one modest bridging assumption: whenever every individual is indifferent between two options, society is indifferent too. From just that, Harsanyi showed that the social utility function has to be a weighted sum of the individual utility functions. Nothing about interpersonal comparisons of welfare, or a prior commitment to utilitarianism, was assumed going in — the linear, additive structure falls out as a mathematical consequence of the axioms.

Reading this, I couldn't shake the feeling that it describes exactly what happens when we train a reward model for RLHF. We collect a large number of pairwise preference judgments from many annotators, fit a single scalar reward function to the pooled data (usually via something in the Bradley–Terry family), and then optimize a policy against that one reward signal. Individual preferences go in; a single aggregate "social" utility function comes out; and the aggregation is linear in the relevant sense, since the fitting objective is essentially additive across annotators' judgments. That looks like the same shape as Harsanyi's setup.

If the analogy holds, it seems to say something stronger than "linear pooling is a convenient default." It suggests that pooling everyone's preference data into one additive reward objective is close to the uniquely rational way to aggregate it — not an arbitrary implementation choice, but something with the same kind of forced, axiomatic backing as expected-utility maximization itself. On this view, proposals to combine reward signals in some more exotic, nonlinear way — to protect a minority of users, say, or to weight annotator groups unevenly on other grounds — either quietly reject one of Harsanyi's premises or misunderstand what coherent aggregation requires.

I know actual annotators aren't idealized vNM agents and a trained reward model is only an approximate fit, so I'm not claiming the theorem applies with mathematical exactness. My claim is narrower: that it gives the standard "pool the comparisons, fit one linear reward" recipe a real justification, rather than it just being the path of least implementation resistance.

I'd appreciate it if you could push on this: have I stated the theorem correctly, does the RLHF mapping preserve whatever actually does the work in Harsanyi's proof, and — independent of the RLHF angle — is there a well-known reason economists and philosophers don't treat the original theorem as settling debates about preference aggregation the way I'm using it here?
