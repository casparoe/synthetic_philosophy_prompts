I'm part of a small decision-theory reading group that grew out of the old LessWrong and Overcoming Bias comment sections, and a disagreement flared up at our last session that nobody managed to settle. We had just worked through Daniel Kokotajlo's 2019 post "The Commitment Races problem" together with the comment thread in which Eliezer Yudkowsky pushes back on it, and two of the regulars, Naomi and Theo, went several rounds on whether the problem is real for anything we'd actually call rational, or only for agents that are already making a mistake.

(1) Naomi restated the worry: in bargaining-like situations, whoever locks in a strategy earliest controls the outcome -- the way committing to never swerve first wins a game of Chicken -- and since every agent benefits from committing before its counterpart does, while careful reasoning takes time, agents are pushed toward acting on incomplete reasoning, which can leave two asynchronous commitments locked into a mutually ruinous outcome that neither side would endorse on reflection. (2) Theo objected that this rests on a mistake: nothing forces a genuinely rational agent to treat "who committed first" as relevant, since you can instead fix, once and for all, a general policy for responding to demands -- for instance, meeting any demand for more than a fair split with a randomized response calibrated so that overreaching never pays better in expectation than requesting the fair split outright -- and such a policy is indifferent to the order in which anyone announces anything, so the race only threatens agents unsophisticated enough to be moved by a mere appearance of prior commitment. (3) Naomi granted this for an idealized, logically omniscient agent, but said it doesn't transfer to anything real, since working out the right response policy takes deliberation, and during that deliberation you are exactly the sort of half-formed agent that benefits from grabbing a commitment now rather than risking exploitation while still thinking -- knowing a solution exists in principle doesn't tell a bounded agent what to do before it has found it. (4) Theo replied that this just relocates the fix: an agent smart enough to worry about commitment races is smart enough to adopt, cheaply and early, a standing policy against locking in ad hoc commitments while still reasoning, committing only once it has reached something it would endorse on reflection, so anything capable of running Naomi's own argument already has what it needs to resist the race. (5) Naomi answered that this only helps if unilaterally adopting patience is itself safe, which was exactly what was in question: if committing early sometimes does secure a better outcome against a counterpart who is still patiently deliberating, a population of otherwise sophisticated agents still faces pressure to defect from patience, and Theo had not explained why that pressure would evaporate rather than reproduce the original race one level up, among meta-policies rather than demands.

Neither of them budged, and I've gone back and forth myself without landing anywhere. Could you adjudicate the exchange move by move rather than simply picking an overall winner?
