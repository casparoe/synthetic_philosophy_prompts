I have been mulling over something from a work incident and would appreciate an outside perspective, particularly since I have no formal training in philosophy or probability theory and suspect there is an established way of thinking about this that I am simply not aware of. For most of last year, our checkout service degraded in a very specific and fully reproducible way: elevated latency and occasional dropped requests, but only during the first fifteen minutes after a deploy, only once traffic crossed a particular threshold, and only on our two busiest release days each week. We documented this pattern in exhaustive detail across three separate postmortems, debated possible root causes without resolution, eventually shipped some defensive retry logic to mask the symptom, and moved on. Every one of those quirks was, by that point, old and thoroughly settled information, known with complete confidence by the entire team.

Some time later, a senior engineer joined, read through the full incident history, and about a week afterward returned with a specific mechanism, something to do with how our connection-pool warmup interacts with a cache-invalidation path under load, that would, if correct, produce exactly that pattern: the same fifteen-minute window, the same threshold, the same two release days. She regards this fit as strong confirmation of her theory and wants to use it to justify a fairly expensive rewrite of the pooling layer. One colleague backs her up, arguing that it would be a remarkable coincidence for an incorrect mechanism to reproduce every one of those independent quirks, so the fit must count as genuine evidence. Another colleague is unconvinced, maintaining that explaining data we were already certain of before she proposed anything is essentially free, and that her theory deserves real credit only once it predicts something new that none of us has yet observed. I described the disagreement to a friend who studied some philosophy in college, and she said it reminded her of a debate from philosophy of science, though she couldn't recall the details.

I keep going back and forth on who is right. Is there a principled way to think about how much a theory that merely accounts for facts we already knew with certainty should move our confidence, compared to one that is subsequently tested against something genuinely new? Or is the distinction I'm drawing less meaningful than it actually is?
