David Lewis's paper "Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?" (Philosophical Papers, 1997) argues that ordinary thinking about punishment is quietly inconsistent. We don't believe a devoted friend could serve a burglar's prison sentence or a murderer's death sentence in his place — the offer strikes us as senseless. Yet we don't object when a friend pays someone else's fine, even a serious fine that functions as real punishment rather than a parking-meter-style fee. Lewis considers the obvious explanation — that we tolerate fine-substitution only because it's practically impossible to prevent, since nothing stops a friend from simply gifting the money — and rejects it: if we were principled opponents of substitution, the unpreventability of fine-substitution should register as a standing complaint against fines as a mode of punishment, and it doesn't. He concludes that everyone, not just Christians who read the Atonement as penal substitution, is "of two minds" here, which he thinks hands the Christian a fair tu quoque against anyone pressing the incoherence charge.

Below are my notes toward an essay resisting that tu quoque.

1. The real disanalogy isn't practical but conceptual: fines are paid in a fungible medium, while imprisonment, flogging, and death are not. Swapping one dollar for another leaves whatever "message" a fine sends completely intact, since dollars carry no identity; swapping one body for another cannot leave that message intact, since the offender's body isn't fungible with anyone else's.

2. Self-objection: Lewis already notes that even when a friend pays the fine, the offender still gets the "opprobrium" and merely trades his debt of punishment for a "debt of gratitude" to the friend — so the fine case isn't full substitution either; something non-fungible still sticks to the offender. That threatens to collapse my distinction into one of degree, not kind.

3. Further complication: in Lewis's own imagined variants — a burglar sentenced to serve as the victim's personal slave, or hanged as a spectacle for the victim's pleasure — imprisonment and death are made to serve a compensatory function, and he grants that substitution would then make sense. Compensatory function and fungibility look like independent variables, so it's unclear whether fungibility is doing any real work here, or whether "serves a compensatory function" was the operative criterion all along, one that ordinary fines happen to satisfy too.

I'd like comments on three things: whether (1) survives the objection in (2); whether (3) shows I've misdiagnosed what's actually driving Lewis's own verdict about fines; and whether there's a cleaner line between the punishments we do and don't tolerate substitution for that I've missed entirely. I'd also appreciate a flag on anywhere I've misrepresented Lewis's dialectic.
