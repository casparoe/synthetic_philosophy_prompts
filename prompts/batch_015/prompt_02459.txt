I'm at work on a novel whose narrator, Elena, is a former derivatives trader who has quit the industry and taken up reading economics and epistemology in the manner of someone trying to justify a grudge. In one scene I want her to unload, at a dinner party full of her old colleagues, an argument she has built around what she calls "the no-trade theorem" — the result, associated with Aumann and later with Milgrom and Stokey, that if two people are both rational, share the same prior beliefs, and it is common knowledge between them that each is rational and knows the terms of a proposed bet, then neither should be willing to take the other side of a wager motivated purely by private information. As she explains it to the table, the very act of offering the bet reveals what you know, so your counterpart updates immediately, you know they'll update, they know you know it, and the whole apparent opportunity for profit unravels before a dollar changes hands.

Elena's punchline, delivered with some relish, is that real markets plainly do not look like this: billions of shares change hands every day, and, closer to the novel's own plot, so does money in a prediction market whose referendum odds she's been obsessively refreshing. She concludes that the theorem must mean the traders on the other side of her old desk were almost all either irrational or quietly acting on information they had no business having, and that anyone who still talks about prices "aggregating information" — whether about a stock or about how a country is going to vote — is describing a mechanism that its own founding theorems already show can't work.

I find the monologue satisfying to write, but I would rather not put an error into Elena's mouth that I haven't noticed myself, as opposed to one I've placed there on purpose. Does her inference from the theorem to her conclusion actually hold up, or is she resting on some assumption — about which kinds of trades the theorem is even about, or about how demanding "common knowledge" really is in practice — that quietly gives way once you look at it closely? And is she entitled to treat the sheer existence of trading volume as evidence against the idea that market prices, including the referendum odds she can't stop watching, are doing real epistemic work?
