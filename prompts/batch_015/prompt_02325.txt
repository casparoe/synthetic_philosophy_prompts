The thing that's been bugging me specifically is the money-pump argument for the completeness axiom, which is sneakier then the standard transitivity one everyone learns first (the A>B>C>A cycle where you trade your way back to the start minus a few bucks). The completeness version starts from a genuine preferential gap between two options — say, moving to a big city for a higher-paying job versus staying near family in a smaller town on less money, where you don't prefer either option to the other but you're also not indifferent between them — and then walks you through a sequence of trades, each one sweetened with a tiny bonus, that ends with you strictly worse off than where you started. The obvious dodge is "just refuse the sweetener when they try to trade you back," but apparently that doesn't fully work, because if it's a genuine gap you have no standing preference for refusing over accepting either, so nothing in your preferences actually mandates the refusal — some versions of the argument are set up precisely so that going along at each step is never irrational by your own lights, even though the whole sequence together is obviously bad for you.

The move I keep seeing to escape this is resolute choice: you commit to a plan ahead of time (e.g. "never trade away from my starting option") and just stick to it even when the sweetener shows up later, instead of re-evaluating trade by trade. I ran this by my advisor and she said it just relocates the completeness requirement rather than avoiding it — now you need a determinate preference for the "stick to the plan" plan over all the alternative plans, and it's not obvious where that determinacy is supposed to come from if your local preferences had gaps in them to begin with.
