I'm taking a philosophy of physics seminar this semester, and we just finished the unit on Bell's theorem after several weeks spent building up through the EPR paper and the CHSH inequality. Something my professor said in passing during the last lecture has been bothering me since. She mentioned that the notion of "locality" Bell's theorem actually rules out is not quite the same as the notion invoked whenever popular accounts reassure us that entanglement is compatible with relativity. To make the point, she introduced the terms "parameter independence" and "outcome independence" as the two conditions that jointly constitute the factorizability assumption behind Bell's inequality, and noted that only one of the two is really responsible for blocking faster-than-light signaling — but she ran out of time before explaining which, or why. We're now supposed to read a chapter from Tim Maudlin's book on quantum nonlocality and its relationship to relativity before the topic comes up again, and I'd like to arrive at that reading already having a grip on the distinction rather than just nodding along in class.

Here is what I cannot reconcile on my own: textbooks insist that no information can be sent faster than light using entangled particles, so that relativity's core prohibition survives quantum mechanics unscathed, yet in the same breath Bell's theorem is described as proving that the world is "nonlocal," since no local hidden-variable theory can reproduce the observed correlations. Could you carefully compare the kind of locality that Bell's inequality actually tests, spelled out through the parameter independence/outcome independence distinction, with the no-signaling condition that supposedly keeps relativity safe? Specifically, I'd like to know whether these two amount to the same requirement wearing different clothes, or whether one is strictly weaker than the other — and, if so, which of the two you think is the philosophically relevant sense of "locality" when physicists and philosophers claim that Bell's theorem forces us to give up locality altogether.
