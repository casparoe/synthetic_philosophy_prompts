So I went down a Kavka's toxin puzzle rabbit hole this week after someone at a rationalist meetup brought it up as basically "Newcomb's problem but for intentions," and now I'm annoyed because I don't think I actually get why its supposed to be hard, as opposed to just going "well obviously you form the intention and then drink the stuff anyway, FDT says so" and moving on with my life.

From poking around, there's apparently a whole zoo of proposed "solutions" that I can't quite tell apart. There's Gauthier's argument that once you sincerely intend to drink it you literally can't reconsider tomorrow, since the rational thing to do is whatever's compatible with your commitment; there's McClennen's "resolute choice" thing, which I gather is supposed to be different from Gauthier's even though people usually cite them as a package (I saw a paper that literally calls the combo "the Rationalist Solution," which made me do a double take given the crowd I run with online); and then seperately there's Bratman's whole planning-theory-of-intention response, where intentions have their own stability/inertia and aren't just predictions of what you'll do, which feels to me like a genuinely different move rather than a variant of resolute choice -- but I'm not confident I'm carving that up correctly.

What I'd really appreciate help with is this: the standard LW-ish move of "just be the kind of agent whose policy is to follow through, problem dissolved" feels like it might be answering a slightly different question than the one Kavka actually asked. My understanding is his own point was less "is it rational to drink the toxin tomorrow" and more "can you even sincerely form the intention tonight, given you already know you won't want to follow through later" -- i.e. it's supposed to be a puzzle about the connection between intention and self-prediction, and about intending and acting coming apart, not just a puzzle about whether some action is rational. Does adopting an FDT/UDT-style resolution actually engage with that specific worry, or does it just route around it by redefining the question as "what policy should an idealized agent have"? Would love it if someone actually walked through the mechanics here rather than re-explaining the eccentric-billionaire setup, which I've got down by now.
