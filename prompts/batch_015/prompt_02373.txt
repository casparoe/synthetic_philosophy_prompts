I've been reworking the akrasia unit of my moral psychology seminar, and there's a comparison I keep gesturing at in lecture without ever quite nailing down. It has started to bother me enough that I'd like to think it through properly, in essay form. Aristotle devotes most of Nicomachean Ethics VII to the puzzle Socrates had raised: how can a person act against what she herself judges, at the very moment of acting, to be best? His answer turns on a distinction between having knowledge and exercising it: the incontinent person, on his account, possesses the relevant knowledge only in the way a sleeping, drunk, or mad person does, which is why she can recite the right principle — Aristotle's own example is a drunk man reciting the verses of Empedocles — without any of it doing practical work. On the more technical version of the story, the practical syllogism at issue has two premises: a universal one telling her that sweet things are not to be tasted, and a particular one, delivered by perception and captured by appetite, telling her that this thing here is sweet; it is the second, appetite-driven premise that actually issues in action, while the universal one sits idle. Aristotle then complicates his own picture by distinguishing two forms of incontinence: impetuosity, in which the agent acts straight from passion without ever deliberating, and weakness proper, in which the agent does deliberate and settle on a resolution, only to abandon it afterward under the pull of passion.

Contemporary decision theory offers what can look like a tidy formal descendant of this very story: hyperbolic (or quasi-hyperbolic) discounting, on which an agent's ranking of a smaller-sooner reward over a larger-later one reverses as the smaller reward draws near in time — the mechanism behavioral scientists since Ainslie have invoked to explain procrastination, addiction, and failures of impulse control, and which has generated the now-standard distinction between "sophisticated" agents, who foresee their own future reversals, and "naive" ones, who don't. But the equation of the two stories is not obviously right. Richard Holton, for one, has argued that weakness of will proper is specifically the failure to maintain a resolution one has already formed, in the face of a subsequent shift in one's judgment about what is best — and that the discounting model, by itself, does not guarantee that any resolution was ever formed, let alone broken, since it specifies only how the agent's evaluative ranking changes over time, not whether she had bound herself in advance to resist that very change.

Write an essay comparing Aristotle's account of akrasia with the hyperbolic-discounting model of temporal preference reversal. Do the two frameworks really pick out the same phenomenon, and does the discounting model correspond to both of Aristotle's forms of incontinence, or only to one of them, if either? Weigh the relevant considerations carefully, but don't leave the matter unresolved: state and defend which of the two frameworks — or which suitably qualified combination of them — gives the more defensible and complete account of genuine weakness of will, and specify what, if anything, an episode of temporal preference reversal would additionally have to involve in order to count as akrasia in Aristotle's own sense.
