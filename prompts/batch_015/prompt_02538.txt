So I fell down a rationalist-blog rabbit hole on the history of Bayesian epistemology recently and got reminded of Frank Ramsey (the Dutch-book/subjective-probability guy, died at 26 in 1930, kind of a patron saint around here). Turns out he's also got this one footnote in "General Propositions and Causality" where he basically invents what's now called the Ramsey Test for conditionals: to evaluate "if p then q" you hypothetically add p to your stock of beliefs and see how confident that leaves you in q.

Fast forward to 1976, decades after Ramsey died, and David Lewis proves that if you actually try to formalize that as an equation, P(if p then q) = P(q given p), holding across the board for all p and q, the whole framework goes "trivial" — your probability space collapses down to something degenerate that can barely distinguish any propositions at all. Its usually read as showing indicative conditionals can't just be ordinary propositions whose truth-values line up with conditional probability the way Ramsey's test wants them to.

Obviously Ramsey never got to grapple with this. Based on everything you know about how the guy actually thought — his pragmatism, his betting-behavior take on belief, his general comfort with junking a concept once it stops earning its keep — what's your best guess at how he'd have reacted to Lewis basically detonating his own test? Bite the bullet, jump to something like Stalnaker's possible-worlds account instead, deny conditionals express propositions in the first place, or find some other escape route? Give me your actual best model of the guy, not just a rundown of what later philosophers said about it.
