ok so I'm technically a phil of mind person but I got roped into a Wittgenstein reading group this term, and we just spent two sessions on the private language material (243 through the beetle-in-a-box bit around 293). Something's been bugging me that nobody in the group could talk me out of. The diary case in 258 seems airtight to me: if all I've got is my own memory to check whether I'm using "S" correctly for some private sensation, then there's no actual difference between "I'm using S correctly" and "it seems to me like I'm using S correctly," so the whole right/wrong distinction just evaporates. Fine, I buy that part.

But here's my issue -- doesn't that same reasoning apply just as well to any solitary language user, not just someone naming inner sensations? Take a Robinson Crusoe type who's alone on an island from birth and invents his own words for the trees and tools around him, zero contact with anyone else, ever. He's checking his current use of a word against nothing but his own memory of what he "meant" before, same as the sensation-diarist is. So it seems like Wittgenstein's argument, if it actually works, proves the much stronger (and kind of absurd-sounding) claim that solitary, never-shared language is impossible in principal, full stop -- not just that inner-sensation words specifically are suspect. Is that really the conclusion he's committed to, or is there some principled reason Crusoe gets to keep his language while the sensation-diarist doesn't? Feels like there should be a difference somewhere but I can't quite put my finger on what's actually doing the work.
