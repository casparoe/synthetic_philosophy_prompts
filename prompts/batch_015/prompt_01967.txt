I am a philosophy PhD student working on decision theory, and I have been following the recent dispute over whether "coherence arguments" really show that any rational agent -- or any sufficiently capable artificial one -- must be representable as an expected-utility maximizer. The dispute resurfaced a few years ago in the AI safety literature, but I would like to see its strongest form actually argued out rather than summarized.

Write a dialogue between two decision theorists, both fully conversant with the literature, who disagree about this. One defends coherence arguments: he maintains that an agent with cyclic preferences can be turned into a sure loss by a money pump, that a structurally similar pump can be built against mere incompleteness (not just intransitivity), and that a sequential version of the Allais paradox shows that any agent whose preferences violate independence can be walked, step by step, into a strategy dominated by one available to it at the outset. The other thinks this proves far less than advertised: she argues that the incompleteness pump smuggles in a substantive "trade whenever the alternative is not strictly worse" disposition that an incomplete agent is free to reject, and that Edward McClennen's resolute choice -- evaluating whole plans rather than choosing node by node -- blocks the sequential pump without making the agent's preferences expected-utility-representable.

Have them work through a concrete case, with real stand-in options and trades rather than bare labels, and have each press the other on whether their favored move is a principled fix or an ad hoc patch designed only to dodge the argument. Don't let either concede by the end.
