I'm a backend engineer, not a philosopher, but a few of us at work run a biweekly interpretability reading group, mostly because half the ML team has started bolting sparse autoencoders and linear probes onto our internal models and I wanted to understand what we're actually claiming when we say a model "has a feature" for something. At last session, after we went through the Othello-GPT saga, two people in the group (not me) got into a disagreement I haven't been able to settle, and I'd like your take on it. Everyone agreed on the history: Kenneth Li's original paper found that a nonlinear probe could decode the Othello board state from the model's internal activations, and that editing the probe's output could push the model to predict legal moves for board configurations that could never actually arise from legal play. Neel Nanda then showed that recoding the board as "mine/yours" rather than "black/white" made that same information linearly decodable, and that this single linear direction could itself be used to edit the model's internal board state and change its predictions accordingly. Then someone brought up the 2024 "OthelloGPT learned a bag of heuristics" follow-up, done inside Nanda's own training program, which argued that the clean board-state story actually decomposes into a large patchwork of localized, special-cased decision rules rather than one unified representation.

That's where Marcus and Elena split. (1) Marcus said this whole trajectory shows neural networks really do have representations in a fairly robust sense: the "mine/yours" direction was causally load-bearing, not just correlated with the board state, and that's exactly the kind of evidence that would convince us a posit is real in any other science. (2) Elena said the "bag of heuristics" result undercuts that, because if the same network's board "representation" fragments into a different story depending on how closely and from which angle you look, then calling it "the" representation is less like discovering a fact about the network's internal ontology and more like a convenient redescription we're imposing on it. (3) Marcus replied that this proves too much: every scientific instrument shapes what we find, from cloud chambers to probes, and we don't conclude electrons are fictions just because our first model of them was crude -- the later paper just replaced one story with a more accurate one about the same underlying thing. (4) Elena said there's a disanalogy: independent measurements of an electron converge because something out there pushes back on all of them the same way, whereas sparse-autoencoder papers on feature "splitting" and "absorption" show that the features you recover can shift with dictionary size or sparsity penalty with no such convergence, and the network was never optimized to have one uniquely correct sparse, human-legible basis to begin with -- so this isn't just the ordinary theory-ladenness of measurement, it may be a case where there's no fact of the matter about which decomposition is "the" right one. (5) Marcus answered that this is a fair complaint about sparse autoencoders specifically, but that Elena was overgeneralizing it into a verdict on representations as such, since the Othello board direction wasn't an SAE latent at all -- it was a single hand-identified direction validated by activation patching, which is a much stronger basis for a realist reading than "some SAE latent lit up on the training set."

Neither of them budged, and I don't have enough philosophy of science background to know whether "representation realism" is even the right frame for this argument.
