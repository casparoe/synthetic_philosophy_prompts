So a while back I went down this random internet rabbit hole on medieval Islamic philosophy (started from a youtube video, don't even remember how) and got kind of stuck on this guy al-Ghazali and his "occasionalism" idea. The example that stuck with me is fire and cotton: when fire touches cotton and it burns, we assume the fire caused it, but Ghazali's whole point is that there's no actual necessary link there, the fire has literally zero power of its own to burn anything, its God who creates the burning fresh each time, and it only looks so reliable because God has just settled into a "habit" of doing it that way. Same logic is apparently how he explains miracles, like fire not burning someone -- God just skips the habit that one time.

Lately I've also been listening to a bunch of pop-science stuff on quantum mechanics, the whole "some things are genuinely random all the way down, there's no hidden variable underneath it" thing (Bell's theorem or whatever it's called). And it made me wonder: if you sat Ghazali down and explained that, would he be thrilled, like "see, no necessary connections between physical events, told you so 900 years before you people figured it out"? Or would actual, from-nowhere randomness be a bigger problem for him than boring old deterministic physics ever was, since his whole point was never "events are uncaused," it was "the cause is God, not the thing you assumed" -- so an event with literally nothing behind it, not even God specifically willing that outcome, kind of breaks his system too, just from a different direction. I mentioned this to a friend who took some philosophy classes and she said "oh that's basically just Hume" and moved on, but that doesn't feel like the full story to me, since Hume doesn't seem to need God doing anything backstage, and Ghazali's whole setup sort of requires it.

Could you actually reason through this like Ghazali himself might -- using his own logic rather than modern assumptions -- would he take quantum randomness as backup for his view, would he say "randomness" is a category error and insist even quantum events are individually and specifically willed by God, or something else entirely? And along the way, could you say where exactly the "Ghazali basically invented Hume's argument" comparison stops holding up once quantum mechanics gets thrown into the mix?
