Over the past year I've fallen into decision theory as a hobby, with no formal background in philosophy or math to speak of -- it started with an argument on a forum about free will that somehow ended with someone bringing up Newcomb's problem, and one link led to another. The thing that has really lodged itself in my head since is a puzzle called "Death in Damascus," which as far as I can tell Gibbard and Harper built out of an old parable about a man who runs into Death, mistakes Death's look of surprise for a threat, and flees to a second city -- not realizing that Death's surprise was simply because Death already expected to meet him there that very night.

In the decision-theory version, a man knows he has a midnight appointment with Death, an excellent predictor of where people will be, and must decide whether to stay in Damascus or leave for Aleppo. Whichever city he settles on, the fact that he's settled on it seems to count as evidence (or, depending who you ask, practically a cause) that Death predicted exactly that, so neither city ever feels like a stable choice once you've actually committed to it -- I gather the technical term is that no pure choice is "ratifiable." The only option that doesn't immediately talk you out of itself, apparently, is to decide by flipping a coin.

What strikes me is that even the people who write the academic papers on this can't resist the pun themselves -- I came across one literally titled "Cheating Death in Damascus." That's what made me want an actual joke about the puzzle, rather than just a clever title. Could you write me one? I don't want a generic joke about Death or fate with the city names dropped in; I'd like the punchline to genuinely depend on some real piece of the theory -- the fact that Death is a reliable predictor, the idea that no fixed choice is stable, the difference between reasoning about causes and reasoning about evidence, or the coin-flip resolution. A sentence or two of setup is fine if the joke needs it, but it should read as something you'd actually tell someone, not an explanation of the puzzle with a smile tacked on at the end.
