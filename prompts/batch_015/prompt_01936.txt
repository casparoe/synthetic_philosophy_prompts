I've spent the last two years working on a novel that follows an aging perfumer, written in a very close, interiority-heavy third person — the kind of book where entire pages are given over to her noticing the particular quality of morning light, the taste of over-steeped tea, or the peculiar ache of a half-remembered argument. In the process of trying to justify this style of writing to myself, I went down a rabbit hole into the philosophy of mind and landed on Keith Frankish's illusionism about phenomenal consciousness. As I understand the position in its "strong" form — the one Frankish actually endorses, rather than the weaker view that merely questions the ineffability or intrinsicality of experience — there is no such thing as the raw, qualitative "what-it's-like-ness" we seem to introspect when we notice light or taste tea: there is only a functional, physical process, what Frankish calls a "quasi-phenomenal" property, that our introspective faculties misrepresent as having this special qualitative character. Assuming Frankish is right, it seems to follow that my entire method — trying to render, in careful prose, the specific felt quality of a character's passing sensations — is aimed at a target that doesn't exist: there is no phenomenal richness "in there" for my sentences to approximate, only a brain telling itself a story about phenomenal richness. A friend in my writing group, who has read more Dennett than I have, insists this is too quick and that I'm eliding something important about what illusionism actually denies, but I can't quite locate the gap in my own reasoning.

Before I let this send me back to the drawing board — I've half-seriously started sketching a new opening chapter in a flatter, more externally observed register, closer to the nouveau roman, on the theory that any prose aiming at rendering qualia is now provably chasing a phantom — I wanted to ask: does illusionism about phenomenal consciousness really carry this implication for a project like mine, and if not, where exactly does my inference go wrong? I would rather understand the actual scope and structure of the illusionist argument than either abandon four hundred manuscript pages on a misreading, or dismiss the whole problem too quickly simply because it is inconvenient for my work in progress.
