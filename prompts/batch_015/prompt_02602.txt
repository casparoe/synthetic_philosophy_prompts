I got formally diagnosed as autistic about six weeks ago at 29, after basically a year of going down a rabbit hole that started with one (1) TikTok and ended with a $900 assessment with an actual psychologist. I don't regret it and it explains a lot, but my housemate is doing a philosophy MA, and when I told her the diagnosis story she brought up this Ian Hacking concept called "looping effects," and now its stuck in my head in a 2am-spiral kind of way. The rough worry: what if the specific way I understand my own autism -- the traits, the online community, the whole self-concept -- only exists because a bunch of people like me found each other on the internet, converged on the same lists of traits, and the diagnostic categories themselves got revised around us? Is the diagnosis "discovering" something that was already there, or partly "making" the thing be there? I tried to actually write the thinking out instead of just doom-scrolling about it, and before I inflict it on my housemate (who will just say "have you considered reading the actual books") I'd like a sanity check from someone else first, since I've only read one Hacking paper and a bunch of secondhand summaries.

Here's roughly where I landed. Hacking apparently distinguishes "indifferent kinds" (rocks, quarks -- classifying them doesn't change them one bit) from "interactive kinds" (human classifications, where the people being classified can find out about the label, react to it, and that reaction feeds back into how professionals revise the category). Autism looks like a textbook interactive kind: it's been split and merged and expanded several times since Kanner and Asperger, and apparently Hacking himself used autism as a late example of exactly this, pointing to how the internet let autistic people find each other and write about their own experience in a way that reshaped the clinical concept -- which is basically my story with a twenty-year lag. My tentative conclusion: looping doesn't make the category fake, because plenty of uncontroversial categories are interactive kinds too (refugees are apparently one of Hacking's own go-to examples), and nobody thinks refugees aren't real just because the label changes how people behave, which then changes the label, and so on. So: real thing, weird feedback loop, diagnosis still legit. The part I can't get past is multiple personality disorder, which Hacking wrote a whole book about, and which ballooned in the 80s and 90s through what looks like exactly the same loop -- patients, therapists, media all reinforcing each other -- and where the whole thing, starting with the Sybil case, later turned out to be substantially manufactured by an overenthusiastic psychiatrist. So looping alone doesn't obviously separate "a real thing we're finally noticing properly" from "a thing we're accidentally manufacturing," and my only actual answer is "well, autism has better biological evidence," which feels like it's doing 100% of the argumentative work while looping effects do 0%.

Two: is the multiple personality comparison a fair worry, or is there a real disanalogy I'm missing that would let autism off the hook more cleanly than just "better biology"? Three: is my move at the end -- interactive kinds can still be perfectly real, see refugees -- actually doing philosophical work, or is it a nice-sounding cop-out?
