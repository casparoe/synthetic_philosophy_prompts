So a couple of my grad students got into a genuinely good argument during my Buddhist philosophy reading group last week, right after we worked through the tetralemma material in the Mulamadhyamakakarika, and I honestly can't tell who came out ahead. Curious what you make of it. Quick context: the catuskoti negates all four "corners" regarding a thing's inherent existence -- it's not the case that X exists, that it doesn't, that it both does and doesn't, or that it neither does nor doesn't. Here's roughly how it went between Jonah and Mei:

(1) Jonah said the honest reading is Graham Priest and Jay Garfield's: Nagarjuna is a dialetheist who accepts true contradictions at the limits of thought, the same way certain self-referential paradoxes in the Western tradition (Russell on classes, Kant on the noumenal, Sextus on appearances) turn out to be genuine contradictions rather than confusions. Denying a claim and its negation already breaks excluded middle, and the third and forth corners aren't even the same kind of trouble logically -- affirming "both" needs a paraconsistent logic, denying "neither" needs a paracomplete one -- so no single move dissolves the whole tetralemma at once.

(2) Mei said this treats the negations as classical when they're not. The traditional line (Candrakirti's, against Bhaviveka's more "implicative" reading) is that these are prasajya-pratisedha, non-affirming negations that refuse a claim without asserting any alternative to it. So "X doesn't exist" isn't classical not-X, it's a refusal to affirm an inherently existing X. On that reading none of the four corners are classical truth-value assignments to begin with, so there's no excluded-middle violation to explain away.

(3) Jonah pushed back: isn't "the negation doesn't mean what it looks like" exactly the move that could rescue any inconsistent-looking text from inconsistency? And doesn't MMK 18.8, where Nagarjuna runs through all four corners as "the Buddha's teaching" without negating any of them, look like he's affirming the corners rather than refusing them -- which is itself a thesis, flatly against Vigrahavyavartani 29's claim that he holds no thesis at all?

(4) Mei answered that Jonah's conflating two seperate things. The "no thesis" claim in VV 29 is about theses involving inherent existence at the ultimate level; conventionally Nagarjuna obviously asserts things all the time -- that's the whole point of the two-truths distinction. And 18.8 doesn't have to be one flat contradiction -- it can be shorthand for "conventionally real, ultimately unreal, both apply at their own level, neither applies absolutely." A real contradiction needs sameness of respect, and two truths is precisely a way of denying that sameness.

(5) Jonah's comeback: fine, but "ultimately, no thesis is ultimately assertable" is itself being offered as the ultimate truth here, so hasn't the problem just moved up a level instead of getting solved? Retreating to two truths only helps if you don't also have to retreat once someone asks about the retreat itself.

They left it at a standoff there (for what it's worth, even Tillemans, who's argued hard against reading Nagarjuna as a full-blown dialetheist, has granted that something weaker in that neighborhood might still be going on -- so it's not like the specialists have this cleanly settled either). Can you go through moves 1-5 and give me a verdict on each? A quick JSON block scoring them from -2 (clearly wrong) to 2 (clearly right) would be great, then justify each score in a couple sentences, and tell me what you think both of them are missing overall.
