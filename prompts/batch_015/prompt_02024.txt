For my school's Model Congress club, I've been assigned to write the case for a mock constitutional amendment that would abolish political parties in federal elections, and while digging for the strongest possible arguments I came across Simone Weil's 1943 essay "On the Abolition of All Political Parties" (I read the NYRB Classics edition, translated by Simon Leys). It's a much stranger and more radical piece than I expected going in. Weil isn't complaining about corruption or gridlock; she argues that any political party, whatever its platform, can be defined by three structural features: it manufactures collective passion in place of individual judgment, it pressures its own members' minds into conformity regardless of their private convictions, and its real, operative goal, as opposed to its stated one, is simply its own unlimited growth. From this she concludes that every party is, in effect, totalitarian in aspiration even when it isn't yet totalitarian in practice, and that this defect is built into the structure of a "party" as such, so that no internal reform, whether primaries, term limits, or campaign-finance rules, could fix it. Only wholesale abolition would.

That's a much stronger conclusion than the one I'm used to seeing in AP Government, where Madison's Federalist No. 10 usually gets treated as the last word on the subject: the causes of faction are rooted in liberty itself, so the only realistic strategy is to control its effects, through things like an extended republic and separated powers, rather than remove the causes, since removing them would mean removing liberty. I'm also bothered by a more empirical worry that Weil's proposal doesn't obviously answer. Nebraska's state legislature has held officially nonpartisan elections since the 1930s, with no party label ever appearing on the ballot, and yet everyone still knows which senators are effectively Republicans or Democrats, and voting coalitions still form in fairly predictable patterns. That makes me suspect that banning the word "party" might just drive the same psychological dynamics Weil describes underground into informal caucuses, rather than actually eliminating them.

Could you write an essay that takes this disagreement seriously and actually defends one specific verdict at the end, rather than concluding that both sides have a point? I'm less interested in ammunition for my assigned side of the debate than in figuring out who is actually right, even if that turns out to be the side I have to argue against on the floor. Please engage directly with Weil's three-part definition of what a party is, since that, rather than her reputation as an extremist, seems to me like the real crux of the disagreement, and address whether something like the Nebraska model counts as evidence against her or is simply not radical enough to be a fair test of her proposal. A hedged verdict is fine if it's precise (something like "abolition is justified only under conditions X, and institutional containment is preferable otherwise"), but it should be an argued, specific position rather than a survey that declines to pick a side.
