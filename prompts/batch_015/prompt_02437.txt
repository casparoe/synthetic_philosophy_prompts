A friend and I spent an evening arguing about a decision-theoretic idea known as "evidential cooperation in large worlds" (ECL): roughly, the claim that because the universe (or multiverse) likely contains vast numbers of causally disconnected agents, some of whom reason similarly to us without being exact copies of us, we have reason to act more like a compromise between our own values and theirs, since doing so is evidence that relevantly similar agents elsewhere are reasoning the same way and reciprocating in kind. My friend insisted this is just Douglas Hofstadter's older idea of superrationality (roughly: sufficiently similar agents facing a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma should cooperate, since each one's choice is evidence of the other's) dressed up in cosmological language. I argued that ECL becomes a substantially harder problem once the cooperating agents are no longer exact copies of one another and can hold differing values. Please compare superrationality and ECL directly. In what precise sense is superrationality a special case of the reasoning behind ECL, and what new difficulties — for instance, how correlated two agents' dispositions must be before cooperation is warranted, or how to define a fair "compromise" among differing values — arise once you generalize from a symmetric pair of identical agents to a large, diverse population of them? On balance, does this generalization leave the case for cooperating with distant, unseen agents basically intact, or does it introduce weaknesses that the simpler superrationality case never had to face?
