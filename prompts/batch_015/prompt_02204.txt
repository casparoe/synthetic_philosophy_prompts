Okay so I've gone down a bit of a rabbit hole with medieval Islamic philosophy lately (started from a random youtube video, don't ask) and I keep coming back to this Avicenna thing called the "flying man" argument. Supposedly he wrote part of this while he was actually locked up in some castle at one point, which is a wild detail if true. Anyway I tracked down an actual translation of the passage from his book on the soul and I've read it over and over but I don't think I'm getting the full logic of it. Basically it goes: imagine a grown adult man is created instantly out of nothing, floating in mid-air or in a void, blindfolded basically (his vision is "shrouded"), not touching anything, with his own limbs held apart so they can't even touch each other, and theres no wind or resistance so he can't feel himself falling either. The translation I found (I think the translator's name was Rahman?) says that even in that state he'd affirm he exists, but "not affirming that it had any length, breadth or depth" - so basically he knows he's there but has zero info about his own size or shape.

I posted the gist of this in a philosophy subreddit a while back and the replies kind of talked past each other, so I still don't feel like I really get two things about it. First, why does Avicenna bother with all these super specific conditions (no touch, no wind, limbs not touching each other, no sight)? It feels like there's a reason each one is there seperately rather than him just saying "imagine you have no senses at all" - like maybe each detail is closing off one particular way you could otherwise sneak in self-knowledge through the body, but I can't pin down exactly how that's supposed to work. Second, even granting that the floating guy still knows "I exist" with zero sensory input, I don't get how that's supposed to prove his soul is a genuinely separate thing from his body, rather than, I dunno, just some function of a body that he currently can't sense. It reminds me of the complaint people make about Descartes, that being able to imagine yourself without a body doesn't actually mean it's possible for you to exist without one. Is there a step in Avicenna's actual reasoning that's supposed to close that gap, or is that a real weak spot that people who study this stuff still argue about?
