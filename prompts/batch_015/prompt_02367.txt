In his Autobiography, Benjamin Franklin describes conceiving "the bold and arduous project of arriving at moral perfection": a checklist of thirteen virtues, tracked daily in a little book, with one virtue drilled per week in a repeating cycle. He reports that Order defeated him completely (he compares himself to the man who settles for a speckled axe rather than finish the tedious work of polishing it bright), that he added Humility to the list only after a Quaker friend told him he was thought proud, and that although he never reached perfection, the attempt left him "a better and a happier man."

Two influential later readers turned this project into an indictment rather than an inspiration. Max Weber, in The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism, cites Franklin's catalogue of virtues as the purest expression of a thoroughly utilitarian ethic, in which honesty, frugality, and the rest are cultivated only because they are useful for acquiring credit and wealth, not valued for their own sake. D. H. Lawrence, in Studies in Classic American Literature, goes further, calling the list a "barbed wire fence" that pens the soul in like "a grey nag in a paddock."

Write an essay assessing whether this Weber/Lawrence charge is correct. Does Franklin's method of self-cultivation -- including his own retrospective account of which virtues he mastered and why -- show that it is essentially calculating and self-regarding in a way that undermines its claim to be a program of genuine moral improvement? Or is this a misreading of what Franklin was actually doing? Weigh the strongest form of each side and defend a definite verdict; do not conclude merely that the truth is "somewhere in between."
