So I spent my career in experimental particle physics, living and dying by the 5-sigma convention for calling something a "discovery" -- always figured that was about as conservative as statistics gets. Now that I'm retired and reading philosophy of science for fun, I ran into the Jeffreys-Lindley paradox and its messing with my head a bit. As I understand it: for a fixed significance threshold, if you just keep piling on more data, the p-value can sit right at that threshold while the Bayes factor swings further and further toward favoring the null -- so "highly significant" and "strong evidence for the null" end up being correct descriptions of the exact same dataset. Can you explain what's actually driving that, not just "well, the priors are different," and why more data doesn't just straightforwardly make a result safer? Does this actually connect to the replication crisis stuff in fields like psychology, or is it a totally separate can of worms?
