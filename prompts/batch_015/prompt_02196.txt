Weird rabbit hole today: theres this result called the no-trade theorem (Milgrom & Stokey's formal version of Aumann's old "agreeing to disagree" result) showing that if traders are rational, risk-averse, and start with the same priors, nobody should ever trade purely on private information -- the mere fact that someone wants to trade with you is itself evidence you shouldn't take the trade. Which is almost the opposite of how Keynes thought markets actually work -- the newspaper beauty contest, animal spirits, his whole point that on the big questions (where's the economy in 20 years?) we're not even dealing with calculable probabilities, we just don't know and are mostly guessing what everyone else is guessing. So: if someone sat Keynes down and explained the no-trade theorem to him, what's your honest best guess at how he'd react? Would he take it as basically vindicating him (real trading volume = proof markets are full of "irrational" actors), or would he go after the theorem's own assumptions -- the common-prior bit especially -- as the more interesting weak point? No fence-sitting, pick one and defend it.
