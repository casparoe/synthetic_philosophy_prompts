So I'm on my high school's mock trial team, and at practice this week our coach — who also teaches AP Gov — used this example to show us how real appellate panels can end up with weird results, and it turned into a whole argument between two of my teammates that I honestly can't stop thinking about. The setup: a 3-judge panel has to decide a breach-of-contract case, and the rule is the defendant is only liable if BOTH (a) there was a valid contract and (b) that contract was breached — if either premise fails, there's no liability. The judges' actual votes were: Judge 1 says contract=yes, breach=no; Judge 2 says contract=no, breach=yes; Judge 3 says contract=yes, breach=yes. Here's the weird part: if you majority-vote on the two issues seperately, contract wins 2-1 (yes) and breach also wins 2-1 (yes), and combining those gives you liable. But if you instead just ask each judge for their own bottom-line verdict and majority-vote THAT, only Judge 3 personally believes the defendant is liable, so it comes out 2-1 not liable. Exact same three judges, exact same individual votes, two completely opposite "official" rulings depending on how you tally it.

My friends Marcus and Elena would not drop this. (1) Marcus said you obviously go issue-by-issue, since that method respects the actual reasons each judge gave, and ignoring the majority position on the sub-questions just throws away real information the panel has. (2) Elena said the only thing anybody was ever voting on is "liable or not," so that's the only vote that should matter, and letting the issue-by-issue method override what most of the judges personally concluded is basically inventing a verdict that a majority never actually endorsed. (3) Marcus shot back that her method lets the panel's official position be flatly inconsistent: a majority officially says there's a contract, a majority officially says it was breached, and yet the panel's ruling is "not liable" — which denies the panel's own stated premises. (4) Elena said fine, that IS weird, but it's not obviously worse than his method manufacturing a "liable" verdict that most of the actual judges, as people, did not vote for — and that "the panel has to be consistent across issues" is a value Marcus is smuggling in, not some neutral law of rationality. (5) Marcus said this whole example proves groups — courts, committees, congress, whatever — can end up being straight-up irrational even when every individual member is perfectly logical and consistent on their own. (6) Elena said that's overselling it big time: all this really shows is that you have to pick some rule for turning individual judgments into a group judgment, and no single rule is gonna handle every case well — she said it reminded her of "Arrow's theorem," which her sister mentioned once from an intro econ class, so maybe it's the same kind of deal.
