I've gotten kind of obsessed with prediction markets over the past year (Polymarket, Kalshi, that whole world) and it sent me down an economics rabbit hole thats messing with my head. I ran into this result that gets called the "no-trade theorem" -- I think the original paper is Milgrom and Stokey, early 80s -- and the pop-econ version of it that keeps showing up in blog posts is basically: if two people are both rational, and its common knowledge between them that they're both rational, and neither one has some outside reason to trade (hedging, needing cash, whatever), then the mere fact that the other guy is willing to take your bet is itself evidence you shouldn't take it, so the trade unravels before it even happens. Somebody nicknamed it the "Groucho Marx theorem," after the joke about not wanting to join any club that would have you as a member -- you shouldn't want to make a trade that someone else is willing to make with you.

This messes with me because markets are obviously full of trading all the time, including people who genuinely seem to have an edge over years, not just one lucky call. So either (a) basically all real-world trading is between people who don't fully believe the other side is rational, (b) the theorem's assumptions (common priors, common knowledge of rationality, no other motive to trade) just never actually hold, so it's a neat math result with zero real bite, or (c) I'm missing something bigger. There's also this other wrinkle I keep seeing mentioned -- that if markets really were perfectly efficient nobody would bother doing the research that makes them efficient in the first place, so "you can't beat the market" and "the market is only smart because some people are out there beating it" seem to be in tension too. I asked a friend with an econ PhD about this over drinks and she basically laughed and said "yeah, welcome to the efficient markets literature" without actually resolving anything for me.

Could you write me a real essay on this, not a listicle -- lay out the strongest version of "informed speculative trading between two people who both think the other is rational is basically never sound," then the strongest objections to that, and then actually land on a conclusion instead of punting with "it's complicated." Something in the spirit of "trading of type X is irrational under conditions Y, but real markets typically fail condition Y because of Z" would be ideal. Bonus points if you can say something at the end about what any of this implies for whether it's ever reasonable for someone like me to think I've got a genuine edge on a specific Polymarket contract, versus just being the sucker at the table who hasn't figured out yet that he's the sucker.
