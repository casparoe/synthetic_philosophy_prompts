My friend Daniel and I had a long, increasingly heated argument over dinner last week about how our company should structure its bonuses, and neither of us managed to convince the other. At the moment, bonuses are calculated purely from individual output — deals closed, tickets resolved, whatever number applies to your role — and Daniel, who has a finance background, thinks that is exactly as it should be. His argument is that anything which looks like a benefit to "the team" is really just individual self-interest operating on a longer time horizon: if covering for a colleague or spending an afternoon training a new hire genuinely helps the department, it will eventually pay off for the person doing it, through reputation, reciprocity, or job security, so there is nothing left over for a team-level bonus to reward except things we are simply too lazy to price into individual incentives directly. I took the opposite side, mainly because of a pattern we both agree is real: our best-performing teams got that way largely because they happen to include more people willing to do this kind of costly, unrewarded helping, and if we only ever pay for individual numbers, that kind of person will keep losing out to colleagues who free-ride on them, even though every team would do better with more such people, not fewer.

What makes the disagreement hard to settle is a detail neither of us disputes: within any given team, the people who help the most are almost never the top individual performers, since the helping comes directly out of time they could otherwise spend on their own numbers — yet teams with a higher proportion of "helpers" reliably outperform teams with a lower proportion, and different teams have ended up with quite different proportions over time, apparently through ordinary hiring and attrition rather than any deliberate policy. Daniel thinks this is a correlation we are over-interpreting, and that treating "the team's mix of helpers" as though it explains anything beyond the individual incentives already in play is a kind of category error: for him, only individuals are ever really competing, and any pattern that shows up at the team level must be a shadow cast by that competition rather than a force in its own right. I am not convinced individual incentives are the whole story, but in the moment I could not locate the flaw in his reasoning. Before the argument comes up again, I would like a clearer answer: is Daniel's reductionist instinct basically correct, or is there a principled reason to treat what happens at the team level as doing real, independent explanatory work, rather than merely standing in for individual effects we have failed to measure closely enough? And depending on the answer, does it actually tell us anything about how the bonus system should be redesigned?
