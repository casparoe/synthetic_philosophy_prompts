I'm working on a longer piece about the ethics of the atomic bombings (loosely timed to the anniversary), and I fell down a bit of a rabbit hole reading Elizabeth Anscombe's old pamphlet "Mr. Truman's Degree" -- the one she wrote, and paid to have printed herself, after Oxford decided to give Truman an honorary degree back in 1956. Most of it is surprisingly readable, she's clearly furious and pretty funny about it, but there's a stretch in the middle where she lays out a technical definition of "the innocent" that's tripping me up, and I want to make sure I actually understand it before I try to summarize it for readers.

Here's roughly how it goes. She's considering the objection that ordinary fighting men are "just conscripts" and so just as innocent as anybody else. Her response is that "innocent" here doesn't track personal responsibility or guilt at all -- it just means "not harming." A solider who's fighting counts as harming, so he can be attacked; the second he surrenders he becomes innocent in her sense and is off-limits. Fine so far. But then she raises an objection to her own view that I actually find pretty sharp: doesn't this imply a soldier can only be attacked while literally in the act of fighting, so that, say, attacking a sleeping camp would be impossible? Her answer is that "what someone is doing" can refer either to what they're doing at that exact moment or to their role in a situation -- a soldier under arms counts as harming in the second sense even while asleep, though she adds that he still can't be attacked more ferociously than necessary to put him hors de combat (had to look that one up -- French for "out of the fight").

I get the broad move -- she wants to strip "innocent" of any whiff of moral blame and turn it into a purely functional, almost causal, notion. What I can't decide is whether the "act vs. role" distinction is doing genuine philosophical work (something like the difference between conduct-based and status-based accounts of who's a legitimate target) or whether it's more of a convenient bit of definitional stretching that lets her keep an absolutist rule against killing the innocent while still licensing pretty much any normal military tactic. Could you help me think through what's really going on there? And separately, in your view, does this technical redefinition of "innocent" actually end up doing real work later when she gets to her verdict on Hiroshima and Nagasaki, or is it more of a detour on the way to the argument that actually matters?
