I'm taking a graduate seminar this term on nineteenth-century British moral philosophy, and for the last two weeks we've been working closely through Sidgwick's *Methods of Ethics*, especially the concluding chapter where he confesses that he cannot reconcile rational egoism with utilitarianism — the notorious "dualism of practical reason." Almost as an aside, our professor mentioned that this same worry is a large part of why Sidgwick spent so much of his later career as a founding and long-serving president of the Society for Psychical Research, sifting through accounts of apparitions and testing mediums for fraud, and that William James was doing something structurally similar across the Atlantic, helping found the American SPR and eventually serving a term as president of the British society, but for quite different philosophical reasons. I'd like to use this comparison as the basis for my term paper, and before I start writing I want to make sure I actually understand it rather than just reproducing the gloss we got in seminar.

Could you compare Sidgwick's and James's engagement with psychical research, focusing on their underlying philosophical motivations rather than the biographical detail (I can get that well enough from Gauld and Oppenheim already)? I'm especially interested in three things. First, whether Sidgwick's decades of psychical research can fairly be read as a genuine, if oblique, continuation of the unresolved problem from the *Methods* — an empirical search for some postulate that would keep the "cosmos" of duty from collapsing into "chaos" — or whether that's too tidy a story to impose on what was, in practice, a fairly diffuse research program. Second, how James's pragmatist theory of truth, his "will to believe" doctrine, and his radical empiricism combined to give him a noticeably different evidential threshold than Sidgwick's, even though James is not usually remembered as epistemically careless. And third, for whatever your judgment is worth here, which of the two you think had the more defensible relationship to their own evidence, given how differently their temperaments come through — Sidgwick's chronic self-doubt and reluctance to conclude anything versus James's willingness to rest a great deal of weight on a single striking case like Leonora Piper. I'd rather you work through the philosophical substance of this than just narrate each man's involvement with the SPR, which we've already covered.
