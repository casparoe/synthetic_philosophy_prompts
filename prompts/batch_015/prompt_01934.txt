Odd one for you today, but I could use a sounding board. I'm partway through my grad seminar on philosophy of mind, and we've spent the last two weeks on Kim's causal exclusion argument and the various non-reductive-physicalist attempts to wriggle out of it. This week's the proportionality move from Yablo's '92 Phil Review paper ("Mental Causation") — you know, the pigeon (Sophie, I believe, in most of the retellings) trained to peck at red things, presented with a scarlet target, and pecks. Yablo's line is that "red" is the proportional cause of the pecking because it's required (no coarser property like "coloured" would guarantee the peck) and enough (no finer property like "scarlet" adds anything beyond what red already gives you) — so "scarlet" gets screened off, and was never really a rival cause to begin with. He then runs the same structure over mental/physical pairs: the mental property plays the "red" role, proportional and doing the causal work, while the physical realizer plays "scarlet," screened off and hence no threat — so Kim's exclusion worry is supposed to just evaporate.

Here's the thing I was arguing about with a colleague over lunch and we couldnt settle it. Required-and-enough is a totally symmetric recipe — nothing in the definition itself priviledges the mental side. So take an effect specified finely enough — not "the organism withdrew its hand" but the exact millisecond-by-millisecond muscle activation pattern, or the precise firing rate in some downstream neuron — and now it looks like it's the *physical* realizer that's required (no coarser physical description guarantees that exact pattern) and enough (no finer physical detail adds anything further), while the mental property, being multiply realizable, is the one that's too coarse to fix the fine-grained effect, and so gets screened off this time. If that generalizes, proportionality isn't a real fix for exclusion at all — it's just a bookkeeping trick that hands the win to whichever level happens to match the grain we've chosen for the effect, and we can gerrymander that grain at will.

What I'd like from you is the strongest, most careful version of this as an honest-to-god argument (not a survey of the literature), aimed at someone who already has Yablo's paper down cold and doesn't need the exclusion argument or the determinable/determinate stuff re-taught. Really I want to know whether this objection goes through as a general refutation, or whether a committed Yabloian has some principled way of fixing the "right" grain for effects (or for mental properties) that blocks the gerrymandering worry rather than just stipulating it away. If you can also say a quick word on whether Bennett's or List-and-Menzies' rival solutions to exclusion dodge this particular problem, great, but that part's a bonus, not the main ask.
