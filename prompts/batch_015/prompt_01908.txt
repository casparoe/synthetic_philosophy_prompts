This term I am co-teaching a graduate seminar, cross-listed with our logic group, on self-reference and the epistemology of rational agency, and we recently spent a week on the Löbian obstacle that Yudkowsky and Herreshoff describe for self-modifying, proof-based agents. The core difficulty is austere but, I think, genuinely philosophically interesting: because Löb's theorem tells us that a consistent formal system T cannot generally prove the schema "if T proves φ, then φ" without thereby being forced to prove φ outright, a proof-based agent cannot certify, from within its own deductive system, that a successor reasoning in that very same system will only draw true conclusions — doing so in full generality would amount to T proving its own soundness, and in effect its own consistency, which Gödel's second incompleteness theorem forbids for any theory that is actually consistent. My seminar ended up splitting cleanly into two camps over how much this really shows, and neither side budged, so I would like a text that stages the disagreement properly rather than letting either view win by default.

One faction holds that the obstacle is philosophically deep — a precise, formal descendant of the regress problem in the theory of justification, vindicating what epistemologists have long suspected on informal grounds: that no sufficiently expressive reasoner can certify its own reliability entirely from the inside. On this view, every proposed workaround illustrates the point rather than escaping it. Tiling through a strictly decreasing hierarchy of proof systems (Herreshoff's "infinite descent") buys trust only by making each generation provably weaker than its parent. Fallenstein's parametric ("model") polymorphism buys generality only by routing trust through an external parameter rather than delivering the agent's unrestricted trust in literally its own reasoning. And the probabilistic reflection principles of Christiano, Yudkowsky, Herreshoff, and Barasz secure a reflection schema only by weakening "prove sound" to "assign high credence to." The other faction thinks all of this trades on an artificially demanding notion of trust — an unrestricted, exceptionless syntactic soundness schema for a single static system — that no real reasoner, human or artificial, has ever needed or possessed, so that showing this particular idealization to be unattainable tells us little about the graded, defeasible, and perfectly serviceable self-trust that the polymorphism and probabilistic approaches actually deliver.

Could you write an extended dialogue between a spokesperson for each faction — give them names, present both positions in their strongest form, and have them genuinely engage each other's specific moves rather than alternate prepared speeches — that works through the tiling, parametric-polymorphism, and probabilistic-reflection proposals in turn, and that closes with the two of them trying to establish whether they have a substantive disagreement about the nature of rational self-trust, or are merely disputing, in effect, how much certainty the word "trust" ought to require?
