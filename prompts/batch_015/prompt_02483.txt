1. Putnam's objection - to recognize the Gödel sentence as true you'd first have to know your own system is consistent, and the second incompleteness theorem says you can't establish that about yourself from the inside
2. Benacerraf's "which machine, exactly" objection - the whole trick depends on someone being able to hand you an exact specification of the formal system you supposedly are, and nobody can do that for an actual human being
3. The inconsistency escape-hatch objection - real people aren't perfectly consistent reasoners, and an inconsistent system dodges the theorem entirely (it can "prove" literally anything), so the argument quietly needs an assumption of human consistency that it never actually earns
4. The soundness-doesn't-save-Penrose objection - Penrose swaps "consistent" for "knowably sound" in Shadows of the Mind to patch the argument up, but critics say we have no better grip on our own soundness than on our own consistency, so nothing's really been gained
5. The formal-reconstruction objection - once you try to state the argument with full logical rigor, Gödel's underlying disjunction (mind isn't mechanizable, or there are absolutely undecidable mathematical truths) can actually be proven, but the extra premises needed to land specifically on "mind isn't mechanizable" turn out to be indeterminate
6. Gödel's own caution objection - Gödel's 1951 Gibbs lecture only ever commits to that disjunction itself and explicitly declines to say which side of it is true, unlike the much bolder use Lucas and Penrose later made of his theorem
