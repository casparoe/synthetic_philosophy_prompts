I've been reading Karl Pearson's The Grammar of Science for a seminar on the history of statistical thinking, and have come across an irony I'd like your help thinking through. Pearson waged a lifelong campaign against causation as a scientific category, dismissing it as a metaphysical "fetish" and insisting that correlation was the more fundamental notion, with causation merely its limiting case. Yet Pearson himself, together with Alice Lee and L. Bramley-Moore in their 1899 study of fertility in humans and fecundity in racehorses, produced what is usually cited as the earliest documented case of what we now call the Yule-Simpson effect: a pooled association that reverses once the population is split into its natural subgroups. Contemporary causal-inference theorists, Judea Pearl above all, treat exactly this kind of reversal as proof that no purely statistical procedure can tell you whether to trust the pooled figure or the disaggregated ones -- only prior causal assumptions, encoded in something like a directed acyclic graph and adjudicated via the back-door criterion, can settle that. Given that Pearson's own notion of "spurious" correlation already seems to presuppose the very causal distinctions he officially rejected, how do you think he would have responded to Pearl's argument? Would he have conceded that the paradox sinks his anti-causal program, or is there a way for a consistent Pearsonian to resist that conclusion on purely statistical grounds?
