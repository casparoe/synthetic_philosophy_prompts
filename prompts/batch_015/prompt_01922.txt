I'm a philosophy PhD student working on perception and the historical rationalism-empiricism debates, and while assembling a reading list for my qualifying exam I ended up spending a few days deep in the empirical literature on Molyneux's problem. A friend in cognitive science pointed me to the 2011 paper by Held, Ostrovsky and Sinha in Nature Neuroscience, which drew on Project Prakash - the program that performs sight-restoring cataract surgery on congenitally blind children and young adults in India - to run something close to a real-world version of Molyneux's original thought experiment. Five subjects who had been blind from birth and could already distinguish simple shapes by touch were tested immediately after surgery on whether they could match those same shapes by sight. They had no trouble matching within a single sense (touch to touch, sight to sight), but performed at essentially chance level when asked to match a shape they knew only by feel to its visual counterpart. Three of the five, however, succeeded at the same task within a few days of ordinary seeing.

This looks to me like a clean, real-world answer to the question Molyneux sent Locke in 1688: a newly sighted person cannot, at first sight, tell a cube from a sphere using knowledge acquired purely through touch. I raised the obvious objection that the subjects' visual systems might simply have been too immature right after surgery for the initial failure to mean much, but my friend pointed out that since three of them succeeded after only a few days, their eyes were evidently working well enough by then, so the early failure has to reflect a genuine cross-modal gap rather than blurry vision. Given that, I'm having trouble understanding why philosophers keep listing Molyneux's problem as an open question in survey articles and seminars, rather than treating it as one of the rare philosophical puzzles that empirical psychology has actually settled.

Part of what's bothering me is that a couple of people I've mentioned this to want to take it even further, treating the result as general support for a strong empiricist picture on which no perceptual concepts are shared across the senses prior to experience. That feels like a bigger claim than one small, rather unusual sample can support, but I'd genuinely like to know whether I'm right to resist it, or whether I'm just underestimating how much weight this kind of experiment can actually bear.
