so I'm supposed to be writing my second-year paper on epistemic utility theory and I got kind of derailed by this one objection that I can't decide is brilliant or just a rhetorical trick. Background in case it matters: the whole "accuracy-first" program starts from Joyce's argument that any credence function violating the probability axioms is accuracy-dominated -- there's always some probabilistic rival that's guaranteed to be more accurate at every possible world, no matter how things turn out -- and a lot of people have since tried to build an entire epistemology on top of that, treating accuracy as basically the one fundamental epistemic good, with justification, updating rules, etc. all supposed to bottom out in promoting it.

Then I read Berker's 2013 Phil Review piece and now I can't unsee the problem he raises. His move is basically: this whole approach does to epistemic value what act-utilitarianism does to moral value, and just like utilitarianism gets accused of ignoring the separateness of persons (screwing over one person is fine as long as the aggregate is good enough), accuracy-first epistemology ends up licensing trade-offs across what he calls "separate propositions" -- so how confident you're entitled to be about proposition A can supposedly depend on totally unrelated accuracy gains elsewhere in your credence function, which seems like it shouldn't be allowed to matter. My officemate thinks this is a nothingburger because propositions don't have interests the way people do, so there's nothing to actually be "wronged," but that feels too quick to me since the cases are meant to be independently counterintuitive, not just bad because someone got wronged.

Please dont just leave it at "reasonable people disagree," I need an actual verdict I can react to.
