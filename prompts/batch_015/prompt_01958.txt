Last weekend a friend and I got into a long, increasingly technical argument about whether Russell's theory of descriptions actually survives Strawson's objection in "On Referring," and I don't think either of us won. My friend's position was that Russell's distinction between the primary and secondary occurrence of a description settles the matter: since "the present King of France is not bald" can be read either as denying that there is a bald King of France (true) or as asserting that there is a non-bald one (false), Russell already has the resources to capture whatever intuition Strawson is pointing at, so there is no real need for talk of presupposition or truth-value gaps. I objected that this sidesteps Strawson's actual target, which is the plain, unnegated sentence "the present King of France is bald." There is no negation there for scope to attach to, so on Russell's analysis that sentence simply comes out false, full stop — and that is exactly the verdict Strawson thinks ordinary speakers would reject, since their instinct is that the question of truth or falsity doesn't arise at all, rather than that the sentence is false. My friend then tried to rescue Russell by suggesting that the existence of a King of France is merely conversationally implicated rather than semantically presupposed, so that the felt oddness of the sentence is a pragmatic effect rather than evidence of a genuine truth-value gap — but neither of us could work out whether that move actually earns its keep or just relocates the problem.

I would like to see this dispute conducted properly, by two people who each have a fully worked-out position rather than by two increasingly tired amateurs. Could you write a dialogue between a committed Russellian and a committed Strawsonian in which the Strawsonian presses exactly the objection above — that the scope distinction is beside the point for the unnegated case, which is where the real disagreement lives — and the Russellian has to respond in full, including whatever can be said for treating existence as implicated rather than presupposed, rather than simply repeating the primary/secondary occurrence maneuver as though it settled everything? I would like both sides to be sharp and genuinely responsive to each other's specific moves rather than talking past one another, and I would rather the exchange end with the disagreement clarified than artificially resolved, since I am not convinced the matter is actually settled in either direction.
