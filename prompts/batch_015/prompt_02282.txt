I've been part of a small EA-adjacent reading group for a few years now — the sort of group where most of us found each other through LessWrong or the EA Forum, and where the conversation after the scheduled discussion often runs longer than the discussion itself. Last month we'd just gone through Peter Singer's "Famine, Affluence, and Morality," and afterward two of the regulars, Theo and Naomi, got into a real argument about a mutual friend, Alina, an ICU nurse. During a brutal flu season a couple of winters back, Alina's hospital was badly understaffed, and she gave up a vacation she'd already paid for to work a run of unpaid double shifts, which by her own estimate kept at least two patients alive who would otherwise have coded on an empty ward. Nobody in the group thinks any less of the three colleagues on her rotation who took their planned leave instead.

The argument went roughly like this. (1) Theo opened by calling this a clean case of supererogation in Urmson's original sense — an act that's praiseworthy to perform but not blameworthy to omit — and said the fact that nobody blames Alina's colleagues is itself the evidence that she did more than duty required. (2) Naomi answered with the logic of the essay we'd just read: if you can prevent something very bad at a cost nowhere near comparable in moral importance, you're obligated to, and a canceled vacation isn't remotely comparable to a preventable death — so the honest conclusion is that Alina was obligated and her colleagues were blameworthy, and we only flinch from saying so because blaming people we're fond of is socially costly, which makes Theo's "nobody blames them" weak evidence at best. (3) Theo replied that this proves too much, since nurses face some version of this tradeoff every shift; treating it as obligatory commits us to a standard of duty so demanding the profession would collapse under it, and that this is exactly the sort of case Samuel Scheffler's agent-centered prerogatives were meant to cover — morality permits agents to weigh their own settled plans more heavily than a strictly impartial calculation would. (4) Naomi shot back that the prerogative move doesn't explain anything yet — it just relabels the intuition that this particular cost was too much to ask, unless Theo can say independently why a paid-for vacation falls inside the protected zone while a shift that would let a patient die doesn't — and, more fundamentally, that on a maximizing consequentialist picture there may be no coherent category of "better, but merely optional" to begin with, so the two of them might be arguing over a distinction that not every moral theory in play can even support.

Neither of them is a professional philosopher, so I'd take the terminology with a grain of salt, but I'd genuinely like a careful outside read on the exchange as it actually happened rather than a general essay on supererogation. Could you go through moves (1) through (4) and say who has the better of it at each step, rather than just naming an overall winner? I'm particularly interested in whether either of them is quietly leaning on a premise the other never conceded, and in whatever both of them might be missing that someone who works on this literature professionally would flag right away.
