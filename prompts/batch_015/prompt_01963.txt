I spent just over thirty years as curator of fishes at a regional natural history museum before retiring five years ago, and I still come in most weeks to help with the collection. Since stepping back from full-time work I've had more time to read, and rather to my own surprise I've fallen into philosophy, mostly through a philosophy of science reading group a former colleague talked me into joining at the university. It has been a useful, if occasionally humbling, complement to a career spent almost entirely with specimen jars and dissecting trays rather than arguments.

A disagreement I've been having at the museum has stuck with me in a way I didn't expect, and I suspect it may be more interesting than either of us realized at the time. We are updating the labels in the vertebrate hall, and the young curator who now runs that side of things wants to drop the word "fish" from the exhibit altogether. Her reasoning is that lungfish and coelacanths are, genealogically speaking, closer kin to cows, ostriches, and us than they are to tuna or herring, so that "fish," read strictly in terms of ancestry, does not correspond to any real branch of the tree of life at all, only to an old, pre-scientific bucket for anything wet and scaled. When I pointed out everything that tuna, herring, lungfish, and coelacanths obviously do have in common - gills, fins, an aquatic way of life, the basic body plan I spent thirty years learning to recognize at a glance - she did not dispute any of it; she simply did not accept that this was the kind of similarity that ought to decide what the "real" categories are. We went around on this for the better part of an hour, and what struck me afterward was that neither of us had made the slightest headway with the other, not because either of us was being obtuse, but because we could not agree on what sort of consideration was even supposed to settle the question in the first place. I am not sure what to make of that. I would welcome your thoughts on whether one of us simply has the better argument here, or whether this is a case where two people can each be reasoning quite carefully and still have no shared way of arriving at a single answer - and if it is the latter, whether there is nonetheless some non-arbitrary basis for preferring one classification over the other.
