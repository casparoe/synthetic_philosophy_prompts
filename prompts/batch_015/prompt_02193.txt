Three weeks ago my father suffered a massive intracranial hemorrhage, and after two separate clinical examinations and an apnea test confirmed the total absence of brainstem function, he was declared dead by neurological criteria — even though his heart kept beating on the ventilator for four more days while our family decided about organ donation. Trying to make sense of what had actually happened to him during that interval, I've been rereading Locke's chapter "Of Identity and Diversity" (Essay II.xxvii), where he insists that the identity of a 'man' — a living, organized body — is a wholly different matter from the identity of a 'person,' a thinking thing constituted by consciousness and memory. Locke, of course, never had to reckon with a machine that could keep a body's circulation running long after every capacity for thought was gone for good. Reasoning strictly from the distinctions he actually draws in that chapter, what do you think Locke would have concluded about a case like my father's: that whole-brain death is enough for the 'person' to have ceased to exist even while the 'man' persists a while longer, that neither has really ended until circulation itself stops for good, or something his own framework doesn't obviously settle?
