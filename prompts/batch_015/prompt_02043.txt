A close friend from graduate school and I spent most of a long dinner last week arguing about vagueness. We started, as one does, with the old Sorites puzzles about heaps of sand and men going bald, but the conversation only really caught fire when she brought up something she had recently read: the Pacific Islands Forum's declarations that a nation's statehood and maritime boundaries should persist even as its physical territory is slowly consumed by rising seas. That raised, in a far more consequential form than any heap ever could, the question of exactly when, over decades of grain-by-grain coastal erosion, a state stops being a state.

We ended up rehearsing the standard split between epistemicist and supervaluationist theories of vagueness, each of us defending a side we found we could not abandon. I argued something close to Williamson's epistemicism: that there is a genuinely sharp fact of the matter, some precise configuration of population, land, and government beneath which a polity has, in fact, ceased to be a state, and that we are simply unable to know where that threshold sits because our concept of "statehood" was never fashioned precisely enough to track it. My friend defended the supervaluationist alternative: that there is no such hidden fact at all, only a range of equally legitimate ways of making the criteria for statehood precise, so that during the borderline decades a claim like "this country still exists" is neither true nor false, and comes out true only insofar as it holds under every reasonable way of sharpening the concept.

Neither of us budged, and I have decided the disagreement is much easier to state than to actually feel the force of. I would like you to try dramatizing it rather than adjudicating it. Write a short story, in the tradition of writers like Ted Chiang or Greg Egan who build narratives around a single technical idea taken utterly seriously, centered on an invented island nation whose territory is dissolving into the ocean over decades, and an international body that must eventually rule on whether it still exists. Build the story around two people on that body, or in its orbit, who embody the two positions above through what they do rather than what they say: one who keeps hunting for the fact that would settle the matter, in old treaties, censuses, or the reef's exact hydrography, and one who treats that search as confused and believes the tribunal's real task is to stipulate an answer rather than uncover one. Let the plot turn on a moment when some irreversible decision, about citizenship, a seat at the United Nations, or the fate of the last few residents, can no longer be put off, and on what each of these two people actually does once the delay runs out.
