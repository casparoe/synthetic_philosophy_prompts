I'm taking a seminar this semester on theories of justice, and this week's session focused on the long-running exchange between Rawls and Harsanyi over what the parties in the original position would actually choose once they set aside all knowledge of their place in society. As I understand it, Rawls's case for the difference principle depends on the parties reasoning according to the maximin rule rather than by maximizing expected utility, and he motivates this by denying that they are entitled to fall back on anything like the principle of insufficient reason: since they have no grounds for assigning determinate probabilities to the position they will end up occupying, treating their situation as a fair lottery over equally likely outcomes would itself be an unjustified assumption (our professor drew a comparison with the sort of ambiguity aversion later associated with Ellsberg's paradox). Combined with the thought that the worst outcomes under a utilitarian principle are ones no one could reasonably be expected to accept, and that a person choosing behind the veil is assumed to care little about gains above a secure minimum, these considerations are supposed to license maximin reasoning and hence the difference principle. Harsanyi's 1975 reply denies that the parties are really facing this kind of radical uncertainty at all: if they truly have no information favoring one position over another, he thinks rationality requires assigning equal probability to each, in which case the ordinary axioms of expected-utility theory yield average utilitarianism rather than maximin. He goes further and describes maximin as encoding a degree of risk aversion well beyond anything already built into standard von Neumann–Morgenstern utility functions — a characterization Rawls resists by insisting that his parties' caution isn't "risk aversion" in the ordinary psychological sense at all.

For next week we've been asked to present this dispute as a dialogue rather than as a pair of summaries, and before drafting my own I'd like to see a strong version of what that could look like. Could you write a dialogue between two of the parties in the original position, deliberating together just before the veil lifts, one arguing for maximin and the difference principle and the other for expected-utility maximization and average utilitarianism? I'd like each to be made to answer the other's strongest objection rather than a simplified version of it — the maximin defender should have to explain why their situation counts as genuine uncertainty rather than a case for assigning equal probabilities, and the expected-utility defender should have to explain why equiprobability is the rational response to not knowing which position, or even which society, one will occupy. I'd rather the dialogue not resolve too neatly, since my sense from the reading is that this remains a genuinely open fault line rather than one where either side has simply won.
