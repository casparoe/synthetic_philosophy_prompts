Since retiring from entomology a few years back I've fallen down the philosophy of mind rabbit hole, which nobody warned me would be this addictive. I spent about three decades running conditioning experiments on honeybees, mostly the classic proboscis-extension paradigm where you pair an odour with a sucrose reward and watch the tongue come out, and I still keep two hives out back more out of nostalgia than data collection these days. Lately I've been trying to bring what I actually know about bee learning to bear on the animal consciousness debate, partly because it seems like half the people writing confidently about whether bees feel anything have never run a conditioning trial in their life.

Specifically, I've been picking apart Ginsburg and Jablonka's "unlimited associative learning" (UAL) idea from their big book on the origins of consciousness, where they argue that a bundle of five learning capacities together (compound-stimulus learning, learning about genuinely novel stimuli, second-order conditioning, trace conditioning, and quick/flexible revaluation of what a stimulus is worth) is a decent marker for minimal consciousness, on the idea that an animal that can pull off all five plausibly needs something like a unified, evaluative point of view to do it. From what I remember of the literature, bees look pretty solid on the first three - theres decent evidence for compound and novel-stimulus learning and for second-order conditioning in both bees and fruit flies - but the trace-conditioning evidence always bugged me, because with olfactory stimuli its genuinely hard to know the odour plume has really cleared before the reward hits, so what looks like "trace" conditioning could just be uncontrolled overlap you failed to rule out. That criterion is supposed to be doing real philosophical work in the argument, since its the one that's meant to show the animal can hold something no longer present in mind rather than just react to what's currently there, so if that's the shakiest leg of the stool, I think the whole case for bee consciousness is weaker than the confident tone of most writing on this suggests. I tried lining this up against Barron and Klein's separate argument, that a structure in the insect brain called the central complex plays a role analogous to the vertebrate midbrain structures they think are sufficient for basic subjective experience, and I couldn't decide whether that argument actually corroborates UAL independently or whether the two are just two shaky pillars propping up the same fashionable conclusion. My old postdoc, who I still have coffee with most weeks, thinks I'm overthinking a distinction that shouldn't change how we treat bees either way, but I'm not so sure.

Could you go through this reasoning with me and tell me honestly where it holds up and where it doesn't? I'm especially interested in whether I'm being unfair to the trace-conditioning research (I'm going on memory here, not a fresh read of the papers), whether the "two shaky pillars" worry is a real problem for these arguments or whether I'm missing some way they're actually independent lines of evidence, and whether there's a stronger case out there, on either side of the bee-consciousness question, that I should be reading instead. Please don't just be polite about it - I'd genuinely like to know if this whole line of thinking falls apart somewhere.
