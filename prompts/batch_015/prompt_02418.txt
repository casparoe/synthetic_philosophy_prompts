I'm in my final year of law school, and this semester's jurisprudence seminar just worked through Derrida's "Force of Law." Derrida spends his opening pages on a phrase he borrows from Montaigne — that laws keep their credit not because they are just but because they are laws — and then on a cluster of fragments from Pascal's Pensées that I had genuinely never encountered outside a footnote to the Wager. Everyone knows the line "Justice without might is helpless; might without justice is tyrannical" (fragment 298, in the old Brunschvicg numbering), but that same fragment ends on a bleaker note: since we cannot make what is just strong, Pascal says, "we have made what is strong just." A few fragments earlier, in a long passage on custom (294), he goes further, calling custom "the mystical foundation" of law's authority and warning that whoever traces it "back to first principles destroys it." I'm about to start clerking, and I can't quite shake these lines: if Pascal is basically right, a great deal of what I'm about to spend my career on looks less like the administration of justice than like careful bookkeeping for an old, successful use of force that has simply outlived anyone's memory of it as force.

What I can't settle for myself is how much of this to accept, because Pascal doesn't stop at diagnosis. Later on, in fragment 325, he turns on Montaigne by name ("Montaigne is wrong"), insisting on a starker version of the same thought: "Custom should be followed only because it is custom, and not because it is reasonable or just." He then immediately admits that ordinary people can never be told this, since they will only obey a law they believe to be just — so the illusion has to be maintained for the whole arrangement to hold together. That is a strange position: not Thrasymachus cheerfully announcing that justice is the advantage of the stronger, but something closer to a kind of political fideism — an instruction to obey without any hope of rational grounding, because the alternative — unsettling established customs "even to their source" — is supposed to do more damage than any injustice they might correct. Much of the jurisprudence I've been assigned this year — Hart's rule of recognition, Fuller's procedural natural law, Rawlsian proceduralism — reads to me like an attempt to escape exactly this conclusion, and I honestly cannot tell whether these are real escapes from Pascal's trap or just more refined versions of the same "mystical foundation" he had already diagnosed three centuries earlier.

Could you write me an essay that takes an actual position on this, rather than just surveying it? I would like it to weigh, first, whether Pascal's descriptive claim — that authority, traced back far enough, always turns out to be force that has lasted long enough to be mistaken for something else — is basically correct; and second, whether his practical conclusion — obey established law and custom precisely because disturbing them does more harm than the injustices they contain — genuinely follows from that diagnosis, or whether someone who fully accepted the diagnosis could still be entitled to something more like principled reform or resistance. Please do not settle for "both sides have a point": commit to a specific verdict (something in the shape of "Pascal is right about X, but the quietism does not follow, because Y" is a perfectly fine answer, so long as it is a genuine commitment) and defend it against the strongest version of the alternative.
