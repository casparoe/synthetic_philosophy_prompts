I spent about thirty-five years as an experimental particle physicist before I retired, and now that I've got time on my hands I've fallen pretty hard into philosophy, which I never had time for when I was working. My old book club (mostly other retired scientists, plus one very patient retired historian who puts up with us) just worked through Bostrom's stuff on the simulation argument, and what's really stuck with me isn't the trilemma itself so much as the physics people have proposed for actually testing it. There's that paper by Beane, Davoudi and Savage from a while back arguing that if the universe really is a lattice simulation, like the lattices we use for QCD calculations, the discreteness should leave a mark: a breaking of rotational symmetry in the highest energy cosmic rays that reflects the cubic structure of the underlying grid, bound up with that GZK-ish cutoff in the spectrum. And seperately (not the same thing, but it got my brain buzzing) the Auger observatory really did find a genuine large-scale dipole anisotropy in ultra-high-energy cosmic rays a few years back, which as I understand it gets attributed to the distribution of nearby galaxies rather than any lattice.

I think this would make a great short story but I don't have the fiction chops for it myself, so I'm hoping you'll take a crack at it. Something like 1000-1500 words, set at a cosmic-ray observatory (Auger-like is fine) where a small team starts noticing that the highest-energy events have a pattern that won't wash out as noise and doesn't match the known astrophysical source distribution -- and bit by bit they realize it looks like the signature the "maybe we're simulated" crowd predicted decades before. I don't want the usual glitch-in-the-matrix schlock though. I want it to feel like actual working scientists: the boring parts, arguing over systematics, somebody terrified of putting Bostrom's name anywhere near the draft because of what it'll do to their career, that sort of thing. And I'd really like at least one character to push back hard on what a finding like that would even mean -- that its wildly underdetermined between "we're in a simulation" and "there's some new Planck-scale physics we don't understand yet," and that Bostrom's actual argument is a probabilistic, anthropic one about civilizations in general, not the kind of thing you can just read off a plot. You don't need to resolve that tension for me at the end -- honestly I think I'd like it better if it didn't.
