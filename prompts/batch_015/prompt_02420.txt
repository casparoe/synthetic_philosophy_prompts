I am working on a novel set in the lead-up to the Roman Inquisition's 1616 condemnation of Copernicanism, and one chapter has Galileo defend his views on scripture using language adapted from his 1615 Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina. Two members of my writing group, Elena and Marcus, disagreed afterward about whether the scene is faithful to Galileo's actual position, and I would like help adjudicating it before I revise.

Elena said the chapter undersells how radical Galileo was: scripture, addressed to ordinary people about salvation rather than nature, deliberately accommodates common speech — she noted he even cites a cardinal's quip that it teaches how to get to heaven, not how the heavens move — so no verse about a fixed earth could ever count as evidence against Copernicus, since scripture was never in the business of making such claims.

Marcus objected that this makes Galileo sound more modern than he was: Galileo never denied scripture bears on physical questions in principle, only insisted that an apparent scriptural claim about nature yields solely to what is strictly and necessarily demonstrated, not to a mere hypothesis, since true scripture and true nature, both God's handiwork, cannot ultimately conflict.

Elena replied that this is a distinction without a difference: since no literal reading may be treated as settled against future demonstration, correction only ever runs from science to scripture, never back, so "conditional authority" amounts in practice to none.

Marcus answered that the distinction mattered exactly where it counted: Bellarmine's reply to Foscarini grants Galileo's own conditional principle and denies only that Copernicus had met it, since saving the appearances isn't proof the earth moves — and when Galileo later staked everything on what he took to be real proof, his argument from the tides, it turned out to be physically wrong, so Bellarmine's caution was not unreasonable by Galileo's own standard.

Neither conceded a thing. Please go through the exchange move by move, say who has the stronger case at each step, and flag anything about Galileo's actual position you think both are missing. End with a final verdict on its own line, in exactly this format: \verdict{Elena} or \verdict{Marcus}, whichever has the stronger overall reading.
