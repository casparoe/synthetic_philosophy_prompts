Random tangent from research for my current book, but it's been stuck in my head all week. Turns out young Kant, in his 1755 cosmology book, seriously argued that thinking beings on colder planets further from the sun (Jupiter, Saturn) would be more rational and spiritually refined than us, while ones closer in (Mercury, Venus) would be coarser and more ruled by the senses -- basically grading souls by orbit. Thats the same guy who, decades later in the first Critique, got very strict about what reason is actually entitled to conclude about the universe as a whole (the antinomies, the regulative vs constitutive distinction). So: if you could sit 1781-Kant down and explain the Fermi paradox to him -- universe's old and huge enough that it should be crawling with intelligent life, yet total silence -- would he treat that as a genuine problem, or would his mature philosophy basically rule the question out of bounds? And would he see his younger self's planet-ranking as a mistake in kind, or just a cruder version of the same move? Walk me through it using his actual principles, not a vibes-based "he'd be curious" answer.
