I'm finishing a doctorate in dynamical astronomy, and the final chapter of my thesis uses long-term numerical integrations of the inner planets, following Jacques Laskar's work on solar-system chaos. While drafting the historical background, I reread Newton's own writings and was struck by how personally this question mattered to him: in his letters to Bentley and in Query 31 of the Opticks, he argued that the planets' mutual perturbations would slowly accumulate until the system needed what he called "a reformation" - a direct divine correction. Leibniz mocked this as making God an incompetent watchmaker forever mending his own defective machine, and Laplace and Lagrange seemed to settle the matter in the clockmaker's favor by proving the secular stability of the planets' mean motions. My own simulations side with Laskar instead: that stability proof only holds under a truncated approximation, and the real dynamics are chaotic enough that Mercury's orbit has a small but genuine chance of destabilizing into a collision with Venus or the Sun within the next few billion years. Given that Newton was no deist - he was a covert Arian who insisted God remained continuously and actively sovereign over nature, not a clockmaker who wound the mechanism once and withdrew - how do you think he would have responded to Laskar's result? Would he read genuine long-run chaos as vindication of his instinct that the cosmos needs ongoing governance, or would the absence of any actual corrective mechanism, given that unstable planets simply collide rather than get fixed, force him to abandon the argument - and if so, for what theological alternative?
