A friend and I spent a long evening arguing about Kurt Gödel's private conviction that human beings survive death — not the famous ontological proof of God's existence that occasionally circulates online, but the separate, much less discussed argument for personal immortality that he laid out only in letters, never in any published work. My friend insisted the whole thing amounts to little more than "the universe looks orderly, so there must be a heaven," but I remembered it as stranger and more interesting than that. As I recall, the argument doesn't treat the obvious unfairness of an ordinary life — most people dying with the bulk of their abilities and potential still undeveloped — as an embarrassment for the claim that the world is rational; it turns that very unfairness into a load-bearing premise for an afterlife. Neither of us could reconstruct the reasoning properly from memory. Could you lay out, as precisely as you can, the actual chain of argument, spell out how that inversion is supposed to work, and then say where you think it is weakest?
