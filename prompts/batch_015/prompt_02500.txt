I'm writing a historical novel about Gottlob Frege's final year, when he was an isolated, embittered, retired mathematician living out his days in the small Mecklenburg town of Bad Kleinen. My research turned up the diary he kept during those months, full of contempt for parliamentary democracy and ugly hostility toward Jews, and I want to build a pivotal scene around a former student who travels there to confront him about it. My editor wants that student to argue that Frege's lifelong insistence on a single, objective logic untouched by psychology or culture is really just his political intolerance showing up in a different key, but I suspect that's a tidy-sounding line that doesn't survive scrutiny. Before I commit to writing it: is there a serious philosophical case that his logical views and his politics share some deeper root, or would my student be putting an argument in his mouth that doesn't actually hold up? And if the two are genuinely unconnected, how do I make dramatic and intellectual sense of a rigorously consistent logician holding such repugnant opinions with no apparent strain between them?
