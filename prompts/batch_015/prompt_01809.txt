I'm a second-year PhD student in economics, and this term my department's graduate seminar on strategic bargaining spent a full session on Thomas Schelling's account of commitment in *The Strategy of Conflict*: his idea that a bargainer can gain power precisely by destroying their own options in advance -- burning bridges, or visibly throwing the steering wheel out the window in a game of Chicken -- so that a threat or a demand becomes credible exactly because backing down is no longer possible. Around the same time, I started a part-time research assistantship with a small AI governance group, and in my very first week my supervisor had me read a well-known 2019 write-up from the AI safety community on what's called the "commitment races problem": the worry that sufficiently advanced, consequentialist AI systems will be driven to lock in binding commitments (e.g., to punish anyone who "bullies" them, or to hold out for a favorable split of some resource) as early and as fast as possible, since whoever manages to commit "first" can force everyone else to accommodate them -- but that a race to commit before thinking things through carefully can leave differently-committed agents locked into mutually catastrophic outcomes, the way two Chicken players can both end up throwing out their steering wheels at once.

When I brought this parallel up in office hours, my professor waved the newer idea off as essentially Schelling's theory "with extra steps." I'm not sure that's entirely fair, especially once the AI safety discussions bring in things like updateless decision theory and acausal bargaining between agents that can, in some sense, reason about each other's source code or dispositions before any real-world interaction has occurred. For a short reflection paper connecting the two sides of my work this semester, I'd like you to compare Schelling's classic picture of commitment as a bargaining device with the "commitment races problem" as raised in the AI safety literature. Where is my professor basically right that it's the same underlying logic in new clothing, and where, if anywhere, does the AI framing introduce a genuinely new structural problem -- for instance, in how commitments get verified, how quickly they can be made, what (if anything) keeps the race from spiraling out of control, or why the logic might apply even between agents that never causally interact at all? Please don't just list similarities and differences -- reach and defend a clear view on how much genuine novelty the "commitment races" framing adds to the older Schelling picture.
