I'm a novelist working on a book set in a fictional republic several generations after its founding revolution, and there's one pivotal scene I keep rewriting. My protagonist, a justice on the republic's highest court, must defend her decision to strike down a wildly popular new law because it violates a right entrenched in the country's century-old founding charter. A dissenting colleague on the bench accuses the majority of letting "the dead hand of the past" rule the living: a handful of long-dead framers dictating terms to citizens who never had any say in the matter. For background, I've been reading Jon Elster's Ulysses Unbound, in which he describes constitutional entrenchment as a society's version of the Ulysses strategy: just as Ulysses had his sailors lash him to the mast and ordered them to disregard whatever he begged for once the sirens' song took hold, a polity binds itself in its founding charter against its own future passions and panics.

I think I've found the key move for my justice's rebuttal, and I'd like your help sharpening it into the intellectual high point of the chapter. The "dead hand" charge only seems damning if you picture the framers as one group of people and today's citizens as a wholly separate one, but really a republic is a single continuous entity across time in exactly the way Ulysses was, so the founding generation binding the present generation is no more troubling than Ulysses's past self binding his future self. And just as Ulysses's future self was presumably grateful once the danger had passed, a later generation that keeps a right entrenched rather than repealing it is implicitly ratifying the framers' foresight. Could you help me develop this argument and make its logic as rigorous and persuasive as possible?
