At work, two colleagues have been arguing for a week about our LLM coding assistant, and I can't tell who's right.

It started when the assistant inserted a call to a helper function that doesn't exist in our codebase, but exists under that name in a well-known open-source library. (1) Priya, who likes philosophy of mind, said the assistant "must have believed this was that other codebase": on Dennett's intentional stance, belief-attribution is warranted whenever it out-predicts the mechanical-level story, and nothing about the weights predicts this bug as well as "it thought this was open source" does. (2) Marcus said this proves too much: a thermostat "believes" the room is cold and "wants" it warmer by the same logic, so the criterion is too cheap to track real belief, and calling it belief just dresses a statistical artifact up as a mind. (3) Priya said that's the point, not a problem: there's no further fact about "genuine" understanding behind the predictive patterns, for the assistant, the thermostat, or us, so Marcus can't demand a thicker notion of belief without begging the question. (4) Marcus answered that Dennett doesn't put the thermostat and the assistant on the same footing as a person: the stance earns its keep only where it explains something no mechanical account can match, and since the weights and training data behind this bug could in principle be produced, belief-talk about the assistant is the dispensable kind -- unlike belief-talk about people, which nothing mechanical has ever replaced.

Neither will budge. Could you adjudicate move by move -- who has the better argument at each stage -- and flag anything both of them are missing? Close with a one-line verdict in exactly this form: \verdict{Priya}, \verdict{Marcus}, or \verdict{Draw}.
