Roger Penrose's Gödelian argument against computationalism, in the form he actually develops it in *Shadows of the Mind*, is more careful than the "a machine can't see the truth of its own Gödel sentence, but I can" line usually pinned on Lucas. Reconstructed formally (following Chalmers' summary of it): suppose my reasoning is captured by some formal system F; since I know I'm sound, so are F and F' (F plus the further premise that I am F); hence I know that G(F'), the Gödel sentence for F', is true; but F' cannot derive G(F'), and since I am F' as well, that's a contradiction -- so no F can knowably capture my reasoning.

I'd like your ranked judgment of the standard rejoinders to this specific version of the argument, not the cruder one it's meant to improve on. Consider at least:

- The claim that we can never actually know ourselves to be sound in the first place.
- The "biting the bullet" response that mathematicians are sometimes genuinely unsound (Kempe's flawed 1879 proof of the four-color theorem stood for over a decade before Heawood found the gap), combined with the charge that Penrose's own fix -- distinguishing correctable slips from "unassailable" convictions -- is too vague to rule this out.
- Penrose's own three-way treatment of whether the relevant algorithm could be one we merely instantiate without ever consciously knowing it, and whether his attempt to dismiss that possibility actually works.
- Feferman's charge that Penrose trades on an ambiguity between full soundness and the much weaker soundness-for-Π1-sentences that the theorem actually needs.
