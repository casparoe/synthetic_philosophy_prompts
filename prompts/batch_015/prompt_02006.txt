Hi! I'm a retired biologist (spent my career mostly on marine invertebrates - octopuses and cuttlefish among them) and I've gotten pretty into philosophy of mind since retiring. I've been chewing on functionalism and multiple realizability and came up with an idea I'd like your comments on - whether it holds up, whether its already a well-worn point I've just rediscovered, or whether i'm just confusing myself.

The standard move (Putnam's, I think, or at least that's how the SEP article tells it) is: mental states like pain are functional/computational roles rather than identical to any particular kind of physical stuff, because the same role could in principle be played by totally different substrates - carbon brains, silicon, octopus neurons, whatever. Fine so far. But biology already has a pair of concepts for "same outcome via different underlying stuff": homology and analogy. The forelimbs of bats, whales and humans are homologous - same underlying structure (the pentadactyl limb), inherited from a common ancestor, repurposed for flying, swimming, grasping. Camera-type eyes in octopuses versus vertebrates, on the other hand, are the classic case of analogous structures: independently evolved, similar function, totally different developmental path.

Half-baked idea: when philosophers reach for multiple-realizability examples, what's actually doing the work is convergence - genuinely independent solutions to the same "problem," like octopus vs vertebrate eyes. That feels like real multiple realizability to me. But a lot of the other cases that get waved around (slightly different neural correlates for "the same" mental state across two humans, or across closely related species) seem more like homology with minor variation, and calling that multiple realizability feels a bit like calling a chihuahua and a great dane "multiply realized dogs" just because their skulls differ.

(Complication I can't quite get past: even the eye case isn't fully clean, because of Pax6 and "deep homology" - the very same master control gene shows up building compound eyes in flies and camera eyes in us, so even the "independently evolved" convergent cases share some deep genetic ancestry. I go back and forth on whether that helps my point or wrecks it.)

If any of this tracks, it seems like it should matter for how much weight multiple realizability can really carry in arguments for the autonomy of psychology from neuroscience - genuine convergence might be fairly rare, and a lot of the rest might just be neuroscience being messy rather than evidence that mental kinds float free of physical kinds. Does this correspond to some actual debate, is it too cute to be useful, or is there a hole in it I'm not seeing? Happy to hear "this is old news, see X" just as much as "this doesn't work because Y."
