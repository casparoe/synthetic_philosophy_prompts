I'm working on a longer feature about the replication crisis in psychology and I've fallen down a bit of a rabbit hole this week -- rereading Daryl Bem's infamous 2011 "precognition" study, the big Open Science Collaboration project a few years later that could only replicate something like a third of the original findings, and all the pre-registration and Registered Reports reforms that grew out of the wreckage. Somewhere around 1am last night, half-jokingly, I asked a philosopher friend what Karl Popper would've made of all this, since he died in 1994, years before any of it blew up, and all she gave me was a shrug and "well, falsifiability," which isn't really an answer.

So I wanted to put it to you properly. Would Popper have seen the pre-registration movement as basically a practical, institutionalized version of his own falsifiability criterion -- forcing scientists to nail down in advance exactly what result would count against their hypothesis, so they can't quietly reinterpret a null finding after the fact? Or would he have thought the crisis actually exposes a hole in his own framework, since a single failed replication never cleanly falsifies a theory on its own -- you can always blame some auxiliary assumption about the sample, the measure, or the setting instead (the old Duhem-Quine problem)? I'd love something that actually engages with his specific arguments about ad hoc "conventionalist twists" and degrees of falsifiability, rather then just the "good science makes falsifiable predictions" line that gets trotted out in every intro philosophy class.
