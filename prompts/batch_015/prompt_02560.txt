As a philosophy PhD student working on confirmation theory, I'd like a dialogue that pushes past the textbook treatment of Hempel's raven paradox.

Most treatments stop at the standard Bayesian fix: a black raven confirms "All ravens are black" far more than a non-black non-raven does, since ravens are such a small fraction of non-black things, so Nicod's criterion and the equivalence condition both survive without real paradox. I'd like the dialogue to engage I.J. Good's 1967 counterexample to this fix. Good specifies background knowledge on which exactly one of two hypotheses holds: either exactly 100 ravens exist, all of them black, or exactly 1,001 ravens exist, 1,000 black and one white, with a million other birds present either way, and the observed bird is sampled at random from all the birds rather than from ravens alone. On this setup, finding the sampled bird to be a black raven actually favors the second hypothesis, so it disconfirms "All ravens are black" rather than confirming it. Hempel's reply was that this background knowledge is illegitimately empirical, and that Nicod's criterion was only ever meant to hold relative to a purely "tautological" background free of contingent assumptions.

Please write a dialogue between two formal epistemologists thrashing this out after a colloquium talk. One holds that the Bayesian resolution survives once we are careful about what the background knowledge is doing; the other holds that the appeal to "tautological" background is either incoherent for a contingent generalization like this one, or else smuggles in an empirical assumption of its own (for instance, that ravens are a fairly homogeneous natural kind). Each should engage the other's strongest argument rather than a caricature of it, and the dialogue may end in genuine, unresolved disagreement rather than a tidy synthesis.
