For a seminar I'm taking on American pragmatism and democratic theory, we just spent two weeks on the Dewey–Lippmann exchange: Lippmann's Public Opinion (1922) and The Phantom Public (1925), read alongside Dewey's reply in The Public and Its Problems (1927). Something about the shape of Dewey's response has been bothering me since our last seminar meeting. Dewey doesn't really dispute Lippmann's diagnosis -- he praised Public Opinion, in his 1922 review for The New Republic, as "perhaps the most effective indictment of democracy as currently conceived ever penned," and he grants that the "Great Society" produced by modern technology has outrun any citizen's or any public's capacity to directly perceive the indirect consequences that, on Dewey's own definition, are precisely what constitute a public in the first place. What he disputes is Lippmann's remedy: rather than routing governance through disinterested "intelligence bureaus" of experts -- Lippmann's proposed answer to an electorate that could never be, in his own word, "omnicompetent" -- Dewey wants to reorganize communication, education, and local association so that a "Great Community" capable of genuine social inquiry can re-emerge.

What I can't decide is whether Dewey actually earns this move by his own lights. His whole account of inquiry -- in politics no less than in logic or the natural sciences -- treats a course of action as a hypothesis whose warrant comes from how its consequences pan out when tested; that is supposed to be the experimentalist alternative to both blind custom and rule by antecedently certified experts. But Lippmann's objection was precisely that ordinary citizens lack the standing, time, and access to run anything like a competent test of consequences on matters of tariff policy or foreign loans, and it isn't obvious that redescribing democracy as cooperative inquiry solves that problem rather than simply relabeling it. So I keep going back and forth on whether Dewey has a real answer to Lippmann on Lippmann's own epistemic terms, or whether he prevails only by quietly shifting the argument onto normative ground -- the intrinsic value of participation -- where Lippmann was never trying to compete in the first place.

Our next short paper is supposed to stake out a position on exactly this, and I'd like to pressure-test mine before I commit to it. Could you write an essay that works through the question? Reconstruct the strongest form of each side, engage specifically with how, or whether, Dewey's general theory of inquiry does the argumentative work that his answer to Lippmann requires of it, and then reach an actual verdict rather than a split-the-difference summary -- something with the precision of, say, "Dewey defeats Lippmann's stated position, but only by changing the criterion of success," or "Lippmann's epistemic point stands, and Dewey's mature view is honestly a wager on institutional redesign rather than a refutation of it," or whatever you conclude is actually correct. I would rather you commit to one specific reading and defend it against the obvious objections than hedge between them.
