A close friend of mine has just signed up with a brain-preservation company: when she dies, technicians will chemically fix her brain's synaptic structure well enough, they claim, that within a few decades it could be scanned and run as a digital emulation. Deciding whether to feel hopeful or uneasy on her behalf sent me back to something I'd half-forgotten from a logic seminar years ago: Kurt Gödel's own, surprisingly rigorous case for an afterlife.

In a series of 1961 letters answering his mother's question about whether they would ever see each other again, Gödel argued, roughly: the world is rational, in the sense that everything in it has a sufficient reason; human beings have a capacity for rational development — above all, the capacity to learn from our own mistakes — that we barely begin to exercise in one lifetime; and a genuinely rational world could not leave so much potential permanently wasted. So each of us must go on developing after death, in a further existence where we finally come to understand, with a clarity he likened to that of elementary arithmetic, what our earthly lives only supplied as raw material. He was explicit that this depended on materialism being false, and thought his own incompleteness theorems backed him up here: in his 1951 Gibbs lecture he argued — in what's now called Gödel's disjunction — that either the mind's power outstrips that of any finite machine, or there are mathematical problems that no mind or machine could ever settle; either way, materialism loses.

Reasoning from these specific commitments, rather than generic "soul versus computer" intuitions, how do you think Gödel would have assessed my friend's arrangement? Would a future emulation, however faithful, count as the kind of reunion his argument was meant to secure, or would his own premises actually rule it out? Make the strongest case you can for his likely verdict, and then say how a defender of uploading might push back.
