I spent my working life as an ornithologist studying avian reproductive biology, and only in retirement have I turned to philosophy of biology in earnest. Gould and Lewontin's critique of the adaptationist programme keeps recurring in what I read, yet almost every discussion of it leans on the same handful of illustrations: the spandrels of San Marco themselves, the panda's 'thumb', the human chin, male nipples, or the mammalian ear ossicles as the textbook case of exaptation. I would like to test the argument on less-trodden ground. Please identify one specific, real trait, ideally not drawn from humans or other primates, that is the subject of genuine and still-unresolved disagreement in the scientific literature: is it best explained as a direct adaptation, a spandrel or architectural by-product, or an exaptation? Lay out the competing accounts even-handedly, and tell me concretely what kind of evidence, comparative, developmental, or experimental, would actually be capable of adjudicating between them.
