I'm a backend engineer with no formal background in philosophy or decision theory, but I went down a bit of a research hole after seeing a post on an AI-alignment forum invoke "Harsanyi's aggregation theorem" as a reason why a system acting on behalf of many people should just normalize and add up everyone's utility functions. As best I can tell, the actual theorem is narrower than that: if a group of individuals and "society" (or a planner) are all von Neumann–Morgenstern expected-utility maximizers defined over the same set of lotteries, and society's preferences satisfy a Pareto condition relative to the individuals', then society's utility function must be an affine combination of the individual utility functions. I can follow, in outline, why that falls out of the axioms, and I understand why it's treated as a semi-formal argument for utilitarianism.

What I can't get an intuitive grip on is a complication that keeps coming up once I read past the standard exposition: the whole setup implicitly assumes everyone agrees on the probabilities of outcomes (Harsanyi's "risk" case), and something breaks once individuals are allowed to hold different subjective beliefs about the world, even holding their values fixed. Authors writing about this distinguish an "ex ante" from an "ex post" version of the Pareto condition, and describe a phenomenon called "spurious unanimity," in which a group can unanimously favor some option purely because its members disagree about what will happen, rather than because they agree on anything real. I gather this was serious enough to be presented as an impossibility result for one style of Bayesian aggregation, and that later work tried to salvage the theorem by restricting the Pareto condition to situations where beliefs already coincide.

Could you walk me through this at a gut level rather than via the full formalism? Specifically, I'd like: (1) a minimal concrete example -- a bet between two people is fine -- showing how differing beliefs alone, without any difference in what the two people value, can generate a "unanimous" preference for some option that no single shared-belief observer could endorse; (2) an explanation of what makes the resulting unanimity "spurious" in a way that's genuinely normatively troubling, rather than a mere bookkeeping curiosity; and (3) your honest assessment of how much this costs the popular claim that Harsanyi's theorem underwrites utilitarianism -- is restricting the theorem to cases of shared beliefs a minor technical patch, or does it quietly concede most of what made the original result interesting?
