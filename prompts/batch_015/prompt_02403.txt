In preparing a graduate seminar on Augustine's absolutism about lying, I have been working through one of the harder arguments in *Contra Mendacium* (*Against Lying*), the treatise he addressed to Consentius around 420 to oppose a tactic apparently favored by some Catholics: infiltrating the Priscillianist sect by pretending to be heretics oneself. The prose is dense and the argument moves quickly, and I want to make sure I have not missed a step. Here is the passage:

"For if lies, not of whatsoever kind, but blasphemous lies, are therefore just because they are committed with intent to detect hidden heretics; it will be possible at that rate, if they be commuted with the same intention, that there should be chaste adulteries. For put the case that of a number of lewd Priscillianists, some woman should cast her eye upon a catholic Joseph, and promise him that she will betray their hidden retreats if she obtain from him that he lie with her, and it be certain that if he consent unto her she will make good her promise: shall we judge that it ought to be done? Or shall we understand that by no means must such a price be paid in purchase of that kind of merchandise? Why then do we not rout out heretics, in order to their being caught, by the flesh committing lasciviousness in adultery, and yet think right to rout them out by a mouth committing fornication in blasphemy?"

What I cannot reconstruct to my own satisfaction is what entitles Augustine to treat "lying to expose a heretic" and "fornicating to expose a heretic" as relevantly parallel cases. Blasphemous lying and adultery seem to be wrongs of quite different kinds, and only the latter obviously wrongs another particular person. Is Augustine tacitly relying on a broader claim—made explicit only a little later, when he invokes Romans 6:13's warning against yielding one's "members as instruments of unrighteousness unto sin"—that no bodily or verbal member may be pressed into an intrinsically forbidden act regardless of the good end sought, so that tongue and flesh are simply interchangeable applications of one rule? And if that is the covert premise, does the analogy do any independent argumentative work, or is it just a vivid restatement of a conclusion already secured elsewhere, e.g. in *De Mendacio*? I would value your reconstruction of the argument's missing premises, plus your own assessment of whether it succeeds against someone who denies that deceiving a heretic about one's religious allegiance counts as "blasphemy" at all.
