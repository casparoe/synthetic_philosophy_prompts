In a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of causation, we recently moved from David Lewis's classic counterfactual analysis to James Woodward and Christopher Hitchcock's interventionist, structural-equations approach, and I've been trying to stress-test the two frameworks against each other using a case of my own devising rather than the standard rock-throwing examples everyone already knows by heart.

Here is the case. An assassin slips poison into a king's coffee. A bodyguard has a strict, reflexive policy: she adds an antidote to the king's cup if and only if she sees poison go in, since she knows the antidote is exactly as lethal as the poison when taken alone. As it happens, the two substances neutralize each other perfectly when combined, so the coffee is safe if it contains both or neither, and fatal if it contains exactly one. On the occasion in question, the assassin does poison the coffee, the bodyguard duly adds the antidote in response, and the king drinks the mixture and survives.

I'd like you to work through what each theory says about whether the assassin's poisoning the coffee is a cause of the king's survival. On the counterfactual side, check both whether the king's survival simply counterfactually depends on the poisoning (letting everything downstream re-adjust accordingly) and, if that test fails, whether Lewis's fallback appeal to chains of stepwise dependence between actual events nonetheless secures a verdict of causation, and if so, how. On the interventionist side, apply the standard procedure of testing a candidate cause along a given route by holding fixed, at their actual values, any variables not on that route, and see what this delivers when applied to the direct link between the poisoning and the survival — bearing in mind that interventionists of this stripe typically deny that causation is transitive, so they cannot simply help themselves to whatever answer the chain-based approach gives. Finally, tell me whether the two theories end up disagreeing on this case, or quietly converging on a verdict that most people would find bizarre, and use your answer to say something about whether Woodward and Hitchcock's apparatus represents genuine progress over Lewis's, or is simply a more technically dressed-up way of running into the same kind of trouble once causal influence can travel to the same effect along more than one interacting path.
