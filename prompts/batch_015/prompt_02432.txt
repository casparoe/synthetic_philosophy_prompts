I'm preparing a graduate seminar on illusionism about phenomenal consciousness (the view that phenomenal consciousness, as ordinarily conceived, does not exist, and that our conviction that it does is itself a product of introspective misrepresentation), and I want to open with the paragraph that later writers, Gilbert Harman among them, turned into the "argument from diaphanousness" against qualia realism. Here it is, from G. E. Moore's "The Refutation of Idealism" (Mind, n.s. 12 (1903), 433–453; repr. Philosophical Studies, 1922, p. 25):

"They have not been able to hold it and blue before their minds and to compare them, in the same way in which they can compare blue and green. And this for the reason I gave above: namely that the moment we try to fix our attention upon consciousness and to see what, distinctly, it is, it seems to vanish: it seems as if we had before us a mere emptiness. When we try to introspect the sensation of blue, all we can see is the blue: the other element is as if it were diaphanous. Yet it can be distinguished if we look attentively enough, and if we know that there is something to look for. My main object in this paragraph has been to try to make the reader see it; but I fear I shall have succeeded very ill."

Two things trouble me about pressing this passage into service for illusionism. First, Moore is himself a paradigm qualia realist, indeed a sense-datum theorist, yet this is the very text credited with originating the transparency observation that anti-realists about qualia now rely on. Second, and more specifically, Moore draws no illusionist moral from the difficulty of introspecting the "awareness" element; he draws the opposite one, insisting it "can be distinguished if we look attentively enough."

Please (a) reconstruct precisely what work this paragraph is doing against the "content" theory of sensation that Moore is attacking, and (b) tell me whether Moore's confidence that attentive introspection does deliver up a distinct awareness-element should be read as exactly the kind of naive introspective judgment illusionists are entitled to debunk, or whether it instead marks a genuine limit on what the transparency of experience can be used to show. I want a considered verdict, defended by close attention to the text itself, rather than a survey of the secondary literature.
