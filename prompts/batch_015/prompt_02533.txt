Basic Bayes says a piece of evidence e only confirms a theory h if learning e would've bumped up your credence in h, which basically needs P(e|h) to be bigger then your prior P(e). Fine so far. But then I realized: if you already know e is true for certain -- like your credence in it is already sitting at 1, its "old evidence" -- the math says there's literally no way e can confirm anything, ever, because your credence can't go any higher than 1 to begin with. Which seems crazy, because the classic textbook example of confirmation IS old evidence: people had noticed since like the 1850s that Mercury's perihelion drifts by about 43 arcseconds a century more than plain Newtonian gravity predicts, nobody could explain the leftover for decades, and then in 1915 Einstein's general relativity just spits that exact number out as a consequence of the equations. Everyone treats that as one of the biggest confirmations general relativity ever got. But the Mercury data was already 50+ years old and known for certain by then, so by the letter of Bayes' theorem it should've been worthless as confirmation. Something's gotta give.

I poked around a bit and apparently this is an actual thing philosophers argue about ("the problem of old evidence"), but everything I found assumed you already speak fluent probability jargon, so I gave up reading and just tried to reason it out myself instead. Here's what I came up with: when we say old evidence "confirms" a theory, we can't literally mean it moved our real current credence, since that's stuck at 1. So it must be shorthand for a counterfactual claim -- something like "if I hadn't already known e, deriving e from h would've raised my credence a ton." On that reading, Mercury confirms general relativity not because it changed anyone's mind the day Einstein worked out the math, but because an idealized scientist who somehow knew Einstein's equations but NOT the Mercury anomaly would've been floored that the theory nails that exact, weird number.
