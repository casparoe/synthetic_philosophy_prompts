A friend of mine and I — both part of a small reading group on ancient theories of language and reference — spent most of a long car journey arguing about Gongsun Long's White Horse Dialogue (白馬論) and never quite resolved it, which has been nagging at me ever since. We agree on the basic shape of the text: a sophist defends the thesis "白馬非馬" ("white horse is not horse") against an interlocutor who insists that a white horse is obviously a horse, and the dialogue offers roughly five distinct arguments for the thesis, among them that "horse" names a shape while "white" names a color, that someone who asks for a horse will accept a brown or black one while someone who asks for a white horse will not, and that "horse" excludes no color while "white horse" excludes several. Where we part ways is over how charitably these arguments can be reconstructed.

My friend defends what I'd always taken to be close to the scholarly default, spelled out at length in Chris Fraser's Stanford Encyclopedia entry on the School of Names: once you set aside the old Platonist gloss on which "horse" and "white horse" name distinct universals, all five of Gongsun Long's arguments turn out, on inspection, to trade on sliding between "is not" as strict non-identity and "is not" as "does not belong to the kind," so that however cleverly dressed up, they are simply invalid. I kept resisting this by appeal to Chad Hansen's "mass noun hypothesis": the idea that bare nouns in Classical Chinese semantically behave more like English mass terms ("water," "furniture") than like count nouns individuated by a sortal, referring to scattered mereological wholes rather than to sets of discrete individuals. On that picture, "horse" refers to all the horse-stuff there is and "white horse" refers to the white-horse-stuff, a proper part of the former, so that "白馬非馬," read with "fei" doing the single, consistent job of denying identity throughout, comes out true on a single, univocal reading, with no equivocation required anywhere. My friend thinks this just relocates the sleight of hand instead of removing it.

I'd like you to help me build the strongest version of my side. Please argue for the position that, under the mass-noun/mereological reconstruction of Classical Chinese nominal semantics, at least some of Gongsun Long's five arguments in the White Horse Dialogue are not equivocations but valid arguments for a true, if deeply counterintuitive, conclusion, and that the "equivocation" diagnosis covertly imports count-noun, sortal-based assumptions about reference that beg the question against the sophist. Please work through at least two of the specific arguments closely enough to show exactly how the mass-noun reading is meant to block the equivocation charge in each case, and address directly what I take to be the strongest objection to my own position: that even a committed mass-noun theorist owes an explanation of why competent speakers of the language at the time treated "白馬非馬" as obviously false, which sits uneasily with the claim that it is simply, literally, and non-equivocally true.
