I'm a philosophy PhD student working on decision theory, and for the last few weeks I've been stuck reading about acausal trade. The idea runs from Hofstadter's writing on superrationality, through Eliezer Yudkowsky's timeless decision theory and its later reformulation (with Nate Soares) as functional decision theory, to Caspar Oesterheld's work on multiverse-wide cooperation: the claim that two agents who can never causally interact—because they are separated by unreachable distances, different branches of a multiverse, or an irreversible arrow of time—might still each benefit the other by simulating what the other would decide and acting as though bound by a bargain that neither could ever confirm the other actually kept. What keeps pulling me back to it is how thoroughly it dispenses with the ordinary bases for trust: no communication, no reputation, no third party able to punish defection—nothing but each party's confidence that the other reasons enough like it does for the correlation between their decisions to hold.

I brought this up recently in a reading group that draws both philosophy and CS students interested in multi-agent systems, half-expecting eye-rolls at how esoteric it sounds, and instead we spent an hour on how strange it is that the idea almost never shows up in fiction, given how well-worn its neighboring tropes are. It struck several of us as close to a mirror image of the dark forest logic from The Three-Body Problem, where the impossibility of verifying another civilization's intentions licenses pre-emptive destruction; acausal trade is the claim that, under the right conditions, that same silence and unreachability could instead license cooperation, provided you are the kind of agent whose reasoning generalizes.

Rather than another explainer, I'd like to see the idea dramatized. Could you write me a short story, somewhere in the 1000–1800 word range, centered on a single character—human, AI, or alien, whichever you think best serves the idea—who becomes convinced they are a party to such a trade and is forced to act at a moment when defecting would cost them nothing detectable and cooperating would never be acknowledged either way? I'd rather the theory stayed implicit in the character's reasoning and the shape of their dilemma than have anyone pause to lecture the reader on decision theory.
