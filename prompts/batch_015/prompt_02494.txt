I'm a philosophy PhD student writing a dissertation chapter on precommitment and rational choice, and for the past semester I've also been sitting in on a reading group — mostly CS and economics students — that works through the AI-safety blogosphere's writing on multi-agent bargaining. Two things we've read back to back have left me stuck, and I'd appreciate help thinking through how they actually connect.

The first is Daniel Kokotajlo's 2019 post "The Commitment Races problem," which argues that "consequentialist" agents capable of making credible commitments face a perverse incentive to lock in a bargaining stance as early as possible, before they've had time to reason things through: in games like Chicken or the Ultimatum Game, whoever commits "first" — in a subjective or logical rather than clock-time sense — effectively dictates the outcome, while delaying just risks becoming logically downstream of somebody else's commitment. Kokotajlo illustrates the danger with two AGIs that each precommit, in apparently sane fashion, to punish demands beyond what they individually regard as a fair split of some good — one using the Nash bargaining solution, the other the Kalai-Smorodinsky solution — and who meet years later and end up at war. In the comments, Yudkowsky objects that this can't happen to a genuinely rational agent, since such an agent simply fixes, once and for all, a policy that is insensitive to the other side's claimed timing — e.g., complying with an unfair demand only just often enough to make issuing it unprofitable, regardless of when the demander says it committed. Wei Dai is unconvinced, replying that he doubts commitment races admit of a good solution even in principle, since agents might just as easily race to lock in incompatible standards of fairness as converge on a shared one.

A commenter on that thread points out that Parfit anticipated a version of this worry back in 1984, in his discussion of threat-fulfillers and threat-ignorers in *Reasons and Persons* — which sent me back to that book, but left me unsure how the two arguments actually bear on each other. Parfit argues that it can be instrumentally rational to make yourself into the kind of agent who ignores threats, even though, at the moment you actually ignore one and suffer the consequences, that act is not what a purely calculating agent would choose in isolation; and he stresses that the fact it is rational to instill some disposition or belief in yourself does not show that the disposition or belief is itself correct, so that the resulting agent is merely what he calls "rationally irrational." Is that observation good news for Yudkowsky's side of the newer debate, since it shows that binding yourself to a fixed policy in advance can be rational even though it looks locally crazy — and looking locally crazy was the whole objection to hasty, "early" commitments in the first place? Or does it actually support Wei Dai's pessimism, since it implies that whatever disposition an agent rationally, but blindly, locks itself into first comes with no guarantee of being the correct one — which is exactly how Kokotajlo's two AGIs end up at war? I'd like a careful account of how these two arguments genuinely bear on one another, rather than a restatement of each in isolation.
