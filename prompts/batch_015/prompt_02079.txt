Been arguing with the other maintainers of an open source project about who should actually get a vote when we make bigger decisions (core committers only? anyone who's ever filed an issue? companies whose products depend on it?). Fell down a wikipedia hole because of it and found out theres an actual name for this in political philosophy: the "boundary problem" — basically, how do you decide who counts as part of "the people" that gets to vote on something, when deciding who's included is itself a decision that needs someone already-eligible to make it. Kind of a bootstrapping problem, like trying to compile the first compiler.

Anyway, turns out there's a handful of competing answers people have proposed over the years:

- all-affected-interests principle (anyone whose interests are impacted by the decision gets a say)
- all-subjected principle (anyone who'd be subject to the resulting rules gets a say, even if it's never actually enforced on them)
- coercion principle (narrower version of the above — only people actually coerced/enforced against get a say)
- stakeholder principle (anyone with an ongoing stake in the community, not just a one-off interest)
- nationality principle (membership tracks a pre-existing shared national/cultural identity)
- territorial-boundedness (demos = whoever already lives inside the state's jurisdiction, defended as basically a precondition for democracy to work at all)
- "whatever the current law/history already says" (no real theory, just defer to existing citizenship rules)
