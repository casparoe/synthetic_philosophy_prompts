So I got into a whole argument with a friend last night about the ESP section of Turing's 1950 paper on machine intelligence (the imitation game one). Near the end he brings up ESP as a real threat to the test - he names four phenomena, telepathy, clairvoyance, precognition and psychokinesis - and worries that a human witness with genuine telepathic or clairvoyant powers could correctly guess the suit of a card he can't see way more often than a machine guessing at random, letting the interrogator tell them apart for reasons that have nothing to do with actual thinking. His fix is to stick both witnesses in what he literally calls a "telepathy-proof room." My friend thought this was just a funny historical footnote, but I got stuck on something: a room, however well sheilded, can only block stuff crossing space in real time, and precognition (by Turing's own list) is foreknowledge, not a signal you could block that way, so its not obvious the room does anything about that one at all. We argued about whether that's an actual hole in his fix or just me being pedantic, and never settled it before we had to leave. Can you make the strongest possible case that Turing's "telepathy-proof room" fails, on his own terms, to rule out precognition specifically, and that this is a genuine gap rather than a technicality? Feel free to also consider whether the room could somehow be patched to cover it, or whether "shielding" was the wrong kind of fix to begin with.
