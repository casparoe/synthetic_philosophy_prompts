I'm a PhD student in epistemology working on the historical-background chapter of my dissertation on testimonial justification, which traces the reductionism/anti-reductionism debate back through Hume (the reductionist ancestor) and Reid (the anti-reductionist ancestor) before turning to the contemporary literature on Coady, Fricker, Lackey, and Burge. My advisor keeps pushing me not to rely on the secondary literature's potted summary of Reid ("testimonial belief is basic, like perception") for a chapter that's supposed to be doing close historical work, so I went back to Section XXIV of the Inquiry into the Human Mind on the Principles of Common Sense (1764) — "Of the Analogy between Perception, and the Credit We Give to Human Testimony" — and found the actual argument harder to pin down than the gloss suggests. Here is the stretch of text I keep circling back to without feeling I've fully reconstructed its logic:

"The wise and beneficent Author of nature, who intended that we should be social creatures, and that we should receive the greatest and most important part of our knowledge by the information of others, hath, for these purposes implanted in our natures two principles that tally with each other. The first of these principles is, a propensity to speak truth, and to use the signs of language, so as to convey our real sentiments. [...] Another original principle implanted in us by the Supreme Being, is a disposition to confide in the veracity of others, and to believe what they tell us. This is the counter part to the former; and as that may be called the principle of veracity, we shall, for want of a more proper name, call this the principle of credulity. It is unlimited in children, until they meet with instances of deceit and falsehood: and it retains a very considerable degree of strength through life.

If nature had left the mind of the speaker in equilibrio, without any inclination to the side of truth more than to that of falsehood; children would lie as often as they speak truth, until reason was so far ripened, as to suggest the imprudence of lying, or conscience, as to suggest its immorality. And if nature had left the mind of the hearer in equilibrio, without any inclination to the side of belief more than to that of disbelief, we should take no man's word until we had positive evidence that he spoke truth. His testimony would, in this case, have no more authority than his dreams; which may be true or false, but no man is disposed to believe them, on this account, that they were dreamed. It is evident, that, in the matter of testimony, the balance of human judgment is by nature inclined to the side of belief; and turns to that side of itself, when there is nothing put into the opposite scale."

There are three things I'd like help untangling here. First, Reid seems to be running at least two distinct considerations for the conclusion that our disposition to trust testimony is an "original principle" rather than the deliverance of induction over speakers' track records: an ontogenetic point about children trusting testimony before they could have amassed the relevant inductive evidence, and the more hypothetical "equilibrio" argument about what testimony would be worth if nature had left speaker and hearer indifferent. Are these meant as independent arguments for the same conclusion, or is one doing the real philosophical work while the other merely illustrates it? Second, I can't quite tell how much weight the comparison to dreams is meant to bear — is "no more authority than his dreams" just a vivid stand-in for "no evidential weight at all," or is Reid trading on some more specific commitment in his philosophy of mind about the epistemic status of dream-content? And finally, given how thoroughly this argument is dressed in the language of what "the wise and beneficent Author of nature" has implanted in us, I'd like to know how much of its force actually depends on that theological premise: could a secular anti-reductionist today — Coady, say, or Burge with his Acceptance Principle — rerun Reid's argument here without loss, or does removing the designer leave an explanatory gap that Reid was using God to close?
