I write literary fiction, and I've just spent two weeks at a residency with five other writers. On the second-to-last night, one of the fiction writers read a chapter in which the narrator's childhood dog is put down, and by the end at least two people at the table were crying, myself included. Over dinner afterward, two of the others, Renata, who did a philosophy MA before an MFA, and Sam, a poet with no patience for what he called "academic hairsplitting," got into an argument about what had actually just happened to us, and I haven't been able to settle it in my own head since. I'm finishing a novel that ends on a comparably engineered scene, so the question isn't purely academic for me either, but I'd like it adjudicated on its own terms rather than turned into writing advice.

Here, as best I can reconstruct it, is the exchange. (1) Renata said that whatever we felt for the dog, it wasn't grief, because grief is for someone or something you believe existed and is now gone, and none of us believes the dog ever existed — so it had to be a lesser, different state, "quasi-grief," existing only inside the game of make-believe the chapter invites us into. She said this was basically Kendall Walton's point about horror movies: his stock example is a man named Charles watching a film with a man-eating green slime, and Walton's claim is that Charles doesn't feel real fear, only "quasi-fear," a state that depends on Charles playing along rather than believing anything. (2) Sam said that was a word chosen to win the argument rather than describe it: his throat had actually tightened and his eyes had actually stung, and he wanted to know what gave Renata the standing to tell him, from outside his own head, that this wasn't grief. (3) Renata answered with a thought experiment: if she falsely told him his own dog back home had died, and he broke down, and she then said she'd made it up, he'd feel a rush of relief, because his grief had been hostage to a belief that turned out false; nothing anyone could ever discover about the dog in the chapter could produce that relief, or its opposite, because there was never a truth at stake to begin with. (4) Sam replied that belief can't be a general requirement on emotion, or ordinary fear becomes a mystery: people are afraid of flying while correctly believing it's extraordinarily safe, and afraid of the dark in a house they believe to be empty. If belief isn't required there, he said, it's unclear why fiction alone should be held to a stricter standard. (5) Renata said the cases weren't parallel: a nervous flier's fear still answers to a real, if tiny, probability of disaster, and could in principle be revised by evidence, whereas nothing evidential could ever bear on the fate of a fictional dog one way or the other, which is a difference in kind, not merely degree. She added that real grief also disposes you to do something afterward — hold a funeral, notice an absence at the table — and that whatever Sam had felt seemed to have evaporated by dessert. (6) Sam said she'd quietly swapped her own test: she'd started by asking what his grief was hostage to, and had ended by asking what it disposed him to do, and that by this second, newer standard his own grief for his father, twelve years on, would count as fake too, since it no longer disposes him to do anything at all. At that point a third writer said, half-joking, "you two are just reinventing the paradox of fiction," which didn't stop either of them.

Could you work through moves (1) through (6) and say, for each, whether Renata or Sam has the stronger position — or whether it's a genuine standoff — naming the assumption doing the real work each time, especially anywhere you think one of them switches criteria without admitting it. Please don't just hand down an overall winner. Then, stepping back from the individual moves: has anything said at that table actually found a way out of the paradox of fiction, or does the paradox survive the whole exchange intact, and what would you say a fully satisfying answer still owes us?
