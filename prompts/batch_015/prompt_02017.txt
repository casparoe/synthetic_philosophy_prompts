I am finishing preparations for the closing sessions of a graduate seminar on epistemology devoted to Wittgenstein's *On Certainty* and its afterlife in contemporary "hinge epistemology," and I want to be able to present one dispute in the secondary literature with real precision rather than the somewhat impressionistic version that keeps creeping into my lecture notes. We have already covered the basic Wittgensteinian idea that propositions such as "the earth has existed for many years" or "here is one hand" are not held on the basis of evidence at all, but instead function as fixed hinges on which our ordinary practices of doubting and justifying turn. What I have not managed to pin down to my satisfaction is how best to adjudicate between Crispin Wright's and Duncan Pritchard's rival accounts of what kind of standing these hinge commitments actually have.

As I understand it, Wright wants to keep hinges inside the space of epistemic evaluation. Even though we have no evidence for what he calls cornerstone propositions, such as "there is an external world" or "my cognitive faculties are reliable," he argues in "Warrant for Nothing (and Foundations for Free)?" that we possess a non-evidential warrant for them, an entitlement of cognitive project, so that trusting the hinges turns out to be rational after all, just not rational by the evidentialist's lights. Pritchard, in *Epistemic Angst*, rejects this move and defends what he calls a quasi-fideist reading, on which hinge commitments are strictly arational: not the sort of thing that can be warranted, entitled, justified, or doubted, closer to what Wittgenstein describes as an animal certainty operating beneath the level at which rational appraisal gets any purchase at all.

Could you compare these two readings for me in some depth? Please reconstruct the strongest version of each position, indicate which passages or themes in *On Certainty* pull interpreters toward one reading rather than the other, and then tell me, with justification, which of the two does more genuine work against the radical skeptic rather than simply relocating the pressure the skeptic exerts. You are welcome to bring in Michael Williams's contextualism or Annalisa Coliva's "extended rationality" account as a foil if you think either usefully sharpens the contrast, but I would like Wright and Pritchard to remain the center of the discussion.
