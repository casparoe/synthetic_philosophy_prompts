Ok so this is a weirdly specific question but it's been stuck in my head all week. I'm in a history of economic thought seminar and we just spent like two full weeks slowly crawling through Book IV of the Wealth of Nations, which was not what I expected going in -- I always pictured Smith as basically just the "invisible hand, markets good" guy, but he's actually pretty savage about the whole colonial project. He keeps hammering on the point that the monopoly of colony trade only pads the pockets of what he calls "a particular order of men" (i.e. merchants) while the actual cost of defending and administering the empire falls on the country as a whole, so Britain as a nation ends up worse off even though a handful of people did great. He's also got that bit about how founding an empire just to create a captive market of customers for your merchants is a project fit for "a nation whose government is influenced by shopkeepers" -- which is apparently where the "nation of shopkeepers" line actually comes from, not Napoleon, which honestly blew my mind.

The detail that's actually stuck with me though is something our prof mentioned almost as a throwaway line: Smith explicitly compares those temporary colonial trading monopolies -- the exclusive charters given to companies that risked their own money openning up trade with, in his words, "some remote and barbarous nation" -- to patents and copyrights. His argument is that granting someone a temporary exclusive privilege is a perfectly reasonable way for the state to reward the risk and expense of a costly venture, whether that venture is a new trade route, a new machine, or a book. So by his own logic, a colonial trading charter and a patent are basically the same policy tool, just pointed at different kinds of risky enterprise.

Given that Smith draws that comparison himself, and given how damning his final verdict on those trade monopolies gets once they matured into things like the East India Company (he straight up calls merchant-companies-turned-sovereigns close to the worst possible form of government for any country), what do you think he'd say about today's global patent regime -- specifically pharmaceutical patents that wealthier countries pressure developing countries into enforcing through trade agreements, like the whole TRIPS/WTO framework and the fights over generic AIDS drugs in the 2000s and then COVID vaccines more recently? Would he read that as a legitimate case of his own "reward the risk-taker" logic, or would he say it curdled into exactly the kind of mercantile monopoly racket he spends Book IV attacking, just running through patent law instead of royal charters this time? Trying to get at how he'd actually reason it through from his own specific arguments against the colonial monopolies, not just a generic "Smith liked free markets" answer.
