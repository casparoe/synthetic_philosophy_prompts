I'm putting together a graduate seminar on Kant's practical philosophy and want to devote a session to the notoriously awkward little discussion of our duties regarding non-human animals that Kant appends, as an episodic addendum, to his treatment of perfect duties to oneself in the Doctrine of Virtue. I've taught the standard summary of this material for years — no direct duties to non-rational beings, only duties "with regard to" them — but a colleague challenged my reading of it at a reading group last week, and I find I can no longer defend it as cleanly as I used to, so I would like a second opinion on the actual text.

Kant's official line is that any impression of owing something to an animal itself rests on a conceptual confusion. He traces it to what he calls an amphiboly of the moral concepts of reflection — reusing, deliberately, the label he gives in the first Critique to Leibniz's conflation of sensible and intellectual grounds of comparison — and describes the error as one of mistaking his duty "with regard to other beings for a duty to those beings" (MS 6:442). Cruelty toward animals is still forbidden, but only because it damages a disposition in the agent that Kant says is "very serviceable to morality" in our dealings with other human beings (MS 6:443); on this official picture, the animal itself drops out as morally irrelevant. And yet, when Kant turns to cases in the transcripts of his ethics lectures, the content of the resulting duty tracks the individual animal remarkably closely: he says that "if a dog, for example, has served his master long and faithfully, that is an analogue of merit" (27:459), which is supposed to generate a requirement to keep and care for the dog once it is too old to work, and he treats turning the animal out instead as a mark of a mean-spirited character — all while insisting that the dog itself, lacking judgment, cannot be wronged by anything we do to it.

What I would like help with is the apparent tension between these two moments: a diagnosis of "amphiboly" that is supposed to dissolve any sense that animals themselves matter, and a working-out of the resulting duty that seems to answer to the animal's own history and capacities in a way that looks like more than bookkeeping about the agent's character. Is there a principled Kantian way of reconciling these — some account of how a merely self-regarding, indirect duty could nonetheless be legitimately shaped, in its content, by facts about a particular animal — or should we conclude that Kant's examples are doing argumentative work that his official theory does not actually entitle him to?
