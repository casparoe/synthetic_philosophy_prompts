I've recently been reading Cheryl Misak's biography of Frank Ramsey, and I keep coming back to what looks like a real tension between two things he wrote before he died in 1930, aged twenty-six. In the 1925 talk later published as "Epilogue," the twenty-two-year-old Ramsey argued that cosmic scale has nothing to do with meaning: he refused to feel humbled by the vastness of the heavens ("the stars may be large, but they cannot think or love"), described his picture of the world as drawn "in perspective, and not like a model to scale," with human beings occupying the foreground and the stars shrunk to the size of threepenny bits, and then extended this same deflationary attitude from space to time, observing that the eventual heat death of the universe has a "present value at compound interest" that "is almost nothing," and concluding: "Nor is the present less valuable because the future is blank." Only three years later, though, in "A Mathematical Theory of Saving" (1928), Ramsey the economist seems to take the opposite view of time: he argued that discounting the welfare of future generations simply because they come later is "ethically indefensible" and "arises merely from the weakness of the imagination" — a line that has become one of the standard reference points in the long-running debate over discounting the future, a debate that present-day "longtermist" thinkers have inherited and pushed much further, holding that because our actions could affect an astronomically large number of people over the long-term future, safeguarding that future should outweigh almost every other moral and political priority we might otherwise have.

Supposing Ramsey had somehow lived long enough to encounter longtermism in something like its current form, how do you think he would have reacted to it, and why? Would he have recognized it as simply the natural extension of his own argument against discounting, and so found its conclusions congenial? Or would the instinct behind "Epilogue" — his impatience with any argument that asks us to be morally overawed by sheer scale, whether of light-years or of numbers of possible future people — have made him wary of the move longtermists often make from "the future could be extremely large" to "therefore it swamps nearly everything else we might otherwise care about"? I'd like you to actually work through the apparent tension between the two texts rather than simply declaring which side he'd have landed on, and to end with a considered, defended guess about where his actual commitments would most plausibly have left him.
