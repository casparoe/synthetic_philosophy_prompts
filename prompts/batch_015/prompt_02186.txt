I don't have any formal training in philosophy or computer science, but for the past year or so I've been reading everything I can find, mostly on old blogs and forums, about how one might make a future superintelligent AI actually good for humanity rather than just powerful. The idea I keep circling back to is Eliezer Yudkowsky's proposal of a "coherent extrapolated volition": instead of programming an AI with what we currently say we want, it should figure out and act on what we would want "if we knew more, thought faster, were more the people we wished we were" — or, to paraphrase the rest of his description, if we had matured together as a species, with our different wishes converging rather than pulling apart. When I first ran into this, I found it genuinely clever, since it seemed to dodge the obvious objection that our present-day wants are shortsighted, inconsistent, and often based on plain factual mistakes.

The more I sit with it, though, the more I keep snagging on the same worry, and I haven't managed to talk myself out of it. Consider how different my own wants are now compared to when I was fifteen: if I imagine a further extrapolated version of myself, shaped by decades more knowledge and reflection than I currently have, that person seems like they could be almost a stranger to me, with priorities I can't presently anticipate or even recognize as mine. So there seems to be a dilemma. If the extrapolation is kept modest enough that the result is still clearly recognizable as me (or us, collectively), I doubt it is powerful enough to resolve the deep confusions and disagreements it is supposed to resolve. But if it is allowed to run far enough to actually do that work, I do not see why I should think of its output as being good for me, rather than as simply the preferences of some other, more idealized being who happens to be descended from my current self. I raised this with a friend who works in software, and she said it sounded like an engineering detail rather than a real problem, but that answer never felt right to me. It seems more basic than that: maybe even a problem for any theory that defines a person's real interests in terms of a wiser, better-informed version of that same person, and not just for this one AI proposal.

Could you help me work through whether this is a genuine, recognized problem for coherent extrapolated volition, and for "idealized preference" accounts of a person's good more generally, rather than just a confusion on my part? I would especially like to understand how someone who still wants to defend some version of extrapolated volition might try to escape the dilemma, and whether doing so forces them to give up either the modest horn or the ambitious one.
