For my high school's philosophy club, we've spent the last couple of meetings on David Lewis's counterpart theory versus the more traditional "transworld identity" view that philosophers like Kripke seem to assume whenever they talk about possible worlds. The part that's really stuck with me is Kripke's objection: on Lewis's view, a sentence like "Hubert Humphrey could have won the 1968 election" is made true by some other person in a different possible world — a "counterpart" who merely resembles Humphrey closely enough — actually winning, rather than by anything that could have happened to Humphrey himself. Kripke's complaint is that this seems to change the subject, since Humphrey himself presumably wouldn't have cared in the slightest about the electoral fortunes of some qualitatively similar stranger in another universe. Lewis does have a reply — he can insist that caring about your counterparts' fortunes just is what caring about your own modal fortunes amounts to — but our club leader pointed out that most of us understood both positions well enough on paper without really feeling why anyone would find either one compelling, or troubling.

So instead of writing the debate up as another essay, I want to try turning it into fiction. Could you write me a short story, somewhere around 1000–1500 words, built around a character who is offered some device or event (a branching timeline, a duplication chamber, or whatever premise works best dramatically) such that they themselves will die or disappear while a qualitatively identical "counterpart" of them goes on to live out their plans in another world — or something else in that neighborhood? The emotional crux I'm after is how much comfort or dread the character should take from knowing their counterpart will flourish. I don't want it to just be a generic parallel-universe tale; I'd like the story to force the reader to sit with the actual question of whether "my counterpart will be fine" is good news for me in anything like the way "I will be fine" is, and ideally to leave open which side of the debate — Lewis's or Kripke's — comes off looking more plausible by the end, rather than resolving things too neatly.
