Had a long argument with my friend Mara last night that I still can't resolve. She clerks for an appeals court and told me about a real quirk in how panels decide cases. Take a 3-judge panel ruling on whether a company breached a contract, where liability needs both a valid contract and an actual breach. Judge 1: contract valid + breached -> liable. Judge 2: contract valid, not breached -> not liable. Judge 3: no valid contract (though privately thinks it'd count as breach too) -> not liable. Each judge is perfectly consistent. But poll the two underlying questions separately instead of the bottom line, and a different majority shows up on each - 2 of 3 say the contract was valid, a different 2 of 3 say it was breached - so mechanically applying the rule to those majorities gives "liable," even though judges 2 and 3, the actual majority, think the company isnt liable. Mara says that's still the more honest way to decide, since a direct verdict vote just hides the disagreement. I said a procedure that hands down a conclusion nobody actually holds is broken, however principled it looks on paper. Neither of us gave in.

Write me a short story - any setting, no judges or courtroom required - that lives inside that specific wrongness: a group reaching an official decision that not one member would sign onto if asked point-blank. Bonus if the characters can feel something's off before they can say what.
