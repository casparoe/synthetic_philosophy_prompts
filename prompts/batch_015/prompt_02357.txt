I'm a retired chemist (37 years in industrial R&D, if anyone's counting), and these days I fill my time reading moral philosophy, which is a strange change of pace after a career where problems actually had answers. I even brought this up at the little philosophy discussion group I joined at the library and mostly got shrugs, so I'm hoping you can do better. Here's the thing that's been rattling around in my head since I heard about a fight that broke out outside a bar in my town a few weeks back.

The short version, from people who were there: two guys, Ray and Dan, got into it late one night. By all accounts Ray started it — shoved Dan first over some petty disagreement, no real provocation from Dan's side at all. Once it turned physical, though, everyone agrees it was a "clean" fight by the usual local standards: fists only, nobody else jumps in, it stops the second someone's down or says stop. Both walked away with a black eye, no weapons involved, no hard feelings after.

What's bugging me is how people talked about it afterward. Everybody agreed Ray was in the wrong for starting things, full stop. But then, almost in the same breath, they described the actual fighting as if Ray and Dan had been on totally equal footing the whole time — each just as entitled as the other to be throwing punches, since both stuck to the same code. Nobody thought Ray's punches counted for less than Dan's, so long as he played by the rules, like whether you had the right to be hitting the other guy was a completely seperate question from who unjustly started the whole mess. I definately think something's off about that, but I can't tell if it's a real philosophical instinct or if I'm just being fussy in retirement. Does following the same rules really put two people on equal moral footing once a fight is underway, no matter who was unjust in starting it? Or does the injustice of how it began somehow travel forward and make one side's blows less legitimate, even though both guys are doing the literal same thing with their fists? I feel like I'm missing the right vocabulary or theory for this one, so I'd love your take — and if it turns out philosophers have actually built careers arguing about exactly this kind of case, all the better.
