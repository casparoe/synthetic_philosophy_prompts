I'm working on a feature about literary and philosophical treatments of the unreality of time, which I eventually want to set alongside physicists' talk of the "block universe," and in researching it I've been rereading Jorge Luis Borges's 1946 essay "A New Refutation of Time" (a rework of a shorter piece he had published two years earlier in the magazine Sur) more carefully than I expected to need to. Most of it reads as a genuinely careful argument rather than a literary flourish: Borges takes Berkeley's denial of mind-independent matter and Hume's denial of an enduring self and pushes them further than either philosopher was willing to go — since both men, for all their skepticism about substance, still took the reality of time for granted — in order to argue that once matter and spirit have been dissolved into a mere sequence of perceptions, there is no principled way to keep time around either, as the single thread that would link those perceptions into a before and after, or into a "now" shared with other people's perceptions elsewhere. He tries to make the point vivid with an odd little illustration involving Huckleberry Finn briefly waking on the raft at night, registering a few stars and the sound of the river, and sinking back into sleep. Then, in the essay's final movement, he appears to reverse course entirely, confessing that time is, in his own words, "the substance I am made of," invoking a river, a tiger, and a fire that constitute him even as he had just spent pages arguing that none of these ultimately exist, and concluding, resignedly, that the world turns out to be real after all.

A friend who teaches philosophy waved this off when I brought it up, saying the ending is either the whole point of the essay or a total capitulation, and that nobody really agrees which — which is not something I can responsibly put in a piece without having a view of my own. So I would like your help actually working through it, rather than simply narrating the argument back to me. First, what exactly is the Huckleberry Finn passage supposed to establish, and does it succeed — is Borges only denying that instants are linked in succession, or also denying that they can be dated and placed alongside simultaneous instants elsewhere in one shared time, and are those really the same claim? Second, is Borges entitled to use Berkeley's and Hume's own premises to reach a conclusion that neither of them drew, or does something go wrong in pushing the argument that extra step? And third, the real crux of it: is that closing concession a plain admission that the whole preceding argument fails, or is there a defensible reading on which Borges is distinguishing time's metaphysical groundlessness from its existential inescapability, such that denying and affirming time are not actually in tension? Please work through these questions in some depth and close with an actual verdict on whether "A New Refutation of Time" succeeds, on its own terms, as a refutation of time.
