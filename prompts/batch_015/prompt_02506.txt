After a thirty-five-year career in condensed-matter physics, I have spent much of my retirement trying to properly learn the philosophy I only ever absorbed second-hand from colleagues over the years. This past winter I finally sat down with Roger Penrose's argument, in *The Emperor's New Mind* and *Shadows of the Mind*, that Gödel's incompleteness theorem shows the human mind cannot be equivalent to any computer or formal system; I also tracked down J. R. Lucas's original 1961 paper making essentially the same case. I found the reasoning quite compelling and brought it to a small philosophy discussion group I have joined at the local university, expecting a lively but broadly sympathetic conversation.

Instead, the two members with formal training in logic dismissed the argument almost out of hand, and I came away feeling I had been handed a verdict rather than a refutation. As I understand it, the argument runs as follows: Gödel proved that for any consistent formal system powerful enough to encompass arithmetic, there exists a true statement that the system itself cannot prove; yet a human mathematician can stand outside the system and recognize that very statement as true. Since no formal system, and therefore no computer program, could ever do what the human mind manifestly does in such cases, it seems to follow that the mind cannot simply be a formal system or algorithm running on neurons. This looks to me like a fairly direct application of a theorem that nobody seriously disputes, so I am struggling to understand how the conclusion could be treated as merely one contestable philosophical position among others, rather than something the mathematics itself has already settled. Could you help me see exactly where, if anywhere, this line of reasoning breaks down, and why it has not simply resolved the debate between mechanists and anti-mechanists in philosophy of mind once and for all?
