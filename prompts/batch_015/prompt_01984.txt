Two colleagues of mine spent most of a departmental reading group on epistemic game theory arguing about what Aumann's Agreement Theorem actually tells us about disagreement in real life. (2) Marcus objected that this overreaches: the theorem's antecedent requires common knowledge of posteriors, not merely mutual explanation, and ordinary conversation, however thorough, never secures the full infinite hierarchy of "I know that you know that I know..." that common knowledge demands -- so two ideal Bayesians could talk for hours and still, quite rationally, never reach the condition the theorem needs. (3) Elena replied by invoking Geanakoplos and Polemarchakis's result that if two people who share a prior simply keep announcing their current posteriors to one another in turn, the sequence is guaranteed to stabilize after finitely many rounds, at which point the resulting agreement is automatically common knowledge; two colleagues who have rehashed the same disagreement for years, she said, have had ample opportunity to run exactly this process, so their unresolved disagreement is still telling. (4) Marcus answered that the Geanakoplos-Polemarchakis result still inherits Aumann's common-prior assumption, that nothing about how two people behave can ever establish that they share a prior rather than merely sharing information, and that any stubborn disagreement can therefore always be redescribed as a difference in priors -- so Elena's argument either presupposes exactly what is in dispute, or, once differing priors are allowed, loses its force against two people who simply started out seeing the world differently.
