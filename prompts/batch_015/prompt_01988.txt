I am a retired astrophysicist, and since retiring I've joined a small philosophy discussion group that recently spent two sessions on the fine-tuning argument for design. At our last meeting, two of the members, Miriam and Colin, got into an exchange the rest of us couldn't settle, and I'd value an outside verdict.

(1) Miriam opened by noting that quantities such as the cosmological constant and the relative strengths of the fundamental forces sit within an extraordinarily narrow range that permits stars, chemistry, and life at all; treating this as a brute fact needing no explanation struck her as intellectually lazy, and she took it as evidence of design or of some other tuning mechanism.

(2) Colin replied that a multiverse dissolves the puzzle without any designer: if countless universes exist with different constants, it's unsurprising that we find ourselves in one that's life-permitting, since no one could possibly exist to observe the others. On his view this is a plain observation-selection effect, not a cosmic coincidence.

(3) Miriam countered that this move commits what she called the inverse gambler's fallacy: someone who walks into a casino, sees a freak run of luck at one table, and concludes there must be many other tables running elsewhere is reasoning badly, since those other tables don't make that particular run any less surprising. A trillion unseen universes, she argued, might make it likely that some universe or other is fine-tuned, but they do nothing to make it likely that this universe -- the one we're actually asking about -- would turn out that way.

(4) Colin answered that the casino analogy breaks down: a gambler could in principle have walked up to any table and seen any result and still be around afterward to think about it, but we could never have found ourselves surveying a universe that wasn't life-permitting, since there would be no one there to do the surveying. Conditionalizing on our own existence, he said, is exactly what changes the odds here, in a way it never does for an ordinary gambler.

Neither of them is a professional philosopher, and neither could talk the other out of their position. Could you go through the exchange move by move, say who has the better of it at each stage and why, and flag anything relevant that both of them seem to be missing?
