Over the past year, my job has shifted from general machine-learning work to a small causal-inference team, where much of my week now involves building structural causal models and using do-calculus to estimate treatment effects from observational data. At a recent journal club, a colleague who did a philosophy PhD before moving into industry mentioned, almost in passing, that everything our team does is essentially an implementation of Woodward's "interventionist" theory of causation, and that this is a genuine rival to the older theory of causation I dimly remember from an undergraduate philosophy elective — the one on which causation is analyzed as counterfactual dependence, cashed out via similarity among possible worlds (Lewis's theory, I believe). When I asked her whether the two views actually disagree about anything substantive, or whether interventionism is really just a formal, graph-friendly restatement of the same underlying idea, she said that question was exactly what divides people in the literature, and then had to dash off before saying anything more. It has been nagging at me ever since.

I would like your help thinking this through, in the form of a dialogue between two philosophers, each a fully convinced and technically sophisticated defender of one of these views: one a committed Lewisian counterfactual theorist, the other a committed Woodwardian interventionist. Rather than trading slogans, I would like them to work through a concrete case together: suppose two independent sensors are both monitoring the same faulty fuse, each wired to trip a circuit breaker the instant it detects an overload; sensor A detects the overload and trips the breaker a few milliseconds before sensor B's own signal arrives, so that B's signal reaches an already-tripped breaker and accomplishes nothing. I want the two characters to argue about whether, and in virtue of what, A's detection counts as a cause of the breaker's tripping, given that the breaker would have tripped at essentially the same instant even without A, purely courtesy of B. Beyond that case, I would also like the Lewisian to press the interventionist on whether defining causation in terms of "interventions" is viciously circular, since an intervention is itself specified in causal terms — and I would like the interventionist to get a genuine opportunity to answer that charge, perhaps by turning it back on the Lewisian's similarity metric, which some critics say is reverse-engineered to fit our causal intuitions rather than independently motivated. Please do not let either side win by default or concede a point just to move things along; I want to see exactly where the disagreement bites once the terminology is stripped away, rather than a mere restatement of each theory's slogans.
