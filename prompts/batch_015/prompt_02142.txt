Two months ago I finished a philosophy MA thesis on full-information and informed-desire theories of well-being, spending most of my time on Richard Brandt's account of a person's good as whatever desires would survive "cognitive psychotherapy" (his term for repeated confrontation with all available, vividly represented, relevant information) and on Peter Railton's rival picture, on which your good is fixed by what a fully informed, fully rational counterpart of you would want you to want. I have since started a short research fellowship at an AI safety organization, and one of the first primary texts I was handed was Eliezer Yudkowsky's 2004 essay introducing "Coherent Extrapolated Volition" (CEV): the proposal that an aligned superintelligence should act on humanity's extrapolated wishes rather than its present and possibly confused ones -- wishes defined as what we would want "if we knew more, thought faster, were more the people we wished we were," with the idealization run not for a single person but for humanity as a whole, and with the further requirement that different people's extrapolated wishes converge rather than pull apart. I have not been able to shake the impression that this is the same idealizing machinery from my thesis, simply re-run at civilizational scale.

I would like you to work out this comparison carefully rather than just noting the family resemblance. First, please do the actual mapping: identify which part of Brandt's cognitive-psychotherapy procedure, or of Railton's fully-informed-counterpart account, or (as a third comparison point, if it helps) Rawls's Sidgwick-derived notion of "deliberative rationality" in A Theory of Justice, section 64, corresponds to each ingredient of CEV -- more information, more speed, more coherence, continued moral growth -- and tell me where, if anywhere, the analogy strains rather than holding up.

Second, I would like an actual verdict on a more pointed worry, not merely a survey of considerations on both sides. Individual-level informed-desire theories ran into two well-known objections: that the idealization procedure may fail to converge on any determinate answer, since small differences in how the relevant information is presented or absorbed can send the idealization down different paths (a worry associated with David Sobel's critique of Brandt and Railton); and that even a determinate idealized self's preferences may carry no real authority over, or motivational purchase on, the actual person, who might reasonably deny that this remote, hyper-rational stranger speaks for them (Connie Rosati's line of criticism). Does moving to the population-scale case -- with CEV's explicit coherence requirement, and its willingness to simply report an unresolved "spread" where people's extrapolated wishes fail to converge -- make genuine progress on either problem, or does it just relocate both of them into a form that is harder to notice, now disguised as facts about aggregation and convergence across billions of extrapolated people rather than facts about one idealized self? Tell me which it is, and explain the mechanism behind your answer; I would find "it is a bit of both, depending on the objection" an acceptable answer only if you can actually show why the two problems come apart under aggregation, rather than merely asserting that they might.
