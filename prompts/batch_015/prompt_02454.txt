I'm working on a piece about thinkers whose politics were shaped — or oddly untouched — by the great ruptures of their age, and there's a Goethe episode I can't quite get to the bottom of. It's from an entry of August 2, 1830, recorded not by Eckermann but by Frédéric Soret, the Genevan naturalist then helping Goethe prepare a French edition of his botanical writings, later folded into the standard English edition of the Conversations of Goethe. News of the July Revolution had just reached Weimar:

"The news of the Revolution of July, which had already commenced, reached Weimar to-day, and set every one in a commotion. I went in the course of the afternoon to Goethe's. 'Now,' exclaimed he to me, as I entered, 'what do you think of this great event? The volcano has come to an eruption; everything is in flames, and we have no longer a transaction with closed doors!'

'A frightful story,' returned I. 'But what could be expected under such notoriously bad circumstances, and with such a ministry, otherwise than that the whole would end in the expulsion of the royal family?'

'We do not appear to understand each other, my good friend,' returned Goethe. 'I am not speaking of those people, but of something quite different. I am speaking of the contest, so important for science, between Cuvier and Geoffrey de Saint Hilaire, which has come to an open rupture in the academy... The matter is of the highest importance, and you can form no conception of what I felt at the intelligence of the sitting of the 19th of July.'"

Goethe carries on like this for some time, plainly more excited by the Académie des Sciences than by the fall of Charles X. I get the surface reference well enough — this is the Cuvier–Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire dispute over "unity of composition" in animal anatomy, a cause Goethe had pushed on his own scientific grounds for decades. What I can't work out is what the episode is doing to, or telling us about, his politics. Is this simply an old man's monomania — changing the subject to his hobby-horse — or is there a real thread connecting his lifelong preference, in nature as in the state, for gradual, law-governed development over sudden rupture? And if that thread is real, how does it survive contact with his well-documented admiration for Napoleon, hardly a gradualist? I'd like help working out how much weight this anecdote, and that broader thesis, can actually bear, ideally grounded in what's known of his political conduct and record rather than the poetry alone.
