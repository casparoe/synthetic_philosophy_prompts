I was covering a mechanistic-interpretability workshop last month, and the Q&A after a talk on sparse autoencoders turned into a genuine disagreement that the moderator eventually had to cut off. (1) The speaker, an interpretability researcher, had just shown that clamping a single 'feature' her team's autoencoder had extracted to an artificially high value reliably steered the model's output in a specific, predictable way, and she argued this shows the feature is a real unit of the network's internal ontology, not an artifact of her analysis. (2) A philosopher of science in the audience objected that predictable steering doesn't establish this. Because of superposition — the network packing more features than it has dimensions by encoding them as overlapping directions — activation space can be sliced into many different overcomplete bases, each yielding directions that are individually steerable; finding one such direction, he said, doesn't show it is a privileged joint in the network's actual computation rather than one of many equally arbitrary ones. (3) She replied that non-uniqueness undermines realism no more than the existence of multiple valid coordinate systems undermines realism about physical quantities, and that the real evidence is that broadly similar features keep recurring across independently trained models and across scale — best explained, she said, by the underlying computation doing the sorting rather than by her choice of dictionary. (4) He answered that the analogy fails: physicists prefer coordinate systems on independent theoretical grounds, such as the symmetries of the system, whereas she chose her basis because it was sparse and legible to humans, so recurring 'features' across models might just mean that different networks land on similar statistical shortcuts that resemble human categories, without those shortcuts being the causal atoms of the computation. Neither gave ground before the moderator moved on.
