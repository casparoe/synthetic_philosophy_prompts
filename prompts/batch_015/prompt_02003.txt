Retired geologist here -- spent my whole career thinking in terms of millions of years, so you'd think time was the one thing I had sorted out, but lately its got me all turned around. I just read Carlo Rovelli's "The Order of Time," where he argues there's no fundamental "now" anywhere in physics -- that time as we feel it, flowing and unified and universal, is basically a low-resolution effect of entropy and our own blurry vantage point on the universe, not a basic feature of reality.

That sent me back to Borges's old essay "A New Refutation of Time," which I'd read once before and never really knew what to do with. Most of it is Borges building a case, out of Berkeley and Hume, that since there's no enduring matter and (per Hume) no enduring self either, every perceived instant might be self-sufficient, linked to the others by nothing but habit -- so maybe there's no real succession at all, just one moment after another with no thread through them. Then in the last couple pages he seems to abandon his own argument, saying that whatever the logic proves, he still can't shake off time, that "the world, unfortunately, is real."

He died in '86, well before this entropic, relational picture of time became a serious position in physics. If you handed him Rovelli's book today, would he read it as vindication of his old essay, or would he say Rovelli's just rediscovered the easy half of the argument and skated right past the hard half -- the about-face Borges himself makes at the very end? Or is there some other reaction you think is actually more likely, given what else he wrote about time over the years? Genuinely curious what you think, and why.
