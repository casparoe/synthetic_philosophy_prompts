I write a science-and-ideas newsletter, and this week I fell down a rabbit hole that started with a philosopher friend explaining, over dinner, why "the cup is on the table" is apparently a genuinely hard problem and not just, y'know, a sentence. It's this classic argument called Bradley's regress: if a lump of sugar is just its qualities (whiteness, sweetness, hardness) bundled together, you need some relation to unify them into one thing -- but that relation itself has to be related to the qualities its tying together, which calls for a further relation to do THAT job, and so on forever. Bradley ran versions of this against relations generally, not just bundles of qualities, and used the whole mess to argue that "many seperate things relating to one another" is ultimately an incoherent way to think about the world -- real unity belongs only to his Absolute. Russell fought about this with Bradley directly in the pages of Mind around 1910, and I gather this is also basically a cousin of the "unity of the proposition" problem (what's the actual difference between the words "wise, Alice, is" and the sentence "Alice is wise"?), which is supposed to bother people who couldn't care less about 19th-century idealism.

Here's my actual ask: could you write me a short but genuinely combative dialogue between a convinced Bradleyan monist and a pluralist who thinks relations really do relate without needing further relations to tie them down (channeling something like Russell's or Armstrong's side of this fight)? I don't want either one strawmanned -- I want the monist to really press on whether things like Armstrong's "states of affairs" or a bare "non-relational tie" are doing actual explanatory work or just relabeling the mystery with fancier words, and I want the pluralist to come back with something better than "well, relating is just what relations do." Bonus points if they notice mid-argument that they're basically also fighting about the unity-of-the-proposition thing, whether or not the monist has patience for reviving 19th century idealism to make that point. Set it wherever feels natural -- a conference afterparty, a whiteboard after a reading group, whatever works.
