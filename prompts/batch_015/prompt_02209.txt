I am working on a piece about the prehistory of social choice theory, and one anecdote keeps nagging at me. When Jean-Charles de Borda's rank-order voting method came under fire in the late eighteenth century for being gameable — a voter could shade a strong rival's score downward rather than ranking honestly, and shift the outcome — Borda is said to have waved off the objection by remarking that his scheme was "intended only for honest men" (the line comes down to us via Duncan Black's 1958 history of committee theory). This was not an idle exchange: Borda spent years sparring with Condorcet, who preferred his own pairwise-comparison method to rank-order counting and used his 1785 essay partly to needle Borda about that very vulnerability to manipulation.

What neither man could have known is that they were circling something far more general than a defect in one voting scheme or a character flaw in one electorate. The Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem, established by Allan Gibbard and Mark Satterthwaite in the early-to-mid 1970s, shows that once there are three or more possible outcomes and voters' preferences are not artificially restricted, every voting rule that is not a dictatorship can be manipulated by someone, in some situation — a result that also catches Condorcet-consistent rules, once they are completed with some tie-breaking procedure for cycles, so Borda's old rival fares no better.

Suppose Borda could be handed this theorem today, in full generality. Would he take it as vindication — evidence that "intended only for honest men" was tracking something structurally unavoidable rather than dodging a fair criticism — or would an eighteenth-century mathematician-engineer have had grounds to resist its sweep? I would also like your view on whether he would have seized on the escape route that domain restrictions offer: preferences that are single-peaked, a notion Duncan Black himself only formalized in 1948, can support voting rules that are both non-dictatorial and strategy-proof, as later work established. Would Borda have read that as rescuing his project for the kinds of committees he actually designed for, or would he have seen it as conceding the general case to his critics? I would rather have your best specific, historically grounded reconstruction of his likely reasoning than a survey of the positions he could in principle have held.
