I am at work on a novel that includes a secondary character, a philosophy professor quietly coming apart, who keeps returning to a single passage from the "Why I Am So Clever" chapter of Nietzsche's Ecce Homo — the one where he holds forth on nutrition. I want his fixation to read as intellectually serious rather than as a tic, which means I need to understand the passage properly myself before I can dramatize his reading of it. Here it is, in the Anthony Ludovici translation:

"I am much more interested in another question, — a question upon which the 'salvation of humanity' depends to a far greater degree than it does upon any piece of theological curiosity: I refer to nutrition. [...] Nothing should be eaten between meals, coffee should be given up — coffee makes one gloomy. [...] Remain seated as little as possible, put no trust in any thought that is not born in the open, to the accompaniment of free bodily motion — nor in one in which even the muscles do not celebrate a feast. All prejudices take their origin in the intestines. A sedentary life, as I have already said elsewhere, is the real sin against the Holy Spirit."

What I can't work out is whether the slide from dietary advice to "all prejudices take their origin in the intestines" is meant as a serious physiological thesis doing real philosophical work, or as a provocation not meant to be read so literally. And if it is meant seriously, doesn't it immediately implicate itself: Nietzsche's own notoriously ruined digestion becomes, by his own logic, a reason to distrust his prejudices too — presumably including whichever ones drive the "revaluation of values" project. Does his broader diagnosis of morality and philosophy as symptoms of physiological states survive that kind of self-application, or is this passage just naturalism eating its own tail? I would like the actual philosophical stakes here, not a summary of what the passage says.
