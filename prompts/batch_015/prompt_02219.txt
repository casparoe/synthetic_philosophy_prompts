I'm researching a magazine feature on the shaky foundations of classical probability theory, and one historical "what if" — raised almost in passing by a mathematician I interviewed for the piece — has been nagging at me ever since. In his 1814 Essai philosophique sur les probabilités, Pierre-Simon Laplace laid out what's now generally known as the principle of indifference: when we have no grounds for favoring one possible case over another, we should treat them as equally probable. Laplace wrote as though this were close to self-evident, applying it confidently to everything from games of chance to the credibility of courtroom witnesses. He died in 1827, more than sixty years before Joseph Bertrand, in his 1889 Calcul des probabilités, produced the counterexample that now bears his name: ask for the probability that a "randomly drawn" chord of a circle is longer than the side of the equilateral triangle inscribed in it, and it turns out there are at least three different, equally symmetric-looking procedures for constructing such a chord "at random" — fixing two random endpoints on the circumference, fixing a random radius and then a random point along it, or fixing a random point inside the disk as the chord's midpoint. These respectively give probabilities of 1/3, 1/2, and 1/4, and nothing internal to the principle of indifference tells us which construction deserves to be called "the" random one.

For the piece, I'd like to include a speculative section on how Laplace himself might have reacted had he lived to see this direct challenge to his signature idea. Drawing on what we actually know about his intellectual habits — his strict determinism, his insistence that probability measures human ignorance rather than some objective feature of nature, and his lifelong ease with symmetry and invariance arguments in celestial mechanics — would he most plausibly have conceded that "equally possible" is simply underspecified once you move to continuous, geometric cases and requires some further principle to pin it down; have dug in and insisted that one of the three constructions is uniquely privileged; or have denied that his principle was ever meant to apply to cases like this one? I'd also be curious whether, in your view, his own way of reasoning, followed through consistently, would plausibly have anticipated something like E. T. Jaynes' 1973 attempt to rescue the principle by demanding invariance under translation, rotation, and scaling. That move, notably, singles out the 1/2 answer among the three — though I wonder whether it required conceptual resources that simply weren't available yet in Laplace's day.
