Quick reminder of the setup in case it's useful context: since "all ravens are black" is logically equivalent to "all non-black things are non-ravens" (the Equivalence Condition), and a white shoe is an instance of the latter, it looks like observing a white shoe should confirm that all ravens are black too. The standard Bayesian move felt like it settled this for me at first — sure, the shoe confirms it, just by an amount so tiny it's not worth acting on, since there are vastly more non-black things in the world than ravens — until I ran into I.J. Good's 1967 counterexample ("The White Shoe is a Red Herring"), where he rigs up background knowledge about two possible worlds such that actually observing a black raven makes it more likely that not all ravens are black. That seems to wreck the "it's just relative frequencies" story, and I honestly can't tell whether Maher's neo-Carnapian reply to Good actually rescues the Bayesian line or just buries the same problem under more machinery.
