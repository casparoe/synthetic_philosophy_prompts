I recently donated a kidney to a stranger, becoming the first link in a paired-exchange chain. Everyone around me describes this as going "above and beyond," yet whenever someone asks why I did it, my honest answer is that it never felt optional: once I knew I could do it safely, declining felt closed to me, not like a permission I simply chose not to use. I have since learned this is not just my own confusion — there is a recognized puzzle in the supererogation literature, sometimes called the heroism paradox, arising from the fact that people who perform paradigmatic supererogatory acts very often sincerely deny that the act was optional for them, which sits uneasily with the standard definition of supererogation as praiseworthy but not required. I would like an example that presses this paradox as hard as it will go: a realistic scenario, not necessarily involving organ donation, featuring an act everyone would classify as supererogatory, in which the agent's first-person insistence that "I had no real choice" can neither be dismissed as false modesty nor accepted at face value as proof that the act was actually obligatory. Please lay out the scenario and explain exactly why both of those standard escape routes fail in this case.
