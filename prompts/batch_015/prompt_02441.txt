I'm reporting a piece on how different philosophical traditions try to talk people out of their fear of death, and in rereading the Zhuangzi in Burton Watson's translation I've gotten stuck on something the secondary literature I've found doesn't quite address. Zhuangzi doesn't seem to offer just one argument for equanimity about death; he seems to offer two, and I'm no longer sure they sit together comfortably. In "Discussion on Making All Things Equal," the second of the Inner Chapters, he tells the story of Lady Li, the daughter of a border guard, who wept bitterly when she was taken captive to the state of Jin and later, once settled into the ruler's palace, wondered why she had ever cried — he uses this to suggest we have no way of knowing whether the dead come to regret how hard they once clung to life. That's an argument from ignorance: we assume death is bad, but nothing entitles us to that assumption. A few chapters later, though, in "The Great and Venerable Teacher," the dying Master Lai describes the "Great Clod" that burdened him with a body, put him to work through his life, eased him in old age, and will now rest him in death, reasoning that whatever made his life good must, by the same token, make his death good too. There Zhuangzi doesn't sound agnostic at all; he sounds like he knows exactly what death is. The same confidence turns up again in "Perfect Happiness," where Zhuangzi is found keeping time on an upturned bowl and singing after his own wife's death, on the grounds that she has only passed through the next stage in the same sequence — formlessness, then breath, then shape, then life — that produced her in the first place, no different in kind from the seasons turning. That passage is from the Outer Chapters, which some scholars think reflect later followers rather than Zhuangzi himself, but the Master Lai scene is not; it sits in the Inner Chapters, so the tension can't simply be waved away as an artifact of later editing.

I'd like to see this disagreement argued out properly rather than smoothed over. Could you write me a dialogue between two contemporary scholars of early Chinese philosophy — equally sharp, equally steeped in the text — who have just finished co-teaching a seminar on the Zhuangzi and end up arguing about this over tea afterward? One should maintain that the epistemic-humility argument, that we simply cannot know death is bad, is the real engine of Zhuangzi's therapy, with the transformation imagery serving mainly as consolation rather than as a load-bearing premise. The other should maintain the reverse: that the naturalistic metaphysics of transformation is doing the actual philosophical work, and that the skeptical passages are only there to loosen the reader's grip on their assumptions before the positive picture is introduced. Have each press the other on the soft spot in their own reading: the first needs to explain how bare agnosticism can deliver any comfort at all, and the second needs to explain how Zhuangzi can claim to know death is "fine" while elsewhere denying that we can know any such evaluative fact. Don't let either side concede early or talk past the other — I want to find out, by the end, whether this is a genuine tension in Zhuangzi's thinking or one that dissolves once it's stated precisely.
