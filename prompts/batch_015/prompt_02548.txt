I spent thirty-five years directing a laboratory devoted to the cell biology of senescence before retiring six years ago, and I have since been working through philosophy in a rather unsystematic way, currently somewhere in the philosophy of medicine. An exchange at a reunion dinner with two former colleagues has stayed with me since, and I would value a more disinterested opinion on it than I was able to offer at the table myself.

Nadia, who practiced as a geriatrician for most of her career, and Callum, who works on the evolutionary genetics of lifespan, ended up disputing whether aging itself ought to be classified as a disease. Callum opened by maintaining that disease, properly understood, is a statistically abnormal departure from species-typical functioning within one's own age-and-sex reference class, and that senescence is about as species-typical as anything gets, since every member of the species who survives long enough undergoes it; by that standard it cannot be a disease any more than puberty is one, but is simply what normal functioning does over time. Nadia replied that this proves too much, since a great many conditions nobody hesitates to call diseases — atherosclerosis, osteoporosis, cataracts, presbycusis — are themselves overwhelmingly common, arguably closer to typical than atypical, in anyone who reaches eighty; if statistical normality within an age-matched reference class were enough to disqualify a condition from disease status, most of geriatric medicine would have to be relabeled normal variation. Callum answered that there is a principled asymmetry: for each condition Nadia named, one can point to a specific structure or pathway behaving otherwise than it was shaped by selection to behave, whereas aging is not the malfunction of any one mechanism but the background decline from which all of them eventually suffer, and since selection has very little purchase on postreproductive survival — exactly what the mutation-accumulation and antagonistic-pleiotropy theories of aging would predict — there was never a design specification for aging as such to fall short of. Nadia's rejoinder was that this simply relocates the dispute onto ground favorable to Callum: if one instead asks whether a state harms someone and whether medicine could intervene on it, aging plainly qualifies, and treating the question as already settled by evolutionary theory has real costs — of exactly the kind that forced the TAME (Targeting Aging with Metformin) trial to be built around a composite of downstream diagnoses rather than aging itself, since regulators do not recognize aging as an approvable endpoint in its own right. Callum's closing remark was that wanting a more convenient regulatory category is a reason to change the regulations, not a reason to change what is true about the underlying physiology.

It struck me only afterward that Callum had been arguing from something like a naturalist conception of disease, of the sort associated with Christopher Boorse, and Nadia from something closer to a normativist one — a distinction I had only just come across in my reading — but I could not work out for myself who had the better of it at each stage, or whether the disagreement is really as symmetrical as it looked. Could you go through the exchange move by move, saying where each of them is right and where they overreach, and then note what, if anything, both of them are missing, including whether some defensible position on aging as a disease survives both of their objections?
