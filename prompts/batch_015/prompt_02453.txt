I'm taking a seminar this term on early modern French moral and political thought, and for the last two weeks we've been working through Pascal's *Pensées*, focusing on the scattered fragments dealing with justice, force, and political authority — mainly the "la justice sans la force est impuissante; la force sans la justice est tyrannique" fragment (Lafuma 103, though the numbering shifts if you're using Brunschvicg or Sellier), the related remarks on how "vérité au-deçà des Pyrénées, erreur au-delà," and the doctrine of the three orders (body/flesh, mind, and charity). My professor mentioned in passing that there's a real interpretive dispute about just how cynical Pascal is actually being here. Some commentators read him as converging on something close to Hobbes's reduction of justice to power: on this view, justice just is force dressed up in respectable language, so that "might makes right" holds in substance even if not in Pascal's own words. Others argue that this flattens his position, and that his considered view is really a two-tiered one, on which force is a practically indispensable but theologically subordinate stand-in for a justice that remains real — it is simply inaccessible to fallen human reason, and so cannot by itself secure obedience or peace in civil life.

I'd like you to write an essay that adjudicates this dispute rather than just laying it out. First, make the strongest textual and philosophical case you can for the "cynical" reading, on which Pascal's remarks on custom, the arbitrariness of law across borders, and the impossibility of ever making justice strong amount to a genuine debunking of justice-talk in politics, not merely a lament about it. Then make the strongest case for the rival "two-orders" reading, on which force governs only the order of the flesh (political power narrowly construed), while a "justice véritable" remains available in principle elsewhere — in the order of charity, say — so that Pascal's realism about political justice needn't commit him to any wider skepticism about justice as such. Please don't just present both sides and stay neutral: take a stand on which reading fits the fragments and Pascal's larger apologetic aims better (or propose a more precise third view, if you think both of these are too coarse-grained), and defend your verdict against the strongest version of the alternative you can construct.
