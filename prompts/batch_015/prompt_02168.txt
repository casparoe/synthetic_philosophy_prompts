I'm TAing our department's normative ethics seminar this term and the demandingness unit sent me down a bit of a rabbit hole. Everyone always stops at the same move: "ok, maximizing act-consequentialism is too demanding, so let's just switch to satisficing -- problem solved." But when I actually sat down and read Slote's original piece next to Bradley's 2006 Utilitas reply, it's way less clean then that. Bradley's whole point is that once you actually spell out what the "good enough" threshold is, every version of satisficing ends up letting an agent knowingly bring about a worse outcome then one they could've easily brought about instead -- not just fail to maximize, but pick the worse of two options sitting right there on the table -- as long as they clear the bar. That feels like a bigger bullet to bite then people treat it as. But I've also seen the reply that once you've already granted morality doesn't require optimizing, it's question-begging to insist that the sub-optimal choice being known and available should matter -- that just smuggles maximizing back in through a side door.

Could you write me a dialogue between a satisficing consequentialist and a die-hard maximizing act-consequentialist actually hashing this out? I want the maximizer to press the Bradley-style objection hard, ideally with their own concrete case rather than staying abstract, and the satisficer needs real moves to push back with, not just a shrug -- and then I want the maximizer to have something substantive to say back to that, and so on. Neither one should be a pushover or strawman; they should both talk like people who've actually read the literature and hold the view, responding to what the other just said rather than reciting prepared talking points past each other. Extra points if it doesn't resolve neatly, since I don't think the real debate does either.
