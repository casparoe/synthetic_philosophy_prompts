So I'm a philosophy PhD student working on formal/social epistemology, and I've fallen into a bit of an economics rabbit hole this month because of Aumann's agreement theorem and its finance-side cousins. My advisor pointed me to the old Milgrom and Stokey no-trade result (the 1982 "Information, Trade and Common Knowledge" paper) — basically, if traders start at a Pareto-optimal allocation, share a common prior, and it's common knowledge that everyone's rational, then purely speculative trade should be impossible, since the mere fact that someone's willing to sell you a stock should make you infer they know something bad about it, so the trade unravels before it even happens. Apparently people call this the "Groucho Marx theorem," after his line about not wanting to belong to a club that would have him as a member, which is a genuinely great name for a theorem.

Except obviously real markets have insane trading volume every single day, so clearly one of those background assumptions is failing constantly out there. We had a reading group argument about this last week — someone insisted liquidity/noise traders explain basically all of it, someone else said that's a cop-out and it's really overconfidence about private signals, a third person brought up that we probably don't even have common knowledge of each other's rationality in real markets — and I walked away with no real sense of who was right, or how you'd even weigh these against each other instead of just listing them.
