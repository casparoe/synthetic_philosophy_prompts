For a history of science elective, I have been preparing a short presentation on Johannes Kepler, and while researching it I ended up spending a whole evening reading about his astrology. What surprised me is that he was not simply a brilliant astronomer who "also happened to believe in astrology," the way one might expect from someone who had merely inherited the superstitions of his era. From what I can piece together, mostly from summaries of his Tertius Interveniens and Harmonices Mundi, Kepler actually rejected large parts of traditional astrology as baseless: he discarded the twelve zodiac signs, the astrological houses, and the idea that particular planets "ruled" particular signs, treating all of it as arbitrary human bookkeeping with no basis in nature. Yet he kept the doctrine of aspects — the angles planets form with one another as seen from Earth — and tied this surviving piece to his broader theory of cosmic harmony, apparently because certain angles correspond to regular polygons that can be constructed with compass and straightedge, and because he believed the human soul carries innate geometric "archetypes" that respond when it registers such configurations.

What I have not been able to reconstruct is why Kepler thought this counted as a principled distinction rather than an arbitrary place to draw the line between "real" and "fake" astrology. Could you walk me through the reasoning he seems to have relied on: what made an angular relationship "geometrically real" to him in a way a zodiac sign was not, and how exactly his theory of the soul's harmonic archetypes is supposed to bridge the gap between abstract geometry and concrete effects on human temperament or earthly events? I would like to understand this as an internally coherent position on its own terms, rather than simply being told that even great scientists sometimes cling to pseudoscience.
