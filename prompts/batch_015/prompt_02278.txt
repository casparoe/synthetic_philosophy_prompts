So I fell down a logic rabbit hole a couple weekends ago and can't shake it. Background on me: I'm a backend/infra engineer, I've used TLA+ and Alloy at work for spec'ing out some nasty distributed-systems edge cases, but zero formal philosophy training beyond casual wikipedia/SEP browsing. A coworker mentioned the Lowenheim-Skolem theorem in passing and that sent me down a hole into Skolem's paradox -- the fact that if ZFC is consistent it has a countable model, and yet that same model still satisfies "there exists an uncountable set," because the model just doesn't contain, internally, any bijection between that set and the naturals.

I get the "official" resolution: countability isn't absolute, the missing bijection exists outside the model (in the metatheory) not inside it, so there's no real contradiction, case closed. But that resolution has always felt like it dodges what Skolem himself was actually worried about in his original 1922 paper, which (from what I can piece together) was something stronger: if literally no first-order axiomatization can ever pin down an intended, non-relative notion of "uncountable" -- if you can always cook up some model satisfying the exact same axioms where the size facts come out different -- then in what sense was there ever a fully determinate, absolute fact about uncountable sets for our axioms to be tracking, versus just whatever collection of models happen to satisfy some formulas we like? Kind of reminds me of a thing that bugs me about writing TLA+/Alloy specs at work, actually: no matter how much you pin down, the checker can always cough up some unintended instance satisfying every constraint you wrote, and the only reason you rule it out is by appeal to what you "really meant," which was never itself formalized. Maybe that's a totally different phenomenon and I'm just pattern-matching on vibes though.

Anyway, what I actually want is for you to argue FOR the position that Skolem basically had it right -- that this isn't just a curiosity fixed by "relativizing" cardinality, but a genuine reason to be a skeptic about whether axiomatized set theory ever had an absolute, model-independent notion of uncountability to begin with, as opposed to a family of equally-legitimate relativized notions. It's apparently a pretty unfashionable position (everything I read says most logicians treat the paradox as a complete non-issue), so don't pull punches defending it. Feel free to bring in the Putnam-style extension of this into an argument about indeterminacy of reference if its actually load bearing for the strongest version of the case, but I saw there's a seperate technical fight over whether Putnam's specific proof even goes through (something about illegitimately applying Lowenheim-Skolem to a proper class), and I don't want that side-fight to hijack the actual philosophical argument. Basically give me the real, one-sided, strongest case you can build, not a "there are many perspectives" survey -- I've already read the SEP page, that's literally where half of this message came from.
