So this is going to sound like a weirdly specific rabbit hole, but I've gotten kind of obsessed with prediction markets over the last few months — I've got actual money on a handful of Polymarket and Kalshi positions now, plus I mess around on Manifold for fun — and it's dragged me into some game theory stuff that's honestly messing with my head a little. A friend from grad school mentioned this thing called the "no-trade theorem," I think originally from Milgrom and Stokey back in the 80s, which as far as I can piece together says something like: if a group of rational traders start from an efficient allocation and its common knowledge that everyone is rational, then purely speculative trades based on private information basically can't happen, because the mere fact that someone else is willing to take the other side of your bet is itself evidence you should update on — and if both sides reason it through all the way, any perceived edge unravels before a trade even occurs. Apparently this connects to Aumann's "agreeing to disagree" result too, though I don't have the background to know exactly how.

This feels completely bonkers to me given that prediction markets (and markets generally) are pretty much just enormous piles of people making exactly this kind of speculative bet, constantly. So I went down a mini research hole trying to resolve it myself, and the standard answer everyone gives is "real traders don't share a common prior, they just have genuinely different beliefs." But then I stumbled on a more recent paper arguing that's kind of a myth — that the classical no-trade result actually still holds even with totally different priors, as long as its common knowledge that both sides are rational, and what you actually need to revive trade is for each side to assign some real probability to the other person just being wrong or irrational. If that's right it seems like a much bigger deal than the "different priors" story, and I don't really get why.

Could you help me actually untangle this properly? I guess what I want is an intuitive, not-too-technical walkthrough of why the no-trade result holds in the first place and how it connects to Aumann's theorem, then some clarity on whether its really the common-prior assumption or the common-knowledge-of-rationality assumption doing the load-bearing work here, and finally your honest take on what all this implies for how much epistemic trust we should put in market prices — prediction markets especially — as aggregators of dispersed information, given that so much of the actual trading volume out there seems to depend on precisely the thing the theorem treats as some kind of failure mode.
