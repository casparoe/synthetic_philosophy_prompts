Random rabbit hole from a couple weekends ago: Turing's 1950 "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" paper ends its list of objections to the Turing test with "The Argument from Extra-Sensory Perception" -- and he doesn't just wave it off. He says the statistical evidence for telepathy is "overwhelming," calls the objection "quite a strong one," and proposes putting the human and interrogator in a "telepathy-proof room" so ESP can't contaminate the test.

Two friends of mine, Dan and Mira, got into an argument about how to read this and I still cant settle it myself.

(1) Dan: this is genuinely damning. A first-rate mind looked at some card-guessing statistics and concluded telepathy was basically real. If Turing can be taken in like that, we should trust our own "I've looked at the evidence and it's overwhelming" feelings a lot less than we do.

(2) Mira: that conflates engaging with an objection and endorsing it. Turing steelmans every objection in that section, including ones he obviously doesn't buy, and "the evidence looks strong" was a fair summary at the time -- this was almost certainly S.G. Soal's card-guessing experiments, which weren't shown to be fraudulent (via Betty Markwick's statistical detective work on his record sheets) until 1978, decades after Turing was dead.

(3) Dan: sure, but he didn't just summarize a literature, he redesigned his own thought experiment around it. Building a specific safeguard into your methodology is a real response to a hypothesis, not neutral reporting.

(4) Mira: patching a test against a low-probability, high-stakes possibility is just good practice, and it's not like anyone else caught the Soal fraud either -- it took decades and a very specific bit of statistical sleuthing.

(5) Then Dan brought up something I hadn't known: after Turings close friend Christopher Morcom died when they were both teenagers, Turing spent a couple of years writing letters and essays (including one literally titled "Nature of Spirit") trying to work out whether Christopher's mind survived his body, at one point leaning on quantum indeterminism to make room for it. Dan's read: that's motivated reasoning, plain and simple.

(6) Mira: having emotional reasons to be interested in a topic isn't the same as showing bias distorted this specific judgment -- by that standard you could disqualify almost any scientist's opinion on anything they happen to care about.

I don't think either of them is totally right or totally wrong. Can you adjudicate move by move -- a score from -2 (clearly wrong) to +2 (clearly right) for each numbered point, briefly justified -- and then say what, if anything, both of them are missing overall?
