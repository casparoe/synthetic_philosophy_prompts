So my housemate (doing a philosophy PhD) brought up this thing called the "dogmatism paradox" over dinner and now I can't stop chewing on it. I think it orginally comes from some unpublished Kripke lectures from the early 70s that Gilbert Harman then wrote up in his book Thought? Anyway, the rough shape of the argument as I understand it goes something like: if I actually know that h is true, then I already know that any future evidence against h would have to be misleading (since h is true, and evidence against something true just is misleading evidence, by definition). But if I know in advance that some possible future evidence is misleading, surely I should just disregard it rather than let it change my mind whenever it actually shows up. Put those two together and it seems like the moment I know h, I'm licensed to ignore, in advance, basically any future evidence that might turn up against it -- which sounds like a recipe for total dogmatism about anything we currently think we know.

Each step kind of seems fine in isolation, but the conclusion is obviously nuts -- nobody actually reasons "well, I know my flight leaves at 6, so I can just ignore the email saying its delayed." My housemate says the standard move is to point out that "knowing h at t0" and "having evidence e against h at t1" are just different epistemic states that can't be chained together the way the argument wants, so you're not being irrational by updating on e even though your earlier self already "knew" e would be misleading. That resolution feels almost too quick to me though -- like it's explaining the paradox away rather than explaining why it felt so airtight to begin with. Could you unpack, on a pretty intuitive/gut level, why each premise is so hard to deny on its own, and then really dig into whether that standard time-indexed response actually dissolves the tension or just relabels it? Also curious, if you happen to know, how this connects to the surprise-exam paradox, since apparently that's the puzzle that got Kripke thinking about this one in the first place.
