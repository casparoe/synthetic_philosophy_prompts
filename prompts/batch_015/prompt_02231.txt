I cover physics and philosophy of science for a general-interest outlet, and I'm several weeks into reporting a feature on how general relativity complicated the old fight between spacetime substantivalists, who hold that space and time exist as a substance in their own right, and relationists, who hold that talk of "space itself" reduces to a system of relations among bodies and events. Every popular treatment I can find, including my own first draft, leans entirely on Newton's bucket and the Leibniz-shift argument, but the two philosophers of physics I've interviewed for the piece keep steering me toward something thornier: Earman and Norton's "hole argument," which uses the invariance of general relativity under active diffeomorphisms (as opposed to mere coordinate relabeling) to argue that substantivalism about the bare spacetime manifold commits the theory to a form of indeterminism with no physical basis. Both of my sources plainly find the other's position not just wrong but a little exasperating, and their quotes keep flattening into soundbites whenever I try to write the disagreement up in ordinary prose.

So I want to try a different approach: could you write me a dialogue -- invented, not a transcript of my actual interviews -- between a committed substantivalist and a committed relationist who end up seated next to each other at the reception after a colloquium on the hole argument, each trying to convince the other that the argument settles the matter in their favor? I'd like the relationist to press the indeterminism worry in its real technical form rather than just gesturing at the bucket, and I'd like the substantivalist to answer with an actual move from the "sophisticated substantivalist" playbook -- denying that spacetime points have primitive trans-world identity, say, or folding the metric into what counts as "the substance" so the offending diffeomorphisms no longer describe distinct possibilities -- rather than retreating to "but space obviously exists." Have them genuinely engage with each other's last point rather than trade monologues: conceding what has to be conceded, and pressing wherever the other's fix looks ad hoc or costly. They needn't reach agreement -- the literature hasn't, after decades -- but I want each to leave having actually rattled the other's best argument. A page or two would be perfect, since I'm hoping to run it as a boxed sidebar alongside the main piece.
