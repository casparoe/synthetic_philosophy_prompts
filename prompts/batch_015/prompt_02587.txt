I am currently taking a small graduate seminar built entirely around Gödel's non-mathematical writings, and the last few weeks have produced a strange juxtaposition. One session was devoted to the four letters Gödel wrote in 1961 to his mother, Marianne, who had directly asked him whether he believed in a Wiedersehen — a reunion after death. His answer amounts to a fairly rigorous rationalist argument for an afterlife: because the world is rationally organized and everything in it has a reason (he draws an explicit parallel to the principle of sufficient reason), it would be absurd for a faculty as developed as the human mind simply to be extinguished at death, so a "next world" must exist. He is careful to distinguish this from ordinary reincarnation — the next stage is not a return to another body in this world, but a higher condition in which the latent memories of this life become available, for the first time, to a far more powerful, "infallible" faculty of understanding, the kind of certainty we otherwise reserve for arithmetic. The following week we read his 1951 Gibbs lecture, where Gödel argues that either the human mind's mathematical powers infinitely exceed those of any finite machine, or else there exist absolutely unsolvable Diophantine problems — and, per Hao Wang's records of their conversations, Gödel's own sympathies (his "rationalistic optimism," in Wang's phrase) lay with the first horn. He apparently put the point himself in a remark Wang preserved: "the incompleteness theorem makes it likely that mind is not mechanical, or else mind cannot understand its own mechanism."

For our response paper, we have been asked to bring these two threads to bear on something Gödel plainly never had occasion to consider: mind uploading. I do not mean survival of death in general, which he obviously took seriously, but the specific technological proposal — taken seriously in philosophy of mind only well after his own death in 1978 — that scanning a brain and running the resulting model on a computer might constitute a literal continuation of the person, a kind of on-demand Wiedersehen. A few of us argued about this after seminar and could not settle it, so I would like your help. Given everything above, would Gödel most plausibly have welcomed successful mind uploading as a technological vindication of the "next world" he described to his mother, or would his anti-mechanism from the Gibbs lecture commit him to treating any uploaded copy as a mere simulacrum — a formal system imitating a person without whatever it is that lets real minds outstrip formal systems in the first place? Please reconstruct the strongest version of each reading, then tell me which one actually sits better with Gödel's broader philosophical temperament — bringing in his mathematical Platonism and his ontological proof of God's existence, unpublished in his lifetime, if you think they bear on the question — and explain why.
