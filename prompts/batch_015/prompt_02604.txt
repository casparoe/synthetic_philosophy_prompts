Been down a bit of a rabbit hole this week for a piece I'm working on about scientists and mathematicians who held surprisingly literal religious beliefs, and I can't stop thinking about something I found buried in Godel's letters. Everyone knows him for the incompleteness theorems and that odd ontological proof of God he circulated near the end of his life, but it turns out he also wrote a series of letters to his mother in 1961 laying out an actual argument for why he was confident we survive death — not "afterlife as comforting myth," but afterlife as the conclusion of a basically Leibnizian argument about rationality.

The key line, in translation, is: "If the world is rationally constructed and has meaning, then there must be such a thing [as an afterlife]." As far as I can piece together from commentary on the letters, the fuller argument runs like this: the world is fundamentally orderly and rational (which he thinks science itself already presupposes, since it assumes everything has a cause or reason behind it); a human life, being part of that world, ought to show that same rational structure; but an ordinary lifetime is nowhere near long enough for a person to use more than a sliver of their potential — he compares it to someone laying the foundation for a house and then just abandoning the whole project. So, on his view, reason itself requires that whatever potential got left on the table has to be used up somewhere else, in some further existence.

Here's the part I'm stuck on: he takes the obvious unfairness of an ordinary human life — the fact that people so rarely become everything they could've been — and uses that as the very thing that forces the conclusion that we must persist after death, rather than treating it as evidence that the world isn't so rational after all. Is that move as circular or question-begging as it sounds to me, or is there a real inferential structure underneath it, like a hidden appeal to the principle of sufficient reason, or a disguised theodicy? Also curious whether you think this "practical" argument is doing seperate philosophical work from his more famous ontological proof, or if it's basically the same rationalist reflex showing up in a different form. Would love a breakdown of what's actually going on logically here.
