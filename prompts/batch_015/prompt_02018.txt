I don't have any background in philosophy, but I've been turning a question over in my head for months now, ever since a conversation with my sister, who volunteers every week at a homeless shelter. When I asked her why she keeps doing it, she said something like, "because these people matter to me, not because I've decided it's my duty." I found that admirable at the time, but it has stuck with me in an uncomfortable way, because somewhere in my own reading (I think this traces back to Kant, though I only know him through summaries, never the original texts) I picked up the idea that an act only has genuine moral worth if it is done because you recognize it as the right thing to do, and that doing the very same helpful thing merely because you are fond of someone, or because sympathy moved you, counts for less, even when the outcome is identical.

The more I sit with this, the stranger it seems. If my sister were instead motivated by a cold, calculated sense of obligation, with no real feeling for the people she helps, I suspect most of us would find her less admirable, not more — it would feel clinical, as though she were going through the motions of virtue. Yet I recognize the opposite failure in myself: there have been times I have done favors for close friends purely because I cared about them, without ever pausing to ask whether what they wanted from me was actually right, and once or twice that got me into trouble I should have foreseen. So plain affection, unchecked by any thought about rightness, does not seem safe either. It is as though acting "because it is right" and acting "because I care" each rule out something the other one needs, and I cannot work out how a person could have both at once, or whether that is even possible.

What I am hoping you can do is treat this as the real, apparently unresolved problem I suspect it is, rather than smoothing it over with a tidy answer. Is there an actual line of argument or disagreement, among people who think carefully about ethics for a living, that corresponds to what I am describing? I would like to understand the strongest version of each side, why the obvious compromise of simply "doing both" does not fully work, and whether anyone has described a way of caring about a person that remains genuinely answerable to what is right without collapsing back into the kind of box-ticking motive that made my sister's remark ring so true to me in the first place. If it turns out nobody has really solved this, I would rather you tell me so directly than have the difficulty explained away.
