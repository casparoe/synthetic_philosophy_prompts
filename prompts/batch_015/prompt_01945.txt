This happened after our local ACX meetup last month -- there'd been a talk on compatibilism, and two people, Nate and Priti, ended up arguing about Frankfurt cases for like 40 minutes afterward. I can't tell who came out ahead and it's bugging me.

The setup they used: a neuroscientist wires a chip into your brain that forces your hand onto a button the instant it detects you're about to do otherwise, but if you press the button on your own the chip never activates. Nate's claim: you're still fully responsible even though you literally couldn't have done otherwise, so the principle of alternative possibilities (that responsibility requires being able to do otherwise) is false.

1. Priti's first move was the "flicker of freedom" reply: fine, you couldn't press a different button, but you could've at least hesitated, or shown some sign of wanting something else, before the chip kicked in -- so a thin alternate possibility survives, and maybe that's what responsibility actually tracks.

2. Nate said: picture two people wired to the same chip, one who never considers pressing anything else and one who briefly wavers before getting overridden. We'd hold both equally responsible, so whatever "flicker" the second one has isn't doing any real work.

3. Priti's comeback: the dilemma defense. The chip has to reliably predict your choice in advance to know when to intervene. Under determinism that's possible, but then the case just assumes determinism is compatible with responsibility -- the very thing in dispute. Under indeterminism, no chip can be a perfect predictor, so a real chance you'd have done otherwise survives after all, just a small one.

4. Nate's comeback: skip prediction entirely -- have a second, fully deterministic process running in your brain the whole time as backup, set to produce the identical button-press only if the first process fails to. No forecasting needed, so the dilemma never gets a grip.

Neither budged, and they both went home pretty sure they'd won, which honestly cant both be true. Could you go through moves 1-4, say who got the better of each exchange, and say whether move 4 really escapes the problem raised in move 3, or if theres a consideration neither of them raised that matters here?
