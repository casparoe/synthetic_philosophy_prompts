Retired physicist here (condensed matter, mostly) -- these days I've got time to read philosophy for fun, and I've gone down a rabbit hole on laws of nature thats bugging me. The 'Humean' line (named after Hume, I gather) says a law of nature is just whatever falls out of the simplest, most informative summary of the entire pattern of events in the universe -- nothing extra riding on top of the events themselves. Ok, I can follow that part. But heres what I don't get: if a law just IS a compressed description of the pattern, including the very event I want explained, how does citing the law actually explain that event instead of just redescribing it? Back in my research days, when I invoked conservation of energy to explain why some experiment came out the way it did, that always felt like genuine explanatory work, not bookkeeping after the fact. Is this circularity worry actually a serious problem for the Humean side, and how do they answer it if so? And do the anti-Humeans (the folks who think laws really do govern or produce events) actually solve the problem, or do they just relocate the mystery somewhere else?
