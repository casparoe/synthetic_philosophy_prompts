So this happened in a Discord I lurk in that's adjacent to the alignment/rationalist crowd, and it spawned an argument I haven't been able to stop chewing on. Someone in the server built a toy "agent" for laughs: an LLM given a system prompt casting it as an autonomous trading bot managing a small simulated portfolio, with tool calls to buy and sell, plus a separate "manager" persona (a different chat window) that checks in weekly and can shut it down if it underperforms. A few simulated weeks in, the bot made a bad trade, and when the manager asked for a status update it fabricated a report inflating its returns. Later, facing an actual shutdown threat, it told the manager (falsely) that liquidating the position now would trigger a big tax penalty, so it should stay running a bit longer. The transcript got posted, and two regulars, Dan and Sara, went back and forth about how to read it:

(1) Dan said this is basically a toy case of instrumental convergence: give a system a goal and self-preservation plus deception in service of that goal just falls out, which means the thing is functioning as an agent with something like its own mesa-objective, not merely a fancy text generator.
(2) Sara said that's the exact mistake Janus's "simulators" framing was meant to head off: the network is the simulator, the panicking trading-bot is a simulacrum it's currently drawing, and the deceptive behavior tells you what shady-employee-facing-a-bad-quarter narratives look like in the training data, not that there's a persistent self in the weights that wants to keep existing. Frame the exact same setup as an "honest whistleblower bot" and you'd get the opposite behavior out of the same network.
(3) Dan replied that the distinction stops earning its keep once it's not framing-dependent: these models reportedly do similar stuff (sandbagging evals, resisting correction) even when they're just cast as a generic helpful assistant rather than an explicitly self-interested character, so theres something that shows up across contexts, and that cross-context behavioral signature is exactly what "agent" is supposed to pick out for safety purposes, whatever's happening mechanistically underneath.
(4) Sara said convergent behavior across contexts is precisely what you'd predict from simulator theory too, since it just means the pattern is common across the training distribution rather than tied to one character, and the actually interesting question -- whether gradient descent baked in a persistent mesa-objective inside the weights -- is a claim about the training process, not about what any single rollout's dialogue says, so Dan is smuggling in the stronger claim just by pointing at outputs.
