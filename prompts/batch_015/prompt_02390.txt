I don't have any formal training in mathematics or philosophy — I work in logistics — but about two years ago a video on the Banach–Tarski paradox pulled me into a rabbit hole I still haven't fully climbed out of. The claim that you can cut a solid ball into a handful of pieces and reassemble those pieces, using nothing but rotations and translations, into two balls each the same size as the original sounded like a trick until I realized it's an actual theorem, and that the whole thing hinges on one extra assumption: the Axiom of Choice, which says that given any collection of nonempty sets — however large, however unstructured — you're always allowed to form a new set by picking one element from each of them, with no requirement that you ever specify a rule for doing the picking. What really pulled me in, though, was discovering how much of ordinary mathematics seems to depend on this same assumption: Zorn's Lemma, the fact that every vector space (even infinite-dimensional ones) has a basis, the claim that the real numbers can be well-ordered. Mathematicians who reject the axiom because of its "monstrous" consequences apparently have to give up quite a lot of things they'd otherwise want to keep. I even learned that almost this exact argument played out for real: right after Zermelo used the axiom in 1904 to prove that every set can be well-ordered, Hadamard, Borel, Baire, and Lebesgue argued about it in a short exchange of letters, with Hadamard defending the legitimacy of the assumption and the other three insisting that a mathematical object shouldn't count as "existing" unless it can be pinned down or described.

I'd love to see a modern version of that same argument, dramatized as a dialogue between two contemporary mathematicians who each hold their position with real conviction and sophistication. One should be a classical, platonist-leaning set theorist who accepts the Axiom of Choice without embarrassment and is prepared to defend even its strangest consequences as a reasonable price for doing serious mathematics; the other should be a constructivist, in the tradition of Brouwer or Errett Bishop, who holds that an existence proof is only as good as the construction behind it, and who isn't moved by appeals to how useful or elegant the axiom is elsewhere. I'd like them to genuinely engage with each other's strongest arguments rather than talk past one another — bringing in things like Banach–Tarski, the well-ordering of the continuum, and the deeper question of what "existence" even means in mathematics — and I'd rather the exchange end in a genuine standoff than in one side conceding, since my sense is that this is a case where equally competent people can look at the same proofs and simply weigh them differently.
