Been down a Thomas Hobbes rabbit hole lately. There's that Aubrey anecdote where Hobbes doesnt even look at geometry til he's 40, stumbles on Euclid's 47th proposition in Book I in some gentleman's library, goes "by God, this is impossible," works backward through the proofs and becomes obsessed. Kind of charming, except it also turns him into a total crank for the rest of his life - decades spent insisting he'd squared the circle, getting publicly demolished over and over by the mathematician John Wallis, never once backing down.

Here's what actually interests me though: in the "Six Lessons" pamphlet from that fight, Hobbes argues geometry and civil philosophy are the only two sciences capable of real demonstrative certainty, and for the exact same reason - because we make the objects ourselves (we draw the lines, we make the commonwealth by covenant), so we can know their causes perfectly. Can you argue that Hobbes's own quadrature disaster isn't just an embarrassing footnote unrelated to Leviathan, but actually undermines his "maker's knowledge" case for why political philosophy can be just as certain as geometry? Actually build the argument, don't just narrate the history for me.
