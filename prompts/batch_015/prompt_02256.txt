I've spent the last few months slowly working my way through a biography of Johannes Kepler, mostly out of personal curiosity, and one detail keeps nagging at me. Kepler is remembered as one of the founders of modern astronomy, yet for most of his working life he was also employed as a court astrologer, and he clearly treated at least part of astrology as a serious subject rather than pure superstition. He is often summarized as having called astrology the foolish daughter of the wise mother astronomy, and as far as I can tell he really did have contempt for the ordinary almanac-astrologers of his day, treating their sign-by-sign fortune-telling as something sold to gullible customers. But in works like Harmonices Mundi he argued that the angles, or "aspects," formed between planets as seen from Earth — conjunctions, oppositions, trines, and so on — correspond to musical ratios and actually resonate with a kind of instinctive, non-rational soul of the Earth, stirring up weather and human affairs. That is a real mechanism he seems to have believed in, not just a concession to his paying customers.

Separately, and earlier in his career, in the Mysterium Cosmographicum, Kepler argued that there could only ever be exactly six planets, because their orbital spacing could be derived from nesting the five Platonic solids between six spheres. He treated this as a serious proof about the structure of the universe, one he kept defending and refining for the rest of his life, rather than a throwaway numerological game.

What I can't stop wondering about is what he would have made of the fact that more planets were eventually discovered that nobody in his lifetime knew existed. Uranus was not spotted until 1781, roughly a hundred and fifty years after Kepler died in 1630, and Neptune followed in 1846, with Pluto added, at least historically, in 1930. The six-sphere argument seems to need exactly six planets and no more, so it looks as though it would simply collapse under these discoveries, whereas the aspect-based astrology doesn't obviously depend on any fixed number of bodies, which is presumably part of why modern astrologers have folded the outer planets into horoscopes without much difficulty. I looked around to see whether historians of science had already worked through this specific comparison, but couldn't find the two halves of his thinking discussed together anywhere, so I would like your own best reconstruction of his reasoning: would Kepler have treated the discovery of new planets as a real crisis for his geometric cosmology, and would his "reformed" astrology have simply absorbed the new planets, or is there something in his actual arguments about aspects and the Earth's soul that would have given him principled grounds to resist doing so? I would rather have a serious attempt at his reasoning on both counts than the easy answer that he was a modern scientist at heart who would have dropped astrology altogether once he had more evidence, since that is not how he treated the parts of astrology he believed he had already put on solid footing.
