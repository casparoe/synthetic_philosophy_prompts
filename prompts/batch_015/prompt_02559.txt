I'm a philosophy PhD student in a graduate seminar on theories of causation, and this week paired Hume's first Enquiry with the two research programs that grew out of his counterfactual clause: Lewis's counterfactual analysis on one side, and the "manipulability" tradition -- von Wright's early agency account, Menzies and Price's 1993 development of it, and Woodward's later interventionist reworking, which explicitly tries to strip out the appeal to agency -- on the other. My background is more in formal epistemology than in the history side of things, and having all of this freshly in mind while rereading Hume himself has left me stuck on something in the primary text that I don't think is quite the standard "two definitions" problem you find rehearsed in the secondary literature.

Here is the passage, from near the end of Section VII, Part II of the Enquiry. It comes just a few paragraphs after Hume has argued at length that we lack any impression of power even introspectively, in willing our own bodily movements -- his conclusion there being that "consciousness never deceives. Consequently, neither in the one case nor in the other, are we ever conscious of any power." With that fresh in the reader's mind, he then writes: "Similar objects are always conjoined with similar. Of this we have experience. Suitably to this experience, therefore, we may define a cause to be an object, followed by another, and where all the objects similar to the first are followed by objects similar to the second. Or in other words where, if the first object had not been, the second never had existed. The appearance of a cause always conveys the mind, by a customary transition, to the idea of the effect. Of this also we have experience. We may, therefore, suitably to this experience, form another definition of cause, and call it, an object followed by another, and whose appearance always conveys the thought to that other. But though both these definitions be drawn from circumstances foreign to the cause, we cannot remedy this inconvenience, or attain any more perfect definition, which may point out that circumstance in the cause, which gives it a connexion with its effect."

Two things are tangled together for me here, and I'd appreciate help pulling them apart. First, at the level of the text itself: Hume introduces the counterfactual clause with an unceremonious "or in other words," as though it merely restated the regularity clause just before it rather than adding anything. Given everything else Hume is committed to, is there a reading on which these really are equivalent, or is that "or in other words" quietly smuggling in more than a pure regularity theory can support -- something closer to what Lewis would later make explicit with similarity orderings on possible worlds? Second, and this is the part I can't settle on my own: the agency wing of the manipulability tradition cashes out counterfactuals like Hume's second clause by appeal to our supposed direct experience of "bringing about" an outcome as free agents -- which sounds exactly like the introspective access to power that Hume has just spent the preceding paragraphs of this very section arguing we don't have, even regarding our own volitions. So is there a real inconsistency between what Hume argues just above and what an agency theorist needs to say about his counterfactual clause, or does Woodward's move to a purely structural notion of intervention -- one that, by his own account, requires no actual human agency at all -- dodge that inconsistency only by abandoning the reductive ambitions that made the counterfactual clause worth building a theory around in the first place? I'd be glad for help thinking through both halves of this.
