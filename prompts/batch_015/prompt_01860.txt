I work as a backend/ML engineer, and part of my job involves checking whether the probabilities our classifiers output are actually well-calibrated — we routinely look at things like Brier score or log-loss on held-out data for this. A friend of mine who is finishing a philosophy PhD recently mentioned that this exact same scoring-rule machinery shows up in a corner of epistemology called "epistemic utility theory" or "accuracy-first epistemology," where philosophers such as James Joyce (the philosopher of science, not the novelist) try to justify probabilism — the thesis that rational degrees of belief must obey the axioms of probability — without appealing to betting behavior or Dutch-book arguments. The idea, as I understand it, is that any credence function violating the probability axioms is "accuracy-dominated": guaranteed to be less accurate than some coherent alternative no matter which possible world turns out to be actual, so incoherence is supposed to be a purely epistemic defect rather than merely a prudential one.

Out of curiosity I read Joyce's original 1998 paper along with a handful of responses to it, and it turns out whether this "nonpragmatic vindication" really works is a much more contested, technical question than the friendly one-paragraph summary suggests. Five considerations that keep coming up are: (a) Patrick Maher's objection that Joyce's own defense of his axioms only establishes a weaker principle ("Weak Convexity"), when the theorem actually requires the strictly stronger "Strong Convexity" axiom, and is provably false given only the weaker one; (b) a recurring worry that the conditions used to pick out a "legitimate" measure of inaccuracy already smuggle in enough probabilistic structure that the argument risks begging the question rather than vindicating probabilism from independent premises; (c) the "evidentialist" objection that the background "veritist" assumption — that accuracy is the sole fundamental source of epistemic value for credences — is too narrow to capture other plausible constraints on rational belief, such as fit with one's evidence; (d) a formal impossibility result (Seidenfeld, Schervish, and Kadane) showing that no strictly proper scoring rule exists once credences are allowed to be imprecise rather than point-valued, which threatens the framework's applicability to realistically fuzzy believers; and (e) a later result, due to Predd and colleagues, showing that the core coherence/dominance theorem holds for any proper scoring rule whatsoever, without needing Joyce's specific convexity and symmetry axioms at all — which arguably rescues the program from objections like (a) and (b), at least for agents with precise credences.
