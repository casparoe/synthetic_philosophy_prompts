I'm writing my IB Extended Essay in Philosophy on higher-order theories of consciousness, and before I expand my outline into a full draft, I'd like some feedback on whether the central argument actually holds up.

Here's where I've gotten to so far. David Rosenthal's higher-order thought (HOT) theory holds that a mental state's being conscious is not an intrinsic feature of that state but a relation between two distinct states: a first-order state (say, a perception of red) becomes conscious only when it's accompanied by a suitable, non-inferential higher-order thought representing oneself as being in it. What interests me is what Rosenthal says happens when this relation misfires. He accepts that someone could have a fully formed higher-order thought ("I'm in pain") even though no first-order state exists at all (a "targetless" HOT), or one whose content doesn't match whatever first-order state does exist (misrepresentation) — and he maintains that either way, the person is having a genuine conscious experience, with its subjective character fixed entirely by the higher-order thought rather than by the first-order state.

My worry is that this concedes too much. If the higher-order thought alone can fully determine what an experience is like even when the first-order state is absent or irrelevant, then the first-order state isn't doing any real explanatory work, and the theory quietly turns into a one-state theory of consciousness while still presenting itself as a two-state, relational one. I can't decide whether moving to a self-representational view instead (where a single complex state represents both the world and itself, rather than positing two separate states) actually escapes this problem, or just relocates the same question to which part of that one state is doing the real work.

Could you help me with three things: (1) whether I've represented Rosenthal's position fairly, (2) whether this redundancy worry is a genuine problem for HOT theory or there's an obvious reply I'm missing, and (3) whether pursuing the comparison with self-representationalism is a promising direction for the essay, or a distraction I should cut given the 4,000-word limit.
