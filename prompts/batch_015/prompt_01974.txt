At a workshop on formal epistemology, I listened to two colleagues -- both decision theorists -- fall into a genuine disagreement over dinner about Kelly betting, and I would value a neutral assessment of how the exchange went.

Rachel opened by arguing that Kelly betting is not merely one reasonable strategy among many but the uniquely rational way to size repeated bets: the wager fraction that maximizes expected geometric growth will, almost surely, leave a bettor with more wealth than any essentially different strategy once the sequence of bets runs long enough.

Sam replied that this "almost sure" dominance is a fact about the shape of long-run sample paths, not a fact about rationality as such. Expected utility theory ties the correct stake size to the bettor's utility function, and the Kelly fraction is optimal only for a bettor with logarithmic utility; a more risk-averse bettor who wagers less than Kelly is not thereby mistaken, even though she will almost certainly end up poorer than a Kelly bettor eventually.

Rachel then invoked ergodicity economics: von Neumann-Morgenstern theory implicitly averages outcomes across a hypothetical ensemble of parallel worlds, but an actual bettor lives through a single realization of a multiplicative, non-ergodic wealth process, so the ensemble average was never the right quantity to maximize. Ask instead what a single agent persisting through time should maximize, and the time-average growth rate -- which is to say, log utility -- falls out as rationally required rather than as one preference among others.

Sam's rejoinder was that this reverses the order of argument: the time-average calculation recovers log utility only because it presupposes that long-run growth is what the agent should care about, and that presupposition is itself a substantive claim about preferences, not a theorem. An agent who wants above all to avoid ruin within a fixed horizon, or who is simply more risk-averse than log, is not thereby confusing time and ensemble averages -- Rachel, on this view, has smuggled her conclusion into her premises.

Neither party budged, and I would rather not referee my own colleagues. Could you work through the four moves in order -- Rachel's opening claim, Sam's reply, Rachel's ergodicity move, and Sam's rejoinder -- and assess each on its own merits, rather than simply declaring an overall winner? I am especially interested in whether there is some consideration both of them are overlooking.
