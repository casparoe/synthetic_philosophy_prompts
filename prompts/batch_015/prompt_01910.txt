I'm putting together a graduate seminar that pairs Kant's first Critique with Kripke's *Naming and Necessity*, and there's a question I keep fielding from students that most secondary literature answers too glibly. Kant holds, in the Introduction to the Critique, that necessity and strict universality are "infallible tests" for a priori knowledge, since experience can never license the judgment that something could not have been otherwise; on his view, the necessary/contingent distinction and the a priori/a posteriori distinction therefore come out coextensive. Kripke's whole strategy in *Naming and Necessity* is to pry these two distinctions apart. Proper names and natural-kind terms like "Hesperus," "water," and "gold" are rigid designators, picking out the same object or kind in every possible world in which it exists at all, which is why identities such as "Hesperus is Phosphorus," "water is H2O," and "gold has atomic number 79" are necessary if true at all -- even though we can only discover that they're true a posteriori. He runs the same trick in reverse with the standard meter stick, whose use to fix the reference of "one meter" (without giving its meaning) yields a contingent truth that its stipulator can nonetheless know a priori.

What I'd like from you is a reconstruction, worked out from within Kant's own system rather than imposed from the outside, of how he would or should respond to this challenge. Simply noting that the machinery of rigid designation was unavailable to him is not an answer. Some avenues worth pressure-testing: conceding synthetic a posteriori necessities and revising the architecture accordingly; treating Kripke's identities as disguised analytic or merely verbal truths; leaning on Kant's own view that empirical concepts can never be given a real definition, only an "exposition" that stays open to future revision, so as to explain away the appearance of necessity; or invoking his distinctions among logical necessity, real possibility, and the empirically conditioned necessity of laws of nature to argue that Kripke's cases are necessary only in some attenuated sense. Reconstruct the strongest Kantian reply you can along these lines (or any other you find more promising), and then tell me plainly whether, in your judgment, it succeeds -- or whether the necessary a posteriori simply sinks the coextensiveness thesis.
