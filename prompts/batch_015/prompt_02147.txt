I have been rereading A. J. Ayer's 1988 essay "What I Saw When I Was Dead" while preparing a graduate seminar on empiricist theories of personal identity — the same unit in which we cover Locke, Hume, and the reduplication problem that motivates Parfit and Shoemaker — and I keep getting stuck on a complication in it that none of the commentary I have come across seems to address. Ayer wrote the essay after choking on a piece of smoked salmon while hospitalized for pneumonia, an accident that stopped his heart for about four minutes. Reflecting on the distinction between that reversible episode and his eventual, permanent death, he asks what a future life would even have to consist in, and arrives at the simplest candidate: a bare continuation of one's stream of experience, with no body involved at all — exactly the sort of picture, he notes, that should appeal to an empiricist standing in the line of Hume and William James, as he does. What he never does is reconcile this with a conclusion he had already reached in his own 1968 book on Peirce and James, The Origins of Pragmatism: that memory and the felt continuity of experience cannot, by themselves, constitute personal identity over time, and that one also needs the persistence of a single body, or sequence of bodies, that the person successively occupies. He nonetheless goes on to report that the whole experience had "slightly weakened my conviction that my genuine death ... will be the end of me" — without ever asking whether a bodiless continuation of experience would, on his own terms, be a continuation of him at all.

I can construct the deflationary reading well enough on my own: that Ayer is not entitled to treat his musings about disembodied survival as bearing on his own fate, given the very bodily-continuity requirement he had independently defended. Please argue for the opposite, reconciling reading instead: that Ayer's bodily-continuity requirement, properly understood, does not actually preclude the disembodied scenario he entertains from counting as a form of his own persistence. Build the case from the inside, engaging with the actual reason Ayer thought a body was needed to fix personal identity in the first place, rather than simply postulating a soul to do the individuating work instead — and feel free to borrow later machinery from Williams, Shoemaker, or Parfit if it helps you locate a resource already implicit in the empiricist picture itself. I am less interested in whether you ultimately find the reconciliation convincing than in how strong a case can actually be built for it.
