Could you write me a dialogue between two members of a hospital ethics committee?

I'm on my school's Ethics Bowl team, and while our group was preparing a case about how a hospital should ration scarce resources like ICU beds during a mass-casualty event, our coach pointed us toward John Taurek's 1977 paper "Should the Numbers Count?" I went in expecting the obvious answer — that you save the greater number — but Taurek actually resists that conclusion. He imagines a situation where you have a drug that can save either one person, David, who needs the whole dose, or five strangers who each need only a fifth of it, and you cannot do both. He argues that treating the five as outweighing David requires adding their separate losses into one big loss that nobody actually experiences — a version of what philosophers sometimes call the "separateness of persons" — and he even suggests that no number of people's mild headaches could add up to something worse than one person's migraine, using that to cast doubt on the whole idea of summing harms across different people. He ends up proposing that if all six people are equally strangers to you, the fair thing to do is flip a coin rather than automatically save the five.

That struck me as strange until I found that Derek Parfit wrote a direct reply, "Innumerate Ethics," arguing that Taurek actually has the logic backwards: giving equal weight to every person's life is exactly why the five should count for more, not less, since each of the five has a claim to be saved that is just as strong as David's, and refusing to let those claims add up effectively discards four of the five claims for no reason. What's been bothering me since is that both philosophers seem to think they're the ones taking equal concern for persons seriously, and I can't tell whether they actually disagree about something substantive or are simply talking past one another.

Could you write me a dialogue between two members of a hospital ethics committee who are drafting a triage policy and end up in exactly this dispute — one persuaded by Taurek that the numbers shouldn't automatically decide who gets saved, the other persuaded by Parfit that they should? I'd like the Taurek side to invoke both the coin-flip proposal and the point about losses not summing across separate people, and the Parfit side to press the equal-weighting argument while pushing back hard on the coin flip itself (for instance, by asking whether the five wouldn't reasonably want better odds than a fifty-fifty split). Please make neither character easy to refute: they should be equally sharp, respond to each other's actual objections rather than restating their own position, and ideally the conversation can end with them at least pinning down the precise point where their reasoning diverges, since neither one seems to be simply confused.
