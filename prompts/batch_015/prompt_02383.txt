At my company, we recently had to build a scripting and automation feature that does essentially the same thing as a well-known competitor's plugin system. Legal insisted we run the project as a strict "clean room" build: one engineer studied the competitor's product and wrote a plain-language specification of how it behaved, a lawyer reviewed that specification line by line, and only then was the rest of the implementation team — myself included — allowed to start writing any code. The explicit condition was that none of us ever look at the competitor's actual source code, internal documentation, or a decompiled version of their binary, even though a colleague on another team had a legally obtained copy sitting right there on a shared drive. When I asked why we couldn't just take a look and use our own judgment about what was worth borrowing, I was told that even glancing at it would "taint" us and expose the company to significantly more legal risk than staying ignorant. Curious, I did some reading afterward and found that the same basic pattern shows up in patent law: companies apparently instruct engineers not to read competitors' patents in their own field, because knowingly infringing one is treated far more harshly than infringing it out of genuine ignorance.

This bothers me more than it probably should, because it seems to cut against something I picked up years ago in a college statistics course, and in some casual reading about decision-making since: a rational person who can look at more evidence for free before deciding should never end up worse off in expectation than if they had decided blind, because at worst they can simply ignore whatever they learn and act exactly as they otherwise would have — meaning free information should never actively hurt you, only possibly help. Yet real companies and their lawyers clearly don't treat this as a loophole or an empty ritual; they treat deliberate ignorance as genuinely sound risk management, worth the cost of duplicated effort and slower development. I brought this up with a couple of friends recently, and nobody could quite say what, if anything, was wrong with my reasoning. Is there an actual rational justification for deliberately avoiding free information in a case like this, or am I missing something in how I'm thinking about it?
