During my career in structural biology, I was always fascinated by how swiftly a newly synthesized polypeptide settles into its native three-dimensional fold, despite the fact that an exhaustive search through all the conformations it could in principle adopt would, as Cyrus Levinthal observed back in the 1960s, take far longer than the age of the universe. Since retiring I have taken up philosophy and have been reading about computational complexity theory, where I gather it has been rigorously proven that finding a protein's minimum-energy conformation is an NP-hard problem. It seems to follow that every cell in the body is, in effect, solving an NP-hard optimization problem within milliseconds, which I take to be fairly compelling empirical evidence against the conjecture that P ≠ NP — or, at the very least, against the Extended Church-Turing thesis, according to which no physical process should be able to outperform an ordinary computer by more than a polynomial factor. Am I reasoning correctly here, and if theoretical computer scientists reject this argument, on what grounds do they do so?
