I'm not a scholar of any kind, just someone who has spent the past few months working through Maimonides' Guide for the Perplexed and parts of the Mishneh Torah in translation, on my own time and out of personal interest. I've written up some notes on a problem I keep running into with his treatment of animal sacrifice, and I would like an outside opinion on whether the problem is real or whether I'm missing something.

In Guide III:32, Maimonides argues that God commanded animal sacrifice purely as a concession to the psychology of the generation that left Egypt. Every nation around them worshipped through sacrificial cult, and human beings cannot jump straight from one extreme to another; in his own words, "it is impossible to go from one extreme to the other suddenly." On his account, God redirected a practice the Israelites were already emotionally and culturally attached to, away from idols and toward Himself, the way one weans an infant off milk rather than removing it all at once. Sacrifice, on this reading, is not good in itself. It is a remedy fitted to a specific historical and psychological condition: a people too recently surrounded by idolatry to be asked, all at once, to worship in a purer and less concrete way.

This seems to be in real tension with two other commitments of his. First, in the Mishneh Torah (Hilchot Melachim, "Laws of Kings," 11:1), he states plainly that when the messiah comes, the Temple will be rebuilt and the sacrificial service reinstated exactly as the Torah describes it, at precisely the point when, by his own account, idolatry will have vanished from the earth and everyone will recognize the one God. If sacrifice was only ever a cure for a specific cultural affliction, it is strange that the cure is scheduled to return in full once the affliction has been entirely eliminated. Second, in the ninth of his thirteen principles of faith, he insists that the Torah will never be changed, added to, or subtracted from. That is a strong thing to say about a law whose own official rationale, on his own philosophical account, is tied to a historical situation that, by his own theology, is destined to disappear completely.

I am aware of one obvious response: sacrifices offered before Sinai, by figures such as Cain, Abel, and Noah, were voluntary and never commanded, so the weaning story only needs to explain the commandment given at Sinai, not the general human habit of sacrificing. But that response defends only the origin of the law. It does not explain why a law that is admittedly remedial in purpose should be fully reinstated at the exact moment, in Maimonides' own messianic vision, that its remedial purpose has already been achieved.

I would like comments on two things: first, whether I have represented Maimonides' position in Guide III:32 accurately, or have flattened or misread it somewhere; and second, whether the tension I have laid out between the Guide, the messianic halakhah in the Mishneh Torah, and the ninth principle is a genuine inconsistency in his thought, or whether there is a real resolution available within Maimonides' own writings. If there is a resolution, I would like it spelled out in some detail rather than just gestured at.
