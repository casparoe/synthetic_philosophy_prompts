In my seminar on decision theory this term, we've been working through program equilibrium as a route to cooperation in the Prisoner's Dilemma. I follow the basic Tennenholtz-style result: when players submit programs with mutual read-access to source code, "cooperate iff the opponent is a syntactic copy of me" is a Nash equilibrium of the resulting program game. But we also covered the more sophisticated FairBot construction from the "Robust Cooperation in the Prisoner's Dilemma" literature: FairBot cooperates with an opponent just in case Peano Arithmetic proves that the opponent cooperates with FairBot, and it is unexploitable in the sense that it never cooperates with an agent that defects against it. The striking claim is that FairBot played against a copy of itself -- call the two instances FairBot1 and FairBot2 -- provably achieves mutual cooperation, and that the reason is Löb's theorem: roughly, if a formal system proves that "provable-P implies P," it can already prove P outright.

What I can't get past is why this isn't viciously circular. FairBot1's action depends on a proof about FairBot2's action, and vice versa, so naively "neither one provably cooperates" looks like at least as stable a resting point as "both provably cooperate" -- and indeed, the possibility of either outcome is exactly what makes the setup look worrying in the first place. Yet only cooperation actually gets proved. Could you explain, at an intuitive rather than symbolic level, what it is about Löb's theorem specifically -- as opposed to self-reference or fixed-point reasoning in general -- that breaks the symmetry in favor of cooperation rather than mutual defection? I'd also like your assessment of whether this asymmetry reveals something genuinely meaningful about rational cooperation between mutually transparent agents, or whether it's really just an artifact of how the "cooperate if provable" rule happens to be set up syntactically -- such that some other, equally natural-looking formalization might just as well have forced mutual defection instead.
