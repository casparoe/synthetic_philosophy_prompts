I'm currently drafting a longer feature on the philosophical status of the Church-Turing thesis, partly prompted by how casually it now gets invoked in arguments about whether large language models "really" compute or reason in some deeper sense. Rather than lean on secondhand textbook summaries, I went back to Turing's original 1936 paper, "On Computable Numbers, with an Application to the Entscheidungsproblem," and got stuck on part of Section 9, "The extent of the computable numbers," where he actually makes his case for the thesis. Tellingly, it isn't the equivalence-of-formalisms argument most popular accounts lead with (the later proof that Turing machines, lambda calculus, and general recursive functions all define the same class of functions); it's an earlier, much more informal and almost introspective argument, and I've been going back and forth with a physicist friend about how to classify it — she reads it as a proof, I read it as closer to a stipulation, and neither of us is fully confident.

Here is the passage, from near the start of Section 9 (Proceedings of the London Mathematical Society, 2nd series, vol. 42, 1936, pp. 249–250):

"No attempt has yet been made to show that the 'computable' numbers include all numbers which would naturally be regarded as computable. All arguments which can be given are bound to be, fundamentally, appeals to intuition, and for this reason rather unsatisfactory mathematically. The real question at issue is 'What are the possible processes which can be carried out in computing a number?'"

A little further on, after introducing a human "computer" — his term for a person calculating by rote on squared paper — who can attend to only finitely many symbols at a glance, Turing writes:

"The behaviour of the computer at any moment is determined by the symbols which he is observing, and his 'state of mind' at that moment. We may suppose that there is a bound B to the number of symbols or squares which the computer can observe at one moment. If he wishes to observe more, he must use successive observations. We will also suppose that the number of states of mind which need be taken into account is finite. The reasons for this are of the same character as those which restrict the number of symbols. If we admitted an infinity of states of mind, some of them will be 'arbitrarily close' and will be confused. Again, the restriction is not one which seriously affects computation, since the use of more complicated states of mind can be avoided by writing more symbols on the tape."

Could you help me pin down exactly what kind of argument this is? I'm especially interested in: (1) whether the premises about a bound on observable symbols and a finite number of "states of mind" are meant as self-evident conceptual truths, as empirical claims about human psychology, or as something else again; (2) why Turing himself flags this style of reasoning as "unsatisfactory mathematically" rather than presenting it as a proof, and what that concession should imply about how confident we're entitled to be in the thesis that grew out of it; and (3) whether, on your reading, this passage pushes the Church-Turing thesis closer to being a theorem, an empirical hypothesis, or a definition of "computation" — and whether Turing's other arguments later in the same section (the equivalence proofs, the appeal to examples) are really answering the same question or quietly shifting to a different one.
