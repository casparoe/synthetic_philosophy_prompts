I spent my career as an evolutionary biologist specializing in human population genetics, and since retiring I have joined a small philosophy discussion group run through our local university's continuing-education program. We are currently working through the debate between naturalism and normativism about disease — Christopher Boorse's biostatistical theory on one side, welfare-based accounts such as Nordenfelt's on the other — and each member has been asked to bring a short position paper to our next meeting. I have drafted mine around an example from my own field rather than the standard textbook cases (homosexuality, obesity, and the like), and before I present it I would very much value your comments, particularly on whether I have represented Boorse's theory fairly and on whether my central example actually accomplishes what I think it does.

Here is the draft. Boorse's biostatistical theory (BST) defines disease as a part- or process-level dysfunction: a statistically species-subnormal contribution to survival and reproduction, assessed against a reference class fixed by age and sex, within an environment normal for the species. Its principal attraction is that health and disease come out as purely descriptive biological facts, free of value judgement. I want to press on that claim with a case from human population genetics: lactase persistence. In most mammals, and in most human populations worldwide, the capacity to digest lactose declines sharply after weaning; lactase non-persistence is both the ancestral condition and, taking the species as a whole, the statistically typical one among adults. Lactase persistence is a derived trait that rose to high frequency independently in only a handful of populations — parts of Northern Europe and, separately, several pastoralist populations in East Africa and Arabia — apparently through selection connected to dairying. Under BST, whether adult hypolactasia counts as "dysfunction" seems to depend entirely on which reference class one selects: relative to the species as a whole it is the norm, and so not disease; relative to a Danish sub-population, say, it is markedly subnormal, and satisfies Boorse's definition of disease to the letter. Boorse permits reference classes to be split by age and by sex, but says comparatively little about when, if ever, a population subgroup should count as a reference class in its own right. My worry is that any principled way of drawing that line — by breeding population, by geographic ancestry, by allele frequency at the relevant locus — has to smuggle in just the sort of classificatory judgement that BST was supposed to make unnecessary, since no amount of raw statistics tells us, in advance of some further decision, which population is the right one to average over. If that is correct, BST does not eliminate value-laden or theory-laden choices from the concept of disease; it merely relocates them into the choice of reference class.

I would be grateful for your reactions on three counts: first, whether this is a fair statement of Boorse's view or whether I have built it into something of a straw man; second, whether there is an obvious naturalist rejoinder I have overlooked — some principled, purely statistical way of individuating reference classes, by local interbreeding population, perhaps, that would blunt the objection; and third, whether this line of criticism is already well established in the philosophy of medicine literature under a name I simply have not yet encountered, since I would much rather learn that now than discover it midway through the discussion.
