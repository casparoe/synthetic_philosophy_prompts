Last night, after a seminar on the semantics of gradable adjectives, a friend and I ended up arguing for a long time about knowledge and stakes, and neither of us gave any ground. We started from one of those familiar bank-style scenarios: Hannah is confident the bank will be open on Saturday, since she was there two Saturdays ago and saw the posted hours; in a low-stakes version of the story everyone is comfortable saying she "knows" this, while in a high-stakes version, where a large payment will bounce unless the deposit clears in time, the very same evidence no longer feels sufficient to license the word "knows." My friend defended the epistemic contextualist line that "knows" is a context-sensitive term, so that the two utterances of "Hannah knows the bank is open Saturday" can differ in truth-value because each expresses a different proposition, fixed by whatever standard is salient to the person doing the attributing. I defended pragmatic encroachment instead: what changes between the cases isn't the meaning of the word but Hannah's actual epistemic standing, since how much is practically riding on being right is itself one of the factors that decides whether her true, well-evidenced belief amounts to knowledge.

Where we really got stuck was on a variant of the case that neither of us could talk the other out of: situations where the attributor and the subject face different stakes altogether, such as an idle bystander with nothing riding on the matter commenting on Hannah's situation while she is stuck in the high-stakes version, or, conversely, someone facing enormous personal stakes gossiping about a subject who is actually in the low-stakes version. My friend wanted the attributor's own context to keep fixing the standard even here, whatever that commits her to; I wanted only Hannah's situation to matter to whether the attribution comes out true, regardless of who happens to be talking about her or how much they personally care. We couldn't agree whether this was real progress toward locating a genuine disagreement between our two views, or just two different bookkeeping schemes assigning different labels to the same underlying facts, and we ran out of energy before settling anything.

I'd like you to write a dialogue between a committed epistemic contextualist and a committed pragmatic encroachment theorist that manages what we couldn't. Have them work through an attributor/subject stakes-mismatch case like the one above in real detail, with each side stating precisely what their theory predicts there and why. Make the contextualist answer for the charge that contextualism requires ordinary, linguistically competent speakers to be strangely oblivious to the very context-sensitivity of "knows" that the theory posits, and make the pragmatic encroachment theorist answer for the charge that pragmatic encroachment allows a subject to lose (or regain) knowledge of a proposition purely because the practical stakes shifted, with no corresponding change in the evidence bearing on the proposition itself. Both characters should be equally sharp, respond directly to whatever the other has just said rather than restate their own position, and by the end they should make a genuine, careful attempt to determine whether their disagreement is substantive or merely verbal, even if they cannot fully settle it.
