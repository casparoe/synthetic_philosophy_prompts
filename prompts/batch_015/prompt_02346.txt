I spent most of my career as an experimental particle physicist – drift chambers and calorimeters, mostly, though I still keep an old cloud chamber in the garage to show the grandkids the vapor trails. Now that I'm retired I've been slowly working my way through an old two-volume history of Indian philosophy that's been gathering dust on a shelf since grad school – no idea why I never got around to it before, if im honest. This week I got completely stuck on the Nyaya school's theory of the pramanas, the valid sources of knowledge. Their definition of perception (pratyaksa) requires actual, direct contact between a sense organ and the object itself, wich they sharply contrast with inference (anumana) – reasoning from a perceived sign to some unperceived thing you conclude must be there, their standard example being inferring a fire on a hill from seeing smoke. Reading that, I about fell out of my chair, because that's exactly the situation I was in for thirty-odd years: nobody ever directly perceives a particle, you see a vapor trail curl into existence, or hear a click from a Geiger counter, and you reason your way from the sign to the thing itself.

So here's my question, and forgive me if I mangle the vocabulary since I only picked it up about two weeks ago. Gangesha, the fellow who more or less founded the later "New Nyaya" school, lived well over five hundred years before anyone had so much as conceived of a subatomic particle, and yet he's apparently the one who introduced the whole ordinary-versus-extraordinary (laukika/alaukika) way of subdividing perception, and he was also notoriously exacting about the conditions a sign has to satisfy before an inference from it earns the status of real knowledge. Given how careful he was on both counts, what do you think he'd actually have ruled if you sat him down in front of a cloud chamber and walked him through what the tracks meant? Would he have found a way to stretch one of his perception categories to cover it, or would he have insisted the whole apparatus of modern physics – electrons, neutrinos, quarks, all of it – falls squarely on the anumana side of the ledger, meaning it has to clear his quite demanding bar for a trustworthy inference before any of us are entitled to believe in it? I floated this at dinner with a couple of old physicist friends and got nothing but polite confusion, so I'm hoping you'll have more to say about it.
