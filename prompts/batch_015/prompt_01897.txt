I'm a second-year politics student, and this term I'm taking a seminar on European political thought between the two World Wars. Nothing we've read bears directly on the situation I want to describe, but the course has put me in a reflective mood about organized political life, which is perhaps why this situation has been on my mind all semester. Since the autumn, I've been an active member of one of the two "slates" that dominate elections for my university's student government: a standing coalition of clubs and individuals who agree on a shared platform, campaign together under a single name, and run a joint ticket of candidates every spring. Joining felt exciting at first: there are weekly strategy meetings, an always-buzzing group chat, matching merchandise, and a real sense of camaraderie built around outdoing the rival slate.

Lately, though, several things about how the slate actually functions have started to bother me. When someone raises a genuine disagreement with a platform position at a meeting, the conversation rarely turns on whether the position is actually correct; it turns instead on whether airing the disagreement publicly would fracture our unity or hand the rival slate an opening before the election. I've caught myself doing the same thing, nodding along in public to planks I privately consider weak, because breaking ranks would look disloyal and could cost us votes. I've also noticed people, myself included, prefacing ordinary opinions with "speaking as a member of our slate," even on questions that have nothing to do with the platform, as though the affiliation itself supplied the reasoning. And when I tally where the slate's energy actually goes, it flows overwhelmingly into recruiting new members, undercutting the rival slate, and winning the next election, far more than into the causes the platform claims to champion. This doesn't even let up once the election is over: students who win office under our name keep voting together as a bloc on the student council, on issues that have nothing to do with anything we ran on, apparently just to keep the slate intact for next year.

A friend on the rival slate tells me this is simply what organized politics always looks like, and that I'm naive to want anything different. Another friend thinks the only honest response is to quit and act purely as an independent from now on. Neither answer fully satisfies me, but I haven't been able to pin down exactly what is wrong with the dynamic I'm describing, or what, if anything, it implies about what I ought to do. I'd appreciate help thinking through what's really going on here and how I should weigh my options.
