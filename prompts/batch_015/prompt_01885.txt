I don't have any formal training in philosophy or logic, but over the past few months I've worked through Aristotle's sea-battle discussion in On Interpretation and several of the later technical responses to it. As far as I can tell, everyone who wants to keep the future genuinely open has to give up either the assumption that facts about the past are always fixed and unpreventable, or the assumption that "it will happen, or it will not" is already true before anything is settled — and each way of giving one of these up seems to come with its own strange cost. The main responses I've run into are: Diodorus Cronus's Master Argument, which instead bites the bullet and denies an open future; Łukasiewicz's three-valued logic, which adds a third "undetermined" truth-value; Prior's Peircean semantics, on which every future contingent simply comes out false; Prior's Ockhamist semantics, on which truth is relative to a history, with no history singled out as the "real" one; the "thin red line" view, on which one branch is metaphysically privileged as the actual future; Thomason's supervaluationism, which leaves contingent statements with a genuine truth-value gap; and MacFarlane's relativist semantics, on which truth is relative to a later context of assessment, not just the context of utterance.
