I'm writing a historical novel with a scene set in 1890 in which a character schooled in Adam Smith's economics reacts to news of Cecil Rhodes's newly chartered British South Africa Company, and I want his objections to sound authentically Smithian rather than like generic modern anti-colonialism. Smith's most sustained institutional complaint in the Wealth of Nations was directed at exclusive trading companies vested with sovereign power, above all the East India Company, whose rule he called "perhaps, the worst of all governments for any country whatever," on the grounds that fusing merchant and ruler in one body gives shareholders every incentive to extract short-term gain from people in whom they have no lasting stake. A century later, Britain revived exactly that instrument for the Scramble for Africa: within a few years of each other in the 1880s, the Royal Niger Company, the Imperial British East Africa Company, and Rhodes's British South Africa Company were all chartered on the East India Company's precedent, each equipped with its own armed forces and treaty-making powers.

Would Smith, confronted with this, have read it simply as history repeating the error he had already diagnosed, or might features of the later situation — changes in company and incorporation law, the diplomatic framework set at the Berlin Conference, the domestic coalitions that lobbied for these charters — have led him to a more complicated verdict? Please reconstruct his likely reasoning as specifically as you can, rather than resting on his general reputation as a critic of empire.
