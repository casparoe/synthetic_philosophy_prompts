I'm a novelist, and my publisher has asked me to write a short craft essay for the launch of my new book — but I've stalled on exactly the question the essay is supposed to resolve, so I'm hoping you can help me reason through it before I write a word of it.

The novel follows a Guatemalan family in Ohio in the months after the father is detained by ICE. Two early readers, independently, used almost the same phrase: it "reads like an op-ed with characters in it." Their complaint isn't that the politics are wrong; it's that the characters seem to exist to make a point, that outrage is doing work craft should be doing, and that message-driven "issue novels" tend to curdle fast once the news cycle that produced them has passed.

My instinct is to push back using Du Bois, but I want to make sure I'm using him honestly rather than just conveniently. Here's the reasoning so far, in note form:

1. In "Criteria of Negro Art" (1926), Du Bois doesn't concede that propaganda undermines art — he insists that "all art is propaganda," and that he cares nothing for art that isn't. If even a critic as invested in beauty as Du Bois believed this, maybe "it reads like propaganda" isn't the indictment my readers think it is.

2. But I suspect that's too quick. Du Bois isn't defending message-driven art in general; he's defending the right of a specific, systematically silenced group to counter-propagandize against a culture already saturated with the other side's propaganda dressed up as neutral fact. That's much narrower than "issue novels are fine," and I'm not sure my book — about a different group, a different injustice, a century later — is entitled to borrow the moral force of an argument built for that particular asymmetry.

3. Alain Locke's rebuttal complicates things further: he thought that even righteous propaganda keeps art reactive, orbiting the very audience it's protesting, rather than self-possessed — and that the artist's real task was individual expression that happened to land as universal. If Locke has a point, my readers' unease might be tracking something Du Bois's slogan doesn't actually dissolve.

4. Tellingly, when Du Bois's own magazine ran its 1926 symposium asking writers how the Negro should be portrayed in fiction, most respondents — sympathetic readers, several of them — still resisted the idea that Black artists owed their work to any cause at all.

So: does this line of reasoning give me a real defense, or does it mostly show that "reads like propaganda" was never quite the right complaint to begin with — in which case I still don't know what to do about my book? I'd welcome comments on whether I've got Du Bois and Locke right, whether note 2's worry about borrowed moral force is fatal to my using Du Bois at all, and whether any of this is a genuinely interesting way into the essay or just a detour.
