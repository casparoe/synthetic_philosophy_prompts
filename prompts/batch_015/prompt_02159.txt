So my friend Priya and I got into it this weekend over, of all things, whether George Eliot would've liked the Sunday Assembly. Context: I'm re-reading Middlemarch for a book club and made some offhand comment about how Eliot basically invented "spiritual but not religious," and Priya (who did her undergrad thesis partly on Victorian religious doubt) would not let that slide. We ended up like an hour deep into her actual biography -- the evangelical phase as a teenager, refusing to go to church with her dad in 1842 after Charles Hennell's book got into her head, then grinding through translating Strauss's Life of Jesus and later Feuerbach's Essence of Christianity, plus that famous bit Myers recorded of her in the Fellows' Garden at Trinity, going on about "God, Immortality, Duty" being inconceivable, unbelievable, and yet absolute, respectively.

Priya's claim was that all of that adds up to Eliot basically being a proto-Sunday-Assembly person -- the actual organization from 2013, "live better, help often, wonder more," singing and readings and no God involved -- like she'd recognize it immediately as her own Feuerbachian project (keep the human meaning, ditch the metaphysics) finally institutionalized. I said no way, that's too neat: the novels are full of characters (Dorothea, Silas Marner, even Bulstrode in his gross way) who's religious feeling is tangled up with unchosen tradition, guilt, ritual, a community you're stuck with rather than one you opt into on a Sunday morning -- and I think Eliot would find something scheduled and branded like that a bit thin, maybe even a little fake, next to all that. We argued about it til we ran out of wine and never actually resolved anything, just got more entrenched in our own positions.

Can you actually adjudicate this properly, using what you know of her religious biography and how it plays out in the fiction -- not just the "famous Victorian agnostic humanist" cliché? I want an actual verdict: would Eliot mostly welcome something like Sunday Assembly as a fulfillment of her own thinking, or would she have real reservations, and if so what exactly would they be grounded in? Don't just give me "it's complicated, here's both sides" -- pick a side and argue it properly, the way you'd have to if you were the one still arguing with Priya.
