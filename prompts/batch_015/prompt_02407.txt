I'm a retired experimental physicist (spent about thirty years doing solid-state work in industry) and these days I fill my time reading philosophy, which I never really had the bandwidth for back when I was working. A few of us retirees started a little reading group at the public library, no formal training among any of us, and this year we decided to tackle some Buddhist philosophy. Right now we're slowly working through Jay Garfield's translation of Nagarjuna's Mulamadhyamakakarika, and I've gotten completely stuck on the tetralemma (catuskoti) stuff, to the point where it's kept me up thinking about it more than once.

The specific passage bugging me is chapter 18, verse 8, where Nagarjuna says "everything is real and is not real, both real and not real, neither real nor not real" and then tells us that this is the Buddha's own teaching, not just a rhetorical flourish. Now, I spent my whole career treating the law of non-contradiction as basically non-negotiable - not so much a "philosophical position" as a precondition for saying anything meaningful at all (you can't design a reliable circuit if a transistor is on and off in the same respect at the same time). So when a serious philosopher calmly asserts that something is real, not real, both, AND neither, my first instinct is that "real" must be equivocating across the four clauses, or that this is poetry rather then logic. To make matters worse, I found out that elsewhere, in the nirvana chapter (25, I think), Nagarjuna runs through the very same four options about whether an enlightened being exists after death, only this time to reject all four instead of affirming them. So even if you wanted to say he's just working with some four-valued logic, he doesn't seem to use it the same way twice.

Could you actually help me work through this, rather than just telling me it's meant to short-circuit conceptual thought? I already got that answer from a friend in the group who's more into meditation than argument, and it didn't satisfy me at all. Specifically: is there a genuinely defensible logical or semantic reading of verse 18.8 - through the two-truths distinction, some restriction on the scope of "real," a "no thesis" reading, or something else Garfield gestures at in his commentary but which I only half follow - that actually dissolves the contradiction? Or is the more honest conclusion that Nagarjuna, at least in a passage like this one, is committed to real contradictions, the kind that some modern logicians have apparently tried to formalize with paraconsistent logic? One request: please don't just tell me it's basically quantum superposition. I've heard that comparison at every dinner party for the last decade and I don't think it actually explains anything - it just trades one mystery for another.
