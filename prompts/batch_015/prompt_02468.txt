I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, but I've fallen down a bit of a rabbit hole recently reading about predictive processing and Karl Friston's free energy principle, after seeing "active inference" mentioned in a few reinforcement learning papers as a supposed grand unifying theory of brains and behavior. The core pitch is elegant: perception and action are both just an agent minimizing "surprise" (formally, variational free energy) with respect to its sensory states, so that seeing is a form of inference and acting is just another way of making your predictions come true. What has been nagging at me is the objection the literature calls the "dark room problem": if an agent's entire business is minimizing prediction error, what stops it from simply seeking out the most static, unstimulating environment available — total darkness, silence, no change whatsoever — and remaining there indefinitely, since that would trivially minimize surprise? I've read the standard rebuttals (prior preferences built into the generative model itself, epistemic or exploratory value terms, homeostatic set-points that make hunger or boredom "surprising" in their own right), and they make sense to me on paper, but I don't yet have a real feel for why surprise-minimization doesn't just collapse into a kind of engineered quietism.

I'd like you to write me a short story of no more than roughly 1,500 words that dramatizes this tension rather than explaining it. The protagonist can be human, animal, or artificial — perhaps a person, a household robot, or an AI assistant — who comes to realize, gradually or all at once, that their own mind or architecture is built entirely around minimizing surprise, and for whom the "dark room" solution starts to look uncomfortably reasonable rather than absurd. I'd prefer that you avoid using jargon like "free energy" or "active inference" directly in the prose itself, so that the ideas are carried by the plot and the character's choices rather than by exposition, and I'd like the ending to actually take a stance on the question rather than resolving it with a shrug.
