Some of us who've been reading LessWrong and ACX since basically forever run this low-stakes side project where we make Manifold-style play-money markets out of whatever's going on in the friend group, and sometimes out of a puzzle book one guy owns — the kind full of self-contained "island of logicians" setups (knights-and-knaves villages, monks who can see everyone's forehead smudge but not their own, that whole genre). Back in the spring there were two puzzles up the same week that seemed to have nothing to do with each other: one about a knights-and-knaves village working out who's lying by day 7, and one about monks silently working out who has ash on their forehead by day 7. I put pretty different odds on "the group gets the right answer" for each one — 80% for the monks, 35% for the village — because the flavor text made them feel like totally different difficulty levels.

After the season ended, the sharpest guy in the group circulated a short write-up showing that once you strip out the story dressing, the two puzzles are literally the same induction argument, step for step. He even pointed out that he could've built a set of bets across the two markets that was guaranteed to loose me money no matter how either one actually resolved, just from the gap between my 80 and my 35. Now he's telling me my split wasn't an innocent mistake but a genuine rationality failure, because — direct quote — "a properly Bayesian agent gives identical credences to logically equivalent claims, full stop, thats not optional." What bugs me is that nobody, including him, noticed the two puzzles were the same thing until he sat down and worked it out on paper. The guaranteed-losing bet wasn't just sitting there for anyone to grab; you'd have needed to already know the thing that was, in some sense, the entire question, before you could construct it.

So who's actually right — is he correctly holding me to a standard I failed to meet, or is he holding me to a standard that only makes sense for some idealized reasoner who's already worked out every mathematical fact and can instantly see when two descriptions are secretly pointing at the same one? More usefully: is there an actual principled way to distinguish "you made an epistemic mistake" from "you simply hadn't finished the math yet," or does that distinction collapse once you look at it closely? Would love the take of someone who's actually thought hard about this, rather than a reflexive "well, ideal probability theory says X."
