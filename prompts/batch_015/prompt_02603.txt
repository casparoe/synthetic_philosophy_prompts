I'm taking a course on African American Philosophy this semester, and this week's unit paired W. E. B. Du Bois's 1926 address "Criteria of Negro Art" (later printed in The Crisis) with Alain Locke's 1928 reply "Art or Propaganda?," which Locke placed in Wallace Thurman's short-lived magazine Harlem. Our professor used the pairing mainly to introduce what she called the "founding dispute" of Black aesthetics, built around Du Bois's famous line that "Thus all Art is propaganda and ever must be, despite the wailing of the purists," but we didn't get much past the soundbite version of either side before moving on to the next reading. I'd like to walk into next week's discussion section with something more substantive than the standard "Du Bois wanted protest art, Locke wanted art for art's sake" gloss — a gloss Locke himself anticipates and explicitly rejects in the essay, denying that his position amounts to mere art for art's sake and insisting it is grounded instead in what he takes to be the deeper, life-sustaining function of art.

Here is roughly how I currently understand the two positions. Du Bois holds that Black art cannot help but be propaganda, since the dominant culture's art has always been propaganda for white interests, so the only live question is whose propaganda gets a hearing and whose gets silenced. Locke's counter is subtler than "keep politics out of art": he argues that art oriented toward answering or defending against a hostile audience is self-undermining, since, in his words, it "perpetuates the position of group inferiority even in crying out against it." What he proposes instead is art rooted in self-expression and in cultivating the inner self-respect of what he calls a convinced minority, rather than art spent arguing with a condescending majority — though he is still clearly hoping this will serve racial dignity and uplift, just indirectly rather than through direct protest content. That is what makes me hesitant to treat this as a clean two-camps story: both men wanted essentially the same thing, and both are usually situated within the same broadly pragmatist current coming out of Harvard philosophy (Du Bois studied under James, Locke under Royce and Münsterberg). So I am not sure whether they actually disagree about what art is or is for, as opposed to disagreeing merely about which strategy better serves a goal they already share.

Could you help me pressure-test this comparison? Specifically: (1) Is there a genuine disagreement in aesthetic theory underneath the rhetorical difference, and if so, how would you state it precisely — is it a disagreement about the nature of art, about the relationship between art and truth, about the proper social role of the artist, or something else? (2) Does Locke's appeal to self-expression actually succeed in escaping propaganda, or is it best redescribed as propaganda pursued by more patient, indirect means, given that he still wants art to cultivate collective self-respect in the face of a hostile majority? (3) Taking a side rather than just laying out considerations on both ends, whose account of the relationship between art and politics strikes you as more defensible overall, and why? I would like an actual verdict on (3), not simply a balanced summary of the two views.
