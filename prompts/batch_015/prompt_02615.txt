In my own reading about the free will debate, the argument I keep coming back to is Galen Strawson's Basic Argument: that nobody can be ultimately responsible for their actions, since this would require being responsible for one's own mental character, which would in turn require having freely chosen that character — but any such choice would only express whatever character one already had, so the whole chain of explanation regresses backward without end. What strikes me most is that Strawson insists this problem holds even if determinism turns out to be false, since a partly random self is no more self-made than a fully determined one.

Then, almost by accident, I discovered that his father, P.F. Strawson, wrote "Freedom and Resentment," a hugely influential essay arguing that our everyday practices of resentment, gratitude, and blame are so bound up with human relationships that the truth of determinism simply has no bearing on whether those practices are justified.

I would like a close comparison of the two positions. In particular: does the father's defense of responsibility, grounded in ordinary human practice rather than metaphysics, actually meet the challenge posed by the son's Basic Argument, or does it quietly change the subject, answering a more modest question than the one the Basic Argument was actually asking? Please give me your own considered judgment on this, rather than just a summary of both views.
