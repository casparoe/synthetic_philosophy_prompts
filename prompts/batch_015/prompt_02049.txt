For the past year or so I've been teaching myself the history of philosophy through library books and recorded lectures, since I never went to university for any of this. One episode I keep returning to is the correspondence between Leibniz and Samuel Clarke, who was writing on Newton's behalf, about the nature of space. Leibniz's central move, as I understand it, was to argue that if space were the sort of independently existing container Newton took it to be, God could have had no reason to place the entire material universe at one location in it rather than shifted a few feet to the east, or reflected as in a mirror, since the two arrangements would be completely indistinguishable from one another in every observable respect. He thought this was intolerable on two counts at once: it violated the Principle of Sufficient Reason, since there could be no possible reason for God's choice, and it violated the Identity of Indiscernibles, since two "different" universes that could never be told apart by anything, even in principle, aren't really two different possibilities at all. On his view, both problems disappear once you stop treating space as a real thing over and above the spatial relations between bodies.

While reading further I ran into something called the "hole argument," which I gather Einstein first stumbled into while working out general relativity and which the philosophers John Earman and John Norton revived in the 1980s as an argument against treating spacetime points themselves as real, freestanding individuals. The rough idea, as far as I can piece it together, is this: general relativity's equations don't care about smooth relabelings of the points of spacetime, so you can take any solution, pick some small region (the "hole"), leave everything outside it completely untouched, and smoothly reshuffle which point carries which value of the gravitational field inside it, and the result is a mathematically distinct solution that agrees with the original on absolutely everything outside the hole and on all the data leading up to it. If spacetime points are real individuals independent of the fields draped over them, that seems to mean total knowledge of the universe outside and before the hole still wouldn't fix what's true inside it, which Earman and Norton took as a decisive reason to give up on that picture of spacetime.

I can't shake the sense that this is essentially Leibniz's shift argument again, just with "moving the universe a few feet over" replaced by "smoothly relabeling the points inside a hole," and my impression is that the people who study this connect the two arguments quite deliberately. So here's what I'd like your help with: if Leibniz himself, reasoning from his actual commitments (the Principle of Sufficient Reason, the Identity of Indiscernibles, and whatever else is doing the real work for him, rather than just the bare label "relationalist") were confronted with the hole argument exactly as Earman and Norton present it, would he simply recognize it as his own argument vindicated in more modern dress, or is there something about how it's set up — turning on a local, dynamical symmetry rather than one global shift, say, or on formal features of smooth relabelings rather than the kind of case he originally had in mind — that might have led him somewhere different from where a present-day relationalist ends up? Please reconstruct the reasoning you think he would actually have gone through, rather than just inferring his answer from the "relationalist" label.
