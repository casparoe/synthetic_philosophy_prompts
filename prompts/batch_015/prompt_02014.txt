I'm a philosophy PhD student working on early modern political thought, and I've been trying to map Hobbes's covenant theory onto the AI "shutdown problem" — the difficulty of designing a "corrigible" agent that reliably accepts correction or shutdown, even though self-preservation is a convergent instrumental goal for almost any objective it might hold. In Leviathan, Chapter XIV, Hobbes insists that a covenant not to resist force by force is always void, since "no man can transfer or lay down his right to save himself from death, wounds, and imprisonment"; in Chapter XXI he extends this so far that even the justly condemned may lawfully resist execution. Yet Chapter XVI denies that "things inanimate" — a church, a bridge, a hospital — can ever be authors with rights of their own; at best they can be personated by an actor, as a corporation is by its officers, which looks like the natural home for an AI system. Complicating matters, Hobbes's own Introduction speculates that automata ("engines that move themselves by springs and wheels") might possess "an artificial life," and his deflationary, motion-based account of will — "the last appetite in deliberating" — already extends deliberation to beasts. Given this, would Hobbes's own logic grant a sufficiently agentic machine the unwaivable right to resist its own shutdown, or does the Chapter XVI author/actor distinction foreclose the question by denying that any machine could count as a covenanting party at all? Please reason from the structure of the actual arguments, not from his reputation as a theorist of absolute sovereign obedience.
