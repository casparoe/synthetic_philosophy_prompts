For the past three years I have been the primary caregiver for my father, who has advanced Alzheimer's disease. In the early stages, looking after him came easily and even gave me a kind of quiet satisfaction. Over the past year, though, as he has stopped recognizing me on most days, that feeling has largely drained away. I still manage his medication, his meals, the nighttime emergencies, all of it, but now because it needs to be done rather than because I want to, and that shift has unsettled me more than I expected.

A friend from a philosophy discussion group I recently joined told me this is close to a scenario Kant actually discusses, and reading the passage left me more disturbed than reassured. I'd like help thinking it through more carefully than I've been able to on my own. Here it is (T. K. Abbott's translation of the Groundwork, First Section):

"...it is always a matter of duty that a dealer should not overcharge an inexperienced purchaser; and wherever there is much commerce the prudent tradesman does not overcharge, but keeps a fixed price for everyone... Men are thus honestly served; but this is not enough to make us believe that the tradesman has so acted from duty... Accordingly the action was done neither from duty nor from direct inclination, but merely with a selfish view.

On the other hand, it is a duty to maintain one's life... But on this account the often anxious care which most men take for it has no intrinsic worth, and their maxim has no moral import. They preserve their life as duty requires, no doubt, but not because duty requires. On the other hand, if adversity and hopeless sorrow have completely taken away the relish for life; if the unfortunate one, strong in mind, indignant at his fate rather than desponding or dejected, wishes for death, and yet preserves his life without loving it - not from inclination or fear, but from duty - then his maxim has a moral worth.

To be beneficent when we can is a duty; and besides this, there are many minds so sympathetically constituted that, without any other motive of vanity or self-interest, they find a pleasure in spreading joy around them... But I maintain that in such a case an action of this kind, however proper, however amiable it may be, has nevertheless no true moral worth, but is on a level with other inclinations... [and] deserves praise and encouragement, but not esteem. For the maxim lacks the moral import, namely, that such actions be done from duty, not from inclination. Put the case that the mind of that philanthropist were clouded by sorrow of his own, extinguishing all sympathy with the lot of others, and that, while he still has the power to benefit others in distress, he is not touched by their trouble because he is absorbed with his own; and now suppose that he tears himself out of this dead insensibility, and performs the action without any inclination to it, but simply from duty, then first has his action its genuine moral worth. Further still; if nature has put little sympathy in the heart of this or that man; if he... is by temperament cold and indifferent to the sufferings of others... would he not still find in himself a source from whence to give himself a far higher worth than that of a good-natured temperament could be? Unquestionably. It is just in this that the moral worth of the character is brought out which is incomparably the highest of all, namely, that he is beneficent, not from inclination, but from duty."

I'd be grateful for help with a few specific things here:

1. Kant clearly doesn't treat these cases as equivalent: the shopkeeper merely "honestly serves" his customers with no moral praise attached at all; the sympathetic philanthropist earns "praise and encouragement, but not esteem"; only the duty-driven case gets "genuine moral worth." What exactly is sorting actions into these tiers, and why does the shopkeeper - who arguably does more reliable good for more people - rank below even the "praised but not esteemed" philanthropist?

2. The passage moves from the philanthropist whose sympathy has been "clouded by sorrow" to a further, seemingly distinct case: a man "by temperament cold" who never had much sympathy to begin with, and Kant says this second case brings out worth that is "incomparably the highest of all." Is that just the first point restated for emphasis, or is losing one's sympathy meaningfully different from never having had it, in a way that would make the second a stronger claim than the first?

3. Read uncharitably, this passage seems to say my caregiving has actually gained in moral worth now that the affection has drained out of it, compared to when I was doing the same things gladly. Is that really what falls out of Kant's view, or is there something in this text - or elsewhere in his ethics - that should stop me from drawing that rather bleak conclusion?
