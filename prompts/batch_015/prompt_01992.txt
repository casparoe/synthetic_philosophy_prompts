This happened at my writing group and I can't let it go. My protagonist Wren has a neural implant, installed by the people who raised her, that overrides her and forces her to finish "the mission" if she ever tries to back out. In my draft she does it on her own, no hesitation, so the implant never fires. I read the scene to two friends to check whether readers would buy Wren as morally responsible, and they ended up arguing with each other instead of answering me.

Cole said she isn't responsible: by construction she couldn't have done otherwise, since the implant guarantees the outcome no matter what she "chooses." Nadia said that's backwards -- the implant never fired, so it played no part in the actual chain of events; judge her by what actually moved her, not a machine that stayed off. Cole shot back: what if she wavers for a split second before catching herself -- isn't that flicker of hesitation the alternative possibility that should matter? Nadia called that a dodge, since I could just write Wren as never wavering, and asked if Cole really wants her to count as *less* responsible the more resolute she is. Cole muttered that the implant needs some earlier signal to react to if its going to work reliably, and admitted that sounded like it assumed the answer rather than argued for it.

There's definately real philosophy buried in here that's over my head. Go through it move by move, say who's got the better argument at each point (not just who wins overall), and flag whatever both of them are missing. End with one line saying who you'd back on balance -- "Verdict: Cole" or "Verdict: Nadia."
