I've spent a long time reading rationalist-adjacent blogs and forums (Overcoming Bias, LessWrong, Astral Codex Ten, and the like), and I've noticed that whenever the Fermi paradox comes up, the conversation tends to gravitate toward the same two or three moves: the Great Filter, the zoo hypothesis, maybe a nod to "rare Earth." A few weeks ago a thread on one of these forums sent me back to Sandberg, Armstrong, and Ćirković's old paper proposing the "aestivation hypothesis," and I think it's stranger and more dramatically promising than its relative obscurity would suggest. The core idea, if I have it right, is that because Landauer's principle ties the thermodynamic cost of irreversible computation to ambient temperature, a civilization that cared about maximizing the total computation it could ever perform would do better to power almost everything down now and simply wait: as the universe's background temperature keeps falling from its present ~2.7 K toward the asymptotic floor of roughly 2.7×10⁻³⁰ K set by the cosmological constant — something that takes on the order of a trillion years — that same fixed stock of usable energy can eventually buy something like a 10³⁰-fold increase in achievable computation. On this view, the silence we observe isn't evidence that intelligent life is rare or self-destructive; it's roughly what you'd expect to see if the universe already contained civilizations that had done the math and concluded that almost everything worth doing is better done later.

I'd love to see this turned into fiction rather than just discussed as a decision-theoretic curiosity, because I think the premise has real dramatic potential that pop-science write-ups of "sleeping aliens" tend to waste. Could you write me a short story, something in the neighborhood of 1000–1800 words, that takes the aestivation hypothesis seriously as a piece of world-building and follows its logic through to something that actually feels unsettling or poignant, rather than mainly serving as an excuse for an infodump? I don't want to over-constrain the plot, but I'd be especially glad if it grappled with one or more of the following: the epistemic problem facing any civilization — ours included, if it ever came to that — of how you could become confident enough in a strategy like this, as opposed to its being a self-serving rationalization for collective passivity, to actually stake your entire future on it; the possibility that a civilization husbanding negentropy for a trillion years hence would have sharp incentives to stop younger, noisier species like us from squandering shared cosmic resources, which gives the whole picture a colder, almost Dark Forest–like edge; or simply the psychological weight, for whichever mind is left "on watch," of staying awake while everything else has gone quiet for a span of time that dwarfs the entire history of life on Earth many times over.

Feel free to set the story among humans, aliens, uploads, or some mixture of the three, and to choose whatever narrative distance — intimate first-person, a cold archival report, or something else entirely — you think best serves the concept; I'd rather see an approach I wouldn't have thought of myself than have you follow my own half-formed ideas about tone. The one thing I'd ask you to avoid is treating the physics as mere set dressing for a generic "ancient sleeping god" story: the specific, slightly absurd rationality of the strategy is what got me interested in the first place, and I'd like the story to earn its ending from that premise rather than from a more conventional twist.
