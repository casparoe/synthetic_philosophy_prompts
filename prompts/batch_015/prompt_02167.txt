This term I'm taking a seminar on nineteenth-century British ethics, and this week's session was devoted to the concluding chapter of Sidgwick's *Methods of Ethics*, where he admits that the conflict between the axioms of Rational Egoism and Rational Benevolence threatens to reduce what he calls the "cosmos of duty" to mere "chaos." We spent a long time on why he found this so distressing: both axioms struck him as genuinely self-evident, yet they can issue contradictory verdicts, so the very possibility of a rational system of conduct seemed to be at stake.

As background reading, our professor also assigned excerpts from Bart Schultz's biography *Henry Sidgwick: Eye of the Universe*, which traces Sidgwick's decades of work for the Society for Psychical Research and quotes an 1887 retrospective in which Sidgwick recalls that, when writing the *Methods*, he had been inclined to follow Kant in "postulating" the soul's continued existence, but preferred to hold that postulate only "provisionally" while pursuing what he called "the serious search for empirical evidence" of survival. Most of my classmates took this as showing that psychical research was simply Sidgwick's more rigorous, empiricist substitute for Kant's postulate of immortality: a philosophically better-motivated route to the very same destination.

I'm not convinced by that reading, and I'd like to pressure-test it for a short paper due at the end of term. Please argue that even a completely successful outcome of Sidgwick's psychical research program -- full scientific proof of personal survival, together with a providential moral order guaranteeing that virtue and self-interest always coincide in the long run -- would still not have resolved the dualism of practical reason in the sense in which Sidgwick himself posed it, and would at most have rendered the conflict practically moot while leaving its theoretical core untouched. I suspect the crux is whether eliminating every actual case of conflict between two principles is the same achievement as showing that they share one underlying ground of rational authority, and I'd like the paper to take a clear stand on that question as it applies to this specific case, rather than just rehearsing the biographical story.
