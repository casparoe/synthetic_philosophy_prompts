I'm a software engineer, and for the past year or so I've been building a formal verification tool at work, which has left me strangely fluent in a subject I never actually studied: Gödel's incompleteness theorems. A coworker recently sent our team channel down a rabbit hole about the Lucas–Penrose argument — the claim, going back to J. R. Lucas's 1961 paper and later developed further by Roger Penrose in "The Emperor's New Mind" and "Shadows of the Mind," that no formal system, and therefore no algorithm, and therefore no computer, can ever be a complete model of a human mind. The core move is that for any consistent formal system F strong enough to encode arithmetic, you can construct a Gödel sentence that essentially says "F cannot prove this sentence"; if F is consistent, that sentence is true but unprovable inside F, and yet we, standing outside F, can apparently just see that it is true. I follow the basic appeal, but the part that keeps nagging at me is the obvious rebuttal: to actually see that the sentence is true, don't you first need to know that F is consistent, and doesn't Gödel's second theorem say a system can't prove its own consistency from within itself? It's unclear to me, then, where the human's certainty about F's consistency is supposed to come from, or why the same doubt wouldn't apply just as well to whatever is producing their own confidence. Penrose apparently tries to reinforce the argument later by demanding soundness rather than mere consistency, but that move looks to me like it just relocates the same problem one level up instead of resolving it.

Feel free to set it somewhere unglamorous — a code review, a courtroom, a chess match, a thesis defense — rather than some far-future setting, since I'd rather the tension come from the reasoning itself than from any sci-fi trappings.
