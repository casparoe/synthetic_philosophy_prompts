So I'm a retired geophysicist (35 years, mostly marine seismic work), and lately I've actually been reading philosophy of science instead of just name-dropping it at conferences -- currently working through Kuhn's Structure of Scientific Revolutions. Had dinner last month with two old grad school friends, Frank and Carol, and they got into a real argument about whether the plate tectonics revolution was an actual Kuhnian paradigm shift, incommensurability and all, or whether that's just fashionable philosophy pasted over an ordinary case of good evidence winning out. Roughly how it went:

1. Frank: it was the genuine article. Pre-1960s "fixist" geologists weren't just short on data -- their whole conceptual kit (what a "continent" was, what "permanent" even meant) was built around a static earth. That's why someone like Harold Jeffreys never came around even decades after seafloor spreading was textbook: not stubborness, he literally couldn't accept the new evidence as evidence, because his own framework had already ruled the mechanism out.

2. Carol: too generous to Jeffreys, too hard on everybody else. Paleomagnetic data, seafloor age data, all public measurements anyone could go check -- and the community converged on them within about a decade. A few old guys staying loyal to a theory they built their career on isn't incommensurability, that's just ordinary scientific conservatism, and you get that in every field.

3. Frank: but Jeffreys wasn't just slow to look at the data, he had his own seismological arguments that the mantle was mechanically too rigid for convection, full stop -- so when the new evidence came in it wasn't "same evidence, different conclusion," it was "framework decides in advance what the evidence could even mean."

4. Carol: that's underdetermination, which is a much weaker and more ordinary thing than what Kuhn's actually claiming. His real claim is that terms stop translating across the divide, that its a different world, a gestalt switch. But everybody else was measuring the same seafloor in the same units and fully agreeing within years. That's not what a real Kuhnian crisis is supposed to look like.

They went a couple more rounds after that and never got anywhere. Could you go through this move by move and say who's got the better of it at each point (not just who wins overall), and flag anything both of them seem to be missing about what Kuhn's thesis actually requires? End with a one-line verdict: \verdict{Frank}, \verdict{Carol}, or \verdict{Neither}.
