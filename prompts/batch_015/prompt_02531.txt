At a charity raffle with 400 tickets sold, my friend Elena, who had bought a single ticket, announced before the winning number was drawn that she wouldn't win, and left the event rather than wait for the result. Her friend Marco objected that she didn't actually know that -- she was just guessing. Elena replied that she wasn't guessing at all: she had a 99.75 percent chance of being right, a far higher degree of confidence than plenty of everyday claims nobody would think twice about, such as "the pharmacy will be open on Monday." Marco said the two cases aren't comparable: if the pharmacy turns out to be closed, there will be some specific reason for it (a holiday, an emergency) that she could in principle have looked into beforehand, whereas her raffle odds rest on nothing but the bare numbers -- and that difference, he said, is exactly why one claim is assertable and the other isn't. Elena countered that this is a distinction without a difference: a 0.25 percent chance of error is a 0.25 percent chance of error, and demanding some further story behind the number is an arbitrary standard she shouldn't have to meet. As it happened, her ticket lost. When she brought this up afterward as vindication, Marco said her being right proved nothing, since by her own account she could easily have been wrong.

Is there a principled standard that would make Elena's original assertion improper despite the odds so heavily favoring her, and does the eventual outcome of the raffle have any real bearing on whether that assertion was proper at the time she made it?
