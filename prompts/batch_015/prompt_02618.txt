Two of my graduate students got into a spirited argument after last week's philosophy-of-mind seminar, and since I was too busy moderating the discussion to adjudicate it live, I'd like an outside opinion.

Elena opened by pressing Kim's causal exclusion argument: if mental property M is distinct from the physical property P that realizes it on some occasion, and P alone is nomologically sufficient for the physical effect that follows, then M's contribution is either idle or amounts to a systematic overdetermination we'd never tolerate outside contrived cases like a firing squad — so non-reductive physicalism collapses into epiphenomenalism unless M just is P. Daniel replied with Shoemaker's subset-of-powers strategy: M and P aren't "wholly distinct" existences competing for causal credit, since M's powers — say, pain's disposition to cause winces — form a proper subset of P's, which, as C-fiber firing, also disposes toward things pain doesn't, like tripping a sensitive scale; so P's producing the wince already partly constitutes M's producing it rather than rivaling it. Elena answered that this only relocates the difficulty: if M's powers are strictly included in P's, then P's powers outside that subset already suffice for the effect on their own, so M is still doing no independent work — "inclusion" is just overdetermination wearing a nicer name. Daniel then invoked Yablo's determinable/determinate model: M and P are more like red and scarlet than like two separate causes, and exclusion only looks threatening because Kim treats them as fully distinct to begin with, which is exactly what supervenience was meant to preclude. Elena countered that determinates of a single determinable are supposed to exclude one another at a time — nothing is both scarlet and green all over — and that nobody has shown mental and physical properties share that structure rather than merely being redescribed as if they did, so the analogy has to be earned case by case, not asserted whenever exclusion threatens.

Please go through the exchange move by move and say who has the better of it at each step, not just who wins overall, and flag any assumption the two of them share that you think neither is entitled to.
