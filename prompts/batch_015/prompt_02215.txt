This term, my school's philosophy elective has been working through a unit on personal identity, and last week we spent a class on the Milindapanha, the Buddhist text in which the monk Nagasena explains the doctrine of no-self (anatta) to King Milinda (the Indo-Greek king Menander) through the well-known simile of the chariot: just as "chariot" is only a convenient name for a wheel, axle, and frame assembled in a certain way, with no further chariot hiding behind the parts, "person" is only a convenient name for the five aggregates (form, sensation, perception, mental formations, and consciousness) arranged and causally connected in a certain way, with no further self behind them. Our teacher then had us read the part of Derek Parfit's Reasons and Persons where he explicitly compares his own reductionist theory of personal identity to this doctrine, cites the Milindapanha directly, and concludes that the Buddha would have agreed with him. Two people in my class, Noah and Ines, ended up arguing about this pairing all the way out to the parking lot afterward, and I still haven't been able to decide who had the better of it.

(1) Noah's opening claim was that the chariot dialogue shows anatta is really just a reductionist thesis about parts and wholes: there is no separately existing self over and above the aggregates, only the aggregates themselves, suitably connected, which is exactly Parfit's conclusion about persons. Since Parfit arrived there independently through his own arguments and said so himself, Noah figured "no-self" isn't some exotic mystical doctrine so much as reductionism about persons that a twentieth-century analytic philosopher happened to rediscover on his own. (2) Ines answered that this flattens two real differences: first, the reasoning behind the chariot simile is completely general, meant to show that no composite thing at all has a "further fact" of identity beyond its parts, whereas Parfit's reductionism is only ever about persons; second, and more importantly, the entire reason Buddhism wants you to see through the self is to loosen the craving (tanha) it says drives suffering (dukkha), so the metaphysics is in service of that practical project rather than standing on its own. Keep the bundle theory and drop the soteriology, she said, and you've kept the shell and thrown out the point. (3) Noah replied that the contrast isn't as clean as Ines wanted it to be, because Parfit makes almost the same move about his own view: he says that realizing there is no further fact about personal identity felt liberating to him, made him less afraid of death, and made him care more about other people, since the line between himself and others started to seem less metaphysically important. So Parfit wasn't just offering a dry technical thesis either; he claimed a therapeutic payoff from it, in basically the same spirit as the Buddhist claim. (4) Ines answered that there is still a real difference between a philosopher noting, in a few pages near the end of a five-hundred-page book, that a conclusion happens to console him personally, and an entire system of ethical training and meditative practice built around actually producing the end of suffering, where a view only counts as rightly grasped if it is doing that work in you. That, she added, is also why the Buddha refused to answer purely metaphysical questions unrelated to that project, in the famous simile of the poisoned arrow — not because he lacked a view, but because answering wouldn't reduce anyone's suffering, which was the only thing he was interested in settling. Parfit's book was never organized around that goal in the same way, so the comparison undersells how different the two projects still are.

Could you go through this move by move and say who has the stronger position at each stage, rather than just picking an overall winner? I would also like to know whether Ines's answer in (4) actually engages with the rebuttal Noah made in (3), or quietly shifts to a different claim than the one she made in (2), and whether there is some consideration neither of them raised that ought to change the verdict.
