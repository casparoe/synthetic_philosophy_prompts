This term I'm taking a seminar on the history of utilitarianism, and we recently spent a week on the concluding chapter of Sidgwick's Methods of Ethics, where he lays out what he calls the profoundest problem of ethics: the dualism of practical reason, or the standoff between the rational authority of egoism and that of impartial, universal benevolence. Almost as an aside, our instructor mentioned that Sidgwick spent much of his adult life as the founding president of the Society for Psychical Research: he sat in on séances, tested mediums such as D. D. Home and Leonora Piper, and investigated apparitions and telepathy, largely in the hope of finding empirical evidence of personal survival after death that could show the universe is arranged so that virtue and self-interest ultimately coincide. Several of us found it jarring that one of the most rigorous philosophers of the period devoted decades of serious effort to what looks, from a distance, like ghost-hunting, and it has turned into a running disagreement in our study group.

One side of the argument holds that this was simply Sidgwick being consistent: he did not think we are entitled to help ourselves to a comforting postulate the way Kant did, so if resolving the dualism genuinely depended on some contingent fact about how the universe is morally ordered, going out and looking for evidence of that fact was the intellectually honest move for someone with his epistemic scruples. The other side thinks the episode exposes a real problem with his position, either because searching for a predetermined, personally needed conclusion sits badly with disinterested inquiry, or because even solid evidence of an afterlife would not have supplied what the dualism actually required: a rational reconciliation of the two standpoints, rather than a fortunate empirical fact that merely happens to make them agree in practice.

Write an essay that takes a side on this dispute. Explain precisely what work evidence of survival was supposed to do within Sidgwick's ethical theory, engage seriously with the strongest version of each interpretation described above, and take into account how his decades of investigation actually turned out. A qualified verdict is fine (for instance, "rational as far as it went, but resting on a confusion about what empirical evidence could establish"), but do not hedge to the point of refusing to take a position.
