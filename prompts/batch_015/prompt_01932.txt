A few friends and I have run the same fantasy football league for eight years, and three of us sit on a small competition committee that our commissioner set up to rule on eligibility disputes, precisely so that no single person has to be the sole arbiter. This week we had to adjudicate a genuinely difficult case: one manager, Jordan, was accused of starting a player who should have been ineligible due to injury, and our bylaws specify that a manager's win can only be overturned if the committee finds, first, that an eligibility rule was actually violated, and second, that the violation conferred a material scoring advantage he would not otherwise have had.

On the first question, two of the three of us — myself and Priya — concluded that the rule had indeed been broken, while Marcus disagreed. On the second question, a different two of us — myself and Marcus — concluded that the advantage was real and material, while Priya did not, since she judged that Jordan would likely have won regardless. So, taken question by question, each part of the two-part test was affirmed by a majority of the committee. And yet, when Priya proposed that we dispense with the two separate questions and simply vote directly on whether to overturn the result, only I voted in favor; Priya voted no because she remained unconvinced there had been any real advantage, and Marcus voted no because he remained unconvinced any rule had been broken. The direct vote therefore came out two to one against overturning, even though a majority had separately endorsed both of the individual conditions our own bylaws treat as jointly sufficient.

Priya and I argued about this for the better part of an hour afterward and never really resolved it. She maintains that the direct vote is obviously the one that should count, since anything else amounts to letting a technicality override what most of the committee actually wants as an outcome. I keep feeling that this can't be quite right either, since it would mean our own two-part rule becomes meaningless whenever someone thinks to frame the vote differently — and it's unsettling that simply choosing how to structure the vote (question by question, versus straight up or down) seems to determine the result all by itself, without anyone actually changing their mind. I'd genuinely like to know whether one of us is missing something obvious, or whether this is a more fundamental snag than either of us realized — and, if it's the latter, whether there is any principled way to decide which approach a committee like ours should actually rely on going forward.
