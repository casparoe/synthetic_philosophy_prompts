My friend and I got into a whole argument last night about modern polyamory, after one of those "compersion" TikToks came up at dinner. I brought up Bertrand Russell, since I had to read Marriage and Morals for a class last year and remembered he was already making basically this argument back in 1929 -- that jealousy isn't some fixed instinct but mostly a product of possessiveness/property-thinking about partners, and that a marriage doesn't need sexual exclusivity to be good as long as people are honest and it isnt messing up the kids. (Random side note: this is the same book that got his job offer at a NYC college revoked in 1940, after a judge ruled his writing on marriage and sex made him unfit to teach, and which Russell later said was basically the reason he got the Nobel Prize in Literature.) My friend's counter was that Russell's own second marriage, to Dora Russell, fell apart in exactly this way -- she had two kids with another guy while they were in an openly agreed non-monogamous arrangement, and by all accounts Russell was quietly eaten alive by jealousy about it the whole time, even though his own book argues jealousy can basically be reasoned and trained away. It ended in a pretty bitter divorce a few years later.

So we couldnt agree on this: would Russell look at how polyamory is practiced and talked about today -- explicit relationship agreements, "metamours," compersion treated as a skill you deliberately cultivate -- and say something like "see, this is what I meant, you just need better structure and less Victorian guilt than Dora and I had"? Or would he actually be the first to admit that his own theory doesn't survive contact with how jealousy really works, regardless of how much vocabulary and negotiation you throw at it? I want your actual best-guess take here, not just "he was progressive for his time so he'd probably approve" -- please really engage with the gap between what he argued in the book and what happened in his own marriage, and say whether you think he'd revise the theory in light of that, or chalk the failure up to something else (Dora specifically, the surrounding social stigma making it harder than it would be now, bad luck, whatever).
