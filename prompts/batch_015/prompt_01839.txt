I'm auditing a course on classical Indian philosophy this semester (not for credit, just because it's fascinating) and we're currently deep in Nyaya epistemology. Something about their theory of testimony (shabda) as a pramana is bugging me, and I can't tell if it's a real problem with the system or if I'm just misunderstanding the lecture. From what I understood, shabda counts as a source of knowledge right alongside perception and inference, and Vedic testimony in particular gets treated as basically infallible -- valid just because it's scripture. That seems like a textbook appeal-to-authority move wearing a fancy epistemological costume: my old intro logic class drilled into us that "X is true because an authoritative text says so" is exactly the kind of move that doesn't count as a real argument.

What makes it extra confusing is that the Naiyayikas were obsessive about cataloguing bad arguments -- they've got this whole detailed taxonomy of inferential fallacies (hetvabhasa) -- so it seems strange that they'd have such a glaring blind spot for their own tradition's sacred texts. Is that actually what's going on, or is there some real philosophical machinery in how they set up shabda that keeps it from just reducing to "trust the authority, full stop"? And if its the latter, does that same machinery end up applying to everyday, non-scriptural testimony too, or is scripture doing something categorically different there?
