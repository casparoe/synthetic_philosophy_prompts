I'm a PhD student in history and philosophy of science, currently drafting a dissertation chapter on Galileo's biblical hermeneutics, and there's an extrapolation I keep circling back to that I'd like an outside reconstruction of.

In the 1615 Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina, Galileo's argument rests on two premises: that scripture and nature cannot truly conflict, since both proceed from God, and that scripture alone is accommodated to the limited understanding of ordinary readers (he leans on Augustine here, plus Cardinal Baronius's line that scripture teaches "how one goes to heaven, not how heaven goes"). From this he concludes that a literal reading should be abandoned only once natural philosophy has produced a genuine "necessary demonstration" against it. Notably, this was also Bellarmine's own stated bar in his 1615 letter to Foscarini — he conceded that a "true demonstration" of heliocentrism would force a reinterpretation of scripture, but insisted none yet existed and that he would not believe otherwise until it was "shown him."

Apply that shared standard, rather than the science-versus-religion cliché built around Galileo in hindsight, to a dispute he never faced: literal Adam-and-Eve monogenism in Genesis versus population-genetic evidence against any two-person human bottleneck. That evidence is statistical, not demonstrative in the quasi-geometrical sense both men had in mind. Would Galileo's own framework actually license treating it as grounds for reinterpreting Genesis, or would it trap him in exactly the wait-and-see posture he found intolerable when Bellarmine imposed it on him? I'd be grateful for your best reconstruction of the argument on his behalf, and for where you think it runs into trouble.
