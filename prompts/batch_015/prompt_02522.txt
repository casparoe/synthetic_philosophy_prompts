I cover the history of science, and I've been reporting on the long feud between Thomas Hobbes and the mathematician John Wallis, which started when Hobbes announced in De Corpore that he'd squared the circle and ran, through several more failed "proofs," until his death. Two of my sources for the piece -- a historian of mathematics I'll call Nora and a Hobbes scholar I'll call Theo -- kept arguing about it after a talk, and I haven't been able to settle things myself.

Nora's opening argument was that Hobbes justifies treating civil philosophy as a demonstrative science by calling geometry "the only science that it hath pleased God hitherto to bestow on mankind" -- yet Wallis showed, repeatedly and publicly, that Hobbes couldn't tell a valid proof from an invalid one, mistaking approximate constructions for exact ones and dismissing clear counterexamples. If he couldn't recognize a failed demonstration in the one field where experts could check him, she asked, why trust his say-so that Leviathan's laws of nature follow with real necessity?

Theo answered that this misses Hobbes's actual argument, in Six Lessons to the Professors of Mathematiques, for pairing geometry with civil philosophy: both are demonstrable because in both we construct the objects we reason about -- lines by the motion of a point, the commonwealth by covenant -- so only there is a maker's knowledge of causes possible. Fumbling a compass-and-straightedge construction, he said, no more undermines that argument than a driving instructor's inability to rebuild an engine undermines his authority to teach driving.

Nora replied that it is the same failure, not a separate one: Hobbes also rejected Euclid's own definitions as inadequate and held that even Euclid's axioms themselves needed demonstrating -- a general, and in mathematics demonstrably mistaken, idea of what a valid demonstration even is, and the same notion doing the work in his maker's-knowledge argument.

Theo's rejoinder was that nobody has to take Hobbes's civil philosophy on faith the way Wallis's contemporaries had to check his geometry: scholars have spent decades poring over Leviathan's chapters on the laws of nature, and their live disagreements -- over whether those laws work more like Euclidean axioms or like Hobbes's own "maker's knowledge" construction -- turn on the text itself, not on his temperament. Raising his stubbornness about circles, Theo said, is an ad hominem dressed up as evidence.

Could you go through this exchange move by move and say who has the better of each point, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'd also like to know whether either of them leans on a shaky premise about how Hobbes's "geometrical method" is actually supposed to work, and what, if anything, the exchange as a whole is missing.
