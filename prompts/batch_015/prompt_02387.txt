I'm taking a philosophy of mind seminar this term, and we recently spent a week on split-brain syndrome, working through the classic Sperry and Gazzaniga lateralization studies. The experimental paradigm still strikes me as remarkably clean: when an image is flashed exclusively to a commissurotomy patient's left visual field, the right hemisphere can direct the patient's left hand to pick out the corresponding object from a set of hidden items by touch, while the patient's speech — produced by the left hemisphere, which never received the image — sincerely reports having seen nothing at all. In the related "interpreter" experiments, when each hemisphere is shown a different, unrelated scene and each hand selects an appropriately associated picture, the speaking hemisphere will confidently fabricate a reason for the choice made by the hand it has no informational access to, apparently without noticing that it is doing so.

This looks to me like about as strong a case as empirical psychology could ever hand a philosopher: two dissociable centers of perceptual access, each able to guide behavior and each with its own mode of report, coexisting under a single skull. If that is right, it seems to follow that the unity of consciousness we take for granted in an intact brain is not a deep fact about the mind, but merely a contingent effect of the corpus callosum keeping two potential subjects wired together — so that a commissurotomy does not create a second mind so much as reveal one that had been there all along, alongside the first.

When I raised this in section and with a couple of classmates afterward, my professor agreed that the data are striking but said my move from "dissociable access and report" to "two subjects, both already present before surgery" is much faster than the evidence permits. She pointed me toward Nagel's 1971 paper on the topic and toward Sperry's own later suggestion that split-brain consciousness might be "Y-shaped" rather than cleanly doubled, but didn't have time to spell out the objection before class ended. I also mentioned a 2017 study I'd come across that pushed back on the two-consciousness reading, though she thought I was putting more weight on it than it could bear. I remain unconvinced that my inference is doing anything illegitimate, since the dissociation seems too clean to be explained away as anything less than two genuinely separate subjects of experience. Could you go through where, if anywhere, this reasoning actually overreaches, and explain what someone who accepts all of the experimental findings but still resists the two-subjects conclusion could be holding onto — or is my professor just being unduly cautious about a question the evidence has, for all practical purposes, already settled?
