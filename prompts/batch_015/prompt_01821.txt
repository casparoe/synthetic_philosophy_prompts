I've been reading rationalist and AI-safety blogs for a long time, and recently went back through the old MIRI material on tiling agents and self-trust: the original Yudkowsky and Herreshoff paper on the Löbian obstacle, the "Robust Delegation" section of the Embedded Agency sequence, and the later work by Critch and the logical induction group. A friend who works adjacent to this material and I have been arguing for weeks about how to read the current state of play without either of us budging, so I'd rather see the disagreement played out properly by two people who know the material cold than keep relitigating it secondhand.

Here's the crux as I understand it. The Löbian obstacle says a formal agent can't fully vindicate trust in a successor (or future self) that reasons at its own strength: proving the general schema "whatever my successor proves is true" would, via Löb's theorem, let the agent prove those conclusions itself outright, collapsing the whole point of delegating -- the same self-referential machinery that yields Gödel's second incompleteness theorem as a special case. The obvious fix, trusting the future self without demanding a soundness proof, runs into the procrastination paradox instead: an agent willing to defer any task it can prove will eventually get done ends up perfectly consistent, and simply never does the task. Everything since -- Critch's parametric-bounded version of Löb's theorem, logical induction's asymptotic guarantee that an agent comes to trust its later beliefs more than its earlier ones -- looks to me like it works by relaxing the original demand, bounding it, making it probabilistic, pushing it to a limit, rather than meeting it as originally posed.

I want to know whether that's a fair reading or a confused one, and I'd rather see it fought out than have it explained to me from one side. Could you write a dialogue between two people who both know this material in depth but land in different places: one holds what I'll call the Limit view, that the Löbian obstacle and the procrastination paradox jointly reveal a genuine, in-principle bound on reflective self-trust that no amount of technical ingenuity removes, so that unconditional trust in an equally powerful successor was never a coherent thing to want, and everything since has quietly substituted an easier problem for the original one. The other holds what I'll call the Dissolution view, that insisting on unbounded proof-theoretic certainty was always the wrong standard for a resource-bounded embedded reasoner, and that once it's dropped, the later results give a real self-trusting agent everything it could need. I'd like the two of them to actually engage with each other's strongest specific moves rather than talk past one another -- for instance, whether an agent coming to trust its later beliefs more than its earlier ones in the limit is a genuine answer to the Löbian obstacle, or just a redescription of what happens once you've quietly stopped asking the original question.
