I started an evening seminar on distributive justice a few weeks ago, and this week's unit was Robert Nozick's entitlement theory from *Anarchy, State, and Utopia*, including his Wilt Chamberlain example about why any "patterned" principle of justice keeps getting upset by free exchange between consenting people. I found it a satisfying bit of armchair theorizing until, almost too neatly, a real version of the same argument broke out among my studio-mates a few nights ago, and I'd now like a more careful opinion than I trust myself to give, partly because I'm one of the five people affected by the outcome. Some background: five of us have run a small ceramics collective, Kilnworks, out of a shared studio for about three years, splitting every dollar the joint shop makes equally, regardless of who threw, glazed, or photographed any given piece, and nobody has ever contested that arrangement. Separately, for roughly the last two years, customers have occasionally commissioned custom pieces directly from individual members, paid by a separate invoice, and by casual, unanimous agreement, whoever did the commissioned work simply kept that money, since the sums involved were small and irregular. Over the past six months, though, one member, Renata, was featured on a well-read design blog for her glaze technique, commission requests naming her specifically have multiplied, and she now personally earns something like four times what the joint shop distributes to each of the rest of us.

This produced an argument between two other studio-mates, Owen and Farah, over dinner, while I mostly listened. (1) Owen opened by saying the commission income isn't really "Renata's" in any morally interesting sense, since customers only find her through Kilnworks's shared Instagram, joint mailing list, and three years of communally built reputation, so the money is really a return on collective assets that happens to be invoiced under a single name. (2) Farah answered that this proves too much: she herself ran a small solo pop-up last year off the same client list and nobody suggested she owed the group anything for it, so singling out Renata's earnings now, only once the number got large, looks more like envy than principle; she added that the joint shop's own revenue is completely untouched and still split five ways exactly as before, so this is new money, not a diversion of the old kind. (3) Owen replied that scale is not a morally neutral detail: a few hundred dollars from a pop-up is nothing like commissions that now dwarf the shop itself, and that treating an old, casual understanding, reached back when nobody thought it would ever matter, as binding at this scale lets a minor rule settle a question it was never designed to answer. (4) Farah said opportunity is not entitlement: everyone had identical access to the same Instagram and mailing list, and only Renata's individual work converted that access into this level of demand; if shared infrastructure plus outsized success generated an automatic claim, then any of them doing unusually well at anything downstream of the shared studio would owe the others in perpetuity, which cannot be right. (5) Owen said fine, he wasn't asking Renata to return money already earned, only proposing that the group renegotiate, going forward, where the line between "shop income" and "personal income" sits, since that line was drawn back when nobody thought it mattered. (6) Farah agreed that renegotiating terms prospectively is legitimate and not itself unfair, but insisted this has to happen with Renata's actual agreement, not by the other four outvoting her because they dislike how a rule they all previously accepted has turned out for one person; imposing new terms on her without her consent would itself be the injustice, just relabeled as a correction.

Nobody landed a decisive blow, and the four of them (Renata included, once she heard about it secondhand) agreed to revisit the question "properly" at the next studio meeting, which is coming up soon and will likely change what the rest of us take home too, so I'd like to walk in with a clearer head than dinner produced.
