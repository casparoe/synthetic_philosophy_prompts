random thing that's been living in my head since my history-of-physics reading group (way more fun to think about than my actual dissertation chapter, tbh). Leibniz's whole case against Newton in the Clarke letters is that space isn't a substance, just the order of relations among coexisting bodies -- he leans on PSR and the identity of indiscernibles to argue that shifting the whole material universe five feet east, or having God create everything a year earlier, is an "impossible fiction," since the two scenarios would be totally indiscernible. Newton's comeback is the bucket: spin a bucket of water and eventually it climbs the sides from true rotation, even at the exact moment the water's rotating right along with the bucket (so zero relative motion between them) -- meaning something absolute, not just relative motion, has to be doing the explaining.

So now show Leibniz a LIGO readout from 2015: a ripple that started when two black holes merged over a billion years ago, propagated for a billion years through space where the "relata" no longer existed as seperate bodies, and showed up at the detector carrying real energy. Does his relational theory have any principled way to accommodate a disturbance traveling through a stretch of nothing, or does this break the theory outright? And does frame-dragging (the Gravity Probe B result -- basically a precision modern descendant of the bucket experiment, a spinning mass measurably dragging inertial frames around with it) help his case or hurt it? Want an actual verdict on which specific moves from the correspondence survive contact with 20th/21st century physics and which don't, not just a "well it's complicated."
