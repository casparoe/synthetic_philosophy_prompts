G. E. Moore observed that a sentence such as "It's raining, but I don't believe that it's raining" is not a formal contradiction, can be true, and yet cannot be sincerely asserted in the first person without absurdity. One common explanation of this absurdity appeals to the pragmatics of assertion: to assert a sentence is to represent oneself as believing it, so a speaker who asserts both conjuncts takes back, in asserting the second, what she has just conveyed by asserting the first. Roy Sorensen instead analyzes such sentences as "epistemic blindspots"—propositions that are consistent and possibly true, yet cannot be known or believed by a particular thinker at a particular time. Explain how the blindspot diagnosis applies to Moore's sentence, and explain why it captures something the pragmatic story leaves unaddressed: namely, that the thought itself, quite apart from any act of assertion, seems just as impossible to believe.
