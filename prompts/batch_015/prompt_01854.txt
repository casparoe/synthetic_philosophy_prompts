Ok, this actually happened at my dinner table, not a hypothetical, and I can't stop turning it over. Two friends of mine (one's an economist, the other trades bonds) got into an argument on saturday about the odds the Fed cuts rates at the next meeting, and it somehow turned into a fight about disagreement itself. I teach philosophy, not finance, so I had nothing useful to add in the moment. Roughly, as I remember it:

(1) Marcus said they'd each told the other exactly what they think the odds are, like four seperate times now, and neither had budged - and that there's literally a theorem for this, Aumann's, which says two rational Bayesians who both know what the other one honestly believes have to end up at the same number. Since they hadn't converged, he said, 'one of us isn't being rational.'

(2) Nina said maybe they just started from different priors before either of them looked at any data, and that's fine - it's not irrational to walk in with a different gut sense than someone else.

(3) Marcus said fine, but if they're both actually rational, shouldn't their starting priors have been the same to begin with? They read the same research, sat through the same seminars - a genuinely rational prior shouldn't depend on whose head it happens to be in.

(4) Nina said even granting that one, just saying their numbers out loud at dinner isn't obviously the same thing the theorem needs. She suspects he's anchored on whatever he guessed first, and figures he'd say the same about her. So it's not clear they actually have 'common knowledge' of each other's beliefs in whatever strict sense the theorem requires. As she put it, 'we've just got two people repeating numbers at each other.'

They both turned to me like I'd have a verdict. I didn't. Can you go through this move by move and say who's got the better of it at each step -- not just who wins overall -- and flag anything important that both of them are missing?
