I was digging through my partner's old philosophy books this weekend and found that little NYRB edition of Simone Weil's essay "On the Abolition of All Political Parties" (written in 1943, the one with the Milosz essay tacked on the back), and it kind of hijacked my whole evening. Her whole argument rests on defining a party by three features: it's a machine for generating collective passion, it's an organization built to exert collective pressure on the minds of its own members, and it's own unlimited growth becomes its real goal even though officially it's just supposed to be a means to the public interest. From those three she concludes every party is totalitarian at least potentially and by aspiration, even in a perfectly healthy multiparty democracy, and that the whole apparatus should be legally abolished so people's individual judgment (she calls it something like inner light, or attention to truth) can actually track justice instead of a party line. Her proposed fix is pretty legalistic too - she wants the law to repress any circle of opinion the moment it crystallizes into a formal membership organization, while still letting loose, fluid intellectual circles (like the ones that form around a magazine) exist freely, since nobody's "trapped" in those the way a card-carrying member is trapped in a party.

This has been bugging me because so much of what she describes now happens through social media instead of formal parties - algorithmically boosted outrage cycles, Twitter/X pile-ons, whole timelines converging on identical talking points within hours, plus the platforms themselves chasing unlimited growth in basically the same way she says parties do. So: using her own three-part test, would Weil actually say an online partisan swarm counts as a "party" in her sense, or is it a genuinely different (maybe scarier) beast precisely because nobody formally joins it and there's no membership card for a law to target? And does the growth-obsessed algorithm itself fit her third criterion better than the mob of users does? I don't want the generic "what would a long-dead philosopher think of the internet" answer, I want you to actually work through this using the specifics of her argument, including whatever she says about clandestine or informal groups persisting even after parties get formally banned.
