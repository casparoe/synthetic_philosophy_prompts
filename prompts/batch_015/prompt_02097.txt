I'm writing a novel in which a character finds her late father's old, heavily annotated copy of Nietzsche's Genealogy of Morals and spends a chapter trying to reconstruct his thinking from the margins. To make those notes convincing I've been rereading the book myself, and I've hit a real crux in the Second Essay that I can't work out on my own.

Here is the passage, from Second Essay, section 2 (Horace Samuel's 1913 translation):

"[W]e find as the ripest fruit on its tree the sovereign individual, that resembles only himself, that has got loose from the morality of custom, the autonomous 'super-moral' individual (for 'autonomous' and 'moral' are mutually-exclusive terms)—in short, the man of the personal, long, and independent will, competent to promise... this lord of the free will, this sovereign—how is it possible for him not to know how great is his superiority over everything incapable of binding itself by promises... The 'free' man, the owner of a long unbreakable will, finds in this possession his standard of value... the sovereign man calls it his conscience."

What I can't square is how this fits with the First Essay, section 13, only one essay earlier, where Nietzsche insists there is no "being" behind doing, working, becoming—that a free subject who could choose whether to exercise its own strength is a fiction smuggled in by grammar, exactly like the illusion of a "lightning" that exists apart from its flash. And it isn't a passing remark; he returns to the same point against a substantial, choosing self throughout the rest of his work. Yet the sovereign individual sounds like precisely that kind of unified, self-possessing subject. So is Nietzsche simply contradicting his own critique of the subject, or should the sovereign individual be read as still ironic—the "ripest fruit," as he calls it himself, of the very morality of custom he elsewhere claims only to be describing from outside, rather than a figure who has actually escaped it? I'd be grateful for your own best reading of the tension, and a sense of whether this is something Nietzsche scholars themselves are genuinely divided over, before I decide how much of it my character's father would have seen for himself.
