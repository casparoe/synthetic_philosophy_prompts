I'm taking a course on early modern philosophy this semester, and we just spent two sessions on Hume's essay "Of Miracles," Section X of An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding (the "first" Enquiry, as our professor keeps calling it, to distinguish it from the one on morals). After the second seminar, two people in my discussion group, Caleb and Meera, got into a running argument about whether Hume's central argument actually works. It started in the hallway and continued over text for the rest of the evening, and since I was included on the thread, I ended up with the whole exchange saved. (1) Caleb opened by calling the argument a shell game: Hume defines a miracle as a violation of a law of nature, says a law of nature is just whatever "a firm and unalterable experience" has established, and concludes that there is always "a uniform experience against every miraculous event." But, Caleb said, calling our past experience uniform already assumes that every previous miracle report was false, which is exactly what's in dispute when new testimony comes in — so the argument doesn't show that testimony can't establish a miracle, it just asserts as a premise that none ever has. (2) Meera pushed back that this isn't quite fair to Hume: he isn't assuming a priori that no violation has ever occurred, only pointing out that our experience of the relevant regularity (say, that corpses don't revive) has been checked constantly and disconfirmed, so far as anyone has carefully verified, never — which is about as strong as inductive evidence ever gets — and that weighing new testimony against that kind of record isn't circular, it's just the ordinary evidential reasoning we'd insist on for any other wildly antecedently improbable claim.

(3) Caleb's comeback was that this Bayesian gloss saves Hume's reasoning only by quietly dropping his actual conclusion. Hume's own maxim is that no testimony could ever be sufficient unless its falsehood would be "more miraculous" than whatever fact it's meant to establish, and that isn't a modest probabilistic recommendation — it's set up so that some mundane story about the witnesses (fraud, delusion, exaggeration, coincidence) will always be rated more probable than a genuine violation, however good the testimony gets, which just smuggles an in-principle guarantee back in under cover of "doing the math." (4) Meera answered that Caleb is only entitled to that complaint against Part 1 of the essay: Hume doesn't actually rest his case on the maxim alone, since Part 2 goes on to argue, as a straightforwardly empirical matter, that no reported miracle has ever come close to meeting reasonable evidential standards anyway (too few credible witnesses of the right caliber, a well-documented human appetite for the marvelous, and so on) — and it's that track-record survey, not the in-principle slogan, that does the real work.
