I sat in on a philosophy of physics workshop last month for a piece I'm noodling on, and during Q&A after a talk on the 'hole argument' two audience members had a genuinely good back-and-forth that the moderator cut off before it resolved. Been chewing on it since and want an outside read.

Quick setup in case its not your area: the hole argument says that if spacetime points are real, individual things (substantivalism), then you can take any solution to Einstein's equations and apply a transformation that's the identity everywhere except inside some matter-free 'hole,' producing a second, distinct solution that agrees with the first on every observable fact outside the hole. If points really do have their own identity, that counts as two different possible worlds -- meaning GR turns out wildly indeterministic even given a complete specification of everything outside the hole. That's what the two of them were arguing about.

One of them, a postdoc, said this sinks substantivalism outright: if believing points are individuals buys you indeterminism with zero physical payoff, the belief has to go -- only the relations among events are real, not the points themselves.

The other, a philosopher of physics a few rows up, said you can keep substantivalism, you just drop haecceitism about the points -- deny they carry any primitive 'thisness' across the two solutions -- and then say the solutions are really the same possible world described twice (Leibniz equivalence). Indeterminism disappears and points stay real.

He said that's relationalism wearing a costume: if nothing individuates a point apart from the relations it sits in, there's nothing left for 'it's a real substance' to mean that a relationalist would deny.

She said that's unfair -- denying primitive cross-world identity is a general move in modal metaphysics, applied to ordinary objects too, not invented just to dodge this argument. You can think points are the basic, underived bearers of the metric without thinking they carry name-tags across possibilities.

He shot back: fine, but then what makes a point in one solution 'the same point' as its counterpart in the other, if not some fact about the point itself? Isn't she just stipulating an answer to make the problem vanish -- every bit as ad hoc as a relationalist stipulating when two relational structures count as 'the same world'?

That's where it got cut off. Can you go through this move by move -- who's got the better of it at each step, not just overall -- and flag anything you think both of them are missing? End with a one-line verdict on who came out ahead overall.
