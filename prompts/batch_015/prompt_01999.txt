Hey, so I've been down a rabbit hole for the past couple weeks trying to connect two things I don't think I've seen put together anywhere: Yudkowsky's old "taboo your words" material from the Sequences (Disputing Definitions, Replace the Symbol with the Substance, Where to Draw the Boundary, etc) and the actual academic literature on verbal disputes and metaontology - Chalmers' 2011 paper, Hirsch's quantifier variance stuff, and Sider's naturalness-based pushback against Hirsch. I wrote up the core of it as a short essay for my rationalist reading group's discord (we've been slowly working through Rationality: From AI to Zombies together, which is where this whole tangent started), but before I post it and potentially embarrass myself I want a sanity check from someone whos not already primed to agree with me. Fair warning, I'm working mostly off memory of one grad seminar plus rereading some SEP-tier summaries, so I could easily be flattening positions that don't actually line up the way I think they do.

Here's the core of it:

Yudkowsky's line, across those posts, is roughly: most arguments over the definition of a word - does a falling tree in an empty forest make a "sound," is Pluto a "planet" - aren't really about the world, they're about which cluster of things gets to keep a label. His fix is to taboo the disputed word and restate your claim in terms of anticipated experience; if nothing you expect to observe changes once the word is gone, there was never a real disagreement, just two people fighting over who owns a dictionary entry.

This is basically a rationalist-forum reinvention of what Chalmers calls a verbal dispute, and "taboo your words" is a folksy version of something like his elimination test: strip the disputed term out of the debate entirely (or tag its rival senses with subscripts) and see whether any disagreement survives. Where it gets more interesting to me is that Yudkowsky's implicit metaphysics of categories - words are labels for clusters in "thingspace," and a good category is one that carves reality at the joints for predictive purposes, which is the explicit topic of "Where to Draw the Boundary" - sounds a lot like Sider's notion of objective naturalness, the very notion Sider uses to argue against Hirsch's quantifier variance. Hirsch thinks disputes in ontology (do arbitrary fusions of matter exist, does a cat survive losing a hair) are merely verbal, because charity to ordinary usage doesn't privilege one meaning of "exists" over another - any sufficiently charitable quantifier meaning is as good as any other. Sider's reply, developed through his "Ontologese" proposal, is that some candidate meanings really do carve nature better than others, so there's a substantive fact about which quantifier serious metaphysics ought to use, even if it's not the one ordinary language happens to track.

So here's my tentative and probably-flawed claim: Yudkowsky wants it both ways. When he's dissolving disputes with "taboo your words," he sounds like Hirsch - there's no fact of the matter, pick a definition and move on. But when he talks about categories that carve thingspace at the joints, he sounds like Sider - some categorizations are objectively better independent of anyone's stipulation. I don't think that's a flat contradiction, since one story is about disputes with zero predictive payoff and the other is about disputes where induction is at stake, but I'm not sure that line is as clean as I want it to be, especially once you ask what makes a "joint" a joint if not some further human interest that seems just as stipulative as the original labels.

Does that basically track the actual Hirsch/Sider disagreement, or am I mushing together two positions that don't line up as neatly as I think? And is the tension I'm pointing to in Yudkowsky real, or is there an obvious reconciliation between "no fact of the matter, taboo the word" and "carve reality at the joints" that I'm just missing? Happy to hear about any other holes too, accuracy or otherwise - I'd rather get told I'm wrong here than in the discord.
