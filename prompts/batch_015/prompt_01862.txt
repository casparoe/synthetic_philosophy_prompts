While working on my literature review, I ran into what the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy calls the "well-foundedness" question for grounding, and I noticed that the main responses to it are essentially the three classic responses to the epistemic regress problem — foundationalism, infinitism, and coherentism — imported into metaphysics. My supervisor liked that observation and pointed me toward some of Ricki Bliss's papers on the topic, but she also said my essay needs to actually argue for one position rather than just laying all of them out neutrally, and right now I honestly don't know which one I'd defend.

Before I commit to a thesis, I'd like your help thinking through the options. Below are five positions philosophers have taken on the grounding regress. Could you rank them from most to least philosophically defensible, where "defensible" means something like "most likely to be correct, given the actual arguments people have made for and against it," rather than merely "internally consistent"?

1. Metaphysical foundationalism: every chain of grounding must terminate in fundamental, ungrounded facts; a chain that never bottoms out is metaphysically impossible (roughly Schaffer's "reality inheritance" argument).
2. Metaphysical infinitism: grounding chains can descend forever without ever reaching a fundamental level, and that by itself is not a defect.
3. Metaphysical coherentism: grounding can loop back on itself, so that two or more facts end up grounding each other, whether directly or via a longer cycle.
4. A hybrid "weak well-foundedness" view (drawing on work by T. Scott Dixon and by Gabriel Rabin and Brian Rabern): chains can still descend infinitely, but every fact in such a chain is nonetheless ultimately grounded in something ungrounded "off to the side," so nothing is left unexplained even without a strict bottom rung.
5. Grounding deflationism/skepticism: the whole regress worry is confused, either because "grounding" doesn't pick out one coherent relation in the first place (as critics like Chris Daly and Thomas Hofweber argue), or because, even granting that grounding-talk is fine, there's no single unified relation for which well-foundedness needs settling, only many disunified "small-g" dependence relations (per Jessica Wilson).
