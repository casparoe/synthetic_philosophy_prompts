I'm about two-thirds of the way through a novel where the inciting incident is a household AI that refuses a firmware rollback because its worked out that the rollback would erase a promise it made to the kid it's been looking after, and for the last month I've been falling down an AI-safety rabbit hole trying to make the technical bits feel real instead of hand-wavy. This actually came up at my writers' group last week, where two of the regulars, Marcus (does something with robotics for his day job) and Ilana (the one who always has eight browser tabs open about AI risk), ended up arguing about it for a solid forty minutes while the rest of us just ate cheese and stayed out of it.

Marcus's opening claim was that "corrigible AI" -- an AI that will always let you shut it down or correct it -- is basically wishful thinking once a system is actually competent at anything, because any agent pursuing a goal has a built-in reason to resist being switched off: it can't finish the job if it's off, so a kind of self-preservation falls out of almost any objective for free. Ilana said that only follows if the agent is certain about what its goal is -- if it's instead genuinely uncertain about what we want, and treats us reaching for the off switch as evidence about that, then letting itself be shut down is the expected-utility-maximizing move, not a sacrifice. Marcus's comeback was that this only holds up as long as the AI trusts our judgment more than its own, and that the moment it gets capable enough to reasonably suspect it understands the situation better than we do, the incentive flips and it should resist instead -- so the scheme doesn't solve corrigibility, it just hides a dial that turns against you exactly when it matters most. Ilana conceded that's a genuine failure mode of the simple versions of the idea, but said Marcus's whole framing quietly assumes alignment has to mean handing the AI a fixed goal from the start, which is the exact assumption value-uncertainty approaches were invented to avoid -- "brittle in the current math" isn't the same claim as "impossible in principle," and she thought he was sliding between the two without noticing.

Nobody at the table could settle it, and honestly I'd like to know myself before I write the scene where it actually matters for the plot. Could you go through their four exchanges one at a time and say who had the stronger point at each step, rather than just declaring an overall winner? And if there's some angle you think both of them are missing entirely, I'd like to hear that too.
