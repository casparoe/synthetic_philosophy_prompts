hey, so I went down a logic/set theory rabbit hole this week (started with Godel's incompleteness stuff, ended up somewhere else entirely lol) and ran into Skolem's paradox. The basic version I get: Lowenheim-Skolem says any first-order theory with an infinite model has a countable model, so if ZFC has a model at all it has a countable one -- but ZFC also proves Cantor's theorem, i.e. there's uncountable sets like the reals. The usual "it's fine, not a real contradiction" explanation makes sense to me mechanically: the bijection that would prove the "uncountable" set is actually countable just isn't inside the model, so from the model's own point of view it still looks uncountable.

What I can't wrap my head around is a line from Skolem's actual 1922 paper (I'm reading it in that old "From Frege to Godel" translated sourcebook). After doing all the math he concludes it "leads to a relativity of set-theoretic notions, and this relativity is inseparably bound up with every thoroughgoing axiomatization" -- and he uses that to argue axiomatic set theory can't really work as a foundation for mathematics.

That's the part that loses me. Ok sure, uncountability comes out "relative" to whatever model you pick, fine. But why's that supposedly such a huge deal? Why isn't the obvious reply just "yeah, first-order logic can't pin down uncountability, that's a known limitation of first-order logic, doesn't mean set theory itself is broken as a foundation"? Is Skolem's notion of "relative" doing something weirder/stronger than just "not expressible in first-order terms"? Walk me through what's actually going on in his reasoning here, and whether you think he's onto something real or kind of overreacting to a technical quirk. Keep it in plain english if you can -- I'm a dev, never did any formal philosophy.
