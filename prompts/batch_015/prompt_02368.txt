So this happened in office hours for my causal inference class and I haven't been able to let it go. We'd just gone over the classic kidney stone example (the 80s study where treatment A beats treatment B within both the small-stone and large-stone groups, but B wins once you pool everyone together) when someone brought up the Berkeley admissions case from the 70s and it turned into a whole debate between two people in my section, Mia and Dev.

Mia's take: the lesson of Simpson's paradox is that you should basically always trust the stratified numbers over the pooled ones, since pooling just smashes together groups that differ in some causally relevant way. She said stone size and department are doing the same job in both examples, so whatever you get after conditioning on them is the 'real' effect.

Dev said not so fast. In the kidney stone case, stone size is a confounder — it affects which treatment a patient gets and how sick they are, so conditioning on it is the right move. But in the Berkeley case, he said, department sits in between gender and admission rather than causing both, so conditioning on it could just as easily hide discrimination as reveal the 'truth' (e.g. if women were funneled toward more competitive departments in the first place). His point was that Mia's 'always stratify' rule can't tell a confounder from a mediator, so its not really a rule at all.

Mia's comeback: confounder or mediator, conditioning on department is still just describing something true about the data, so it's not 'wrong' to report it — it just answers a narrower question than the pooled number does.

Dev's reply: sure, but that's exactly the issue, because Simpson's paradox always gets taught as 'the subgroups reveal the truth, the aggregate is misleading,' and that framing quietly assumes the third variable is confounder-shaped. Sometimes the aggregate is the number you actually care about.
