I'm a PhD student in philosophy of religion, and this term our departmental reading group has been working through the small analytic literature on the coherence of penal substitutionary atonement. Two sessions ago we discussed David Lewis's brief 1997 note "Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?" (Philosophical Papers 26, no. 3: 203–209; later reprinted in Michael Rea's edited volume Oxford Readings in Philosophical Theology: Trinity, Incarnation, and Atonement) — the Princeton metaphysician, not C. S. Lewis, a mix-up that catches out at least one member of my cohort every year. Lewis's diagnosis is that all of us, believers and atheists alike, are "of two minds" about penal substitution: we recoil at the thought of a friend serving a criminal's prison sentence or death sentence, yet we see nothing wrong with a friend simply paying that criminal's fine for him. He suggests that if punishment is understood as discharging something like a debt owed to the victim, rather than simply visiting desert on the guilty party, then whoever actually supplies the payment stops mattering, and the fines case starts to look like a genuine, uncontroversial instance of penal substitution. He tentatively concludes that this shared, if inarticulate, acceptance is itself weak evidence that the doctrine might be coherent more generally — atonement included.

What I keep coming back to is that Lewis died in October 2001, two years before Steve Chalke and Alan Mann's The Lost Message of Jesus touched off the "cosmic child abuse" controversy in British evangelicalism by describing penal substitution in exactly those terms. That charge is doing something quite different from what Lewis was addressing: it isn't a claim that transferring punishment is logically or conceptually incoherent, but a moral charge about what it says about God's character that a father would require, or inflict, suffering on an innocent son as a condition of forgiving anyone else. I'd like your best reconstruction, argued closely from the actual moves in Lewis's paper rather than from generic atonement-debate talking points, of how he would most plausibly have responded to Chalke. Does the debt/compensation model Lewis proposes give a defender of penal substitution any real resources against the "cosmic child abuse" framing? Does it instead leave that objection completely untouched, since it was built to answer a coherence worry rather than a question about divine character? Or is there a case that recasting the atonement in more transactional, creditor-debtor terms actually hands Chalke's side more ammunition rather than less? Please reason through these options rather than just laying the two controversies side by side.
