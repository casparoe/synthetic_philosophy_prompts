Three months ago, the autonomous shuttle my city introduced along my commute route braked hard to avoid a jaywalking cyclist and threw me into a seat divider hard enough to crack two ribs. I work in urban planning, so I had already been following the shuttle program with some professional interest before I ended up, rather more personally, on the receiving end of it. While I have been going back and forth with the transit authority's insurer about my claim, I started reading more widely about the ethics of autonomous systems, and that is how I came across the idea philosophers call the "responsibility gap" (I gather the term goes back to a 2004 paper by Andreas Matthias).

As I understand the argument, once a machine's behavior emerges from a learning process rather than from rules an engineer explicitly wrote, nobody neatly satisfies the usual conditions for being held responsible when it causes harm: the manufacturer did not foresee or intend this specific outcome, so it seems unfair to blame them for it in the way we would blame a careless human driver, and the shuttle itself plainly is not the kind of thing that can be praised, blamed, or meaningfully punished. What I cannot quite square is that our transit authority's operating contract already contains a no-fault clause obligating them to cover any injury the shuttle causes regardless of who or what was at fault, much like workers' compensation schemes or certain strict-liability rules for defective products.

That seems to me like it should dissolve the whole puzzle: there is now always a specific, contractually designated party who has to pay for the harm, so is not the responsibility gap simply closed by arrangements like this one? I mentioned this to a friend who is finishing her philosophy PhD, and she said that no-fault liability "doesn't actually touch the gap" and might even be answering a completely different question, but she had to leave for a meeting before she could explain what she meant. I would like to understand what, if anything, I am missing, since once a payer has been named by contract, it is not obvious to me what further question about responsibility is supposed to remain open.
