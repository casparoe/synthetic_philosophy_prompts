While reporting a feature on end-of-life care, I sat in on a conversation between two physicians on a hospital's palliative care team that turned into a real argument about the ethics of their own practice.

It started when Renata described 'terminal sedation' — gradually raising a dying patient's sedative dose to control otherwise intractable pain, even though the team knows this may hasten death — and defended it via the doctrine of double effect: the physician intends only to relieve suffering, so the earlier death is a foreseen but unintended side effect, not euthanasia. Marcus objected that this is a distinction without a difference in practice, since no one can observe another person's intention; all anyone ever sees is an escalating dose and a time of death, and clinicians know exactly which vocabulary keeps them on the right side of policy. Renata replied that intention isn't merely a private feeling — it shows up in observable practice, like titrating to the smallest dose that controls the symptom and holding there, rather than jumping to one with no plausible palliative purpose. Marcus answered that, in his experience, that kind of 'titration' routinely overshoots comfort by a wide margin and still gets documented as appropriate care, so a line this easy to talk your way around isn't doing much moral work, whatever its status in theory.

Could you go through their exchange move by move, saying who has the better of it at each stage, and close by flagging anything important you think both of them are missing about how the doctrine of double effect is actually supposed to work?
