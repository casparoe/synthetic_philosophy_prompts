For my decision theory elective we just covered the "Death in Damascus" case - the Gibbard and Harper one, not the "road to Damascus" Bible thing - where you've got an appointment with Death at midnight, Death is basically a perfect predictor of where you'll be, and you get to choose whether to stay put in Damascus or flee to Aleppo. The twist is that whichever one you pick immediately becomes evidence that Death already guessed right and is waiting there, so there's no choice that actually feels stable once you factor in what your own choice tells you about the world. My professor kept calling it "decision instability" and using it to poke holes in evidential decision theory, though apparently causal decision theory doesn't come out looking great either since there's no option that's stable once you've picked it.

So now I can't stop wondering what would happen if you actually put the Death in Damascus scenario in front of Pascal himself.

I'd genuinely love it if you could reason through this using the specific stuff we know about his views - the actual structure of the Wager, the Jansenist position on grace and predestination, his fight with the Jesuits - rather than giving me a generic "a 17th century Christian would probably just say providence" answer. I want to know whether his particular commitments actually pull in different directions here, and if so which one you think would win out for him.
