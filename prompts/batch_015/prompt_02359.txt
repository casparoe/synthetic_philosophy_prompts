Ok so I've been on an Overcoming Bias / early LessWrong nostalgia binge lately and fell back down the common prior assumption rabbit hole. You know the setup: Aumann's agreement theorem says two Bayesians with common priors can't agree to disagree once their posteriors are common knowledge, and Harsanyi basically built "everyone starts from the same prior, divergence only comes from information" into how game theorists model incomplete information. What I can't shake is how much weight is riding on that common-prior part, since vanilla Savage-style subjective expected utility theory never actually requires that two different agents start from the same place to begin with.

I went back and reread Hanson's "Uncommon Priors Require Origin Disputes" (the one with the pre-rationality condition, where your prior is supposed to cohere with what you'd have predicted about yourself before knowing which agent in the setup you'd turn out to be), plus the Gul/Aumann back-and-forth in Econometrica '98 where Gul argues the common prior assumption is basically incompatible with the whole Savage/personalist picture of probability, and Aumann fires back in his reply. My rationalist friends mostly just treat "rational agents share a prior" as the default, so that any leftover disagreement not explainable by different info is automatically a sign someone's being irrational -- but the more of this I read the less obvious that seems, and I don't think its actually as settled as people on the forums act like it is.

Could you write me an actual essay on this, not just a survey of "here's view A, here's view B"? Lay out the strongest case that ideal rationality requires a common prior, the strongest case that fully rational agents can have irreducibly different priors, and then actually pick a side and defend it. Please don't cop out with "it depends" as your final answer -- if your honest view is genuinely conditional, that's fine, but tell me on what it's conditional and where you personally come down.
