I spent about 35 years as an atmospheric scientist doing probabilistic forecast verification (basically, scoring how good weather forecaster's probability numbers actually turn out to be) before I retired, and for the last year or so I've been falling down a philosophy rabbit hole through a little discussion group that meets at my local library - half of it goes over my head, but I'm having a blast. A few weeks back someone mentioned "accuracy-first epistemology," and I about fell off my chair, because it turns out the whole apparatus (Jim Joyce's argument, and a Pettigrew book, Accuracy and the Laws of Credence, that I picked up from the library) runs on proper scoring rules - literally the same Brier-score machinery I used for decades to judge whether a "70% chance of rain" forecast was any good. Small world - turns out philosophers and meteorologists have been doing the same math without knowing about each other.

Anyways, I got a bit obsessed and wrote up some notes trying to pin down something that's been bugging me since, and I'd like some blunt feedback on whether it actually holds up. Here's the gist: as I understand Joyce's argument, if your credences don't obey the probability axioms, there's always some rival, fully probabilistic credence function that's more accurate than yours in literally every possible world - so, treating your credences as a kind of choice evaluated by their "epistemic utility," you're guaranteed to be in a worse position no matter how things turn out, no betting behavior or Dutch-book stuff required. Elegant. But the proof only goes through for what they call "legitimate" measures of inaccuracy, and the more I read, the more it looks like legitimacy is picked out by whatever makes the theorem come out true, rather than by some independent story about why this family of scoring rules (and not some other, equally natural-looking one) really tracks "closeness to the truth." That smells like begging the question. In my old line of work, we didn't get to choose a verification score because it flattered our favorite forecast model - a score earned its keep by being "proper" in an independently checkable sense, namely that a forecaster genuinely cannot raise their expected score by quoting anything other than their honest probability. That's a real, non-circular constraint. I can't tell yet whether "propriety" is doing that same honest work over in the epistemology literature, or whether it's just circularity wearing a nicer outfit.

So: does this objection actually land, or am I conflating "propriety" with some fuzzier, more contested condition that Joyces argument also needs? And is this a known soft spot in the whole accuracy-first program that I've just stumbled onto independently, or have I gone wrong somewhere more basic? Please don't go easy on me - everyone in my library group is very kind, but none of them can check my math, and I'd rather get corrected now than keep repeating a confused argument at every meeting for the next year.
