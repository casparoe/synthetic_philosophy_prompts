I've been re-reading Yudkowsky's proposal to aim a superintelligence at humanity's "coherent extrapolated volition" -- our wish "if we knew more, thought faster, were more the people we wished we were." It strikes me as the latest entry in an older philosophical lineage: the full-information (or ideal-advisor) theories of well-being associated with Richard Brandt and Peter Railton, on which your good is fixed by the desires of a fully informed, fully rational idealized version of yourself. That lineage runs into a serious problem, pressed hardest in David Sobel's 1994 paper on full-information accounts of well-being: judging between sufficiently different possible lives seems to require an advisor who has experientially lived through both, yet no single continuous agent can do so without thereby ceasing to be a version of you whose verdict carries any special authority over your actual choices.

Write a dialogue between a rationalist defending CEV-style idealization as an alignment target and a philosopher who presses Sobel's dilemma against CEV specifically. Neither should concede quickly; have each respond to the other's strongest actual move, so the exchange moves beyond an initial statement and rebuttal.
