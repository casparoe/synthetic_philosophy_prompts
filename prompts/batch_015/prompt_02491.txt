For my AP English Language and Composition class we spent a period doing a rhetorical analysis of Jonathan Edwards's sermon "Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God," and my teacher mentioned in passing that the same Edwards also wrote a huge philosophical treatise called Freedom of the Will (1754), defending the Calvinist view that every human choice is necessitated, against Arminian theologians who held that the will can determine itself independently of any prior cause. That offhand comment sent me down a rabbit hole over the weekend: I found the full text on a public-domain archive and got stuck on Part I, Section V, where Edwards lays out exactly what he means by "liberty" before he starts arguing for his actual position. I think I get the big picture — he wants to show that a choice can be completely necessitated by whatever motive appears strongest to the mind and still count as "free" in every sense that matters — but two specific moves in this excerpt have me stuck, and nobody I know can help:

"The plain and obvious meaning of the words Freedom and Liberty, in common speech, is The power, opportunity, or advantage, that any one has, to do as he pleases. Or in other words, his being free from hindrance or impediment in the way of doing, or conducting in any respect, as he wills. — And the contrary to Liberty, whatever name we call that by, is a person's being hindered or unable to conduct as he will, or being necessitated to do otherwise.

[...] For that which is possessed of no will, cannot have any power or opportunity of doing according to its will, nor be necessitated to act contrary to its will, nor be restrained from acting agreeably to it. And therefore to talk of Liberty, or the contrary, as belonging to the very Will itself, is not to speak good sense; if we judge of sense, and nonsense, by the original and proper signification of words. — For the Will itself is not an Agent that has a will: the power of choosing, itself, has not a power of choosing. That which has the power of volition is the man, or the soul, and not the power of volition itself. And he that has the Liberty of doing according to his will, is the Agent who is possessed of the Will; and not the Will which he is possessed of. We say with propriety, that a bird let loose has power and liberty to fly; but not that the bird's power of flying has a power and Liberty of flying.

[...] But one thing more I would observe concerning what is vulgarly called Liberty; namely, that power and opportunity for one to do and conduct as he will, or according to his choice, is all that is meant by it; without taking into the meaning of the word, any thing of the cause of that choice; or at all considering how the person came to have such a volition; whether it was caused by some external motive, or internal habitual bias; whether it was determined by some internal antecedent volition, or whether it happened without a cause; whether it was necessarily connected with something foregoing, or not connected. Let the person come by his choice any how, yet, if he is able, and there is nothing in the way to hinder his pursuing and executing his will, the man is perfectly free, according to, the primary and common notion of freedom."

My first confusion is about that last paragraph. Edwards says it makes no difference to a person's freedom whether the choice was caused by a motive, caused by "some internal antecedent volition," or "happened without a cause" at all — as long as nothing stops you from acting on it, you're "perfectly free." But I thought his whole project was to prove that choices are never uncaused, that they're always necessitated by the strongest motive. So why does he build a definition of freedom loose enough to also cover a completely uncaused choice? Is that a hole in his argument, or does that possibility simply not matter for what he's actually trying to establish? My second confusion is about the bird analogy right before it. He insists that, strictly speaking, only an agent (a man or a soul) can have "liberty," not a mere power like the will itself — you can't say a bird's power of flying has its own power and liberty of flying. But then isn't his own title, Freedom of the Will, exactly the kind of loose phrase his own argument says we shouldn't use? Is he being inconsistent, or is there a real reason this isn't actually a problem? I'd like to actually understand the logic behind both points rather than just get a modern paraphrase of the passage.
