I am a novelist currently working on a speculative novel, and for the past few weeks I have justified not writing a single new page of it by telling myself that I am "doing research" — which in practice has meant reading and re-reading Kurt Gödel's 1961 correspondence with his mother, Marianne, alongside Hao Wang's transcripts of his later conversations with Gödel. One strand of the book follows a present-day logician who must decide, on behalf of her dying father, whether to accept an offer from a company that performs whole-brain emulation: a high-resolution scan taken near the moment of death, from which a computational copy of the person is run indefinitely afterward, marketed quite openly as a technological afterlife. I would like her reasoning about this offer to be built on real philosophical material rather than the usual generic hand-wringing about whether an upload is "really you," which is why I keep returning to Gödel, one of the few serious thinkers of the last century who treated personal survival after death as something closer to a conclusion than an article of faith.

Here is the part I cannot work out for myself. Gödel's answer to his mother's question of whether they would have a Wiedersehen, a reunion, after death rested on the premise that the world is rationally and purposively ordered, so that a mind's development being cut off after a few decades of barely-begun growth would be senseless; he was explicit that this required materialism to be false, since a merely material world seemed to him incapable of that kind of purpose. Separately, in his 1951 Gibbs lecture he went further than the neutral either/or for which that lecture is usually cited — either the mind outstrips every finite machine or there are absolutely undecidable mathematical problems — and defended, as his own considered opinion, the first branch: that the human mind cannot be reduced to, or reproduced by, any finite mechanical procedure.

Taking those two commitments together, how do you think Gödel himself would have reasoned about whole-brain emulation? Would he have been willing to count a sufficiently faithful computational copy of a person as even a partial realization of the afterlife he described to his mother, or do his own premises commit him to treating such a copy as an elaborate replica of exactly the kind of purposeless material arrangement his argument was meant to rule out as the whole story? I would much rather have your own working-through of his actual premises, disagreements between them included, than a general survey of what contemporary philosophers say about mind uploading, since it is that specific reasoning I need in order to write my character's interior monologue honestly.
