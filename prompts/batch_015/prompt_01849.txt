I've been working through the epistemic foundations of game theory in a graduate seminar, and we recently spent a session on the centipede game and the backward induction paradox. The standard story feels reasonably clear to me: backward induction implies that both players should defect at the very first opportunity, even though almost any amount of continued play would leave both of them better off, and the experimental literature shows that real subjects very rarely play this way. What I'm stuck on is a more specific dispute among game theorists over whether the usual justification for backward induction — via "common knowledge of rationality" — is even a coherent premise in the first place.

As I understand it, the worry raised by Reny and Bicchieri, among others, runs roughly as follows: if common knowledge of rationality is supposed to hold throughout the game, then reaching any node beyond the very first should already be impossible, since a rational opponent, reasoning in exactly the way that generates the backward induction solution, would never let play continue that far to begin with. So once the game has actually reached some later node, doesn't the very question "what should a rational player do now, given common knowledge of rationality" become self-undermining, since the premise it invokes has arguably already been falsified by the fact that we got there? And yet Aumann has a well-known formal argument that common knowledge of rationality does entail the backward induction outcome regardless, a result that provoked real, substantive disagreement from people like Binmore and Stalnaker rather than settling the matter.

Could you walk me through, in fairly intuitive terms, where exactly this disagreement lies? Specifically, I'd like to understand: (a) why some theorists think common knowledge of rationality is inconsistent or ill-defined once off-path nodes are allowed for in a perfect-information game like this one; (b) how Aumann's argument is built so as to sidestep that problem, and what notion of "knowledge" or counterfactual belief it relies on to do so; and (c) your own sense of whether this is ultimately a disagreement about formal modeling choices — how to represent counterfactuals and off-path beliefs — or whether it points to something genuinely unresolved about the rational justification for backward induction itself.
