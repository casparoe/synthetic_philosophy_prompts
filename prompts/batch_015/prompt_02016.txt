I've been slowly working my way through an old Book of Common Prayer that belonged to my great-grandmother, mainly to track down some family christening records, and I ended up reading the Thirty-Nine Articles printed near the back purely out of curiosity. I have no background in theology or philosophy at all, so most of it is heavy going, but Article XIV, "Of Works of Supererogation," brought me to a full stop. It reads:

"Voluntary Works besides, over, and above God's Commandments, which they call Works of Supererogation, cannot be taught without arrogancy and impiety: for by them men do declare, that they do not only render unto God as much as they are bound to do, but that they do more for his sake, than of bounden duty is required: whereas Christ saith plainly, When ye have done all that are commanded to you, say, We are unprofitable servants."

Out of curiosity I looked up the word "supererogation" afterward, and discovered it is still a live term in moral philosophy, used for actions that go beyond what duty strictly requires. The example I kept running into was something like a bystander risking their life to pull a stranger from a burning car: an act that is admired as extraordinary without anyone thinking a bystander who simply walked away had done something wrong. That struck me as almost the reverse of what this Article seems to be saying, since it treats the very idea of doing "more" than what is required as a kind of arrogance, even impiety, rather than as something to be praised. I don't fully understand what specific practice the sixteenth-century reformers had in mind when they wrote this (I gather it has something to do with indulgences, monastic vows, or a "treasury of merit," but I'm hazy on the details and on why it mattered so much to them), and I don't have anyone nearby who could walk me through it. I'd appreciate it if you could first unpack the passage on its own terms: what exactly is being rejected, and why the reformers regarded it as so dangerous. Then, if possible, help me work out whether their objection has any real bearing on the modern secular concept of supererogation, or whether philosophers who use that word today are doing something conceptually quite different, so that the same term just happened to survive into two otherwise unrelated debates.
