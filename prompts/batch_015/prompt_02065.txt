I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, but a friend who's finishing her philosophy PhD has had me reading chunks of Hegel's Philosophy of Right with her over the past few months, and the section on war, in his discussion of the state's foreign relations (roughly §321–§340), is the part that's stuck with me most. Hegel's basic claim is that war is not an unfortunate accident that a sufficiently rational world would eventually engineer away: sovereign states have no common authority standing over them the way a court stands over individuals ("there is no Praetor to judge between states," as he puts it, while explicitly dismissing Kant's proposal for a League of Nations to secure perpetual peace), so when their wills genuinely conflict, war is the only possible resolution. More provocatively, he thinks periodic war is actually what keeps a nation's ethical life from going stale, since without it "corruption in nations would be the product of prolonged, let alone perpetual, peace." And crucially, what supplies war's positive ethical content for Hegel isn't the violence itself but courage: it is the individual soldier's willingness to sacrifice his own particular, finite existence for the sake of the state as a universal that makes war an expression of freedom rather than mere killing.

What surprised me is that Hegel had already thought about technology complicating this picture. In his discussion of courage, he notes that the invention of the gun changed warfare so that bravery stopped being primarily a matter of personal, individual heroics and became something more mechanical — the act of an interchangeable member of a larger whole rather than of any particular hero — and he's explicit that he doesn't see this as a corruption of true courage, but as continuous with, even required by, the spirit of the modern world.

That's exactly the tension I'd like help extending, because a few people I used to work with are now doing defense-related ML — targeting pipelines, autonomous drones — and modern warfare seems to be heading toward a limit case where the side with the technological edge risks no human lives at all, with lethal decisions carried out by systems that were never capable of fear to begin with. Separately, the great powers have maintained something close to the stagnating peace Hegel worried about for eighty-odd years now, not through any Kantian federation but through mutual nuclear terror, even though Hegel also thinks a legitimate war has to leave open the possibility of a subsequent peace, which a full nuclear exchange obviously would not. So: is full battlefield autonomy just one more turn of the same mechanization Hegel already accepted with gunpowder, such that a Hegelian could shrug and say it's the state's will and sovereignty at stake regardless of who or what pulls the trigger — or does removing every human from the loop cross some threshold his account of courage can't survive, since there's no longer anyone left to do the sacrificing? And would he count nuclear deterrence as exactly the stagnating peace he warned nations against, or does living under permanent threat of annihilation supply its own version of the mortal earnestness he thought only actual war could provide? I'd like you to actually commit to a position on these questions and defend it using his own arguments, rather than surveying both sides and leaving it there.
