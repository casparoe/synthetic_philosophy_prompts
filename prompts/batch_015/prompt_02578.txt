Okay so this happened during a hiring debrief at my job last week and it's been stuck in my head ever since. I'm one of three people on the panel for a new analyst role, and our rubric says we only extend an offer if the candidate clears BOTH the technical case-study round AND the "collaboration" round — no exceptions, it's literally written into the doc our team lead sent around. In the debrief we couldn't agree on a final call, so out of curiosity I tried tallying our individual scorecards two different ways. First I looked criterion by criterion: on the case study, 2 of the 3 of us (me and Priya) scored it a pass; on collaboration, 2 of the 3 of us (me and Marcus) scored it a pass. So majority passed both criteria, which by the rubric means we extend the offer. Then, just for comparison, I asked each of us to just state our own personal yes/no on hiring, given everything — and it came out 2-1 against, because Priya's one no (on collaboration) and Marcus's one no (on the case study) were each enough to sink their own individual vote. Same three scorecards, same three people, completely opposite recommendation depending on which way I added it up.

I brought this up with my friend Dana over beers afterward and we argued about it for like an hour. Her take is that this is basically just Simpson's paradox and there's no real puzzle — you just pick whichever voting convention your committee likes and move on. That answer feels to easy to me though, because it's not like anyone's numbers are being weighted differently, or there's some hidden subgroup skewing an average — its the exact same three people and the exact same three opinions, just sliced two different ways, and yet the committee ends up flatly contradicting itself about whether this candidate should be hired. So who's actually right — is Dana's diagnosis basically correct and I'm overthinking a total non-issue, or is there something else going on that actually explains the reversal? And if there is something else going on, is one of my two ways of tallying the genuinely "correct" one, or are we just stuck with the fact that a committee can contradict itself like this no matter how carefully each individual person reasons?
