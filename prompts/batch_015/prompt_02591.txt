I'm taking a seminar this term on the ethics of privacy, and one contrast that keeps coming up is between views that treat privacy as basically a matter of secrecy or of controlling who has access to information about you, and Helen Nissenbaum's contextual integrity framework, on which a privacy violation happens when information moves in a way that breaches the norms attached to the context it was originally shared in, whether or not that information is secret in any absolute sense.

I bring this up because something that happened in my friend group turned into a real disagreement about exactly this, between two people who had nothing to do with the original incident, and I haven't been able to work out who is right. Last fall, my friend Naomi told our mutual friend Priyanka, one-on-one, that she had started seeing a therapist for anxiety after a difficult breakup. A few weeks ago, when Naomi skipped a group dinner at the last minute, Priyanka mentioned in our group chat, which includes a few people outside our closest circle, that "Naomi's still dealing with some anxiety stuff," mainly to stop people from being annoyed that she had flaked. Naomi was upset when she found out. At a get-together last weekend, two other friends of mine, Deniz and Farrah, argued about whether Priyanka had actually violated Naomi's privacy, and I'd like to reproduce their exchange, since I think they were each onto something real.

(1) Farrah said Priyanka did nothing wrong: Naomi never asked her to keep it secret, seeing a therapist isn't shameful, and Naomi had already told two other people in the group and posted something vague about "working on myself" on Instagram, so Priyanka wasn't exposing some hidden fact. (2) Deniz replied that this misses the point: Naomi shared the information in a private, one-on-one conversation, and moving it into a group chat with people she's far less close with crossed from one context into another with different norms about who gets to know what, and that crossing is what makes it a violation, not the secrecy of the underlying fact. (3) Farrah countered that if crossing contexts is enough on its own, then a therapist who mentions a case at a dinner party without naming the patient, but with enough detail that a mutual acquaintance would recognize who it is, should count as violating that patient's privacy too, which seems far too strong; surely how widely something is already known has to matter somewhere. (4) Deniz granted that wider knowledge can lessen the damage a disclosure does, but denied that this changes whether it counts as a violation in the first place, since how bad something is and whether it happened at all are separate questions. (5) Farrah pushed back that this move makes the whole idea unfalsifiable: if any transfer of information can be called a "context violation" independent of its effects, Deniz owes an account of which transfers are actually out of bounds, rather than just asserting that this one was. (6) Deniz answered that not every transfer counts: what makes a transfer acceptable is whether it serves the purpose the information was shared for in the first place, and explaining Naomi's absence for Priyanka's own comfort in the group, rather than for Naomi's sake, doesn't qualify. Could you go through Farrah and Deniz's exchange move by move and say who has the better of it at each step, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'd also like to know what you think both of them are overlooking about what actually makes something a privacy violation in a case like this.
