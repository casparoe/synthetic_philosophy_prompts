Last month I was appointed as the public member of our county's Pretrial Risk Assessment Oversight Committee, filling a seat that opened up after another member resigned. The committee exists because our courts use a proprietary recidivism-risk tool built on the same basic approach as COMPAS, the instrument ProPublica investigated back in 2016: defendants are scored on a numeric scale, and judges consult that score, loosely, when setting bail or deciding on pretrial detention. I expected my first few meetings to be dry discussions of validation statistics, but at the second one, two other members got into a real argument about whether the tool is racially biased. The committee is due to vote on renewing the tool's contract later this year, so I'd like to actually get to the bottom of their disagreement rather than just sit through it again next time.

The disagreement was between Renata, a data scientist who audits the tool for the county, and Devon, a public defender who deals with its output at bail hearings every week. It went roughly like this. (1) Renata opened with the tool's calibration: among defendants who receive any given score, say a 7 out of 10, the actual reoffense rate is essentially the same for Black and white defendants. To her, that settles things — the score means the same thing regardless of race, so the instrument itself isn't the problem. (2) Devon countered with the statistic that made COMPAS famous in the first place: among defendants who did not go on to reoffend, Black defendants were flagged high-risk something like twice as often as white defendants, and among defendants who did reoffend, white defendants were far more likely to have been scored low-risk. Calibration, he said, is perfectly compatible with the tool routinely offloading its mistakes onto Black defendants, and that is what actually matters to someone sitting in jail because of a bad score. (3) Renata replied that this gap isn't evidence of anything sinister; it's a mechanical consequence of the two groups having different base rates of rearrest in the historical data. Build two separate, equally well-calibrated models for each group from the same legitimate risk factors, she said, and you get the same gap in false-positive and false-negative rates purely from the base-rate difference, with no animus anywhere in the pipeline. (4) Devon granted the math but said it doesn't do the work she wants it to: if you know in advance that calibrating will force an unequal burden of errors onto one group, choosing to calibrate anyway is still a choice about who absorbs that burden, and it needs its own justification rather than a shrug at the arithmetic. (5) Renata's closing move was that the alternative Devon seemed to want — equal false-positive and false-negative rates across the two groups — can only be achieved by applying a different score threshold for "high risk" depending on a defendant's race, which is a far more direct, and probably illegal, form of disparate treatment. So, she said, there is no neutral option available at all: you either accept unequal error rates or you accept race-conditioned thresholds, and choosing the former is at least the more defensible of two imperfect options.

Neither of them budged, and the meeting ended with the chair tabling the whole discussion for another session. Could you go through the exchange move by move? I would like a verdict on each of the five numbered moves — right, partly right, or mistaken, and why — rather than just an overall winner, and I'd also like to know what, if anything, both of them are missing about how these two notions of fairness relate to each other more generally, beyond this one tool.
