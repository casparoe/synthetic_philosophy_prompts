Over the past two years, my postdoctoral research has centered on glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) — an enzyme most molecular biologists know primarily as the humble glycolytic housekeeping protein routinely blotted as a loading control. Along the way I've become fascinated by its double life as one of the textbook examples of a "moonlighting protein": under various stress conditions, the very same polypeptide that oxidizes glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate in glycolysis translocates to the nucleus and takes part in processes such as base-excision repair of damaged DNA and the induction of apoptosis, with no obvious connection to carbon metabolism. I recently submitted a manuscript describing one such nuclear activity, and a reviewer objected to my describing it as "a function of GAPDH," arguing that only the glycolytic role deserves that label, since nothing in GAPDH's evolutionary history plausibly selected for the nuclear activity in the way natural selection shaped its catalytic site for glycolysis. On the reviewer's view, the DNA-repair activity is merely an incidental side effect of the protein's abundance and chemical promiscuity, not a genuine function at all.

Trying to work out whether the reviewer had a real point sent me down a rabbit hole into philosophy of biology, where it turns out this exact disagreement has a name: the dispute between "etiological" and "causal-role" theories of biological function. As best I can tell, one camp, tracing back to a 1970s paper by Larry Wright and later refined by philosophers such as Karen Neander and Ruth Millikan, ties a trait's function to its selection history, while the other, associated with Robert Cummins, defines a component's function purely in terms of its current causal contribution to some capacity of the system that contains it, with no reference to history at all. What I'd find genuinely useful is a clear, non-superficial explanation of what actually divides these two camps and why the choice is supposed to matter — this surely isn't meant to be just a verbal dispute over how to use the word "function" — together with an account of how each theory would actually rule on a case like GAPDH's moonlighting DNA-repair role. And if you have a view of your own: is there a principled way this kind of case gets adjudicated in current philosophy of biology, or should I conclude that my reviewer and I are simply working with two different, equally defensible notions of "function" that talk past one another?
