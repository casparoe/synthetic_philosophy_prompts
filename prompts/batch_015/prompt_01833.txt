Ok, random one from the ancient-phil/bioethics overlap. I've been buried in Aristotle's biology for a seminar paper and its wrecking my ability to take our current "how do we define death" debates at face value. Turns out Aristotle is fully cardiocentric: the heart houses the "common sense" and is the seat of perception and vital heat, while the brain's whole job is just to cool that heat down (a radiator, not a control center). Death, per the vital-heat treatise (On Youth and Old Age / On Life and Death), is basically the heart's fire finally going out -- which then plugs straight into his usual hylomorphism, soul as the form/actuality of the body, death as that form losing its grip on the matter.

So: if you dropped Aristotle into today's fight over whole-brain death vs. higher-brain/cortical death vs. straight-up circulatory-respiratory death (the stuff that flared back up with the whole Shewmon "brain death isn't real death" line of argument), which criterion does his own system actually commit him to? Does he have to bite the bullet for circulatory death since that's literally where he locates the soul's main activity, even though we now know the heart isn't doing any of that? Or does his more abstract picture of the soul as a bundle of functions (nutrition, perception, thought) let him detach from his bad 4th-century physiology and land on whole-brain death instead? Don't just hedge -- commit to a reading and defend it.
