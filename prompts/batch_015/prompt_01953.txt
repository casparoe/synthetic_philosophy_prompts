I'm writing a novel whose central crisis turns on a decision made by a field medic during a mass-casualty incident: she uses her last vial of antitoxin to cure one critically poisoned patient rather than dividing it into fifths to treat five others in a neighboring ward, each of whom could be saved with a smaller dose. Months later, she must defend that choice before a hospital inquiry, and I don't want her defense to read as mere sentiment — I want it to rest on a genuine philosophical position, in the spirit of Taurek's "Should the Numbers Count?" Please construct the strongest available argument that she was not morally required to let the larger number decide. It should explain why the separate losses of five distinct people cannot simply be summed and weighed against the loss of one; engage with Taurek's own suggestion that only a randomizing procedure, such as a coin flip, truly expresses equal concern for every person, and consider whether her non-randomized, split-second choice can still be defended on similar grounds; and directly answer the objection that this reasoning would seem to commit her to tossing a coin even if the choice had been one patient against fifty.
