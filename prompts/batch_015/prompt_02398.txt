So I'm a 3rd-year in a philosophy PhD (formal epistemology / decision theory track), and our reading group just spent two sessions on van Fraassen's old Reflection Principle from "Belief and the Will" and I honestly can't stop thinking about it. Quick recap in case its not fresh for you: Reflection says your current credence in some proposition A, conditional on being certain that your future self will assign credence r to A, should also be r — basically, rationality requires treating your own future (rational) self as an expert and deferring to them now, and van Fraassen backs this up with a diachronic Dutch Book argument.

The part thats been bugging me is the caveat: this only seems to work if you're confident the future update will actually be a rational one. Christensen and others have pushed hard on this, and there's a whole family of memory-loss counterexamples in the literature (Talbott's old "spaghetti" case is the classic one) where you're currently dead certain of something totally mundane, but you also know that a year from now you'll have forgotten it entirely and will assign it some low base-rate credence instead — and obviously you shouldn't discount your current certainty just because future-you will be ignorant. So van Fraassen basically needs a "no anticipated irrationality or memory loss" clause tacked on, but nobody seems to have a clean way of cashing that out from the inside — how do you know, right now, whether an anticipated future change in your beliefs counts as learning or as degradation?

My officemate joked that this is basically a short story premise waiting to happen, and now I want to see someone actually do it properly. Could you write me a short story (maybe 1000-1800 words) built around exactly that tension — a protagonist who gets certain, verified access to what they'll believe about something later (a letter from a future self, an oracle, a brain implant, a time loop, whatever mechanism you like), but who has independent reason to suspect that future belief might come from something compromising rather than genuine learning? I don't want a generic "can you trust future you" prophecy story — I want the actual dramatic and intellectual crux to be that Reflection-style dilemma, and honestly bonus points if you don't resolve it too neatly at the end, since I think the fact that van Fraassen's own caveat is so hard to pin down is the most interesting part.
