I'm a HPS (history and philosophy of science) PhD student and I TA an undergrad "Minds and Machines" seminar where the first reading every single year is Turing's 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence." Everyone remembers the imitation game itself, but almost nobody remembers that near the end he spends a whole page taking "the argument from extra-sensory perception" seriously — he says the statistical evidence for telepathy is basically overwhelming, worries a psychic contestant could unfairly beat the machine by reading the interrogator's mind, and proposes fixing this by running the whole test in a "telepathy-proof room." This year two students in my section (I'll call them Nina and Devon) got into a real argument about it that ran way past when I needed to move on, and I told them i'd think it over and follow up — so now I'm bringing it to you instead of admitting I never actually settled it in my own head.

(1) Nina's opening move was that this section should lower our confidence in Turing generally — if he was willing to call the evidence for telepathy "overwhelming," that's a red flag about his judgment on other empirical/statistical questions, maybe even the imitation-game methodology itself, and we shouldn't just wave it off as a weird one-off. (2) Devon shot back that this is basically chronological snobbery: Cambridge academic culture at the time took psychical research seriously as an open question (he brought up C. D. Broad, an actual Cambridge philosophy professor of that exact era who worked on it right alongside logic and metaphysics), so Turing wasn't being uniquely credulous, he was a pretty normal product of his intellectual milieu, and it's cheap to dock him for it with seventy years of hindsight. (3) Nina came back with: fine, grant all that, but there's a deeper problem Devon's letting him off the hook for — Turing doesn't just note ESP as a passing curiosity, he explicitly worries that "thinking is just the kind of phenomenon where E.S.P. may be especially relevant," and that sits really awkwardly next to his whole project of arguing thought could be pure mechanism, since if thinking is exactly where physics-defying causation sneaks in, that threatens to make thought non-mechanical after all. (4) Devon's last move was that this reads way too much into one hedge: Turing's only saying that if ESP exists at all, an information-guessing task is where you'd expect it to show up, not digestion or photosynthesis, which is just sensible scoping of an unconfirmed effect — and the fact that his proposed fix is an experimental control (the room) rather than a revision to his theory of mind shows he meant to neutralize the problem, not build it into his account of thinking.
