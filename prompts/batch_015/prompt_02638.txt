So I was up way too late last night arguing with my friend Jae about weakness of will and I need a tiebreaker. It started because I keep hitting snooze on my alarm even though past-me sets it specifically because morning-me can't be trusted, and Jae's whole position was that this is basically a non-mystery now — hyperbolic discounting explains it away completely, you just have a discount curve that overweights whatever's temporally closest, so 5am-me valuing ten more minutes of sleep over the abstract benefit of a productive day isn't "weakness," its just garden-variety time-inconsistent preferences, no real puzzle there. I said that can't be the whole story because it doesn't capture the specific first-person weirdness of the thing — like, in the moment, some part of me still seems to think I should get up even as another part wins out, and that feels different from just having different preferences at different times that happen to conflict.

Somehow this spiraled into us pulling up Aristotle at like 1am, because I remembered he spends a big chunk of Nicomachean Ethics Book VII arguing against Socrates about this exact issue — whether the "incontinent" (weak-willed) person really knows, in the moment, that what they're doing is bad. We found the passage but neither of us could actually untangle it. Here's the bit we got stuck on (W.D. Ross translation):

"And further (c) the possession of knowledge in another sense than those just named is something that happens to men; for within the case of having knowledge but not using it we see a difference of state, admitting of the possibility of having knowledge in a sense and yet not having it, as in the instance of a man asleep, mad, or drunk... The fact that men use the language that flows from knowledge proves nothing; for even men under the influence of these passions utter scientific proofs and verses of Empedocles, and those who have just begun to learn a science can string together its phrases, but do not yet know it; for it has to become part of themselves, and that takes time; so that we must suppose that the use of language by men in an incontinent state means no more than its utterance by actors on the stage. (d) Again, we may also view the cause as follows... The one opinion is universal, the other is concerned with the particular facts... When, then, the universal opinion is present in us forbidding us to taste, and there is also the opinion that 'everything sweet is pleasant', and that 'this is sweet' (now this is the opinion that is active), and when appetite happens to be present in us, the one opinion bids us avoid the object, but appetite leads us towards it... so that it turns out that a man behaves incontinently under the influence (in a sense) of a rule and an opinion, and of one not contrary in itself, but only incidentally — for the appetite is contrary, not the opinion — to the right rule."

Can you help me actually get clear on what's happening here? Specifically: (1) what work is the "asleep, mad, or drunk" comparison doing — how is "having knowledge in a sense" different from just being temporarily ignorant, (2) what's going on with the reciting-Empedocles/actors-on-a-stage bit, is Aristotle saying the incontinent person's "knowledge" is more like memorized lines than an actual belief, and (3) when he brings in the universal-premise-vs-particular-premise thing at the end, is that basically Aristotle inventing his own version of a discounting story (the general premise stays intact, it's the "particular, right-now, this-is-sweet" premise that gets hijacked by appetite), or is that reading modern behavioral econ into a passage that isn't doing anything of the sort? Jae thinks Aristotle basically anticipated hyperbolic discounting twenty-three centuries early and I think that's way too generous, so I'd love an actual referee here.
