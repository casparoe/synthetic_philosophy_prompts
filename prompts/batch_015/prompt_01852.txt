Six weeks ago I underwent a routine cardiac ablation that went wrong: my heart stopped for what the anesthesiologist afterward clocked at just under three minutes before the crash team got a rhythm back. I don't have anything as clean as a memory of pain, but I do have a compressed, disordered sequence of impressions from that gap that I've been reluctant to describe to anyone besides my partner, mostly because it sounds embarrassingly close to the stock near-death-experience narrative. Since then I've been reading everything I can find on the subject, which is how I stumbled back onto A. J. Ayer, a philosopher I encountered only briefly in an undergraduate course years ago. Ayer's own heart stopped for about four minutes in 1988 (he choked on smoked salmon while recovering from pneumonia, of all things), and he wrote the experience up in an article titled "What I Saw When I Was Dead" — then, a couple of months later, published an odd walk-back called "Postscript to a Postmortem," insisting that what the episode had shaken was not his belief that death is simply the end, but only, in his words, his "inflexible attitude" toward that belief. His own doctor, for what it's worth, apparently thought Ayer had been considerably more confused and oxygen-starved during the episode than the composed narrative in his article let on.

That distinction between shaking a belief and shaking one's attitude toward it is what I keep turning over, because it sits oddly with everything else Ayer stood for. He built his career on verificationism — the view that a claim is meaningful only insofar as we can say what observations would count for or against it — and used that principle to dismiss most talk of God and the soul as strictly senseless rather than merely false. On that view, I'd have expected him to say either that survival-of-death claims are meaningful empirical claims, in which case his experience is at least weak, contestable evidence and it would be irrational not to shift his credence a little; or that such claims remain exactly the kind of metaphysical claim his whole philosophy was built to dissolve, in which case no experience, however vivid, could be evidence one way or the other, and his hedge is just sentiment dressed up as philosophy. The middle position he actually staked out — unchanged belief, loosened attitude — doesn't obviously correspond to either option.

I'd like you to write me an essay that works through this properly rather than either mocking Ayer's wobble or treating it reverently. Reconstruct his verificationist grounds for treating talk of surviving death as suspect in the first place, then weigh whether his belief/attitude distinction is a genuine, defensible way of letting an experience like his exert some rational pressure without abandoning empiricism, or whether it's really an ad hoc face-saving move from a man who'd spent fifty years as Britain's most public atheist. Don't hedge your way to "it depends on your framework" — reach an actual verdict on whether a consistent verificationist in Ayer's position was entitled to be moved, even slightly, by what happened to him, and tell me what you think that verdict implies for how I should treat my own three minutes.
