I'm a software engineer, and over the last year or so I've fallen into decision theory as a hobby, mostly because a friend of mine who's finishing a philosophy PhD keeps sending me papers connected to the AI-safety/rationalist discourse around Newcomb's problem. The example I can't stop turning over is "Death in Damascus": a man learns he has an appointment with Death at midnight and can spend the night in either Damascus or Aleppo; Death is stipulated to be a reliable predictor of wherever the man ends up, and will already be waiting there. This is a classic problem case for causal decision theory (roughly, the view that you should pick whichever option has the best expected outcome given what your choice actually influences): whichever city the man settles on, his having settled on it is itself evidence that Death predicted that very city, so from where he's standing he always looks like he would have done better in the other place. Neither pure choice is stable under its own light — I gather this gets discussed under the heading of an option failing to be "ratifiable."

The standard way out that I keep running into is that the man should instead flip a coin and randomize between the two cities: provided Death can't predict the coin, this mixed strategy is the one policy that doesn't undercut itself once adopted, so it gets treated as the rational resolution. It reminds me of a fixed-point condition, or of a mixed-strategy Nash equilibrium in a matching-pennies game, from the one algorithmic game theory elective I took years ago. But something about the move bothers me: the entire bite of the thought experiment comes from Death being an outstanding predictor of the man's behavior — that is the whole source of the instability in the first place — and the fix seems to carve out a convenient, separately unargued-for exception exactly where the argument needs one, by declaring this same predictor suddenly helpless in front of a coin flip. I raised this with my friend, half expecting her to talk me out of it; instead she agreed the tension was real but had to catch a flight before we could work out whether it's a genuine problem or just a scope confusion on my part.

Could you construct the strongest version of the argument that randomizing does not actually rescue causal decision theory from the instability in Death in Damascus — that the coin-flip "solution" just relocates the same problem into an arbitrary asymmetry in what Death is allowed to foresee? Please don't stop at asserting that the asymmetry is arbitrary: engage with the most plausible reason someone might give for thinking it is principled (for instance, some genuine line between predicting an agent's deliberate choices and predicting the output of a physically chancy device like a coin), and explain concretely why that reason does or doesn't actually save the fix.
