Okay so I was up way too late last night arguing with my friend Alex about evidential cooperation in large worlds -- this all started because I sent him a podcast episode on weird decision theory stuff and he latched onto one idea from it. The basic idea (ECL for short) is that you should partly act on behalf of other value systems out there in the universe, even ones you can never causally contact, because your decision is evidentially correlated with similar agents elsewhere making similar decisions -- basically a trade thats enforced by correlation instead of a contract. Alex's whole counterargument was that this entire framework only works if the many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics is true, since branching MWI worlds are supposedly the only way you actually get all these correlated versions of yourself "out there" to cooperate with. And since MWI is just one interpretation of QM among several live options (Copenhagen, pilot wave, etc) that plenty of physicists reject, he said the whole ECL edifice is basically betting everything on one unproven, contested physics interpretation. On top of that he threw in that even granting MWI, all you'd get is copies of yourself in other branches, and a copy of you by definition makes the exact same choice you do -- so theres no real "cooperation" happening there, just a bunch of redundant agents doing what you were always gonna do anyway.

I didn't have a great comeback in the moment (it was like 2am and we'd both had a few beers) but it's been nagging at me since. Was Alex basically right that ECL falls apart if many-worlds turns out to be false, or is he missing something about how the "large worlds" part of the argument is actually supposed to work?
