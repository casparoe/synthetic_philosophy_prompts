So I fell down a rabbit hole reading old MIRI papers on 'tiling agents' and ran into this thing called the Löbian obstacle. From what I can tell: because of Löb's theorem, a formal system can't fully trust the proofs of a copy of itself (or a successor running on the exact same axioms) as being sound, unless it could already prove the thing directly — so an AI that only self-modifies when it can *prove* the successor is safe hits a wall even when the successor is identical to it. People have proposed workarounds, like making the successor strictly weaker, building an infinite tower of stronger and stronger systems, or swapping in probabilistic/logical-induction style reasoning instead of strict proofs, but I honestly can't tell if those actually solve the problem or just kick it down the road.

Can you write me a dialogue between two AI safety researchers hashing this out at a whiteboard? One thinks the Löbian obstacle points to something real and basically unavoidable about the limits of self-trust for any self-improving system. The other thinks its more of a solvable engineering problem — an artifact of demanding a way stronger notion of trust than any real system, human or AI, actually has. I want them to really engage with each others specific points back and forth, not just restate their own view, and neither should obviously win — I'd like to end up genuinely torn on it.
