I'm putting together a session on Frege for a graduate seminar on the origins of analytic philosophy, and some recent reading has unsettled a question I thought I had already worked out for myself. Everyone in the seminar already knows the broad outline: in 1954, while going through what survived of Frege's Nachlass, a young Michael Dummett came across a diary Frege had kept for a few weeks in the spring of 1924, and was dismayed to discover that the philosopher he would spend much of his subsequent career establishing as a founding figure of analytic philosophy wanted Jews stripped of political rights and, ideally, expelled from Germany, wanted women and socialists to lose the franchise as well, and voiced agreement with the views of Ludendorff and, in passing, even Hitler -- this at the very moment, that same spring, when both men were standing trial for treason over the Beer Hall Putsch. Dummett said as much, briefly and unhappily, in the introduction to Frege: Philosophy of Language in 1973; the diary itself was not published until the 1990s, first in German and then, in Richard Mendelsohn's translation, in the journal Inquiry.

What I had not appreciated until recently is how much further this goes. In a piece previewing his forthcoming book Frege and Fascism, Stephen D'Arcy argues that Frege's political writings -- the 1924 diary, but also an earlier memorandum on electoral reform -- do not merely vent private prejudice; they press his own technical apparatus into its service. Frege apparently reasons about the logic of genealogical sequences (a topic descending from his own treatment, in the Begriffsschrift, of the ancestral of a relation) to justify preferential treatment on grounds of descent, and worries, in terms continuous with his lifelong insistence that a properly defined concept must have sharp boundaries, that vague racial predicates would make antisemitic legislation unworkable. He also asked his son to preserve these writings, confident that posterity would value them more than his contemporaries did. Not every Frege scholar reads the material this way: Mendelsohn's own preface to the Inquiry translation dismisses an earlier attempt by Hans Sluga to treat Frege's logic and his politics as of a piece, calling it tendentious and unfair.

Against that background, here is what I would like from you. Please write me an essay of roughly 1,200 to 1,600 words that weighs this evidence and defends a specific verdict on the following question: does Frege's political writing -- and especially the fact that he seems to have reached for his own logical machinery in its service -- give us genuine philosophical reason to reassess his logical work itself (its motivations, its claim to topic-neutrality, perhaps even doctrines like the demand for sharply-bounded concepts), or is the connection, in the end, biographically damning but philosophically inert, the way a mathematician's private vices need not infect their theorems? You may find it useful to test your answer against the case of Heidegger, where the corresponding question is usually taken to have a different, or at least a more straightforwardly affirmative, answer, and to say something about why the two cases are alike or unlike. Please do not retreat into a non-committal wait-and-see conclusion: I want an actual verdict, argued for.
