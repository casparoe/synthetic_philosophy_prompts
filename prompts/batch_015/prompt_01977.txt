Since retiring from a career in experimental physics, I have been working my way through Helmholtz's *Treatise on Physiological Optics* — a text I had previously associated only with thermodynamics and electromagnetism, not with the psychology of vision. In the third volume he introduces what he calls "unconscious inference," and defends the term this way:

"The psychic activities that lead us to infer that there in front of us at a certain place there is a certain object of a certain character, are generally not conscious activities, but unconscious ones. In their result they are equivalent to a conclusion […]. But what seems to differentiate them from a conclusion, in the ordinary sense of that word, is that a conclusion is an act of conscious thought. […] Still it may be permissible to speak of the psychic acts of ordinary perception as unconscious conclusions, thereby making a distinction of some sort between them and the common so-called conscious conclusions."

A few pages on, discussing why a setting sun still appears to slide behind a motionless horizon, he adds that this happens even though "we are well aware that the sun is fixed and the horizon moves," and that no amount of astronomical knowledge dispels the impression.

I cannot work out how these two claims are meant to sit together. An inference, on any ordinary understanding of the word, is answerable to its premises: revise what someone believes, and a properly functioning inference ought to revise its conclusion too. Yet Helmholtz's "unconscious conclusions" are, by his own account, indifferent to exactly the beliefs that would have to inform them if the analogy with inference were doing real explanatory work, rather than merely dressing a brute causal mechanism in logical vocabulary. Is there a principled way, either within Helmholtz's own text or in how later writers on the cognitive penetrability of perception have refined his picture, to preserve the word "inference" here without letting it collapse into a mere figure of speech?
