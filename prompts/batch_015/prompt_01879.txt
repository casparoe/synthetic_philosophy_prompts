Jeff McMahan's revisionist account of the ethics of war holds that liability to be killed in war tracks moral responsibility for contributing to an unjustified lethal threat, so that combatants who fight for an unjust cause are not, in general, the moral equals of those who fight for a just one. Traditionalists have replied with an epistemic argument: since ordinary soldiers are rarely in a position to know, with anything like the confidence this picture requires, whether their side's cause is just, it is unfair to treat them as individually liable on that basis, and something like the old doctrine of the moral equality of combatants should still govern how we judge and treat combatants in practice. Write an essay assessing whether this epistemic argument succeeds in rehabilitating combatant equality as a norm for practical judgment, even granting that McMahan is right about the deep morality of killing in war. Engage with McMahan's own reply -- that the argument conflates the excusability of unjust combatants with the justifiability of what they do -- and weigh the strongest considerations on each side before closing with a definite, defended verdict rather than a mere summary of the dispute.
