Random thing I can't stop chewing on: I was covering a panel on evolution and ended up at the bar afterward with a postdoc who studies microbial cooperation and a philosopher of biology who'd been in the audience, and the two of them got into it about units of selection. Couldn't referee it in the moment, so here's roughly how it went if you want a crack at it.

(1) He opened with the standard line that the fight's over: Hamilton's rule and kin selection explain cooperation fine, group selection almost never wins because variation within groups swamps variation between them, so Dawkins was basically right and old-school, Wynne-Edwards-style group selection deserved to die.

(2) She said that only kills naive group selection — the modern version (she kept citing Sober and Wilson) uses the Price equation to split total selection into within- and between-group components, and it's been shown that kin selection and multilevel selection are mathematically equivalent, just two bookkeeping schemes for the same process. So this isn't an empirical fight anymore, it's an accounting choice.

(3) He said fine, but then she doesn't get to also say this "vindicates" the old group selectionists over Dawkins — either something's really being selected at the group level, or it's bookkeeping, and if it's bookkeeping nobody was vindicated because there was nothing to be right about.

(4) She said not all bookkeeping is equally arbitrary: the two schemes always sum to the same total, sure, but when there's real assortment — trait-groups, or transitions like eusociality — the group-level partition tracks seperate, manipulable variables (group size, assortment) that the gene's-eye version only redescribes after the fact. So it's not symmetric.

Neither budged and I lost track of who was actually ahead. Can you go through it move by move, say who's more right at each step (not just an overall winner), and flag anything both of them are missing? A one-line bottom-line at the end would help too.
