For my Bentham seminar this term (its basically a whole semester of just him, pre-Mill) we got assigned this strange unpublished essay from around 1785 called "Offences Against One's Self," where he goes through the legal case against what he calls "paederasty" — consensual sex between men, a hanging offense in England at the time — and tries, seemingly in good faith, to find a utilitarian ground for punishing it. He can't: no real pain to the parties involved (it's consensual and by his lights pleasurable), no real alarm to the public, no real threat to population, so he concludes the whole edifice rests on bare antipathy, which he then compares directly to the antipathy that got Moors and Jews burned alive elsewhere in Europe. Apparently he just sat on the essay his whole life, too scared of the fallout to publish it (some scholar finally transcribed the manuscript in the 20th century) — kind of wild that this was sitting in the same drawer as the felicific calculus.

Two people in my discussion section, Nate and Sofia, got into a whole thing about how "progressive" this essay actually is, and I've been chewing on it since. Nate's position: this is basically a proto-gay-rights document. Bentham runs the exact same mischief-based calculus on this "offence" as on everything else, concludes the law is propped up by nothing but prejudice, and even says that calling the desire unnatural just because it isn't necessary for survival is no better than saying "the taste a man has for music is unnatural" — i.e. he's putting it on the same footing as any other harmless preference. Nate also thinks this is just Bentham applying, to one hot-button case, the same takedown of antipathy as a legitimate ground for law that he gives more abstractly elsewhere in his work. Sofia pushed back: sure, but that's not the only register the essay is written in — Bentham also can't stop himself calling the same desire a depraved, corrupted, miserable taste, and elsewhere an eccentric, unnatural, even preposterous propensity, and he says at one point he can't imagine any man who really appreciates women ever seriously worrying that boys could rival them. Her point was that Bentham never argues the preference is equally as good, only that even a low, contemptible taste doesn't deserve hanging — which is a much thinner, more backhanded kind of tolerance then Nate was crediting him with, more "still disgusting, but not a capital case" than real acceptance. Nate's comeback was that we shouldn't confuse Bentham's era-bound personal reflexes with what the argument itself establishes, which does real egalitarian work regardless of his private squeamishness, the same way we don't usually say Mill's feminism is worthless because of his own occasional Victorian hedging. Sofia said that move is too convenient: you can't just sort the bits you like into "the real argument" and the bits you don't into "mere reflex" — if the argument itself only ever gets you to "don't punish it" and never to "this is just as legitimate," that's a substantive limit on what the essay achieves, not a stylistic tic we get to bracket off.

Neither of them budged and I honestly couldn't tell you who won. Could you go through the exchange move by move — including both comebacks — and say who's got the better of it at each point, rather than just declaring an overall winner? And then say what you think is the most defensible reading of the essay once you've weighed both sides: is Bentham's tolerance here closer to genuine equal standing, closer to a bare "don't kill people over this," or does the essay just not settle that question one way or the other?
