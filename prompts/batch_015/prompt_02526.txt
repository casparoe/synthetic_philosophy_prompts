A friend of mine, who is finishing a doctorate in philosophy, and I spent most of last night arguing about something that began almost by accident. I had mentioned a walled garden I once stumbled across on an abandoned estate, completely overgrown and, as far as I could tell, unseen by anyone for years, and remarked that it seemed a little tragic for so much beauty to exist with no one around to appreciate it. My friend pushed back by invoking G. E. Moore's old argument in Principia Ethica: he asks us to compare two worlds, each existing with absolutely no mind anywhere to perceive it — one as beautiful as we can imagine, full of mountains, rivers, and sunsets, the other simply a heap of filth — and asks whether the beautiful world's existence isn't nonetheless better than the ugly one's. Moore thought the answer was obviously yes, and this "isolation" reasoning — imagining a thing stripped of every relation, including any relation to a mind that might experience it, and asking whether its existence would still be good — is the same method he later uses to argue that aesthetic appreciation and personal affection are the two greatest goods we know. Where we got stuck was on whether the argument generalizes across those two goods equally. If isolation vindicates unwitnessed beauty, my friend insisted, it should equally vindicate "unwitnessed" affection: silently admiring someone's integrity, say, even if they never know it, never reciprocate it, and it never affects how I treat them, ought to count as good in exactly the same way, purely in virtue of existing. That struck me as wrong, though I couldn't say why — especially once I recalled that Moore, when he actually discusses personal affection at length, seems to insist that its value depends heavily on the admired qualities being bound up with a person's expressed, embodied presence (their words, looks, actions), rather than with a purely private appreciation of their character in the abstract.

I'd like an essay that actually adjudicates this instead of just listing considerations on each side. First, weigh whether Moore's isolation argument is any good at all: does it really show that beauty can have value independent of any experience of it, or does it quietly trade on an intuition it hasn't earned? Second, and this is the part my friend and I could never settle: does whatever conclusion you reach about beauty carry over to personal affection, or is there a principled disanalogy between the two goods — perhaps rooted in Moore's own remarks about embodiment, or in his broader idea of "organic unities" — that lets the isolation test succeed for one and fail for the other? Please take a definite position on both questions and argue for it; "it depends" is not an acceptable place to end up, though your route to a conclusion should engage seriously with the strongest considerations on both sides.
