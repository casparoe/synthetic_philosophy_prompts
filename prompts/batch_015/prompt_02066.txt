I've spent a lot of time reading Less Wrong and adjacent rationalist blogs, and one thing that comes up periodically in discussions of updateless and functional decision theory is the idea that an agent's rationality should be cashed out in terms of what it's disposed to output across a whole range of possible inputs, including counterfactual ones it never actually receives. That got me curious about how professional metaphysicians treat the concept of a disposition in the first place, and I ended up reading around the older literature on the "conditional analysis" of dispositions -- the idea that calling a glass fragile is just shorthand for saying it would break if struck. Apparently this simple analysis ran into two importantly different families of counterexample: one pressed by C.B. Martin and David Lewis in a pair of 1990s Philosophical Quarterly papers, involving what got called "finks," and another due to Mark Johnston and Alexander Bird, involving "masks" (Bird calls them "antidotes").

As far as I can piece together, a finkish case is one where the very stimulus that's supposed to test the disposition would itself switch the disposition on or off before it has a chance to manifest -- the standard image is a sorcerer who dotes on a fragile glass and, an instant before it's struck, casts a spell changing its molecular structure so that it won't break, so "it would break if struck" comes out false even though the glass really is fragile. A masking case is supposed to be a different animal entirely: the disposition itself is left completely untouched, and something merely external blocks its manifestation -- the same glass, now just packed in bubble wrap instead of enchanted, is still every bit as fragile, it simply won't break when dropped because the wrapping absorbs the shock; similarly, a dose of poison stays just as poisonous when swallowed together with its antidote. People in this literature treat the distinction between removing a disposition and merely blocking its manifestation as hugely important for whether the conditional analysis can be repaired -- apparently some repairs handle finks just fine while failing completely on masks -- though I don't have a solid, gut-level sense of why that asymmetry should hold.

Could you write me a dialogue between two philosophers working through this over coffee after a seminar? Call one of them the Reductionist, who still thinks some conditional (or near-conditional) analysis of dispositions can be salvaged, and the other the Primitivist, who takes the fink and mask cases together to show that dispositions, or causal powers, can't be reduced to conditionals at all and have to be taken as metaphysically basic. I'd like the Reductionist to actually propose a specific repair to the simple analysis in light of the glass cases, the Primitivist to press back with a real counterexample or objection rather than just reasserting that reduction is hopeless, and the Reductionist to get at least one good reply in before things wind down. Both should be equally sharp and should genuinely respond to what the other just said rather than delivering prepared speeches past each other -- and given that this dispute doesn't actually have a tidy resolution in the literature, it's fine, even preferable, for the dialogue to end without one.
