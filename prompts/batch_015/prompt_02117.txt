I'm writing a historical novel that includes a scene in which a fictionalized Isaac Newton, late at night in his Trinity College rooms, walks a skeptical friend through the private textual scholarship he never dared publish in his lifetime. I want the dialogue to compress a real piece of his reasoning without falsifying it, and I'm stuck on the logical mechanics of one move. The material comes from his 1690 letter to John Locke, published only posthumously (1754) as "An Historical Account of Two Notable Corruptions of Scripture," where Newton argues that 1 John 5:7 (the "three heavenly witnesses": "the Father, the Word, and the Holy Ghost: and these three are one") was a later scribal insertion, and that the original text ran straight from verse 6 into verse 8 ("the Spirit, and the water, and the blood: and these three agree in one"). Here is the passage, lightly stripped of its footnote markers:

"These places of Cyprian being in my opinion genuine seem so apposite to prove the testimony of the three in heaven, that I should never have suspected a mistake in it, could I but have reconciled it with the ignorance I meet with of this reading in the next age amongst the Latines of both Afric & Europe as well as amongst the Greeks. For had it been in Cyprian's Bible, the Latines of the next age, when all the world was engaged in disputing about the Trinity, could never have been ignorant of a text which, in our age, now the dispute is over, is chiefly insisted upon. In reconciling this difficulty I consider therefore that the only words of the text quoted by Cyprian in both places are, And these three are one: which words may belong to the eighth verse as well as to the seventh. For Eucherius, Bishop of Lion in France & contemporary to S. Austin, reading the text without the seventh verse, tells us that many then understood the Spirit, the Water & the Blood to signify the Trinity. And S. Austin is one of those many, as you may see in his third book against Maximus, where he tells us that the Spirit is the Father, for God is a spirit; the water the Holy Ghost, for he is the water which Christ gives to them that thirst; and the blood the Son, for the Word was made flesh. Now if it was the opinion of many in the western Churches of those times that the spirit, the water & the blood signified the Father, the Son, & the Holy Ghost, its plain that the testimony of the three in heaven in expresse words was not yet crept into their books, & even without this testimony it was obvious for Cyprian, or any man else of that opinion, to say of the Father & Son & Holy Ghost: it is written, And these three are one."

Cyprian appears to quote the disputed verse almost word for word, yet Newton treats this as no evidence at all for its authenticity. Could you reconstruct, step by step, the inference by which he neutralizes this apparent quotation, and spell out exactly what would have to be true of Cyprian's actual text and beliefs for Newton's alternative reading to work? I'd also value your honest judgment of whether this is a genuinely tight piece of reasoning from evidence, or a plausible conjecture with a real gap in it somewhere -- I don't want to hand my character an argument that's cleverer than it actually is.
