I am preparing a graduate seminar on philosophies of education, and one passage in Whitehead's 1922 address "The Rhythm of Education" (later incorporated into The Aims of Education and Other Essays) keeps resisting my best efforts at a charitable reading. Here is the passage:

"I think that Hegel was right when he analysed progress into three stages, which he called Thesis, Antithesis, and Synthesis; though for the purpose of the application of his idea to educational theory I do not think that the names he gave are very happily suggestive. In relation to intellectual progress I would term them, the stage of romance, the stage of precision, and the stage of generalization.

The stage of romance is the stage of first apprehension. The subject-matter has the vividness of novelty; it holds within itself unexplored connexions with possibilities half-disclosed by glimpses and half-concealed by the wealth of material. In this stage knowledge is not dominated by systematic procedure. Such system as there must be is created piecemeal ad hoc. We are in the presence of immediate cognizance of fact, only intermittently subjecting fact to systematic dissection. Romantic emotion is essentially the excitement consequent on the transition from the bare facts to the first realizations of the import of their unexplored relationships.

The stage of precision also represents an addition to knowledge. In this stage, width of relationship is subordinated to exactness of formulation. It is the stage of grammar, the grammar of language and the grammar of science. It proceeds by forcing on the students' acceptance a given way of analysing the facts, bit by bit. New facts are added, but they are the facts which fit into the analysis. It is evident that a stage of precision is barren without a previous stage of romance: unless there are facts which have already been vaguely apprehended in their broad generality, the previous analysis is an analysis of nothing.

The final stage of generalization is Hegel's synthesis. It is a return to romanticism with added advantage of classified ideas and relevant technique. It is the fruition which has been the goal of the precise training. It is the final success."

What I can't reconstruct is what genuine work the Hegelian labels are doing. Whitehead asserts the mapping (romance/thesis, precision/antithesis, generalization/synthesis), but nowhere in the essay does he actually argue for it, and he even admits he does not think Hegel's own names are "very happily suggestive" for educational theory—which suggests he is uneasy with the fit himself even as he keeps the triadic structure. Yet on his own description, precision doesn't sound like a negation of romance at all; it sounds like an additional constraint laid over the same material (it "also represents an addition to knowledge"). And generalization is glossed as "a return to romanticism," not as anything that sublates a prior opposition. So is there a defensible sense in which precision opposes or negates romance rather than merely supplementing it, and in which generalization does the work of a synthesis rather than just a happy homecoming? Or is Whitehead simply borrowing Hegel's prestige for a threefold pattern that a plain notion of "stages of increasing organization" would capture just as well, without any dialectical machinery? I would like your own best reconstruction of the strongest version of Whitehead's position, not just a paraphrase of the three stages.
