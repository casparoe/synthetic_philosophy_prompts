I'm plotting a scene for a novel in which a character who has spent her career on Hegel is pressed at a dinner party to say what he would actually have thought about nuclear deterrence — not the usual caricature of him as a philosopher who simply relished war. I want her answer to hold up, so before I write it I'd like your own best reconstruction, argued from Hegel's actual position on war and international relations in the Philosophy of Right rather than from his general reputation. Here is the specific tension I want her to confront: Hegel thought a long, let alone a genuinely permanent, peace was corrosive to the "ethical health of nations" (§324), and he rejected Kant's proposal for a federation of states as a real solution to conflict between sovereign states that recognize no common judge over them — yet the major nuclear powers have now maintained something like a permanent peace among themselves for the better part of a century, for the specific reason that a war between them would be mutual annihilation rather than any kind of trial of courage or comparative strength. Would Hegel, arguing consistently from his own premises, have to call this the very stagnation he warned against, some strange fulfillment of his logic by other means, or a case that simply falls outside what his concept of war can handle? I'd like the reasoning itself, not just a label.
