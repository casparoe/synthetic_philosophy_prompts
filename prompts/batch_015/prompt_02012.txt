A friend and I spent a long evening a few days ago arguing about the moral status of the Hiroshima and Nagasaki bombings, and neither of us really persuaded the other. My friend's position was a kind of scrupulous consequentialism: that deliberately killing civilians can be justified, or at least excused, when the alternative is a sufficiently greater catastrophe, and that the projected casualties of an invasion of Japan made 1945 plausibly such a case. I kept coming back to Anscombe's argument in "Mr. Truman's Degree" and "War and Murder" — that intentionally killing the innocent as a means to an end is murder regardless of the consequences, and that Truman's decision was exactly this, not a case of merely foreseen collateral harm protected by the doctrine of double effect. Trying to find some middle ground, I brought up Michael Walzer's notion of a "supreme emergency," since I remembered that although Walzer thinks Britain's 1940 bombing campaign might be excused as a response to a genuinely existential threat, he apparently denies that the same reasoning saves Truman's decision, on the grounds that Japan's defeat was already close to assured by 1945 and the U.S. faced nothing like Britain's predicament. We couldn't agree on what to make of this, and I'd like a more rigorous take than we managed.

Could you compare these two ways of condemning the bombings? On one side, Anscombe's route: an exceptionless prohibition on intentionally killing the innocent, grounded in the intention/foresight distinction at the heart of double effect, such that no calculation of lives saved could have made the bombings permissible even in principle. On the other, Walzer's route: a framework that does allow the prohibition on targeting civilians to be overridden in a genuine supreme emergency, but which judges that 1945 simply didn't meet that threshold. I'm especially interested in whether these amount to fundamentally different structures of moral reasoning — an exceptionless rule versus a defeasible one with an extremely high bar — or whether they're closer than they look once you notice that both treat the killing of civilians as needing a justification that ordinary wartime necessity can never supply. Concretely: is there a realistic case in which Walzer's doctrine would actually permit what Anscombe's absolutely forbids, or does his own reasoning about what counts as a "supreme emergency" end up doing the same work as Anscombe's absolutism in every case that could plausibly arise? Please justify your answer by engaging with the internal logic of each view rather than just your own intuitions about the bombings themselves.
