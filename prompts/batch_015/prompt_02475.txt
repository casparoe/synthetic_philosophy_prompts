So in my medieval philosophy course we just started the unit on the Arabic/Islamic tradition and this week was all about Avicenna's psychology, specifically the "flying man" argument. Quick recap in case it's useful context: you're supposed to imagine a person created instantaneously as a fully grown adult, just floating in mid-air, limbs spread out so nothing is touching (not even his own limbs touching each other), and totally deprived of sight, so he's got zero sensory input, not even of his own body. Avicenna's claim is that even in this state the guy would still be aware that he exists, aware of his "self" (or whatever the actual Arabic term is, I'm blanking), even though he wouldn't be aware of having a body at all. From that he wants to draw a conclusion about the soul being distinct from the body.

My prof mentioned offhand that there's actually a real fight in the secondary literature over what this argument is supposed to establish -- like, is it a genuine metaphysical proof of dualism (soul = immaterial substance, really distinct from body), or is it way more modest than that, just showing that self-awareness is immediate and doesn't depend on awareness of the body at all? Apparently theres a whole named objection to the strong reading, the "masked man fallacy" (something like: me not knowing that x = y doesnt mean x isnt y, so the flying man failing to recognize his body as himself doesn't actually show that his self and his body are distinct), and one of our assigned readings (an older article, I think by a guy named Marmura?) spends a ton of time wrestling with exactly this.
