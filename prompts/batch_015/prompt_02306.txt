In my seminar on decision theory and self-locating belief, we covered Piccione and Rubinstein's "absent-minded driver": to get home, a driver must exit at the second of two highway intersections that look identical to her, with no memory of which she's already passed. Exiting at the first is worth 0, at the second worth 4, and missing both (a night at a motel) worth 1. Since she can't tell the intersections apart, her strategy is a single probability p of exiting at either one; the textbook answer is that the ex-ante optimal p is 1/3, worth 4/3 in expectation. Two classmates, Nadia and Owen, argued afterward about what this means once she's actually driving.

(1) Nadia: sitting at an intersection, not knowing which, the two look identical, so by indifference she should assign 1/2 to each. Given the "optimal" plan of exiting with probability 1/3, a 1/2–1/2 credence makes exiting now worth 0.5(0)+0.5(4) = 2, while continuing and following the 1/3 rule thereafter is worth only 0.5[(1/3)(4)+(2/3)(1)]+0.5(1) = 1.5. So in the moment she'd seemingly do better abandoning the very plan that's supposed to be optimal — despite no new evidence and no change in preferences.

(2) Owen: the 1/2 is unearned. She only reaches the second intersection if she didn't already exit at the first, which happens 2/3 of the time under the 1/3 rule, so the two aren't equally likely conditional on being at one — the ratio should be 3:2. Credence has to be derived from the policy, not assumed independently of it.

(3) Nadia: that's just choosing whatever credence rationalizes the policy you wanted anyway. If "correct" belief means "whatever makes the plan look consistent," the standard has no bite — it could rescue any policy at all.

(4) Owen: not any policy. Redo the sums with the 3:2 credence (3/5, 2/5) and exiting now is worth exactly 8/5 — identical to continuing. No temptation to deviate. Try the same move on "always exit" or "always continue" and each collapses: the belief it implies recommends a different action than the one assumed. Only the 1/3 rule reproduces itself, so the inconsistency Nadia describes comes from an unmotivated 1/2, not from anything wrong with planning under imperfect recall.

Neither budged. Go through moves (1)-(4) and say who has the better of it at each step, working the arithmetic where the disagreement turns on it, and say what, if anything, both are still missing.
