Having followed rationalist blogs and forums for a long time, I've noticed that van Fraassen's Reflection Principle (from his 1984 paper "Belief and the Will") and Yudkowsky's "Conservation of Expected Evidence" (from the Sequences) tend to get treated as though they were the same insight. Van Fraassen's principle says, roughly, that if you are now certain your credence in P at some future time will equal x, your current credence in P conditional on that supposition must already equal x — and this is meant to hold no matter how that future credence comes about. Yudkowsky's principle, derived from the law of total probability, claims only that your expected posterior must equal your prior when the future credence results from conditionalizing on evidence. Compare the two in some depth: is Reflection simply this Bayesian result restated in more general, temporal terms, or does its indifference to the source of the future credence make it a substantially stronger and more easily refuted claim? Illustrate the difference with a case where credence shifts for a reason other than rational updating — anticipated memory loss or persuasion by a bad argument, say — and state which principle better survives it.
