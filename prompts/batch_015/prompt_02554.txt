I spent my career as an experimental physicist, and in retirement I have been working through the literature on formal epistemology. I recently came across Bas van Fraassen's "Judy Benjamin problem," and -- perhaps because of a lifetime spent with the thermodynamic sense of the word "entropy" -- I cannot decide what to make of it.

Here is the setup. Judy's platoon is dropped into a region she believes is divided into four equally likely zones: Red Headquarters, Red Second Company, Blue Headquarters, and Blue Second Company. Just before the radio dies, headquarters manages to convey only this: conditional on her being in Red territory, it is three times as likely that she is in the Headquarters zone as in the Second Company zone. Nothing at all is said about either Blue zone.

The natural first thought is that this message is simply irrelevant to the Blue zones, so Judy's credence that she is in (safe) Blue territory should stay fixed at one half. But if she updates by minimizing cross-entropy (relative entropy) against her prior, subject to just that one constraint on the Red conditional odds, her credence in Blue rises above one half -- which chimes with van Fraassen's own suspicion that the shift looks like formalized wishful thinking. That seems a serious strike against cross-entropy minimization as a rule for absorbing conditional evidence. Yet the "irrelevance" intuition has a problem of its own: push the stated ratio to its limit, so that the message becomes equivalent to simply ruling out one of the two Red zones altogether, and plain conditionalization on that fact does move Judy's credence in Blue -- to two-thirds. So the very invariance we wanted to insist on breaks down exactly where the correct update rule should be least controversial.

Please write an essay on how Judy should revise her credence in the Blue zones, and, more generally, on whether minimizing cross-entropy is a defensible way of incorporating conditional information of this kind into one's beliefs. Weigh the considerations on both sides, but reach and defend a definite verdict rather than leaving the matter open.
