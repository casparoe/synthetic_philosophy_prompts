So a few weeks back I fell down this random rabbit hole reading about Kurt Gödel (the incompleteness theorem guy) and it turns out in 1961 he actually wrote a bunch of letters to his mom laying out a real argument for why there has to be an afterlife. The gist is: the world is obviously rational and orderly (he thought science basically proves this), and since almost everyone's life gets cut short before they finish developing whatever potential they had, it would be flat out irrational for a rational universe to just let all that go to waste - so there has to be some "next round" where it gets sorted out, what he called in German a "Wiedersehen," basically a seeing-each-other-again. He even lists, as one of his own personal points, that there are other worlds out there with rational beings "of a higher kind" than us. Seperately, ten years earlier in a 1951 lecture, he used his own incompleteness theorem to argue that the human mind can't just be a machine running fixed rules, like no algorithm could ever fully capture everything a human mind can do.

What's been bugging me is that those two beliefs seem like they'd pull against each other once you throw in stuff Gödel obviously never got to think about, like mind uploading, or "digital immortality," or the simulation hypothesis (the idea that we might already be inside some other, higher level world running on a computer). Say future scientists scan someone's brain right before they die and run a full working copy of them on a machine forever after - does that count, by Gödel's own logic, as the actual "Wiedersehen" he was hoping for? Or does his own mind-isn't-a-machine argument basically force him to say no, that's just a mechanical copy, not the real person continuing on, since whatever the "real" mind is, it's specifically the part that can't be reduced to code running on hardware?

I've got zero background in philosophy so maybe I'm missing something obvious, but I'd genuinely like your best reconstruction here, not a cop-out like "he never mentioned computers so we can't know." Walk me through how his afterlife letters and his mind/machine argument actually interact, then commit to an answer: would Gödel, given everything he actually believed, count mind-uploading as genuine survival of death, or would he reject it as a fake? And throw in a quick take on the simulation hypothesis too - would he have seen that as good news for his "other, higher worlds" idea, or as a totally different, more materialist thing he'd have wanted nothing to do with?
