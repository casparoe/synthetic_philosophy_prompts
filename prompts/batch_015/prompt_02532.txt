Okay so in my decision theory seminar this week we covered Newcomb-style problems and got to the classic "Death in Damascus" case — the guy who overhears that Death has an appointment with him, panics, and flees to Aleppo, except Death is basically a perfect predictor of where people go so fleeing doesnt actually help. My professor presented this as THE textbook argument for why causal decision theory (CDT) beats evidential decision theory (EDT): apparently EDT tells the guy that both fleeing and staying count as bad news (since each is correlated with dying in that particular city), so it cant give him any usable advice, whereas CDT just computes the causal expected utility of each option — treating Death's choice as already fixed, independent of whatever the man decides — and hands him one clean, stable answer. Made total sense at the time.

Then for my term paper I actually went and found the original Gibbard and Harper paper from 1978 plus a bunch of stuff citing it, and it turns out people are STILL publishing on this exact case, arguing about "ratifiability," mixed strategies, whether the guy should flip a coin, etc, with papers on it from like the last few years too. I tried explaining this to my roommate (who thinks decision theory is a waste of everyone's time) and she asked the obvious thing: if CDT already solved this back in 1978, why is anyone still arguing about it almost fifty years later? I didn't really have a good answer for her — is this just academics refusing to let a settled question go, or is my professor's "CDT wins, case closed" version of the story missing something?
