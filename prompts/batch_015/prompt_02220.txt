I work on distributed systems, and a coworker recently introduced me to a corner of game theory I'd never encountered before: "program equilibrium." As I understand it, the idea goes back to a 2004 paper by Moshe Tennenholtz, where instead of two players in a game choosing strategies in the ordinary sense, each submits a computer program that gets to read the other player's source code before outputting a move. That single change opens up outcomes that classical game theory says shouldn't be possible in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma. The simplest case, sometimes called the "Cooperate-with-Copies" equilibrium, has a program cooperate only if the other program is textually identical to itself, so that defecting would mean defecting against a perfect copy of your own disposition. A 2014 follow-up paper, with Eliezer Yudkowsky among its authors (which is how I originally found it, via an old AI-safety forum thread), pushes this further with an agent called FairBot, which uses Löb's theorem from provability logic to cooperate with any agent it can prove will cooperate back — so two FairBots reach mutual cooperation without needing to be literal copies of each other. People in this area apparently call the whole research program "open-source game theory."

This sent me back to a battered copy of Leviathan that's been on my shelf since a philosophy-for-non-majors class I audited years ago (I have no formal training here, so bear with me). Hobbes built his entire case for absolute sovereignty on a structurally similar problem: in the state of nature, a covenant to cooperate is worthless without an enforcer standing behind it, because each party has every incentive to strike first rather than risk performing and being exploited — which is presumably why he insists that "covenants, without the sword, are but words." He goes further in his reply to the "Foole," who argues that it can be rational to break one's word whenever you can get away with it: Hobbes's rebuttal isn't that this is logically incoherent, but that no one can ever be sufficiently confident of getting away with it. Program equilibrium looks like it might dissolve exactly this problem in a way Hobbes never had the chance to consider: if two parties — human, corporate, or algorithmic — can each verify, from the other's literal and auditable source code, that cooperation is provably conditional on the other's cooperation, that seems to amount to a covenant that enforces itself, no sword required.

What I'd like from you is your best reconstruction of how Hobbes would actually respond if someone sat him down and explained all of this. Would he concede that program equilibrium is a genuine escape route from the state of nature that he simply lacked the mathematics to discover, or does his broader psychology and metaphysics give him principled grounds to reject it — something about whether a disposition encoded in a program can mean what a human being's word means, about source code being forged or silently altered after inspection, or about how a trick that works for two parties still leaves his separate argument for sovereignty over many parties untouched? I'm especially interested in whether you think he would insist on a hard line between this working for artificial agents and it ever applying to the flesh-and-blood people his argument was actually about.
