I'm working on a longer piece about the "possible worlds" stuff in metaphysics, sort of riding on how mainstream multiverse talk has gotten thanks to physics podcasts and Marvel, except the real philosophical version is so much weirder than anything on screen. I interviewed a philosopher who works on modality for background, and she walked me through the standard Lewis vs. Kripke split — counterpart theory vs. transworld identity. I already know the classic "Humphrey" complaint against Lewis (Humphrey doesn't care that some counterpart of his won an election in another world, he wanted to win), that ones in every intro textbook, so you don't need to rehash it for me.

What actually hooked me was a more obscure puzzle she mentioned, usually credited to a philosopher named Hugh Chandler, about a bicycle. Say your bike was originally built from a frame and two wheels. It seems totally fine to say the bike could've come into existence with one different wheel — objects aren't that fussy about every single original part. But it seems crazy to say the bike could've come into existence sharing only one of its three original parts (just the frame, say, both wheels different) — at some point that's obviously just a different bike. Here's the kicker: you don't even need a long chain to get in trouble. Swap one wheel, and by that "tolerant" judgment it's still your original bike. Now swap the other wheel on THAT bike, and by the same tolerant judgment it's still that bike too. Two steps, two individually very plausible judgments, and plain transitivity of identity now forces you to say the twice-swapped bike — sharing only the frame with the original — is identical to your bike, which is exactly the verdict that seemed crazy a second ago. The standard counterpart-theory fix is to say "could have been" claims run through a similarity-based counterpart relation instead of strict identity, and, crucially, that relation just isn't required to be transitive — so bike-B can be a counterpart of bike-A, and bike-C a counterpart of bike-B, without C having to be a counterpart of A. No contradiction, everyone goes home happy. It's usually presented as one of the cleanest wins counterpart theory has going for it.

So here's what I'd love your help with: argue, as hard as you can, that this fix is a way bigger bullet to bite than it gets credit for. My hunch (tell me if I'm wrong) is that loosing transitivity there quietly wrecks a lot of perfectly ordinary "could have been" reasoning that chains through a single person or thing over several steps — the sort of everyday counterfactual thinking like "if I'd taken that other job I'd have moved to Chicago, and if I'd moved to Chicago I probably would've married someone completely different, so I could've ended up a totally different person" — reasoning that silently assumes the chain holds together the whole way through, not just link by link. Make the strongest possible case that giving up transitivity here is a worse cost than whatever the transworld-identity side has to swallow instead (bare identities, primitive haecceities, whatever), and if you can, head off the obvious comeback that ordinary reasoning never actually needed full transitivity anyway.
