I'm doing a PhD in political theory (history of political and economic thought track), and for comps my reading group has been ploughing through Book IV of the Wealth of Nations more or less line by line, mainly because our advisor is convinced nobody in the cohort has actually read past the invisible hand paragraph in ch. 2 (fair, honestly). This week was the colonies chapter, which is enormous, like a hundred pages, and gets skipped even by people who work on Smith for a living. I got completely stuck on a passage near the end of part III, where Smith pivots out of all his monopoly-trade bookkeeping into this sharp little aside about shopkeepers. Quoting it exactly (this is off the Marxists.org text, so no idea if the pagination lines up with whatever edition is standard for you):

"To found a great empire for the sole purpose of raising up a people of customers may at first sight appear a project fit only for a nation of shopkeepers. It is, however, a project altogether unfit for a nation of shopkeepers; but extremely fit for a nation whose government is influenced by shopkeepers. Such statesmen, and such statesmen only, are capable of fancying that they will find some advantage in employing the blood and treasure of their fellow-citizens to found and maintain such an empire. Say to a shopkeeper, 'Buy me a good estate, and I shall always buy my clothes at your shop, even though I should pay somewhat dearer than what I can have them for at other shops'; and you will not find him very forward to embrace your proposal. But should any other person buy you such an estate, the shopkeeper would be much obliged to your benefactor if he would enjoin you to buy all your clothes at his shop."

Right after this, Smith goes on, completely straight-faced as far as I can tell, to seriously work out what would happen if Britain voluntarily gave up all authority over the American colonies and let them elect their own governments and make their own laws, while saying in the very same breath that no nation on earth has ever done such a thing or ever will. Two things about this are tripping me up and I honestly can't tell if I'm overthinking it. First, what's the actual logical move in "unfit for a nation of shopkeepers, but fit for a nation whose government is influenced by shopkeepers"? It reads like a throwaway rhetorical flip, but it feels like its doing real argumentative work, almost like a public-choice or regulatory-capture point avant la lettre, about whose costs and whose benefits we're actually talking about. Second, given that Smith admits in the same passage that the decolonization proposal is politically impossible, is his subsequent detailed defense of it ironic, a kind of reductio, or a sincere counterfactual argument he just doesn't expect anyone to act on? Would love a careful unpacking of the actual logic here rather than a paraphrase, since paraphrase is exactly where I keep losing the thread.
