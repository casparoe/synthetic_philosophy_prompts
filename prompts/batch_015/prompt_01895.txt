I've been chewing on an inconsistency in my own newsroom that I suspect is more interesting than it looks, and I'd welcome an outside perspective, since none of us can quite talk each other out of our positions. At the paper where I mostly cover science, investigative and political stories are still governed by an old rule that newsroom lore traces back to the Watergate era: no claim of wrongdoing runs unless it has been confirmed independently by at least two sources who couldn't plausibly have compared notes beforehand. My own beat runs on a completely different norm. Last month I wrote up an unpublished claim about a room-temperature superconductor based on a single phone call with the lead researcher, attributed to her by name, without lining up any second, independent confirmation of the underlying result. When I asked my editor why the standards diverge so sharply, she said, in effect, that we're entitled to take a credible scientist's word for what happened in her own lab, absent some specific reason for doubt, in a way we're not entitled to take one unnamed source's word for a politician's wrongdoing.

The trouble is that I can't reconstruct the principle behind that answer. If a single person's say-so is never, by itself, enough to justify believing a claim — which is what the two-source rule seems to presuppose — it shouldn't matter whether that person is a disgruntled staffer or a tenured professor with nothing obvious to gain from lying; the rule ought to apply across the board. But if one credible person's word can, in fact, be enough on its own, as my own reporting practice assumes every week, then the two-source rule starts to look less like a fundamental rule about evidence and more like a defensive habit built for one particular kind of adversarial story. I put this to a colleague on the investigations desk, who insisted the two-source rule is just common sense, and that treating a lone claim as sufficient is how reporters get burned — but that can't be the whole story either, since her own scoops, like my science stories, ultimately rest on somebody's word at some point down the chain, with nothing underneath it but the source's say-so.

What I'd like help thinking through is whether there's a real, principled way to draw this line, rather than just a professional custom that happens to differ by beat. Are there genuine conditions under which one person's word ought to be enough, by itself, to justify believing something, as against cases where it structurally can't be, no matter how credible the person seems? Or is the gap between my beat's practice and the investigations desk's practice really just an unprincipled compromise that has hardened, over time, into two different house rules with no deeper theory unifying them?
