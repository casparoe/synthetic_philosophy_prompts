Over the past year or so, entirely on my own time and without any formal training in philosophy or economics, I've been slowly working through discussions of utilitarianism that circulate in effective-altruism and rationalist online communities, and that is how I first came across Harsanyi's aggregation theorem. As I currently understand it, the theorem shows that if every individual's preferences over uncertain prospects satisfy the standard expected-utility axioms, if society's preferences satisfy those same axioms, and if society is never willing to rank two prospects differently whenever every individual is indifferent between them, then the only social ranking consistent with all of this is one given by a weighted sum of the individual utilities. People in these communities tend to present this as turning utilitarian aggregation from a contestable ethical stance into something closer to a mathematical consequence of a few fairly modest rationality and unanimity assumptions, which struck me as a remarkable claim if it really holds up.

What has kept me turning this over, though, is pushback I later found along two directions that seem related but not quite the same. The first, which I gather traces back to a 1967 paper by the economist Peter Diamond, involves a case where flipping a fair coin between two people with equally strong claims to some single indivisible good seems clearly fairer than simply handing it to one of them outright, yet the theorem's own logic seems to leave it indifferent between the two options whenever the two people are otherwise identical. The second objection says that even granting the mathematics is beyond reproach, summing utilities across separate people treats society as though it were one individual gambling with itself, in a way that misses what gets called the separateness of persons. What I find especially interesting is that Rawls used almost the same "imagine not yet knowing who you will turn out to be" device as Harsanyi, yet arrived at a maximin conclusion rather than a utilitarian one, largely by denying that the parties behind such a veil are entitled to assume any particular probability, let alone an equal one, of turning out to be any given person.

I would like to understand this disagreement in terms of its actual moving parts rather than as a one-line verdict, so I was hoping you could write me a dialogue between two people who both know this material in real depth: one persuaded that Harsanyi's theorem gives principled, near-decisive support to utilitarian aggregation, and the other persuaded that the fairness and separateness-of-persons difficulties show that satisfying the theorem's formal conditions is not enough to settle how welfare ought to be aggregated across people. I don't want either character to be a strawman for the other's position; I would like them to genuinely engage with each other's specific technical points, including what the Pareto condition is actually doing, why the coin-flip case is supposed to be a problem for the theorem's own axioms rather than just a competing intuition, and whether a formal rationality condition of this kind could, even in principle, settle a genuinely ethical question, rather than each side simply restating its conclusion in different words.
