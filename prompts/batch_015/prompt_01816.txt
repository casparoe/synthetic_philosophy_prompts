ok, decision theory rabbit hole from quals prep — want an actual argument built this time, not just a summary of the mess. There's this Pasadena game (Nover & Hájek): flip a coin til first heads on toss n, you win 2^n/n if n's odd and pay 2^n/n if n's even. Summed in the obvious order that's just the alternating harmonic series, ln(2), but its only conditionally convergent, so the terms can be rearranged to sum to literally any number, or to +/- infinity — meaning the expected value isn't infinite like St Petersburg, its just flat out undefined.

The standard move (Colyvan) is to bite the bullet: fine, some gambles have no expected utility, cordon them off, move on with life. My advisor thinks that settles it, I don't buy it, because of what people call the "contagion problem" — once you admit even a tiny credence that you might be facing something pasadena-like, ordinary expected utility comparisons between completely mundane options stop being well-defined too, so cordoning off the weird cases doesn't actually work. I want to argue that the only move which genuinely blocks contagion is going full Arrow: rational utility functions have to be bounded, not as an ad hoc patch but as the only thing that keeps gambles like this from ever being assignable nonzero credence in the first place. Can you write the strongest version of that argument, and also take a real swing at the obvious complaint that bounding utility just arbitrarily caps how good an outcome is allowed to be?
