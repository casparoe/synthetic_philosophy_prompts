A friend of mine, who is taking a graduate seminar on experimental philosophy this term, brought up the Knobe effect over dinner last night, and what started as a casual mention turned into a nearly two-hour argument that neither of us won. We agreed on the phenomenon itself: in Knobe's original chairman vignette, people are far more willing to say a side effect was brought about "intentionally" when it is harmful (something like 82 percent of respondents, in the case of the executive who greenlights a program that incidentally damages the environment) than when the identical setup instead produces a benefit (roughly 23 percent, when the side effect is helping the environment) — even though the chairman is equally indifferent to the environment in both versions of the story. Where we split was on why this happens. She defended Sripada's Deep Self Concordance account: the idea that we implicitly distinguish an agent's "Acting Self" (the transient desires and intentions that are the immediate causal source of a choice) from their "Deep Self" (stable values and life commitments), and that we only count a side effect as intentional when it concords with what we already assume about that Deep Self — so the chairman's stated indifference confirms an assumed uncaring Deep Self when the outcome is harm, but tells us nothing when the very same indifference produces a benefit, since helping was never the default we would have attributed to him anyway. I kept citing follow-up work — Cova and Naar's experiments, and a paper called "Deep Trouble for the Deep Self" — that seems to show the concordance mechanism predicting patterns that don't actually hold up once you test it directly, and argued that a plainer moral-valence story might fit the data just as well without the extra apparatus. Neither of us budged.

What I would like from you is a joke — a one-liner, a short back-and-forth, or a tiny scene, whichever serves the punchline best — that turns specifically on the Concordance Criterion mechanism itself, rather than on the more generic (and far more commonly told) observation that morality distorts our judgments of intentional action. The joke should depend on the specific, almost unfair-feeling asymmetry Sripada's model posits: that an agent's indifference is read as damning evidence about who they "really" are when it precedes something bad, but as uninformative when it precedes something good. Someone who actually knows the deep self literature should catch an extra layer of the joke that a person who only knows the bare Knobe effect would miss entirely, and, more importantly, it should actually be funny, or at least wry, rather than a dressed-up restatement of the theory with a rimshot attached.
