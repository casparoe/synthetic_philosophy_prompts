ok so i had this whole argument with my friend last night about zhuangzi's view on death and neither of us backed down lol. her take is that zhuangzi actually thinks death might be BETTER than life somehow, like a real awakening, and mine is that thats reading way too much into it, that the whole point is there's no ranking at all, good/bad doesn't even apply, you just roll with whatever transformation comes next. we got stuck arguing about this one passage, from the death of Zilai in the "Great and Most Honoured Master" chapter, in the old Legge translation, which is honestly kind of rough to parse:

"There is the great Mass (of nature);--I find the support of my body in it; my life is spent in toil on it; my old age seeks ease on it; at death I find rest on it:--what has made my life a good will make my death also a good.

"Here now is a great founder, casting his metal. If the metal were to leap up (in the pot), and say, 'I must be made into a (sword like the) Mo-yeh,' the great founder would be sure to regard it as uncanny. So, again, when a form is being fashioned in the mould of the womb, if it were to say, 'I must become a man; I must become a man,' the Creator would be sure to regard it as uncanny. When we once understand that heaven and earth are a great melting-pot, and the Creator a great founder, where can we have to go to that shall not be right for us? We are born as from a quiet sleep, and we die to a calm awaking."

that last line is what broke our brains. seems backwards to me - shouldn't we wake INTO life and fall asleep INTO death, not the other way round? my friend thinks thats exactly the point, that death is the real waking up (she's dragging in that whole later bit in the same chapter about not knowing if we're dreaming or awake to back herself up). i think its just clunky 1891-translation phrasing that doesnt carry that much weight. can you help me work out what that line is actually doing, whether it sits ok with the "life good = death also good" bit right before it (which reads way more neutral/symmetric), and how much interpretive weight a single clause like that can even bear given its a 130+ year old translation and not the original Chinese?
