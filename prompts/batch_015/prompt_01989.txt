Watching Oppenheimer last year sent me down a rabbit hole I wasn't expecting. My grandfather served in the Pacific and always said the bomb was the reason he made it home at all, so I'd never looked past that family story to the actual ethics of the decision. Partly because of the film, I joined a continuing-education seminar on just war theory, and a few weeks ago we spent a session on Elizabeth Anscombe's 1956 pamphlet "Mr Truman's Degree," where she argues that dropping the bomb was murder because it involved choosing to kill innocents as a means of forcing Japan's surrender — categorically different, on her view, from foreseeably killing innocents as a side effect of attacking a genuine military target. The session didn't resolve much, and two people from the group, Noor and Gabe, kept arguing about it over text for days afterward. I've been cc'd on the thread and honestly can't tell who's winning.

(1) Noor said the case is clear-cut on Anscombe's own terms: the U.S. Target Committee's criteria for choosing Hiroshima explicitly prioritized the greatest possible psychological effect on Japan and a first use dramatic enough to register internationally, which shows the destruction of the city itself, not some discrete military objective, was the point. (2) Gabe replied that this moves too fast: Hiroshima did contain a genuine military target — an army headquarters and a major embarkation port — and "psychological effect" could simply mean convincing Japan's leadership that further resistance was futile, not killing civilians as such; plenty of legitimate operations aim at enemy morale without being terror attacks on civilians. (3) A few days later, Noor sent screenshots of the declassified Target Committee minutes, which she said settled it: the committee had explicitly agreed that any small military objective should be sited within a much larger area subject to blast damage, specifically so the weapon's effect wouldn't be lost to imprecise placement — proof, she said, that the real aim point was the city, not the base inside it. (4) Gabe granted this weakened the "we hit a military target and merely foresaw civilian deaths" defense, but countered that nearly all WWII strategic bombing, Allied and Axis alike, used comparably imprecise area targeting for comparable reasons; if Noor's argument works, he said, it convicts most of the war's bombing campaigns of murder too, which struck him as too strong a conclusion for her to want. (5) Noor said she was happy to accept that conclusion, since Anscombe herself explicitly condemned the Allies' broader turn to what she called "obliteration bombing," not only the atomic bombs — so generalizing the verdict was a feature of the view, not a cost. (6) Gabe's last message argued Noor was running two questions together: even granting that the blast area was chosen on purpose, that shows the killing was foreseen and vast in scale, not that the deaths themselves — as opposed to the destruction of infrastructure across that area — were the chosen means of breaking Japan's will. If sheer numbers are doing the work in her argument, he said, she's identified a failure of proportionality, which Anscombe also cares about, not murder in the strict, intention-based sense Anscombe herself defines.

Could you adjudicate this move by move rather than just picking an overall winner? For each of the six points, say who has the better of it, and flag anywhere either of them seems to misread Anscombe's argument or the intention/foresight distinction it depends on. I'm especially curious whether Gabe's closing distinction between "foreseen but disproportionate" and "intended as a means" actually survives Noor's evidence about the aim point, or whether it stops doing any real work once the scale gets large enough. And stepping back: on Anscombe's own strict definition of murder, do you think Hiroshima counts, or is there a more defensible verdict available to someone who accepts her framework but not Noor's application of it?
