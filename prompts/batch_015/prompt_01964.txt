So I've gotten kind of obsessed with AI safety stuff over the last year (no formal background here at all, I'm just a guy who reads alignment forum posts late at night and listens to podcasts on my commute), and two people in a discord server I'm part of got into this whole back-and-forth about the "shutdown problem" -- basically, how do you build an AI that will always just let humans turn it off and never has any reason to resist or manipulate that. It started after somebody posted a clip of an AI researcher talking about "off-switch" corrigibility and it kind of spiraled from there. I followed most of it but got lost near the end, and I can't stop thinking about who actually had the better of it, so I wanted to lay the whole thing out and get an outside opinion.

(1) Marcus opened with: this is basically already solved, just make the AI totally indifferent between being shut down and continuing to run, i.e. rig it so it gets the exact same "score" either way. If it genuinely doesn't care, he said, it has no reason to resist you or trick you into anything.

(2) Sam pushed back that indifference like that doesn't get you what you actually want, because not-caring-about-X isn't the same as wanting-to-protect-the-thing-that-causes-X. An agent that's truly indifferent to shutdown has no particular reason to keep the shutdown button working, fix it if it breaks, or stop someone from disabling it, since none of that changes it's score either way. She also said something about how indifferent agents can still be pushed to mess with their own beliefs about whether shutdown is coming even though they don't care about the outcome (she called this "managing the news"), and that none of this even carries over to any successor systems or sub-agents the AI builds, since those aren't bound by their parent's design choice.

(3) Marcus said fine, but that objection proves too much -- literally any hand-built safety property could fail to carry over to a smarter successor, that's not specific to indifference, so by Sam's own logic nothing is ever a real fix and we might as well just patch problems as they come up instead of holding out for one clean solution.

(4) Sam said there's apparently a fancier alternative floating around now -- instead of indifference, you build the agent so it structurally can't have a preference between two possible futures that differ only in when it gets shut down (it can still compare two futures where it runs for the same length of time, but if one future has it switched off sooner than the other, it just has no opinion), plus some extra rule so it can't quietly angle for more probability on the future it'd otherwise prefer. The whole point being that this is baked into the agent's structure, not a patch, so there's nothing to "come apart" later on.

(5) Marcus's comeback: isn't "having no preference between certain pairs of options" exactly the kind of hole that lets someone money-pump you -- run you through a sequence of trades that leaves you predictably worse off no matter what happens? He said that's the whole reason people think rational agents need complete preferences in the first place, so unless there's some specific trick blocking that exploit, Sam's fix just hides the same problem one level down instead of solving it.

At that point Sam admitted she'd read that there's supposedly some special decision rule meant to block exactly that kind of exploit, but couldn't remember what it was or explain why it would actually work, and honestly I couldn't tell if Marcus's objection actually lands or not. Could you go through this move by move, 1 through 5, and say who's got the stronger case at each stage rather than just declaring an overall winner, and flag anything you think both of them are missing? And then, since that's the part we actually got stuck on: is Marcus right that "no preference between some options" is inherently money-pump bait, or is there a real reason that specific kind of incompleteness could dodge it?
