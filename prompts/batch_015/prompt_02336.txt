I run a small formal epistemology reading group with a few colleagues, and last session we got stuck on van Fraassen's Reflection Principle — roughly, the idea that your current credence in some proposition A, conditional on being certain that your credence in A tomorrow will be some value r, should itself be r. The usual gloss is that you should treat your future, presumably-better-informed self the way you'd treat an expert: defer to it now, even before you know what it's going to say. Van Fraassen backs this with a diachronic Dutch book argument, the same style of argument used to justify ordinary conditionalization.

Everyone in the group knows the classic objection: unrestricted Reflection gets refuted by memory-loss cases (this is most of what Christensen, Titelbaum and co. are on about), so defenders patch the principle by restricting it to situations where the agent isn't anticipating memory loss, manipulation, or otherwise going irrational in the interim. Half the room seemed to think that settles things — patched Reflection survives as a genuine requirement of ideal rationality, full stop. I wasn't convinced but couldn't say why on the spot, and its been bugging me since.

So here's what I'd like from you: argue that even the patched, restricted version of Reflection is not a requirement of ideal rationality — at best it's good advice for epistemically limited creatures like us. To have any bite, the argument needs its own example, and please don't reach for Sleeping Beauty — half my students this semester turned in papers that are just the halfer/thirder fight with the names changed, and honestly I think the self-locating-belief stuff is a seperate can of worms from whatever's really at stake in Reflection. Build a case involving an ordinary, non-indexical proposition, where the agent isn't anticipating memory loss and doesn't currently regard her future updating process as irrational or non-evidential (otherwise you're just re-hitting the case the patch was designed to exclude). Then deal with the obvious comeback: once you've stipulated that the future update is fully rational and evidence-driven, deferring to it now just falls out of conditionalization plus a bit of self-trust, so your example hasn't shown anything distinctively "Reflection" is doing any work. I want to know whether there's real daylight between "ideal rationality requires this" and "this is what a well-functioning epistemic agent does" — or whether the patched principle is really just conditionalization wearing a trenchcoat.
