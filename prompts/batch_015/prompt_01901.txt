Popular advice about self-control glosses over a real dispute between philosophers and behavioral economists about what weakness of will actually is. Behavioral economists, following George Ainslie's "picoeconomics," treat it as a straightforward consequence of hyperbolic, rather than exponential, discounting: because a hyperbolic curve inflates the value of near rewards, an agent's preference between two options can consistently reverse as the sooner one draws close, with no special irrationality and no distinct faculty of "will" needed to explain the switch. The philosopher Richard Holton has argued that this leaves out the phenomenon itself: for him, weakness of will is the too-ready abandonment of one's own resolutions — a distinct psychological capacity that no preference curve, however shaped, can capture on its own.

Write a dialogue between a philosopher defending Holton's resolution-based view and an economist defending the hyperbolic-discounting account, set in the moments just after a mutual friend, Maya — who announced over breakfast that she was quitting smoking — accepts a cigarette at a party that evening. Each should raise specific, substantive difficulties for the other's account of what just happened rather than simply restating their own position, and neither should have the easier side of the argument.
