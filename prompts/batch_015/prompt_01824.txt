I've spent a good part of the weekend falling down a rabbit hole (pun intended) that started with some old rationalist-adjacent discussions of Machian relationalism and Julian Barbour's "shape dynamics," and ended with me reading through the Stanford Encyclopedia's entry on the "hole argument" in general relativity. As a quick refresher, since the details are pretty obscure: Einstein first devised the argument in 1913 while wrestling with generally covariant field equations, and it was revived in the philosophical literature in the 1980s, most influentially by John Earman and John Norton, as an argument against spacetime substantivalism. The core move is that if spacetime points are individuals in their own right, existing independently of whatever fields happen to be defined on them, then given any solution to the Einstein field equations you can smoothly drag the metric around inside some bounded "hole" region while leaving everything outside it untouched, producing a second, mathematically distinct solution that agrees with the first on every invariant quantity and every observation outside the hole. Since nothing in the theory can decide between the two ways of filling in the hole, substantivalism seems to buy a strange, entirely idle form of indeterminism. The standard escape is to deny that the two "draggings" represent distinct physical possibilities at all -- a move Earman and Norton dubbed "Leibniz equivalence," expressly in homage to Leibniz's own taunt to Newton's defender Samuel Clarke that swapping East for West across the entire material universe, or having God create the whole of it some ages sooner than He did, would change nothing whatsoever, since no possible observation could ever tell the two scenarios apart.

That homage is what's been nagging at me. Leibniz's shift arguments were aimed at a static, Euclidean, absolute space with no dynamics of its own, and his positive alternative -- of space as a merely relational "order of coexistences," ultimately grounded, on the deeper monadological story, in the perceptions of simple substances rather than in relations among bodies themselves -- was worked out with no conception of fields, metrics, or diffeomorphisms whatsoever. So here's what I'm curious about: if you could sit Leibniz down, walk him through the hole argument in full technical detail, and ask him to rule on it, what do you think he'd actually say? Would he simply claim total vindication, on the grounds that this is precisely the sort of empirically idle "distinction" his own principle of sufficient reason and identity of indiscernibles were designed to forbid? Or would his other commitments make him uneasy with how contemporary philosophers actually try to save a modest substantivalism from the argument -- for instance "sophisticated substantivalism," which grants that spacetime points exist but denies that they have primitive transworld identity (anti-haecceitism), so that the two draggings turn out not to be genuinely distinct possibilities without requiring anything like Leibniz's full relationalism? Would he recognize that move as a natural ally, or as smuggling back in exactly the kind of primitive individuation he spent the Clarke correspondence attacking? And, more broadly, do you think Leibniz's metaphysics is even flexible enough to accommodate a spacetime whose geometry is itself a dynamical, contingent field, or would stretching his system this far mean rebuilding it almost beyond recognition?
