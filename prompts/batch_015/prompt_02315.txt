I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, but I've been working through G. E. Moore's *Principia Ethica* on my own and got stuck on something. For context: earlier in the book (Chapter III, §50), Moore runs his well-known "two worlds" thought experiment, comparing an exceedingly beautiful world to an equally unperceived world that is "simply one heap of filth," with no consciousness anywhere to perceive either one, and concludes that the beautiful world is still better — i.e., that beauty has some intrinsic value quite apart from anyone ever being aware of it. It's basically an isolation test, not unlike isolating a variable when you're trying to debug where a value is actually coming from.

Here's a later passage (Chapter VI, §113) that I can't quite square with that argument:

"By far the most valuable things, which we know or can imagine, are certain states of consciousness, which may be roughly described as the pleasures of human intercourse and the enjoyment of beautiful objects. No one, probably, who has asked himself the question, has ever doubted that personal affection and the appreciation of what is beautiful in Art or Nature, are good in themselves; nor, if we consider strictly what things are worth having purely for their own sakes, does it appear probable that any one will think that anything else has nearly so great a value as the things which are included under these two heads. I have myself urged in Chap. III. (§ 50) that the mere existence of what is beautiful does appear to have some intrinsic value; but I regard it as indubitable that Prof. Sidgwick was so far right, in the view there discussed, that such mere existence of what is beautiful has value, so small as to be negligible, in comparison with that which attaches to the consciousness of beauty. This simple truth may, indeed, be said to be universally recognised. What has not been recognised is that it is the ultimate and fundamental truth of Moral Philosophy. That it is only for the sake of these things — in order that as much of them as possible may at some time exist — that any one can be justified in performing any public or private duty; that they are the raison d'être of virtue; that it is they — these complex wholes themselves, and not any constituent or characteristic of them — that form the rational ultimate end of human action and the sole criterion of social progress: these appear to be truths which have been generally overlooked."

He then goes on to describe both personal affection and aesthetic appreciation together as "highly complex organic unities."

What I can't work out is whether Moore has quietly given away the point of his own thought experiment. He originally ran it specifically to show that Sidgwick is wrong to tie all value to consciousness, but here he concedes that unperceived beauty's value is "negligible" next to the value of consciously appreciating beauty — which sounds very close to Sidgwick's actual position, even if Moore insists on a technical, non-zero difference. After this concession, is there any real, load-bearing disagreement left between the two of them, or has Moore only preserved it verbally? And does the same problem carry over to friendship: since he never seems to run an analogous isolation test on personal affection the way he does on beauty, do we actually have any grounds for thinking he'd assign it value if nobody were ever conscious of it, or could it turn out to be even more thoroughly consciousness-dependent than beauty is?
