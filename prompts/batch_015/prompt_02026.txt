I'm a software engineer (backend, mostly), not a philosopher, but I've been pulled into some AI-alignment reading at work — we keep arguing about whether, and how, you could ever encode something like "human values" into a reward model — and that rabbit hole eventually dropped me into the more general philosophical problem of moral uncertainty: how should an agent act when they aren't sure which ethical theory is correct, only that they have different degrees of confidence in several of them? A colleague pointed me toward some of Will MacAskill's writing on this, and after a few evenings of note-taking I've pieced together roughly five competing proposals, though I suspect I'm still missing nuances a specialist would catch.

As far as I can tell, the main contenders are: (1) "Maximize Expected Choiceworthiness" (MEC), which treats credence-weighted choiceworthiness across theories the way expected-utility theory treats credence-weighted payoffs, but which needs some way of comparing "units" of rightness across theories that were never built to share a common scale; (2) "My Favorite Theory" (MFT), where you simply act on whichever single theory you find most credible and ignore the others entirely; (3) the "Moral Parliament" model, where each theory gets bargaining power roughly proportional to your credence in it, and the eventual choice falls out of a negotiation between delegates rather than a calculation; (4) Borda-count-style aggregation, which only needs each theory's ordinal ranking of the available options rather than cardinal comparisons of value, but which apparently runs into trouble over how finely you're allowed to individuate the options being ranked; and (5) a more skeptical stance that treats the whole enterprise as confused, on the grounds that credences over moral theories aren't the kind of thing that can be meaningfully combined or compared the way credences over empirical hypotheses can.

None of these has fully satisfied me, but I'd rather get an outside, structured opinion than keep going in circles with my own half-baked one.
