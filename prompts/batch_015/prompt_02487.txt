I collect weird true footnotes from the history of science to turn into short fiction, and this week's find might be my favorite yet. In the actual 1950 Turing test paper, right after laying out the imitation game, Turing spends a whole page taking ESP dead seriously as a threat to it. His example: a good telepathic guesser goes up against a computer guessing card suits, the psychic gets something like 130 out of 400 right instead of the ~100 you'd expect from chance, and the interrogator correctly picks him out as the human -- except that "win" has nothing to do with intelligence. Then it gets weirder: he worries the interrogator's own psychokinesis could reach through and skew the machine's random number generator, so even the control cant be trusted. His actual proposed fix, no joke, is a 'telepathy-proof room.'

Can you write me a short story (900ish words) that takes this specific problem seriously -- someone running some version of the test has to sit with the fact that a clean result might not mean what everyone assumed going in, and that no room you build really seals the problem off. Please, no haunted-computer vibes, I want the dread to come from the logic itself, not from ghosts. Time period and setting are your call.
