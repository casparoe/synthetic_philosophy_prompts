I am preparing a graduate seminar session on the identity of indiscernibles, and I would like help thinking through how a passage from the Leibniz–Clarke correspondence bears on the standard "Black's spheres" objection to Leibniz's principle. Here is Leibniz, writing in his fifth paper to Clarke (August 1716, §§23, 25–26, in Clarke's own 1717 translation):

"I said, that in sensible things, two, that are indiscernible from each other, can never be found; that, for instance, two leaves in a garden, or two drops of water, perfectly alike, are not to be found. [...] When I deny that there are two drops of water perfectly alike, or any two other bodies indiscernible from each other, I don't say, 'tis absolutely impossible to suppose them; but that 'tis a thing contrary to the divine wisdom, and which consequently does not exist. I own, that if two things perfectly indiscernible from each other did exist, they would be two; but that supposition is false, and contrary to the grand principle of reason."

In my own lectures on this material, I typically present Max Black's (1952) thought experiment — two qualitatively identical iron spheres, alone in an otherwise empty and symmetric universe — as a counterexample showing that the identity of indiscernibles cannot be a necessary truth. But Leibniz, in this very passage, already grants that indiscernible things are not "absolutely impossible to suppose"; he locates their non-existence in considerations of divine wisdom and sufficient reason, not in brute logical or metaphysical necessity. Given that concession, what exactly would Black's thought experiment be refuting? I'd like your help working out whether there is a textually defensible distinction between the (perhaps merely contingent) principle Leibniz actually defends here and the (apparently necessary) principle his spheres are supposed to defeat, or whether that distinction collapses under scrutiny and Black's argument goes through regardless.
