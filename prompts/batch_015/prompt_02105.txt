I'm a third-year mathematics student currently taking a course on set theory and the foundations of mathematics, and this week's material has stuck with me more than I expected. We covered Zermelo's original 1904 proof of the well-ordering theorem, the equivalence of the Axiom of Choice with Zorn's Lemma and the well-ordering theorem, and then, as something of a grand finale, the Banach–Tarski paradox: the result that a solid ball can be partitioned into finitely many pieces and reassembled, using only rotations and translations, into two solid balls each identical to the original. Our professor also had us read the "five letters" exchanged in 1905 between Baire, Borel, Hadamard, and Lebesgue, in which they argued over whether Zermelo's principle was even a legitimate mathematical tool, since it asserts the existence of a choice function without ever specifying a rule for making the choices. A few of us ended up arguing about this well past the end of class: is the Axiom of Choice just a convenient assumption we keep around because we like its consequences (every vector space has a basis, arbitrary products of compact spaces are compact), or does its independence from the rest of ZF, shown by Gödel in 1938 and Cohen in 1963, mean that accepting it is closer to a matter of mathematical taste, or even faith, than to discovering a fact about the mathematical universe?

I'd like to sit with that question in fictional form, and would appreciate your help writing a short story of roughly 1000–1500 words built around it. You have real freedom in choosing an angle: you might write historical fiction that reimagines the correspondence between Baire, Borel, Hadamard, and Lebesgue as a more dramatized confrontation; or invent a community of mathematicians (human or otherwise) who must decide, with real stakes for their world, whether to adopt an axiom that hands them enormous power at the cost of monsters like non-measurable sets; or personify the axiom itself as a character offering a bargain whose fine print nobody quite reads before signing. Whichever direction you take, I'd like the story to treat the underlying mathematics and philosophy as load-bearing rather than decorative: it should reflect genuine features of the debate, such as the non-constructive character of the axiom (existence asserted without any witness ever being produced), its logical independence from the rest of ZF, and the fact that the paradox's strangeness comes from non-measurable pieces with no well-defined volume, not from any trick of physical cutting. What I'm hoping for, in short, is a story that someone who has actually sat through the proof of Banach–Tarski would recognize as true to the mathematics, not merely evocative of it.
