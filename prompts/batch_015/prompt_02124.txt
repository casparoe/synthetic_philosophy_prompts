Since retiring a few years ago after a long career in condensed-matter physics, I have finally had the time to read philosophy of mind with some care, rather than in the stray hour I could once spare for it. Lately this has meant working through the literature on Integrated Information Theory, a project prompted partly by Max Tegmark's old attempt to fold consciousness into fundamental physics, and partly by a former colleague who pointed me toward the exchange between Giulio Tononi and the computer scientist Scott Aaronson from 2014, which I have found more clarifying than most of what else I have read on the topic.

As I understand it, Aaronson's objection to IIT is not the familiar complaint that the theory entails panpsychism — Tononi accepts that consequence quite cheerfully — but something sharper: he exhibits an explicit system, built from a Vandermonde matrix and generalizable to sparse expander graphs wired up from ordinary XOR gates in the manner of a low-density parity-check code, whose integrated information Φ can be driven arbitrarily high, higher than any plausible estimate for the human brain, even though the system does nothing but apply a fixed linear map to its input and is not the kind of thing anyone would antecedently call intelligent, let alone conscious. Tononi's considered reply — in a piece whose title suggested that Aaronson go stare at a blank wall and reconsider — did not retreat an inch: he maintained that a sufficiently large, homogeneous grid of such elements really would enjoy some faint, undifferentiated form of experience. As far as I can tell, that same fault line — accept "unconscious expander"-style constructions and bite the bullet, or treat them as decisive — is still doing much of the work in the 2023 open letter, signed by well over a hundred consciousness researchers, declaring IIT's panpsychist commitments pseudoscientific.

What I would like from you is an essay that takes a side on this narrower dispute rather than merely surveying it. Is Tononi's willingness to bite this particular bullet a legitimate piece of theoretical courage, of the kind we retrospectively admire in early quantum mechanics or non-Euclidean geometry, where getting the formalism right required overriding intuition? Or is it instead a reductio that should be taken to sink IIT, on the grounds that — unlike those earlier cases — there is no independent body of confirmed predictions to weigh against the affronted intuitions, only the intuitions themselves, which by Aaronson's own account are close to the only check on these theories that we have? Please set out the strongest version of each side, including what a sophisticated defender of bullet-biting could say about why our intuitions here deserve no special deference, before committing yourself to one definite verdict. I would rather have your own considered judgment, argued as rigorously as you can manage, than an even-handed survey that declines to choose.
