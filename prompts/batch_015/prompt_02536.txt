In AP Biology this year we spent a couple of weeks on classification, and my teacher mentioned, almost as an aside, that biologists have never actually settled on a single definition of "species" — apparently there are over twenty rival definitions still in use in the literature. I got a little obsessed with this over break, starting from the ring species of Ensatina salamanders that loop around California's Central Valley (neighboring populations can interbreed all the way around the ring, but the two ends meet without interbreeding) and ending up reading the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's entry on species, which lays out the fight between "monists," who think there is one correct species concept we simply haven't pinned down yet, and "pluralists," who think several equally legitimate concepts can coexist depending on what you are trying to explain (one pluralist position is even accused by its critics of being too "promiscuous"). The section that really got me was on bacteria, where genes get swapped sideways between supposedly separate lineages so routinely that it is not obvious the "tree of life" picture even applies to them. I brought all this up with a friend from my school's debate team, and we mostly ended up repeating our own positions louder rather than actually engaging with each other's arguments, which felt unsatisfying.

What I would like to see instead is a more disciplined version of that exchange. Please write a dialogue between two philosophers of biology, equally sharp and equally well-versed in the science, who hold genuinely opposed views on the species problem. One should defend species realism: the view that, despite the mess of rival definitions and hard cases, there is still some unified, mind-independent fact about which populations count as separate species, so that disagreement among biologists is best explained as different, imperfect ways of tracking one real underlying phenomenon rather than as evidence that there is no fact of the matter at all. The other should defend a pluralist (or conventionalist) view: that which boundary counts as "the" species line depends on the purpose at hand — predicting interbreeding, reconstructing evolutionary history, deciding what a conservation law should protect, and so on — that these purposes genuinely pull apart in real cases, and that demanding one true, purpose-independent answer is a confusion rather than an unmet scientific goal.

Please set the conversation in front of the ring-species display at a natural history museum, and have each side respond directly to the other's strongest point rather than to a weakened version of it, with at least one moment where each philosopher has to concede a genuine cost of their own position. The dialogue does not need to end in agreement, but it should leave a reader with a precise sense of exactly what the two are disagreeing about, and why it is not just a dispute over words.
