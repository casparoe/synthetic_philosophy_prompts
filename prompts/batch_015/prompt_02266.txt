In the Pensées' cluster of fragments on "the reason of effects" (Brunschvicg 294, 298, 326, 338), Pascal argues that, since we cannot make what is just strong, we have instead "made what is strong just": custom, which is really force by another name, "creates the whole of equity, for the simple reason that it is accepted," and is itself "the mystical foundation of its authority." Yet he insists this diagnosis must be kept from ordinary people. Because "it is dangerous to tell the people that the laws are unjust," the wise should teach obedience "because they are laws," and legislators should conceal the law's origin in usurpation, precisely so as to prevent sedition. Taken by itself, this looks like a Machiavellian doctrine of the noble lie, in which seeing through justice's dependence on force licenses an enlightened few to manage the illusions of the many for the sake of order. But Pascal also holds that "true Christians" comply with such political follies not out of calculation but in obedience to God's punishment of human vanity after the Fall.

Please argue that Pascal's counsel of concealment is not, appearances notwithstanding, a version of Machiavellian noble-lie politics, and that his theology of the Fall and concupiscence changes its moral character. Engage directly with the "dangerous to tell the people" passage and Pascal's remarks on concealing the law's origin, rather than setting them aside as rhetorical excess.
