I'm designing a graduate seminar on the ethics of migration this term, and alongside the contemporary readings I'd expect to assign — Wellman's freedom-of-association defense of a state's right to exclude, Carens's cosmopolitan case for open borders, and so on — I want to pair a few historical sources, on the theory that students will engage more critically with the contemporary debate once they see how old some of the underlying disagreements really are. The trouble is that I keep getting stuck myself on exactly what Kant is committing himself to in the third definitive article of "Perpetual Peace," and I would value a second opinion before I stand in front of a room and pretend to have this settled. Here is the passage, in Ian Johnston's translation, which is the one in our course packet:

"The issue here, as in the previous article, is not one of philanthropy, but of right, and here hospitality (Wirtbarkeit) means the right of a stranger who arrives in the land of someone else not be treated by him in a hostile manner. The latter can turn the stranger away, if this can be done without bringing about his death, but so long as the new arrival acts peacefully, he cannot treat him as an enemy. The stranger cannot claim the rights of a guest (which would require a special charitable compact of friendship to make him for a certain period a member of the household) but rather the rights of a visitor. All human beings share in a right to present themselves to society, by virtue of their right to a common possession of the surface of the earth, on which, since it is a globe, people cannot be scattered for infinite distances but finally have to put up with living in close proximity to each other. But no one originally had more right to a particular place on earth than anyone else."

What I can't pin down is how much weight the distinction between the rights of a guest and the rights of a visitor is actually meant to bear. Kant grounds the stranger's claim in something that sounds quite strong and universal, an original common possession of the earth that seems to put everyone on equal footing with respect to any given territory, and he makes turning the stranger away conditional on not causing his death, which reads as a real constraint rather than an empty one. Yet the entitlement he arrives at is only a right to show up and attempt contact, with everything that would make the presence more than transient, above all any right to remain, left entirely to the discretion of whoever already occupies the land. Is there a principled rationale, on Kant's own premises, for why common possession of the earth should generate a right this thin rather than something closer to a right of residence, or is the guest/visitor distinction doing more rhetorical than argumentative work at this point in the essay? And however that question is resolved, does the passage actually give either side of the contemporary debate over a state's right to exclude any real ammunition, or is it too slender a reed for either Wellman or Carens to lean on?
