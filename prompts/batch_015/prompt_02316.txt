So I went down a bit of a rabbit hole recently on evidential cooperation in large worlds (ECL) -- started with that old Oesterheld paper on multiverse-wide superrationality and then fell into a pile of EA forum posts about "cooperating with aliens and AGIs" via correlated decision-making instead of actual causal contact. The basic pitch makes sense to me on paper: if the universe (or multiverse) is big enough, there's almost certainly a huge number of agents out there similar enough to us in how they reason that our choice to act cooperatively toward the values of distant civilizations is correlated with those same civilizations choosing to cooperate toward ours, so everyone does better in expectation even though nobody ever communicates or gets to verify anything. I can follow the logic fine (its basically Newcomb's problem / superrationality blown up to a cosmic scale) but it stays purely abstract for me -- i don't have any gut sense of what it would actually feel like to be the agent making that trade-off.

Could you write me a short story that gets at that feeling? Doesn't need to be hard sci-fi or technical at all, I just want it to follow one character (or a whole civilization, whatever works better) who has genuinely bought into ECL and has to decide whether to pay a real, painful cost right now -- resources, values, maybe lives -- for the sake of minds they will never meet, never get evidence about, and can only reason about probabilistically as "the kind of agents who'd exist somewhere in a big enough universe." I'd like it to actually sit with how strange and vertigo-inducing that is instead of resolving it too neatly -- does the character ever get to feel good about this, or does it end up feeling like faith dressed up in decision theory? If it helps as a tonal reference, think something a bit like Greg Egan or Ted Chiang: ideas-forward, but still grounded in one character's actual experience. Somewhere around 1500-2500 words feels right, though don't stress about hitting that exactly.
