Random research rabbit hole, but I need a sanity check on it. I'm about a third of the way into a novel that follows three generations of one family through a housing discrimination case, and my writing group keeps giving me the "this is getting a little preachy" note. So I went back and reread W.E.B. Du Bois's 1926 speech "Criteria of Negro Art" — you know, the one everybody quotes the "all art is propaganda" line from — except this time I read the whole thing instead of just the pull-quote, and now I cant stop poking at something that doesnt sit right.

A few paragraphs before the famous line, Du Bois says the artist uses Truth not for its own sake but as a "handmaid of imagination," and that going after Beauty honestly makes you an apostle of Truth and Right "not by choice but by inner and outer compulsion" — which sounds like propaganda is just a side effect of doing beauty right, not something you deliberately aim at. Then a paragraph or two later he says he doesn't care about art that isn't "used for propaganda," which sounds like the opposite: point your work at a cause on purpose. And later still he goes after white writers for the kind of propaganda that distorts the truth about Black life, as if truthfulness itself is some outside standard that tells good propaganda from bad. Those can't all be the same claim, right?

So here's what I want: make the strongest case you can that this is an actual inconsistency in the essay, not just a speech-giver's loose rhetoric he gets a pass on. And if you're convinced it's a real tension, tell me which of the two notions of propaganda — the inevitable byproduct of honest beauty, or the deliberate weapon aimed at a cause — is doing the real philosophical work, and which one is just coasting on the other's credibility. Mostly I'm trying to figure out if I can retire "is this propaganda" as a question I ask about my own book and just ask "is this true" instead.
