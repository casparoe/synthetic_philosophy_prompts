I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for long enough that the Dutch book argument for probabilism feels like old news at this point: if your credences violate the axioms of probability, a sufficiently clever bookie can offer you a set of bets that each look individually acceptable by your own lights but that combine into a guaranteed loss, so coherence is required on pain of a sure loss. What I'd never properly chased down, until a friend from my local meetup passed around Richard Pettigrew's SEP entry on epistemic utility arguments, is the "accuracy-first" alternative that keeps getting mentioned as a more purely epistemic rival to the Dutch book story — the line of argument, originating with Jim Joyce's 1998 paper, that you can derive the very same requirement of probabilistic coherence from the goal of accuracy alone, with no bets or bookies anywhere in sight.

Here's where I get stuck. As I understand it, you measure how accurate a credence function is at a world using some "legitimate" (i.e., strictly proper) scoring rule, with the Brier score as the go-to example, and the theorem says that any credence function violating the probability axioms is accuracy-dominated: there exists a coherent credence function that beats it in every possible world simultaneously, not merely in expectation. That's a considerably stranger and stronger claim than the Dutch book result, and while I can recite it, I don't yet feel why it's true.

Could you walk me through the underlying intuition — ideally via the geometric picture involving convex sets and projections (I gather this is how the later Predd et al. proof cleans up Joyce's original argument) — for why incoherent credences always end up dominated in this way? I'm especially interested in what concretely breaks in the argument if the scoring rule is merely proper rather than strictly proper, or if you tried to run the same argument with a rule that isn't proper at all, like plain absolute error. I'd like to actually come away seeing why incoherence has to be dominated, rather than just trusting the citation.
