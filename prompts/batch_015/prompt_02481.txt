I'm a backend software engineer, and over the past year my day-to-day work has increasingly involved wiring products up to large language models. That got me curious enough to track down and actually read Searle's 1980 paper "Minds, Brains, and Programs" rather than just absorb the pop-science version of the Chinese Room argument that circulates everywhere. Having read it, I ended up in a fairly spirited debate about it with two colleagues who studied philosophy before moving into software.

My position was this: the entire thought experiment is built around a person inside a room manually consulting a paper rule book to swap one string of Chinese symbols for another — essentially a slow, serial, old-school, rule-based symbolic AI system. Modern transformer-based LLMs don't work anything like that: there's no rule book and no discrete symbol-swapping, only a massively parallel network of simple units passing continuous-valued activations to one another, which seems structurally much closer to a brain than to a filing cabinet. Since Searle's argument seems to depend essentially on that specific serial, symbol-manipulating setup, I concluded that it was never even aimed at, and therefore has nothing to say about, a distributed, connectionist system like a neural network — so invoking the Chinese Room against claims that an LLM "understands" language struck me as a straightforward category error.

My colleagues insisted that philosophers dealt with exactly this "it's just a serial symbol-shuffler" objection decades ago, but neither of them could reconstruct the actual response when I pressed them on it. Before I either concede the point or go back and defend it further, I'd like to understand: is there a genuine philosophical reply to the idea that the Chinese Room's setup is simply the wrong kind of system to tell us anything about parallel, connectionist architectures — and if such a reply exists, what is it, and does it actually hold up?
