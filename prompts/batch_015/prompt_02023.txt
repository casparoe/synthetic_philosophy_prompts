I teach a graduate seminar on decision theory, and after a session on Newcomb's problem, one of my students raised the notion of "acausal trade" that circulates in rationalist and effective-altruism-adjacent writing online. As I understand its lineage, the idea descends from Douglas Hofstadter's older notion of "superrationality," was named and developed within the rationalist online community associated with Eliezer Yudkowsky -- including, early on, Rolf Nelson's 2007 proposal for "AI deterrence" -- and later received a more formally worked-out treatment in Caspar Oesterheld's writing on multiverse-wide cooperation. The core claim is that two agents who cannot causally affect one another at all -- whether because they are separated beyond any possible contact, occupy different branches of the quantum multiverse, or stand in a purely one-directional relationship in which one exists only as a model inside the other's reasoning -- can nonetheless each benefit by acting as the other would wish, provided each has good grounds to think its own choice is correlated with the other's, typically because each can predict or simulate the other with some reliability.

I would like to set my students a worked example, but I want to avoid the illustration that appears in almost every online explainer of this idea, in which two causally isolated civilizations simulate each other's values and cooperate to promote them. Could you construct a different, precisely specified scenario of this general kind? Please identify the two parties and what each wants, describe what each does, and specify exactly what grounds the correlation between their decisions given that no causal channel exists between them -- for instance, a correlation rooted in shared or overlapping decision-making architecture rather than mere physical symmetry, or an asymmetric case in which one party exists only within the other's model of the world. I would also like you to explain why, by the lights of ordinary causal decision theory, each party's action in your example would look like a needless sacrifice, and why a proponent of something like functional or updateless decision theory would deny this.

Finally, I would be grateful if you would take on what strikes me as the sharpest objection to the whole framework: in the complete absence of a causal link, a reputational mechanism, or third-party enforcement, what is actually supposed to stop either party from quietly reneging once it takes itself to have already secured the other's cooperation? Does the appeal to prediction and correlation supply a principled answer to this worry, or does it merely relocate the same problem to the question of how good the relevant predictions really are?
