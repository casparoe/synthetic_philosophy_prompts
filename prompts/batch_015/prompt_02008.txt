I'm plotting a novel whose emotional turning point involves a woman in her sixties discovering that the defining friendship of her adult life -- the relationship she has always privately counted as the best and most stabilizing thing that ever happened to her -- was built on a sustained deception: the friend she believed to be selflessly devoted to her had, for decades, been performing a role for reasons that had nothing to do with genuine affection. Before I write the scene, I want the philosophical ground under it to be solid rather than resting on the reader's vague intuitions about "authenticity," and I keep coming back to G. E. Moore -- usually remembered only for the naturalistic fallacy -- who actually works out a quite specific, and to me still surprising, position on exactly this kind of case in "The Ideal," the sixth chapter of Principia Ethica.

As I understand him, Moore holds that personal affection and the contemplation of beauty are the two greatest goods there are, that both are "organic unities" combining a cognition of some fine quality with a fitting emotional response to it, and -- the part that matters for my scene -- that whether the underlying belief is true or false changes the intrinsic value of the resulting state even when we hold the subject's felt experience completely fixed. He says this explicitly of personal affection, not only of aesthetic contemplation. It sits alongside an even stranger claim he makes earlier in the book, arguing against Sidgwick, that an exquisitely beautiful world with no one in it to ever perceive it is better than an equally unwitnessed, hideous one -- as though, for him, truth and reality can be sources of value even where there is no experience for them to improve.

Could you write me a dialogue between two contemporary philosophers working through this, in a way I could plausibly adapt for my characters? Elena should defend a broadly Moorean line: that those decades of contented, loving experience were, as a matter of intrinsic value rather than mere consequences, less good than they seemed -- not only now that the truth has come out, but all along, at the time they were being lived -- because the belief on which they rested was false. Marcus should hold that this gets the metaphysics of a life's value wrong: intrinsic value has to supervene on what an experience is like from the inside, so nothing about those decades themselves has changed, only her situation now. I'd like them to push hard on each other's strongest moves rather than talk past one another. At some point I want Marcus to press Elena on whether she's really committed to there being plenty of ordinary, presently happy friendships that are already quietly worthless in just this way, unbeknownst to anyone -- and to see whether she bites that bullet or finds a principled way to avoid it. I also want Elena to press Marcus, in turn, on why the discovery of a betrayal so insistently feels like it reaches back and spoils the past, rather than merely souring the present. Please don't tie the disagreement up neatly at the end -- I'd rather have a real, still-live impasse than a tidy resolution, since that's closer to what the novel actually needs.
