I've got a character in my new novel who spends the whole book fleeing the anguish of his own freedom -- basically a walking case study in Sartrean bad faith. There's a dinner-party scene where a rival character, an insufferable philosophy grad student, needs to rip the concept apart in front of everyone -- not the standard "nobody's really that self-deceived" line, something sharper. Here's the crux I want him to hammer: Sartre says bad faith can't be ordinary lying, since deceiver and deceived are literally the same consciousness, so you'd have to already know the truth to hide it from yourself. He rejects Freud's censor as a fix for that, and instead leans on non-thetic self-consciousness plus "metastable" concepts like sincerity to explain how you can evade a truth without fully not-knowing it. Argue that this doesnt actually solve the paradox, it just relocates it one level down -- give me the strongest one-sided version, no hedging, something that'd sound genuinely devastating said out loud.
