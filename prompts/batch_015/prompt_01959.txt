I recently overheard an argument between two acquaintances of mine, Mia and Theo, and I'd be grateful for your help adjudicating it, since I think it runs into real subtleties in the philosophical literature on hypocrisy and the standing to blame -- subtleties that neither of them, not being philosophers, was well placed to work through on their own.

Some context. For the past couple of years, Owen has been a mild but persistent scold about leisure flying, routinely telling friends that vacationing abroad is irresponsible given its climate impact. It recently emerged that Owen himself took four long-haul leisure trips this year, whose combined emissions dwarf what most of the friends he's needled will produce from flying over the next several years. Mia and Theo argued at length about what to make of this. As best I can reconstruct it:

(1) Mia said Owen has no business lecturing anyone about flying anymore: you don't run up that kind of footprint yourself and then scold others for doing far less of the same thing.

(2) Theo replied that this is a textbook tu quoque. Either leisure flying is bad for the climate or it isn't, and Owen's own habits don't change that arithmetic one way or the other; pointing to his hypocrisy instead of his argument just dodges the argument.

(3) Mia said she wasn't disputing the arithmetic at all. Her claim was narrower: there's a difference between a claim being true and a person having earned the standing to press that claim on someone else as a personal rebuke.

(4) Theo objected that this has an awkward upshot: it implies that the people who best appreciate how bad flying is -- because they do it, and have had to think hard about it -- are the very people least entitled to say so. "No standing," he suggested, starts to look less like a principled objection and more like a way of not having to hear something true.

(5) Mia answered that standing isn't about who best appreciates the facts; it's about whether you're addressing someone as an equal. When Owen scolds a friend, he's implicitly holding that friend to a standard he exempts himself from, and that is a distinct wrong, independent of whether the standard itself is correct.

(6) Theo then mentioned that Owen, when confronted, said he regretted the trips and had since turned down an employer-paid flight to compensate. Does that restore his standing, Theo asked, or is he simply barred from ever raising the subject again? If regret and reform fix things, then Mia's objection was never really about the flights as such, but about whether Owen actually believes what he preaches; and if regret and reform don't fix things, it's unclear what work the flights are doing that some unrelated flaw of Owen's could not have done just as well.

Please go through the exchange move by move and say who has the better of the argument at each point -- not just who wins overall. Then close with (a) whether some further distinction would clarify what is really at stake between Mia and Theo, and if so, what it is, and (b) a one-sentence verdict on whose overall position holds up better across the exchange.
