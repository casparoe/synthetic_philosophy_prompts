I'm working on a novel that turns on a scene I've rewritten four times: a small-town reporter has promised an anonymous source complete confidentiality, then discovers that the source's identity is the one piece of evidence that could stop an innocent man's execution. At my writers' group, two members ended up arguing about whether the scene even holds together as a genuine dilemma, rather than about the writing itself.

Nora said there's nothing really torn about it: a life obviously outweighs a confidentiality agreement, so the reporter should simply break the promise, and if I'd written the choice as tragic rather than obvious, I was manipulating the reader. Felix said that's too quick -- reporters who've actually done something like this still spend years feeling they owe the source something, and if the promise's force had simply vanished the moment it was outweighed, there would be nothing left to feel bad about; he called this leftover pull "moral residue" and took it as evidence that the obligation had been real, not just heavy. Nora answered that this only shows people feel bad about hard calls, not that two incompatible obligations both held: if the reporter genuinely ought to keep the promise and ought to break it, that's a straightforward contradiction, and feelings are a poor guide to what was actually still required of her. Felix replied that logical tidiness is a fact about how we've chosen to formalize obligation, not about obligation itself -- a person can owe money they currently can't repay, and the debt doesn't dissolve simply because repaying it is momentarily impossible, so why assume a promise works any differently?

Neither budged. Could you go through the exchange move by move, say who has the better of it at each point rather than just declaring an overall winner, and flag anything you think both of them are missing?
