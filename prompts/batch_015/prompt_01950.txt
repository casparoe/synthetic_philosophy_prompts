I'm writing a novel whose narrator knows, and fully believes, that her flight is perfectly safe, yet is seized by dread the moment it leaves the runway. I'd rather the scene be quietly informed by the actual philosophical difficulty in such cases than lean on the cliché that "the heart has its reasons" — so no need to survey the standard menu of emotion theories for me. As I understand it, theories on which emotions are, or essentially involve, evaluative judgments struggle here, since they seem committed to saying she judges the flight both dangerous and safe at once. Perceptual theories avoid this by treating her fear as a Müller-Lyer illusion of danger: she goes on feeling the flight as threatening even after "measuring" it and finding it safe, the way a line can go on looking longer than another after a ruler confirms they're equal. I'd like a precise account of how a sophisticated judgment theorist resists the charge of attributing outright contradiction to her, and then the specific respects in which philosophers have argued this perceptual analogy itself breaks down.
