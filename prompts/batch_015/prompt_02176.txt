In a graduate seminar on vagueness, we've been working through the two most influential "conservative" responses to the sorites paradox — views that try to preserve classical logic rather than revise it. The first is Timothy Williamson's epistemicism, on which predicates like "heap," "bald," or "tall" have perfectly sharp extensions that we are constitutively unable to know, a limitation Williamson traces to margin-for-error constraints on any knowledge that depends on inexact discriminative capacities. The second is the supervaluationist tradition originating with Kit Fine and developed further by Rosanna Keefe, on which vague predicates are associated with a range of equally admissible ways of making them precise ("precisifications"); a sentence is true (super-true) just in case it comes out true on every admissible precisification, false (super-false) just in case it comes out false on all of them, and neither otherwise.

What I haven't been able to get entirely clear on is how the two views compare once higher-order vagueness enters the picture — that is, once we notice that the line between the clear cases and the borderline cases is itself blurry, so that there seem to be borderline-borderline cases, and so on up. My impression from the assigned readings is that epistemicism handles this comparatively gracefully, simply reapplying the same margin-for-error story at each level, whereas supervaluationism runs into a much harder problem: introducing a "definitely" (D) operator to express super-truth seems to require that operator itself to have borderline cases, which forces a choice between (a) treating "admissible precisification" as itself vague, triggering another round of supervaluation and a potential vicious regress, or (b) stipulating some sharp cutoff on admissibility, which reintroduces exactly the sort of unmotivated sharp boundary the theory was designed to avoid.

Could you evaluate whether this is a fair characterization of the dialectic? Please lay out the strongest version of the higher-order vagueness objection to supervaluationism, present the most serious attempts to defuse it on the supervaluationist's behalf (for instance, by constructing a hierarchy of higher-order precisifications, or by denying that higher-order vagueness is a genuine phenomenon requiring separate treatment), and then give your own verdict on which of the two theories offers the more stable and principled treatment of higher-order vagueness overall.
