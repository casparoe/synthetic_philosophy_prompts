I'm a backend engineer, and a while back I fell into a bit of a rabbit hole that started with Dan Piponi's old blog post "Löb and möb: strange loops in Haskell," which describes a combinator called `loeb` (`loeb :: Functor f => f (f b -> b) -> f b`) for building self-referential, spreadsheet-style computations. Chasing down the reference to the actual Löb's theorem in provability logic (roughly: for any theory at least as strong as Peano Arithmetic, if the theory can prove "a proof of P implies P," then it can already prove P outright) eventually led me to MIRI's old "tiling agents" paper by Yudkowsky and Herreshoff. As I understood it, the theorem blocks a self-modifying agent from ever fully trusting, in the sense of formally certifying in advance that its conclusions will be correct, a successor (or a future version of itself) that reasons with an equally strong proof system: it can only certify something strictly weaker than itself. I've since read a bit about the proposed fixes: Fallenstein's trick of having each generation of the agent rely on a strictly weaker theory than the one before, so that trust descends rather than staying level, and the later move by Christiano and coauthors to swap out binary provability for calibrated probability, so that the syntactic Löbian argument no longer goes through directly.

I mentioned all this to a friend doing a math PhD (not in logic), and she was pretty dismissive: in her view, this is obviously just an artifact of insisting on Peano-strength first-order proof as the mechanism of trust, and the existing fixes — or simply building a bounded, probabilistic reasoner from scratch — make the "obstacle" disappear entirely, so the result can't really be telling us anything general about self-trust. I'd like a considered case for the opposite view. Please argue that the Löbian obstacle is not a quirk of one particular, now-superseded formalization, but a genuinely unavoidable feature of self-trust for any consistent, sufficiently expressive, self-referential reasoner — that the descent trick and probabilistic reflection specifically relocate the problem rather than eliminate it, so that neither actually gives a system the ability to fully vouch, in advance, for an exact copy of its own reasoning process. I'd like the argument to engage with the actual mechanics of why those two fixes fall short, rather than just gesturing at "Gödel says there are limits" — and if you think the position holds up, say a little about whether the same underlying argument has any force against a human trying to fully vouch in advance for the reliability of their own future reasoning.
