I've been working on a feature about the current wave of "harder" paternalist policy tools—binding gambling self-exclusion registers, mandatory cooling-off periods on high-interest credit, pension auto-enrolment schemes deliberately designed to be difficult to unwind—and how their defenders square them with the ostensibly liberty-respecting rhetoric of "nudge" policy more generally. That sent me back, as these things always do, to Mill's On Liberty. I expected the harm-principle passage from Chapter I to be the load-bearing text, but several of the philosophy papers I've been reading on the ethics of self-binding keep converging instead on a much less-quoted passage from Chapter V, where Mill explains why a contract of self-enslavement ought to be void even on his own liberty-maximizing terms. I've copied the paragraph below and would like help working through its logic more carefully, because the more times I reread it the less sure I am what kind of argument it actually is.

"In this and most other civilized countries, for example, an engagement by which a person should sell himself, or allow himself to be sold, as a slave, would be null and void; neither enforced by law nor by opinion. The ground for thus limiting his power of voluntarily disposing of his own lot in life, is apparent, and is very clearly seen in this extreme case. The reason for not interfering, unless for the sake of others, with a person's voluntary acts, is consideration for his liberty. His voluntary choice is evidence that what he so chooses is desirable, or at the least endurable, to him, and his good is on the whole best provided for by allowing him to take his own means of pursuing it. But by selling himself for a slave, he abdicates his liberty; he foregoes any future use of it, beyond that single act. He therefore defeats, in his own case, the very purpose which is the justification of allowing him to dispose of himself. He is no longer free; but is thenceforth in a position which has no longer the presumption in its favor, that would be afforded by his voluntarily remaining in it. The principle of freedom cannot require that he should be free not to be free. It is not freedom, to be allowed to alienate his freedom. These reasons, the force of which is so conspicuous in this peculiar case, are evidently of far wider application; yet a limit is everywhere set to them by the necessities of life, which continually require, not indeed that we should resign our freedom, but that we should consent to this and the other limitation of it."

What I can't quite pin down is whether this is really a derivation from the liberty principle or a piece of paternalism smuggled in under liberty-principle vocabulary. Mill seems to want to say that respecting a person's present choice is only ever instrumental to protecting their own good, so that a choice which permanently forecloses future choice defeats its own rationale and therefore falls outside the principle altogether—but taken at face value that would seem to license overriding a great many binding long-term commitments, and Mill all but admits this himself before immediately hedging, without ever quite specifying where the line falls. Could you help me reconstruct the argument's actual structure: what exactly is the "presumption" he invokes supposed to track, why does he think it evaporates at the moment of sale rather than having to be weighed against the value of honoring a person's own earlier self-binding choice, and how is the reasoning meant to stop short of also condemning the marriage contracts, employment contracts, and ordinary promises he goes on to discuss in the very same chapter? And, separately, if you think the argument does succeed on its own terms, I'd be curious what it implies for present-day "self-binding" tools that people sometimes later regret and try to escape—gambling exclusion orders, or apps that lock users out of their own savings—which the nudge literature tends to wave through as unproblematic exercises of autonomy rather than as anything Mill would have needed a special argument to permit.
