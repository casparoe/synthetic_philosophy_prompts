Okay so I've been chewing on this question for a while now on my own time (never took any philosophy or psych classes, just something that bugs me): when you feel scared, or angry, or whatever - is that actually just your body doing stuff that you're noticing, like your heart racing, or is it more like your mind making a judgment about the situation, or is it some third thing entirely that's neither of those? A couple weeks back I fell down a rabbit hole and read the actual original 1884 essay "What is an Emotion?" by William James (the psychologist, brother of the novelist Henry James). Most of it I can follow fine - his big claim is that we don't run because we're afraid, we're afraid because we run, so the emotion basically just IS the feeling of the bodily reaction happening. Kind of a wild claim but I get the logic of it.

Where I got stuck is a section near the end where he brings up stuff like the pleasure of a math proof or admiring a piece of music, stuff he calls "cerebral" or intellectual emotions, and he seems to admit these might not follow the same rule as fear or rage. Here's the exact part that's tripping me up:

"But a sober scrutiny of the cases of pure cerebral emotion gives little force to this assimilation. Unless in them there actually be coupled with the intellectual feeling a bodily reverberation of some kind, unless we actually laugh at the neatness of the mechanical device, thrill at the justice of the act, or tingle at the perfection of the musical form, our mental condition is more allied to a judgment of right than to anything else. And such a judgment is rather to be classed among awarenesses of truth: it is a cognitive act. But as a matter of fact the intellectual feeling hardly ever does exist thus unaccompanied. The bodily sounding-board is at work, as careful introspection will show, far more than we usually suppose. Still, where long familiarity with a certain class of effects has blunted emotional sensibility thereto as much as it has sharpened the taste and judgment, we do get the intellectual emotion, if such it can be called, pure and undefiled. And the dryness of it, the paleness, the absence of all glow, as it may exist in a thoroughly expert critic's mind, not only shows us what an altogether different thing it is from the "standard" emotions we considered first, but makes us suspect that almost the entire difference lies in the fact that the bodily sounding-board, vibrating in the one case, is in the other mute."

I've read that paragraph about five times now and I honestly can't tell if James is quietly admitting that this whole category of "intellectual" feeling is basically just a cognitive judgment with no bodily component required (which seems like it would poke a pretty big hole in his own theory), or if he's saying that pure case basically never happens in real life so it doesn't actually matter for his argument. Can you break down in plain terms what he's actually claiming here, and whether its consistent with the rest of his theory or not? And once thats untangled, where does it leave you on the bigger question - do you think this passage ends up supporting the idea that emotions are just feelings of bodily states the way James wants, or does it accidentally sneak in a concession to the "emotions are really judgments" camp?
