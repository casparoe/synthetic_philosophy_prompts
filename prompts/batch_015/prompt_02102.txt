So I went down a rabbit hole this week that started with an old ACX comments thread arguing about whether LLMs can "really" understand math, which (as these things do) mutated into people lobbing Gödel's theorem at each other, and that's how I ended up back at Lucas and Penrose. Everyone who's spent five minutes on this already knows the standard dunk on the 1961 version: Lucas says he can see that the Gödel sentence for any formal system F is true even though F itself can't prove it, therefore he isn't F — and then everyone points out this only works if Lucas also knows F is consistent, which by Gödel's second theorem F can't establish about itself, and there's no obvious reason to think Lucas can establish it about himself either. Fine, case closed, I thought I basically understood this whole debate.

Except then I actually read the relevant bit of Shadows of the Mind, and Penrose's 1994 version seems built specifically to route around that exact objection: instead of just assuming F is consistent, he has you assume you know that F is sound, constructs a new system F' (F plus the extra premise "I am F"), and grinds out a contradiction from there. That's a genuinely different and more careful argument than the 1961 one, and I don't think "well, you can't know you're consistent" actually lands on it as stated — Penrose isn't just asserting consistency out of nowhere, he's asking what it would even take for anyone to be entitled to trust a formal system's output in the first place.

I'd like you to reconstruct that 1994 F/F' argument properly (not the popsci version that just conflates it with the 1961 one) and then make the strongest case you can that it still fails, but for a real, different reason than "the consistency objection wearing a trenchcoat." My half-baked hunch — picked up from some Penrose-response piece I read ages ago and may well be misremembering — is that the argument quietly slides from "sound enough for Gödel's theorem to bite" (which is really just consistency, a comparativley cheap fact about F) to "sound in some much stronger, know-it-with-Platonist-certainty sense" that's doing all the actual work in the "I know I'm sound" premise. Feel free to run with that if it holds up under pressure, but if you think the real failure point is somewhere else entirely, I'd rather hear that argued properly than have my hunch confirmed just because I said it first.
