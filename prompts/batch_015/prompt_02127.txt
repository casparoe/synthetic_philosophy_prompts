I'm putting together a graduate seminar on medieval theories of political resistance, and Aquinas's remarks on tyrannicide have turned out to be far less tidy than the usual textbook gloss suggests. A student last week cited him, in the same breath as John of Salisbury's Policraticus, as just another medieval authority who straightforwardly sanctioned killing a bad ruler, and I realized I couldn't give a confident answer about whether that does justice to the primary texts. I'd like your help working through the question properly, in essay form.

Here is the difficulty as I see it. In the youthful Commentary on the Sentences (Book II, distinction 44, question 2, article 2 — the article that asks whether Christians are bound to obey secular power, "and especially tyrants"), Aquinas seems to endorse, almost in passing, the claim that a man who kills a tyrant to free his country is to be praised and rewarded — though it has long been disputed whether he's asserting this in his own voice or simply relaying a view he found in Cicero. Compare this with the later De Regimine Principum, where, in the chapter on safeguarding a kingdom against tyranny (editions differ on the exact numbering; it's usually somewhere around Book I, chs. 6–7), he raises the biblical precedent of Ehud's killing of Eglon, king of Moab, apparently to endorse it, and then immediately takes it back: Eglon, he points out, was a foreign oppressor of Israel, so Ehud really killed an enemy rather than assassinated a domestic ruler; he pairs this with the counter-example of the assassins of King Joash, who were punished despite Joash's own apostasy. He concludes that a private citizen who moves against a tyrant on his own initiative endangers the whole community, and that dealing with tyranny belongs to public authority. That restriction fits with two further passages: his aside in the Summa (II-II, q. 42, a. 2, ad 3) that disturbing a tyrannical regime is not itself the sin of sedition, and his general doctrine on lawful killing (II-II, q. 64, a. 3), where he compares putting evildoers to death to a physician amputating a diseased limb — a task for whoever has charge of the whole body, i.e., public authority, and never a private individual acting alone.

Write me an essay that reaches an actual verdict on how these passages fit together, rather than resting on "the texts are ambiguous." Specifically: is there any sense in which Aquinas's considered doctrine licenses a private citizen to kill a tyrant, and if so, which kind — a bare usurper with no legitimate title at all, as against a duly established ruler who has simply become oppressive? Or does his mature position restrict all legitimate killing of tyrants, of either kind, to public or communal authority, in which case the Sentences passage needs to be explained away rather than reconciled with the rest? Please say explicitly how much weight the usurper/oppressor distinction can really bear here, given that Aquinas's own handling of Ehud suggests the distinction doing the real work in his mind may be between a foreign enemy and a domestic ruler rather than between two kinds of tyrant. You may bring in how later commentators such as Cajetan or Suárez read him if it helps sharpen your case, but I want the essay to defend your own reading of Aquinas rather than survey the wider scholastic debate, and I want it to end with a clear, defensible answer, even if that answer has to be hedged or conditional.
