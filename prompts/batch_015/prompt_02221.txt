For my school's philosophy elective, we've been assigned an excerpt from Hume's An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, and I'm writing a short response paper connecting it to the modern debate over laws of nature. I follow the headline argument -- that we never actually perceive a "necessary connexion" between cause and effect, only constant conjunction -- but I keep getting stuck on the passage below, from near the end of Section VII, Part II, where Hume finally tries to define "cause":

"It appears, then, that this idea of a necessary connexion among events arises from a number of similar instances which occur, of the constant conjunction of these events; nor can that idea ever be suggested by any one of these instances, surveyed in all possible lights and positions. But there is nothing in a number of instances, different from every single instance, which is supposed to be exactly similar; except only, that after a repetition of similar instances, the mind is carried by habit, upon the appearance of one event, to expect its usual attendant, and to believe, that it will exist. This connexion, therefore, which we feel in the mind, this customary transition of the imagination from one object to its usual attendant, is the sentiment or impression, from which we form the idea of power or necessary connexion. [...] Similar objects are always conjoined with similar. Of this we have experience. Suitably to this experience, therefore, we may define a cause to be an object, followed by another, and where all the objects, similar to the first, are followed by objects similar to the second. Or in other words, where, if the first object had not been, the second never had existed. The appearance of a cause always conveys the mind, by a customary transition, to the idea of the effect. Of this also we have experience. We may, therefore, suitably to this experience, form another definition of cause; and call it, an object followed by another, and whose appearance always conveys the thought to that other. But though both these definitions be drawn from circumstances foreign to the cause, we cannot remedy this inconvenience, or attain any more perfect definition, which may point out that circumstance in the cause, which gives it a connexion with its effect."

Two things are confusing me. First, Hume gives two definitions of cause here, and they don't obviously pick out the same thing: one is about a regularity among external events (all A's followed by B's), the other about a habit of expectation formed in the mind of an observer. He seems to sense the gap himself -- he admits both are "drawn from circumstances foreign to the cause" -- but never tells us which one is actually supposed to capture what causation is. Second, my teacher mentioned that this passage is usually treated as the historical seed of "Humeanism" about laws of nature, the view associated with David Lewis on which laws are nothing but a compact summary of the total pattern of events. But if it's really the second, psychological definition doing the philosophical work for Hume, his position sounds like a claim about facts concerning our minds, not about what is out there in the world -- which seems like a much more deflationary claim than what contemporary Humeans about laws actually hold.

Could you help me work out what Hume is actually committed to here, and then explain how direct the line from this passage really is to the present-day dispute between Humeans (Lewis's regularity/best-system account) and anti-Humeans (Armstrong's necessitation relations, Maudlin's primitivism, or dispositional essentialism) about laws of nature? I'd rather understand the actual argumentative move than just repeat the "no necessary connexion" slogan.
