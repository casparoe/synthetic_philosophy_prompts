For my school's philosophy club, we each had to pick a historical thinker nobody else would choose and give a short presentation on some lesser-known part of their work, so I went with Henry Sidgwick's involvement in the Society for Psychical Research. While researching, I was struck that he wasn't a credulous Victorian chasing ghosts for entertainment: by most accounts he was almost painfully rigorous, to the point that once his colleague Edmund Gurney discovered that the Creery sisters (who had seemed to perform genuine telepathy in card-guessing tests) had actually been using a secret signaling code, Sidgwick excluded their results from consideration entirely, even though that case had originally been one of the Society's most promising. I also found out that this wasn't just idle curiosity for him: it connected to a real gap in his ethics, the "dualism of practical reason," where he worried that without some guarantee that virtue and self-interest ultimately coincide, for instance through personal survival after death, morality and rational self-interest could turn out to be in permanent, unresolvable conflict. He apparently found Kant's approach of simply postulating immortality unsatisfying and wanted genuine empirical evidence instead.

This got me wondering what Sidgwick would have made of a much later episode that, in a strange way, echoes his own card-guessing experiments: Daryl Bem's 2011 paper "Feeling the Future," which used completely conventional, peer-reviewed experimental-psychology methods to report statistically significant evidence for precognition, and which then became one of the opening scandals of the replication crisis in psychology once other labs failed to reproduce the results and critics pointed to weaknesses like flexible analysis choices. If Sidgwick could somehow assess both Bem's original methodology and the field's later reaction to it, how do you think he would have judged them, given what we actually know about his standards for statistical evidence in psychical research and his very personal stake in wanting such claims to hold up? I'd like an answer that genuinely engages with Sidgwick's documented methods and motivations rather than just concluding that he would have been generically "skeptical" or generically "open-minded."
