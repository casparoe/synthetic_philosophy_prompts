Last night a friend and I got into a long argument about what makes a life meaningful. She defended a broadly Aristotelian, eudaimonist view: meaning comes from sustained, purposive activity directed at worthwhile ends -- projects and goals that a life builds toward over time. I pushed back with an idea from Moritz Schlick's essay "On the Meaning of Life": that purposive activity, precisely because it treats the present as a mere means to some future payoff, can never itself be where meaning lives, and that only self-sufficient activity done for its own sake -- what Schlick calls play, and links to youth -- actually fuses action and outcome instead of endlessly deferring value forward. Neither of us landed a real knockout blow. I couldn't answer her charge that Schlick's view commits him to meaning fading as play recedes with age, and she couldn't really explain why Aristotle's virtuous activity -- also supposed to be an end in itself rather than a mere means -- isn't just Schlickian play by another name.

Write a dialogue between a committed Schlickian and an equally sharp Aristotelian who argue this out properly: whether the work/play dichotomy is genuine or overdrawn, and whether Schlick is really stuck saying meaning declines with age. Each should engage the other's strongest points rather than a caricature, and the exchange shouldn't resolve too easily in either direction.
