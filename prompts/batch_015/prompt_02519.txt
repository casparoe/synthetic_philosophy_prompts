I spent something like 35 years as an analytical chemist before I retired, and now I've definitly fallen into a whole philosophy phase, much to my wife's amusement. My library book club has been slowly working through some old political philosophy from the 1920s – Walter Lippmann's argument in Public Opinion and The Phantom Public that the "omnicompetent citizen" is basically a myth, and then John Dewey's reply in The Public and Its Problems, which I found tougher going but genuinely interesting, about the "eclipse of the public" and this idea that democracy is itself a form of inquiry, not so different in spirit from how we do science (though as an old lab guy I have my doubts about how far that analogy really stretches once you leave the lab). It got me thinking back on all the town meetings I used to sit through where ordinary voters were asked to decide yes-or-no on something technical, like whether to fluoridate the water supply, when most people in the room, present company excepted, I like to think, had no real grip on the underlying chemistry.

Could you write me a dialogue, set at one of those town-hall meetings, between a committed Deweyan experimentalist and a Lippmann-style defender of leaving technical calls to insulated experts, arguing over whether a question like that should really be put to a direct public vote? I'd like the Lippmann character to press the strong version of his case – not just "voters are ignorant," but the deeper epistemic claim that the "pictures in our heads" never quite line up with "the world outside," so that real self-government on technical matters is something of an illusion – and I want the Deweyan to actually have to answer that, maybe by getting into what Dewey meant by inquiry, communication, and his notion of the "Great Community," rather then just retreating to "more participation is always good." Neither one should get the easy last word here; I'd like them genuinely responding to each other's specific points back and forth rather than just talking past one another, since both sides clearly have something real going for them.
