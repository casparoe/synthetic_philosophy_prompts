Been meaning to ask this since an old LessWrong thread about Clifford's "Ethics of Belief" got revived and reminded me how much this stuff still bugs me. Clifford's argument always struck me as basically proto-rationalist orthodoxy: the shipowner who talks himself into believing his rickety emigrant-ship is seaworthy (because an inspection would be expensive and delay the sailing) is guilty of everyone's death when it sinks, and Clifford is very insistent he'd be *equally* guilty in the version of the story where the ship happens to limp into port safely, because the wrongness is in how the belief got formed, not whether it turned out true. He generalizes this into the famous "wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone to believe on insufficient evidence" line, plus a less-quoted move where he argues even a purely private, harmless false belief is still wrong, because indulging it corrodes your general capacity to test evidence and, in aggregate, poisons the epistemic commons.

James's reply in "The Will to Believe" doesnt really attack any of that head on, he just carves out a narrow exception for "genuine options" -- choices between live hypotheses that are simultaneously forced and momentous, where the intellect genuinely can't settle the matter on the merits. His best cases for this (whether a stranger will end up liking you, and the mountaineer who has to leap a crevasse and does better if he just believes he'll make it -- though I think that example's actually from "Is Life Worth Living," not the Will to Believe essay itself, if my memory of the reading group discussion serves) all lean on the belief being at least partly self-verifying, so that holding it shifts the very odds its supposedly tracking.

Here's my actual hangup: rationalist-adjacent culture is extremely Cliffordian on paper -- calibration, "make your beliefs pay rent," don't fool yourself -- but also full of stuff that's pretty Jamesian in practice, like telling founders to believe in their startup well past what the base rates support, or CFAR-adjacent "hero licensing" type advice, or "leave a line of retreat" (which only really makes sense if you're not supposed to actually use the retreat very often). So: what are the most important implications of James's self-verifying-belief argument, taken fully seriously on its own terms, for that tension? I'm especially curious whether a careful enough Bayesian can just absorb James's point for free -- fold the belief's causal effect on the outcome into the likelihoods, so it was never really "insufficient evidence" once you count that in, meaning Clifford and James were never actually disagreeing about anything a Bayesian couldn't already handle -- or whether James is smuggling in something a strict evidentialist can't accommodate even in the limit of ideal reasoning, and if so, what exactly. Don't need a full defense of either side, just the handful of implications that actually matter here.
