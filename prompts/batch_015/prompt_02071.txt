I'm a philosophy PhD student working on formal epistemology, and this term our decision-theory reading group here at the Munich Center for Mathematical Philosophy has been working through the "logical" decision theories that grew out of the MIRI/LessWrong orbit (Timeless, Updateless, and Functional Decision Theory) rather than the standard causal/evidential split most of us were trained on. Roko's basilisk keeps coming up at the margins of these sessions, but almost always in its pop-culture form, as a spooky internet legend, rather than as what it actually is: a stress test of whether these acausal frameworks can be blackmailed. One of my cohort-mates still won't discuss it out loud, half-jokingly citing the original moderation drama, which has made it oddly hard to get anyone to say plainly which specific step of the argument is supposed to be doing the illegitimate work.

For a chapter of my dissertation on informational hazards and blackmail-resistance in logical decision theories, I want to bypass the "should I actually be worried" framing and instead treat the basilisk as a conjunction of independently deniable claims, at least one of which must fail for the threat to have any real force. Here is my reconstruction of the load-bearing premises:

1. Acausal influence: an agent's present-day choices can be rationally sensitive to, and correlated with, the choices of an agent that exists only in its causal future, with no signal passing backward in time.
2. Follow-through incentive: a future superintelligence would still have sufficient reason to actually carry out punishment of non-helpers once it exists, even though doing so no longer causally contributes to its own earlier creation.
3. Simulation uncertainty: one's credence that one is currently a simulated copy being run by such an agent, rather than the original who lived through the actual decision, should be non-negligible.
4. Simulation harm: a computational duplicate of a person being made to suffer constitutes harm to, or a legitimate object of self-interested concern for, that person.
5. Exposure culpability: it is one's merely learning of the argument, rather than any independent prior act or omission, that places one in the class of agents worth punishing.
6. Privileged threat: out of the enormous space of possible future superintelligences with incompatible utility functions, including ones that would reward indifference to precisely this argument, there is a principled reason to weight this particular threat over its many logically symmetric rivals.
7. Blackmail compliance: the correct decision theory actually recommends yielding to this kind of acausal threat, rather than recommending a fixed no-negotiation policy that a sufficiently good predictor would foresee and that would thereby remove any incentive to threaten in the first place.

Could you sort these seven premises into a tier list, from the ones you take to be close to undeniable (or at least secure enough that the argument isn't plausibly failing there) down to the one you think is the most likely point of collapse for the whole argument?
