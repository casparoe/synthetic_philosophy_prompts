I've spent the past couple of months reporting a long-form feature on the afterlife of illusionism about consciousness — the view, most associated with Keith Frankish, that phenomenal experience in the philosophers' strong sense (intrinsic, ineffable qualia that we supposedly know just by having them) does not actually exist, and that the only real puzzle is why it seems to. Since Daniel Dennett's death last spring left Frankish as the position's most visible defender, my editor wants a section that goes beyond the usual "surely consciousness obviously exists, this is absurd on its face" reflex and instead lays out the objection to illusionism that I find hardest to shrug off, in a way that is honest about the view rather than a caricature of it. I have been working through Frankish's papers and blog, along with some of the replies from Kammerer and others, and I have drafted the following paragraph for that section. I would be grateful for your comments before it goes to my editor.

Here is the draft: "Illusionists do not deny that we have experiences; they deny that those experiences have the properties philosophers have traditionally ascribed to them. What we actually have, on Frankish's account, are 'quasi-phenomenal' properties — complex functional states of the brain that our introspective machinery misrepresents as phenomenal, rather as a desktop icon misrepresents a file as a small blue rectangle. The mystery of consciousness, on this view, turns out to be a mystery about introspection. But that move just relocates the puzzle to what some philosophers have started calling the Phenomenal Representation Problem: the introspective representation that supposedly generates the illusion has to be some kind of mental event, and it is unclear what makes it a misrepresentation of phenomenality, rather than simply a false belief about brain chemistry, unless it already carries some phenomenal charge of its own. Frankish's answer is that 'seeming,' here, is purely functional: a bundle of dispositions to judge, react to, and find certain claims about our experience compelling, with nothing it is like to have those dispositions. That reply seems to me to prove too little. Disposition-talk can explain why I would assert that my pain feels a certain way; it does not obviously explain why the assertion goes on feeling forced on me even once I have granted, quite sincerely, that illusionism is probably true. Ordinary false beliefs loosen their grip once we see through them. This one does not — and 'it is just an unusually stubborn disposition' sounds to me less like an explanation of that stubbornness than a redescription of it in a lab coat."

Three things would help most. First, tell me whether I have represented Frankish's actual position fairly, or whether I have quietly straw-manned the functional-seeming move somewhere in there. Second, tell me whether the "stubbornness" point in the closing sentences adds anything to the Phenomenal Representation Problem as it is usually stated, or whether it just collapses back into that problem with extra steps — and if illusionists already have a standard answer to it, I would much rather hear it from you now than discover it after the piece runs. Third, since this has to work for intelligent readers who will not follow a technical dispute about functionalism, tell me honestly whether the paragraph makes the stakes feel real, or whether it reads as a quibble over definitions.
