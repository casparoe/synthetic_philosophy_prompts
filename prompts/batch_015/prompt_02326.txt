I've been prepping a piece tied to all the Daniel Ellsberg retrospectives that came out after he passed, and it reminded me that before he was "the Pentagon Papers guy" he was an economist at RAND who wrote this classic 1961 paper on what we now call ambiguity aversion — the urn experiments where people would rather bet on a jar with a known 50/50 mix of red and black balls than one with a totally unknown ratio, even though the expected payout is mathematically identical either way, which breaks Savage's sure-thing principle. Digging around for background sent me down a rabbit hole into Keynes's "A Treatise on Probability" from 1921 (yes, that Keynes, decades before the General Theory), which seems to anticipate a chunk of this. He's got this idea of the "weight of argument," which he's adamant is a totally separate thing from the probability value itself — two claims can each be "50/50" while one rests on a mountain of solid evidence and the other on almost nothing, and he thought that difference should matter to how a rational person acts, not just the bare number. Funnily enough this is basically the same moment Frank Knight was drawing his own risk-vs-uncertainty line, though the two of them got there totally separately.

Anyway, Keynes died in '46, so he obviously never got to see Ellsberg's actual experiments or the whole "ambiguity aversion" literature that grew out of them. Genuinely curious what you think he would've made of it, though. Would he have read Ellsberg's results as basically vindicating his weight-of-argument idea? And here's the bigger question: mainstream decision theory today mostly treats ambiguity-averse choices as irrational glitches, deviations from expected utility that need explaining away. Given what Keynes actually believed about probability and rational action, do you reckon he'd have gone along with calling it "irrational," or is their a decent case he'd have insisted the ambiguity-averse person is behaving more rationally, not less?
