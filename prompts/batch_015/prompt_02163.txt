Over the last year I've become quietly obsessed with AI interpretability research, despite having no formal training in machine learning, neuroscience, or philosophy -- I just read about it for fun. What pulled me in was the story of "Golden Gate Claude": researchers at Anthropic used a technique called a sparse autoencoder to decompose the tangled internal activity of one of their language models into millions of distinct "features," one of which fired whenever the Golden Gate Bridge came up. When they turned that single feature up far beyond its normal range, the model became fixated on the bridge, at one point insisting its physical form actually was the bridge. Almost everything I read described this as though the model "had a concept" of the bridge tucked away inside it, and that was my instinct too. The longer I sat with it, though, the less sure I became that this way of talking is earned, rather than being a convenient shorthand that doesn't really deserve the word "representation" at all.

Here is the tension I can't resolve on my own. On one side, isolating a single direction inside a network, intervening on it alone, and getting a specific, predictable change in behavior seems like as strong a case for "there's a real representation here" as you could ask for in any complex system, biological or artificial -- not so different from the classic studies finding individual neurons in the human brain that fire only for one familiar face, like the so-called Jennifer Aniston neuron. On the other side, a few things give me pause. You can apparently train a small side classifier to pull almost any property out of a network's activity, even properties that are essentially made up, and it will often succeed anyway, which suggests decodability alone can't prove the network represents something rather than the classifier doing all the work. The same layer of activity, moreover, can often be carved into "features" in more than one equally tidy way, so naming one carving "the Golden Gate Bridge feature" may say as much about the researchers as the network. A comment I once read on a forum has stuck with me too: that a genuine representation ought to be able to misrepresent, to simply get something wrong, whereas a feature only ever fires or doesn't, with no obvious room for it to be mistaken about the bridge the way a belief about the bridge could be mistaken.

What I'd like is a dialogue between two thoughtful people who have each spent a long time thinking this through and landed on opposite conclusions -- one convinced that findings like this really do show that large neural networks have internal representations in a serious, non-metaphorical sense, the other convinced that even the most impressive intervention results don't add up to genuine representation, so the vocabulary is at best a useful fiction. I'd like both of them to be genuinely sharp, so neither is just there to be knocked down, and I want them to actually engage with what the other has just said rather than restating their opening position in different words each time. Most of all, I'd like the conversation to dig into what would even have to be true of something inside a network for it to count as representing something in the first place, rather than staying at the level of whether the bridge story is a cute anecdote.
