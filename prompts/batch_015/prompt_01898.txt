I have no formal training in philosophy, but I've spent a long time thinking through Kavka's toxin puzzle and reading how professional philosophers try to resolve it. Nearly everyone I've found who concludes that the toxin must actually be drunk agrees on that verdict but not on why. One line of response, which I believe traces back to David Gauthier, holds that forming the intention creates a genuinely new, backward-looking reason to drink it — something owed to your own earlier rational self — that doesn't reduce to the forward-looking cost-benefit reasoning telling you, once the money has cleared, to just skip the pain. A different response, which I believe comes from Edward McClennen's idea of "resolute choice," seems to need no new kind of reason at all: you're still simply assessing things by their consequences the whole time, only now at the level of the entire plan rather than the isolated act, so that a plan worth adopting the night before isn't open to piecemeal renegotiation the next afternoon. I can't tell whether that's a real difference in what rationality demands, or just two different-sounding descriptions of the same underlying move.

Write a dialogue between a proponent of each position. Set it the day after each has separately taken up the billionaire's offer and collected the money, so that both are now sitting together moments before noon with their own vials in front of them. Have each press the other on the hardest cases they can think of rather than settle for a quick truce, and let the conversation actually work out whether the two views could ever come apart in what they recommend, or whether one of them secretly collapses into the other.
