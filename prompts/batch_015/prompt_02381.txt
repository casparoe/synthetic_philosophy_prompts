I'm teaching a graduate seminar on modality this term, and two of my students, Marcus and Sonia, have basically hijacked my office hours for the last two weeks arguing about Kripke's footnote 13 in Naming and Necessity against Lewis's counterpart theory. I'm too close to the material to referee it myself at this point, and I don't think either one is strawmanning the other -- they're both making moves I can see the real appeal of -- so I want an outside read on who's actually landing punches where, versus just restating the same point in fancier language.

Here's roughly how it's gone. (1) Marcus opened with the standard Humphrey complaint: if what makes "Humphrey could have won the election" true is just that some other guy, merely resembling Humphrey, wins in a different universe, then Lewis hasn't told us anything about Humphrey at all -- he's changed the subject and dressed it up as if he hadn't, since the actual Humphrey couldn't care less how his lookalike fares. (2) Sonia said that only sounds damning if you assume there's some further fact about "what really happens to Humphrey himself" that counterpart theory is failing to capture, and nobody has that fact sitting around unanalyzed, including Kripke, who has to stipulate which world he's talking about rather than go find Humphrey in it. On Lewis's view, having a winning counterpart just is what "Humphrey could have won" comes to -- that's a reductive analysis, not an omission, so there's nothing left over for Humphrey to fail to care about. (3) Marcus said that's too fast, because the footnote doesn't stop at the "who cares" gag -- Kripke goes on to note that Lewis's counterpart relation need be neither symmetric nor transitive, and that once an individual can have more than one counterpart in a world, ordinary substitution -- Leibniz's Law, universal instantiation -- stops behaving: you can get "everything might not have been Humphrey" coming out true while "Humphrey might not have been Humphrey" comes out false. Kripke calls that "intuitively bizarre," and Marcus thinks it's not cosmetic -- it shows a resemblance relation isn't built to do identity's logical work. (4) Sonia answered that Lewis has a reply to exactly this in the 1983 postscript: modal operators are unselective binders capturing every free variable in their scope, so needing a "no capturing" proviso on substitution isn't a special embarrassment for counterpart theory, it's just what happens whenever you quantify into a binding context. And multiple counterparts are doing real work, not just causing trouble -- Lewis's own paper on persons and their bodies gives someone a person-counterpart relation and a seperate body-counterpart relation precisely so that a claim like "I might have switched bodies yesterday" comes out true or false the way we'd actually want it to. (5) Marcus shot back that this is exactly where Sonia's example bites her: Lewis needs two different counterpart relations to pull that off, and by his own admission in that very paper, he has no general procedure for saying which relation is operative for a given sentence -- it's just read off from context, case by case. So the view owes us a non-arbitrary way of picking the relevant respect of similarity every time, on top of everything else, whereas the Kripkean only has to stipulate once, about which world -- not separately, predicate by predicate.

They've gone another round or two since then, but that's the substance of it. Could you go through moves (1) through (5) and say, for each one, whether it actually lands against whatever came right before it, then tell me who's ahead once the dust settles -- and whether there's some consideration neither Marcus nor Sonia has raised that should actually be doing work here?
