I've spent the last couple of weeks working through Nick Bostrom's "Infinite Ethics" paper, having first come across the topic through an essay by Joe Carlsmith that circulated in a LessWrong-adjacent reading group I'm part of. I think I have a solid grip on the headline problem: if the universe (or whatever domain we're summing value over) is canonically infinite, containing unboundedly much positive and negative value, standard aggregative consequentialism collapses into what Bostrom calls "infinitarian paralysis" — since no finite act can change an already-infinite total, saving a life and ending one turn out to be ethically indistinguishable in expected-value terms. That part I follow, and it's exactly as bad as it sounds.

What I can't get a solid grip on is the main family of proposed repairs: dominance-based rules that rank infinite worlds by comparing them across expanding regions rather than via a single cardinal sum. Vallentyne and Kagan's strengthened principles, for instance, say roughly that world A is better than world B if, for every sufficiently large way of expanding some starting region, A eventually pulls ahead of B and stays ahead no matter how far you keep expanding. But this only delivers a determinate verdict once you've picked a "natural" sequence of expanding regions to compare along, and Bostrom is explicit that Vallentyne and Kagan restrict the whole approach to domains that have what they call an "essential natural order" — spatial and temporal regions plausibly qualify, since "everything within radius r" or "the first million years" are natural expanding sets, but a population of people plausibly does not — and, stretching the point past what's actually in the paper, I'd guess the branches of a quantum multiverse don't either. This feels like more than a technical wrinkle to me: if the verdict on which of two infinite worlds is better depends on which equally-natural-seeming enumeration you expand along, and different enumerations can reverse the verdict, then the dominance approach hasn't dissolved the indeterminacy that produced paralysis in the first place so much as relocated it — the ethical analogue of how a conditionally convergent series can be rearranged to sum to any value you like.

I'd like your help thinking through two things. First, is this order-dependence worry actually fatal to the whole "extensionist" strategy, or is there some principled way of privileging certain orderings (via causal structure, or some notion of explanatory priority) that would block the rearrangement objection without just being stipulated for convenience? Second, does Bostrom's alternative fix, the Extended Decision Rule — which tries to avoid ever having to rank infinite worlds by instead comparing the probability that an act leads to unbounded good against the probability it leads to unbounded bad — actually escape the order-dependence problem, or does it just swap it for the "fanaticism" issue of letting arbitrarily small differences in those probabilities outweigh any finite stakes whatsoever? I'd honestly rather hear "this is a real, unresolved crack in the theory" than a resolution that quietly smuggles in an arbitrary choice.
