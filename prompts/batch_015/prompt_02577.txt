I'm a PhD student in philosophy working mainly in formal epistemology, and this term I've been sitting in on a seminar that spent a couple of weeks on Richard Jeffrey's rule for updating on uncertain evidence. The basic idea is familiar enough: rather than assuming new evidence always arrives as a proposition you simply conditionalize on, Jeffrey's rule (often called "probability kinematics") lets the evidence instead take the form of new, less-than-certain probabilities over some partition of possibilities; the old conditional probabilities relative to that partition are held fixed, and the rest of one's credences are updated via the law of total probability using the new weights.

What's been nagging at me is a case my advisor mentioned almost as an aside: van Fraassen's "Judy Benjamin" puzzle (the name, I gather, is a nod to the 1980 film Private Benjamin). Judy, a soldier lost during a war-games exercise, starts out assigning probability 1/4 to each of four possibilities: she's on her own side's ("Blue") territory, in either its Headquarters Company area or its Second Company area, or she has strayed into the opposing ("Red") territory, again in either sub-area. Just before the radio cuts out, headquarters tells her only this: they don't know whether she has strayed into Red territory, but if she has, the odds are 3:1 that she's in Red's Headquarters Company area rather than its Second Company area. On its face this message says nothing at all about Red versus Blue — it's a purely internal claim about how Red territory divides up — so the naive expectation is that her credence in being in Blue should stay exactly at 1/2. But the natural way of extending Jeffrey's approach to this conditional, odds-form evidence — take the distribution over the four-cell partition that minimizes relative entropy with respect to her prior, subject only to the stated 3:1 constraint — yields a posterior credence in Blue of about 0.53 (van Fraassen's own figure is .5327), not 0.5. This is genuinely contested rather than a settled technicality: Grove and Halpern, and later Douven and Romeijn, have argued that 1/2 is in fact the right answer and that van Fraassen's entropy-based solution is the one that goes wrong.

I can run the entropy-minimization calculation myself, but I still don't have a real gut-level story for what's happening, and going back and forth about it with a fellow student hasn't settled anything. Could you help with two things: first, an intuitive (not just algebraic) explanation of why evidence that looks like it's "only about" the internal Red split ends up shifting credence between Red and Blue at all; and second, your own view on the moral usually drawn from the case — that there's no fully principled, non-arbitrary way to generalize Jeffrey conditionalization to evidence in this conditional form, as opposed to evidence that directly hands you new probabilities over a partition. Do you think that pessimistic conclusion is warranted, or is there a good sense in which 0.53 is simply the uniquely rational answer, with the "it must stay at 1/2" intuition being the thing that actually needs to be explained away?
