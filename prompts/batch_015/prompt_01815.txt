I got really into birding this year after a coworker basically dragged me out at 6am one Saturday, and now I'm the guy with a life list and everything. Went on a group outing with the local Audubon chapter last month, and after we dipped on a flycatcher, two of the more experienced birders in the group ended up arguing about it in the parking lot afterward, and I honestly can't stop thinking about who was right.

It started because someone brought up how in 2023 the AOS lumped Pacific-slope and Cordilleran Flycatcher back into one species (Western Flycatcher) -- they'd been split in '89 mainly on subtle differences in their calls, but later research found no consistent vocal, physical, or genetic differences where their ranges actually meet, just a mess of intermediates. Denise said this proves "species" is basically made up: a committee split them on a hunch in 1989 and un-split them on a hunch now, so nothing about the birds changed, only what taxonomists felt like calling them. Marcus said that's backwards, that's science self correcting -- they went and tested whether the claimed isolation actually held up in the field, it didn't, so they fixed it, which is the opposite of arbitrary. Denise said fine, then explain the Cassia Crossbill: a population in Idaho that looks basically the same as every other red crossbill (you need calipers to even notice the bill difference) and still got promoted to full species status in 2017, basically on the strength of its flight call -- so apparently a vocal-only difference is sometimes plenty and sometimes worthless, and nobody can tell you in advance which. Marcus said the difference is principled: Cassia Crossbills stay distinct even though other call types pass through their range every single year, while the flycatchers turned out to interbreed wherever they actually meet -- its not "vocal difference," its whether the isolation survives contact. Denise said that standard doesn't even survive contact with Mallards and American Black Ducks, who've been hybridizing for decades and are still counted as separate species by the same authorities, so apparently isolation surviving contact isn't required after all, and Marcus's "principled" line just moves whenever it's convenient.

Can you go through this move by move and say who's got the better of each exchange, not just an overall winner? And is there something they're both missing about what "species" even means here, or were they onto something real about the concept being broken?
