I spend a lot of time reading rationalist and effective-altruist blogs, and one move I keep seeing made with great confidence is the Singer-style equation of failing to prevent a death with actively causing one: if you could have saved a stranger's life for the price of a pair of shoes and didn't, you are supposedly implicated in the death in more or less the same way you would be had you killed the person outright. That always struck me as quietly sliding from an ethical claim about what we owe each other to a metaphysical one about whether omissions can be causes at all, so I went looking for what philosophers of causation actually say about the metaphysical half, half-expecting a tidy answer. Instead I found a surprisingly gnarly literature that, as far as I can tell, never comes up on the blogs themselves.

The specific knot I can't untie is what that literature calls the profligacy problem. Take its stock example: Barry promises to water Alice's plant, doesn't, and the plant dies — intuitively, his failure to water it caused the plant's death. But by the same bare counterfactual test ("if X had watered the plant, it would not have died"), the Queen of England's not watering the plant satisfies the test just as well, and so does the omission of every other person on Earth who also failed to water it. So counterfactual theorists in the tradition of Lewis, if they want Barry's omission to count as a cause while the Queen's does not, need to supply some further principle — appeals to duties, norms, or statistical normality tend to get invoked — for keeping the causal net from swallowing the whole planet, without simply smuggling ethics in to do metaphysics's work. The rival move, associated with process theorists like Phil Dowe and broadly Humean skeptics like Helen Beebee, is to deny that any omission is ever a literal cause: Dowe allows only "quasi-causation" where there is no physical process linking cause and effect, while Beebee argues we are simply confusing causal explanation with causation itself. Both camps end up committed to some fairly deflationary claims about how negligence law and everyday blame for "letting things happen" actually work.

What I'd like is to see this fought out rather than summarized. Could you write a dialogue between two equally sharp philosophers of causation — one who holds that Barry's omission is a genuine cause of the plant's death and has an actual proposal for blocking the profligacy problem, and one who denies that any omission is ever a literal cause and is willing to bite the bullet on what that implies for blame and legal liability — as they argue it out while standing over Alice's dead plant? Neither should be a pushover: each should press the other on the real weak point of their position (the suspiciously ethics-laden principles doing the work in the first view, the deflationary verdicts about responsibility in the second) and get a substantive answer back, rather than the dialogue just alternating unrebutted position statements.
