I've been down a rabbit hole for the last few weeks, reporting a piece on paradoxes of rational choice, and I can't quite let go of the centipede game. The setup is well known: in Rosenthal's original 1981 version, traditionally a hundred rounds long — hence the name, since the game tree's decision nodes are meant to look like a centipede's legs — two players alternate between taking an ever-growing pot and passing it along. Backward induction says a purely rational first mover should take the tiny opening pot immediately, since that's what rationality demands at the last node, which makes taking rational at the second-to-last node too, and so on back to move one. The usual way this gets written up for a general audience is as an embarrassment for economic theory: real subjects, as McKelvey and Palfrey found when they first tested it, keep passing for many rounds instead, so the moral becomes "people are nicer than rational choice theory gives them credit for." That framing has always struck me as too easy.

What keeps drawing me back is a harder problem that has nothing to do with any experiment. The backward-induction argument seems to require common knowledge, from the first move onward, that both players will act rationally at every node — including nodes that, by the argument's own logic, should be reachable only if somebody has already failed to act rationally. Once a player actually finds themselves at such a node, it's genuinely unclear what "common knowledge of rationality" is still telling them to do, if anything. This is more or less what divided serious decision theorists from the late eighties onward: Aumann argued that common knowledge of rationality, carefully enough defined, still forces the backward-induction outcome even off the equilibrium path, while Binmore, and separately Pettit and Sugden, argued that the whole argument collapses the moment a single node is reached that it says shouldn't have been reachable at all.

For the piece, I'd rather dramatize this tension than summarize it again in my own words, and I suspect a dialogue will do more work than another paragraph of exposition. Could you write one, set at round 90 of a 100-round centipede game — pot substantial, both players long past the point where backward induction said a rational agent would ever have stopped — between two characters, a game theorist and a philosopher of rationality, who disagree about what to do next? One should maintain that backward induction still governs play from here, whatever got them to this point; the other should argue that reaching this very node has already falsified whatever common-knowledge-of-rationality assumption was supposed to generate that recommendation, so simply invoking it now is question-begging. I want real back-and-forth, with each character pressing the other's actual argument rather than a caricature of it, and neither conceding too easily. They needn't settle who's right, but by the end I'd like them to at least isolate the specific claim about rationality, knowledge, or counterfactuals that would have to be true for one side to be correct rather than the other.
