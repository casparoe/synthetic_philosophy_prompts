I am several drafts into a novel set in the courts of Hanover, Paris, and the Dutch Republic in the late 1680s, one of whose principal characters is a philosopher modeled closely on Leibniz. The book's central set piece is a confrontation, invented but historically anchored, with a Jansenist theologian modeled on Antoine Arnauld: I've learned that the real Arnauld, on receiving a summary of Leibniz's Discourse on Metaphysics through their mutual go-between, the Landgrave Ernst von Hessen-Rheinfels, fixed almost obsessively on a single article out of thirty-seven, the thirteenth, convinced it hid a fatalism that made God the author of sin and left no room for human freedom. I'd like my philosopher-character's rebuttal, several chapters later, to track what Leibniz actually argued in that article rather than settling for the shorthand you get in survey courses — "everything happens for the best, so stop worrying about it." Rereading the article itself, though, I find the actual machinery harder to hold onto than I expected, and I'd like help getting it straight before I put words in his mouth.

Here is the passage (Section XIII of the Discourse, in George Montgomery's early-twentieth-century English translation):

"But does it not seem that in this way the difference between contingent and necessary truths will be destroyed, that there will be no place for human liberty, and that an absolute fatality will rule as well over all our actions as over all the rest of the events of the world? To this I reply that a distinction must be made between that which is certain and that which is necessary. Every one grants that future contingencies are assured since God foresees them, but we do not say just because of that that they are necessary. [...] In order to meet the objection completely, I say that the connection or sequence is of two kinds; the one, absolutely necessary, whose contrary implies contradiction, occurs in the eternal verities like the truths of geometry; the other is necessary only ex hypothesi, and so to speak by accident, and in itself it is contingent since the contrary is not implied. This latter sequence is not founded upon ideas wholly pure and upon the pure understanding of God, but upon his free decrees and upon the processes of the universe. Let us give an example. Since Julius Caesar will become perpetual Dictator and master of the Republic and will overthrow the liberty of Rome, this action is contained in his concept, for we have supposed that it is the nature of such a perfect concept of a subject to involve everything, in fact so that the predicate may be included in the subject ut possit inesse subjecto. We may say that it is not in virtue of this concept or idea that he is obliged to perform this action, since it pertains to him only because God knows everything. [...] I say that that which happens conformably to these decrees is assured, but that it is not therefore necessary, and if anyone did the contrary, he would do nothing impossible in itself, although it is impossible ex hypothesi that that other happen. For if anyone were capable of carrying out a complete demonstration by virtue of which he could prove this connection of the subject, which is Caesar, with the predicate, which is his successful enterprise, he would bring us to see in fact that the future dictatorship of Caesar had its basis in his concept or nature, so that one would see there a reason why he resolved to cross the Rubicon rather than to stop, and why he gained instead of losing the day at Pharsalus, and that it was reasonable and by consequence assured that this would occur, but one would not prove that it was necessary in itself, nor that the contrary implied a contradiction, almost in the same way in which it is reasonable and assured that God will always do what is best although that which is less perfect is not thereby implied. [...] Now every truth which is founded upon this kind of decree is contingent, although certain, for the decrees of God do not change the possibilities of things and, as I have already said, although God assuredly chooses the best, this does not prevent that which is less perfect from being possible in itself. Although it will never happen, it is not its impossibility but its imperfection which causes him to reject it. Now nothing is necessitated whose opposite is possible."

A little further on, in Section XXX, applying the same reasoning to the case of Judas, Leibniz adds:

"The only question, therefore, which remains is why this certain Judas, the betrayer who is possible only because of the idea of God, actually exists. To this question, however, we can expect no answer here on earth excepting to say in general that it is because God has found it good that he should exist notwithstanding that sin which he foresaw. This evil will be more than overbalanced. God will derive a greater good from it, and it will finally turn out that this series of events in which is included the existence of this sinner, is the most perfect among all the possible series of events."

What I can't quite pin down is what independent work the distinction between "certain" and "necessary" — or between a connection that is "necessary ex hypothesi" and one that is "absolutely necessary" — is actually doing. Leibniz wants to hold, all at once, that Caesar's crossing the Rubicon is written into his complete concept from eternity, that God installs him in existence only because the entire series of events he belongs to is "the most perfect among all the possible series," and yet that Caesar could have stopped at the river without any contradiction resulting. Is the appeal to "hypothetical necessity" doing genuine philosophical work here, or is it simply ordinary necessity relabeled, so as to dodge the charge of fatalism without actually meeting it?

I suspect part of the answer turns on a background doctrine that this passage presupposes without quite stating: that a possible individual is only fully specified relative to the entire possible world it belongs to, so that a "Caesar" who stops at the Rubicon would, strictly speaking, be a different individual belonging to a different, merely possible world, and never a live alternative for our Caesar. If that's right, I'd like to understand how much weight that background doctrine is actually carrying, and whether it rescues the freedom Leibniz wants or just relocates the fatalism from the level of the individual to the level of God's choice among whole worlds. I'd rather my philosopher-character make the strongest honest version of this argument, pressure points and all, than have him retreat into the kind of vague gesture at "predestination versus free will" that would be easy to write but wouldn't really be Leibniz.
