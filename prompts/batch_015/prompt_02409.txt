Okay so here's a rabbit hole I fell into last week: I was reading up on decision theory (started from an AI alignment blog, then went way off the rails on wikipedia) and ran into this old thought experiment called "Death in Damascus." From what I can tell the setup is: a guy in Damascus has a midnight appointment with Death, and Death is basically a perfect predictor of where the guy's gonna be. So the guy tries to dodge Death by fleeing to Aleppo instead -- except whichever city he actually picks turns out to be exactly where Death already decided to show up. The annoying part is theres no stable answer: if you convince yourself Death will be waiting in Damascus so you flee to Aleppo, you now have reason to think Death predicted THAT and is waiting in Aleppo, so you should've just stayed, and round and round it goes. Apparently this is a classic case where causal decision theory gets stuck (no choice is "ratifiable," or w/e the term is), while evidential decision theory just shrugs and says both options are equally doomed.

I tried explaining this to a couple coworkers over lunch and mostly got blank stares, so now im convinced it'd land way better as a joke than as a logic puzzle. I don't want a generic "a philosopher and Death walk into a bar" type thing -- I want the actual punchline to hinge on the specific weirdness of the setup (the perfect predictor, the flip-flopping with no stable choice, CDT vs EDT disagreeing about it, ratifiability, whatever angle works best). Basically, someone who actually knows this stuff should get an extra laugh that a random listener wouldn't, not just a generic "haha nerds" reaction.
