Been lurking on rationalist-adjacent blogs/forums for like a decade now and this argument happened at my LW meetup reading group last week and it's been living in my head since. Someone had linked Scott Alexander's "My Left Kidney" post (the one about non-directed kidney donation to a stranger) and it turned into a whole thing between two people, Marcus and Ines, about whether donating a kidney to a stranger is supererogatory or actually obligatory. Trying to reconstruct it fairly:

1. Marcus: if the expected-value math in that post is even roughly right (small risk to you, years of healthy life for a stranger), then not donating isn't just "failing to go above and beyond" — its straightforwardly wrong, same logic as Singer's drowning child. "Supererogatory" is just a label we invented so we don't have to feel bad about not doing the math.

2. Ines: says thats too fast. Morality has to leave some agent-centered slack — your own body and risk tolerance have to count for something in your own decisions, or the demands of morality become limitless and eat themselves. If Marcus is right about kidney #1, the same argument runs on kidney #2, bone marrow, a liver lobe, eventually all of it, which is absurd, so something upstream in his reasoning has to give before we get there.

3. Marcus: ok, but then where's the principled stopping point? Nobody's ever given a non-arbitrary line between "drowning child case, everyone agrees you must act" and "donate all your spare organs, nobody thinks that." A continuum with no defensible cutoff is evidence there's no real obligatory/supererogatory distinction, just vibes dressed up as a category.

4. Ines: no sharp line doesn't kill the distinction — plenty of real categories are vague at the edges (bald vs. not-bald) without the difference between clear cases being fake. Marcus is basically running a sorites argument, and sorites arguments don't actually prove nobody's bald.

5. Marcus: fine, granting that — but then she owes an actual answer for the kidney case specifically, since by Scott's numbers it isn't a borderline case, and she's using "vagueness in general" to dodge doing the math on this particular one.

6. Ines: the math already smuggles in the thing in dispute — that a stranger's marginal welfare and your own bodily risk convert at some fixed exchange rate. Whether they do is exactly the agent-relative-vs-impartial question they're arguing about, so leaning on "just run the numbers" here is question-begging.

They both dug in and it fizzled out unresolved. Can you go through this round by round and say who's got the better of it at each step (not just an overall winner), and flag anything about supererogation as a concept that you think both of them are missing which would've moved the conversation forward?
