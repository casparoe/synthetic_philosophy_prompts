I'm taking a seminar this semester on nineteenth-century American constitutional thought, and we recently spent two sessions on Frederick Douglass's break with the Garrisonians, especially his 1860 Glasgow address "The Constitution of the United States: Is It Pro-Slavery or Anti-Slavery?" What struck me is how self-consciously textualist his method is there: he refuses to rest anything on what the framers privately wanted, or on the fact that many of them were slaveholders who surely meant to protect their property, and instead argues clause by clause — the three-fifths clause, the migration-or-importation clause, the fugitive-slave clause, the insurrection clause — that on its actual words the document nowhere names or requires slavery, while the Preamble commits the government to objects (union, justice, domestic tranquility, the general welfare, the blessings of liberty) that he judged slavery to contradict at every point. A few legal historians I've come across now describe this as anticipating "original public meaning" originalism by more than a century, as against the older "original intent" approach.

That background is what makes Douglass's afterlife in current constitutional law so interesting to me. Justice Thomas opened his Grutter v. Bollinger dissent by quoting the "let him alone" passage from Douglass's 1865 speech "What the Black Man Wants" against race-conscious admissions, and two decades later, in Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard, he wrote separately to offer what he called an originalist defense of "the colorblind Constitution" — while Justices Jackson's and Sotomayor's dissents in that very same case invoked Douglass right back, on behalf of the opposite conclusion. A friend of mine in law school and I got into a long argument about this after a talk on campus, and neither of us could really settle it, which is partly why I'm bringing it to you. It's a strange situation: sitting justices on opposite sides of the very same case, each certain that Douglass is on their side, when in that same Reconstruction-era period Douglass was also demanding vigorous federal enforcement of Black suffrage, land, and civil equality for the freedpeople — which doesn't sound like a general brief for laissez-faire, colorblind government.

So here's what I'd actually like from you: not a survey of who quotes him, but your own best reconstruction of his view. Reasoning from Douglass's actual arguments and commitments rather than his general reputation as an antislavery icon, how do you think he would have come down on the substantive interpretive question at stake in a case like Students for Fair Admissions — does the Fourteenth Amendment's original meaning, read the way he read the Constitution in 1860, mandate strict colorblindness, or does it permit, or even require, race-conscious remedies for the specific group it was written to protect? And, relatedly, would he recognize today's original-public-meaning originalists as continuing his own hermeneutic, or would he conclude that they have kept his rhetorical style while quietly discarding the substantive commitments that gave his textual arguments their force in the first place?
