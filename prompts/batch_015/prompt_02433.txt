I'm reporting a piece on the philosophical arguments behind claims that sufficiently advanced AI systems will inevitably converge on stable, coherent goals, and before writing a word about AI, I want to understand the underlying decision-theoretic argument on its own terms, independent of what it might mean for machines.

The core claim is that an agent with incomplete preferences — some pairs of options it simply refuses to rank — can be money-pumped into a guaranteed loss, which is why completeness gets defended as a genuine requirement of rationality, with transitivity, continuity, and independence following once completeness is secured. The most developed version of this argument, due to the philosopher Johan Gustafsson, uses a "forcing" pump: the trades are arranged so that the agent ends up, no matter what it chooses at the critical node, at a point where a standing assumption — that what's rational to do at a later choice node cannot depend on the path taken to reach it — implies it isn't irrational to pay for something it could have had for free.

The philosopher Elliott Thornley, in a paper pointedly titled "There Are No Coherence Theorems," has objected that the trap only closes if you accept that assumption. Deny it, and allow an agent's assessment of its options to depend on its own history of refusals — on which options it already turned down earlier in the sequence — and the same agent can preserve its gaps in preference while walking away from every trade unscathed.

Write an essay that reconstructs this move and countermove with enough precision for a reader who already understands ordinary Dutch-book reasoning, and then argue for a verdict: is that path-independence assumption itself a requirement of rationality, so that the pump succeeds and coherence really does demand complete preferences — or is history-sensitive choice rationally permissible, in which case the coherence case for expected-utility maximization fails at its very first axiom? Don't hedge — commit to a side.
