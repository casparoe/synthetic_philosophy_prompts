I've spent a fair amount of time on rationalist-adjacent forums over the years, and the two-envelope paradox is one of those puzzles that keeps resurfacing in ACX open threads and old LessWrong-style discussions, usually accompanied by someone confidently declaring that "it's just an improper prior" before everyone moves on. Having gone back and actually read around in the small technical literature on this (Broome, Christensen and Utts, Jackson/Menzies/Oppy, Clark and Shackel, Chalmers, and others, scattered mostly across Analysis and Mind, with a stray entry or two in the statistics journals, through the 1990s and 2000s), I've come to think the situation is considerably murkier than that one-liner suggests, especially once you bring in Chalmers' 2002 "St. Petersburg" variant of the paradox, where the prior over envelope contents is perfectly legitimate and normalizable, yet has infinite expectation, and the pull to switch reappears regardless.

For context, the bare-bones setup is: you're holding an envelope with some amount of money, you're told the other envelope contains either twice or half as much, and the suspicious argument runs that, whatever amount X you find inside, the other envelope's expectation is (1/2)(2X) + (1/2)(X/2) = 5X/4, so you should switch — which can't be right, on pain of the same argument applying symmetrically to the other envelope. Given that setup, I'd like you to rank the following six diagnoses of what exactly has gone wrong, ordering them from the one that comes closest to a full, correct dissolution of the paradox (including the hard, infinite-expectation version) down to the one that is least successful or most confused: (1) the switching argument tacitly assumes a flat prior over all positive amounts, and no such normalizable prior exists; (2) the argument equivocates between two different quantities both called "the smaller amount" — one appropriate to the "you're holding the larger envelope" branch, one to the "you're holding the smaller" branch — so the algebra never legitimately goes through; (3) the puzzle conflates two distinct scenarios, an unopened-envelope version that is genuinely symmetric and gives no reason to switch, and an opened-envelope version where switching can be rational given some specific prior, with the illusion of a universally compelling argument arising from sliding between the two; (4) Chalmers' observation that a legitimate, countably additive, properly normalized prior can still have infinite expectation, so a version of the paradox survives the "improper prior" fix, with the real lesson being about the perils of naive expected-value reasoning under infinite expectations rather than about normalizability as such; (5) the point that for any single coherent prior you actually commit to, switching turns out to be favored for some values of X and disfavored for others, so there is no prior that vindicates "switch no matter what you see" — the inference is just invalid, full stop, rather than revealing of anything deep; and (6) the deflationary line that phrases like "the expected gain from switching, unconditionally" are not well-formed without a specified reference class, so the whole appearance of paradox is a symptom of loose talk about expectation rather than a substantive puzzle about probability or rationality.
