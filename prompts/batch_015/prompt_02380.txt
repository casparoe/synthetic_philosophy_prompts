Ok this has been rattling around in my head since election night. There's this old result — Good's theorem, from a 1967 I.J. Good paper — showing that a Bayesian expected-utility maximizer should always assign non-negative value to free evidence, basically because worst case you just act exactly like you would've without it. Seems like rock solid math. But last November I made the mistake of glancing at a forecasting model like an hour before polls closed, saw it was already like 97% for the winner, and it just completely deflated the whole night for me even though there was nothing left for me to actually decide or do differently either way — I was gonna sit there and watch the results roll in regardless. Same thing happens with sports scores and book/show spoilers all the time. By the theorems own logic that kind of info should be worth exactly zero to me if it can't change my actions, not negative, but it sure felt negative. Am I just misapplying it, or is this basically the gap that all the "information hazard" talk on here is gesturing at? Curious what you think are the most important implications of Good's theorem, and where it actually stops applying, for how much weight I should put on the very LW-coded intuition that a good bayesian always wants more evidence.
