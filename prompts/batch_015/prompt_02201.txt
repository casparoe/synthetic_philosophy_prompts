So a couple nights ago a friend and I got into a two-hour arguement (derailed our entire movie night, whoops) about Parfit's hitchhiker -- the case where you're dying of thirst in the desert and a driver will only give you a ride if she's confident you'll actually pay her once you're safe in town, and she's basically a perfect lie detector so bluffing won't work. We agreed fast that you should pay once you get to town, since otherwise you never get picked up to begin with, but then we spent way too long arguing about *why* that's supposed to be the rational thing to do, which somehow turned into a fight about whether two different "fixes" from decision theory are actually the same fix wearing different clothes.

My friend kept insisting that Gauthier's "constrained maximization" and this "resolute choice" thing from McClennen (I'd first run into it through Kavka's toxin puzzle, not the hitchhiker) are basically the same move: pick a good policy ahead of time and stick to it even once sticking to it stops looking good in the moment. I felt like that was flattening a real distinction but couldn't actually say what the distinction was, and it was midnight so we just agreed to disagree, badly.

Can you settle this properly? I don't want the "here's what the hitchhiker problem is" explainer -- I basically know the setup already. What I want is a real breakdown of how constrained maximization and resolute choice differ as answers to why paying is rational: like, are they even evaluating the same kind of thing (dispositions vs. plans vs. something else), is there some variant of the hitchhiker or a nearby case where they'd actually recommend different actions, and does either of them do a better job than the other of cashing out what Parfit himself meant by "rational irrationality"? And if it turns out my friend is basically right that it's a distinction without a difference, I want to know that too, and why people in the literature bother treating them as separate positions.
