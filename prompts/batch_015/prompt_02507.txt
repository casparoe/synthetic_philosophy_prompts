So this came up in my philosophy of science reading group last week and it turned into a whole thing. We'd just covered the "problem of old evidence" -- the standard example is that the weird precession in Mercury's orbit (about 43 arcseconds a century that Newtonian physics couldn't explain) had been known for like 50 years before Einstein, and yet GR "predicting" that exact number is treated as huge confirmation of the theory even though no new data came in. Thats awkward for Bayesians, since if you already know something for certain, conditionalizing on it can't move your credence in anything.

Theo's fix (basically Garber's move): what actually confirmed GR wasn't the old datum itself, it was Einstein discovering that GR entails that number. Before he did the math, nobody -- including him -- knew for sure the theory implied exactly that, so the logical fact itself was new, and updating on that is what did the confirming. Naomi said that's a dodge: Bayesian agents are supposed to be logically omniscient -- they're assumed to instantly know every logical consequence of everything they believe, no lag for working things out -- so you can't lean on "he didn't know the entailment yet" without quietly junking the idealization that makes the whole framework rigorous in the first place. Theo said every real application of Bayesian reasoning to actual science already drops logical omniscience somewhere, so this isn't a special exception invented to dodge one embarrassing case, its just being honest about who's doing the reasoning. Naomi replied: fine, then tell me how you assign a prior probability to a bare logical claim like "GR implies that precession value" -- that's not a contingent fact about the world, so where does the number even come from? Without an answer you haven't solved the puzzle, you've just relocated it somewhere harder to inspect.

At that point Theo switched to Howson's idea instead: forget priors over entailments, just ask what your credence in the Mercury data would've been if you hadn't already known it, and use that counterfactual number to measure the confirmation. Naomi wasn't buying that either -- she said this means imagining a version of you that's missing one specific belief while everything else stays put, and there's no fact of the matter about what that hypothetical person would believe, so now the "solution" is just whatever number you feel like plugging in.
