I don't have any background in philosophy or psychiatry, but over the past year I've been slowly working through Ian Hacking's books after finding a secondhand copy of "Mad Travelers" in a used bookshop, and I've gotten fairly absorbed in his idea of looping effects. What stuck with me most from that particular book is the "ecological niche" framework he uses to explain the nineteenth-century fugue epidemic: his claim that a transient mental illness needs four things lined up at once, which he calls vectors — an existing medical taxonomy for the new behaviour to slot into (fugue was awkwardly positioned between hysteria and epilepsy, the two dominant neurological categories of the day), a cultural polarity that lets the same behaviour be read as either virtuous or vicious (the fugueur could be seen either as an adventurous traveler or as a common vagrant), observability (the behaviour has to be the kind of thing other people notice as strange), and release (the diagnosis has to offer some kind of escape). I keep a small personal blog where I write up whatever I've been chewing on, and I've drafted a post trying to run the "TikTok tic" phenomenon from around 2020–2021 — where a large number of teenage girls started presenting with sudden Tourette's-like tics that clinicians eventually described as a form of "mass social media-induced illness" rather than true Tourette's — through those same four vectors. I mentioned the draft to a friend who studied philosophy years ago, and something she said has been nagging at me since: that I might be quietly sliding between "looping effect" and plain old social contagion as though they were the same thing, though she couldn't say more without rereading Hacking herself.

The mapping in my draft goes roughly like this: the pre-existing taxonomy is supplied by Tourette syndrome and functional neurological disorder, two recognised and actively studied categories the new cases fell between, much as fugue once fell between hysteria and epilepsy; observability is satisfied about as completely as it could be, since the whole phenomenon exists on video; release comes from the sympathy, community, and exemption from ordinary expectations that a diagnosis-shaped label provides; and for the cultural-polarity vector I've argued the relevant virtue/vice pair isn't tourism and vagrancy but something more like authentic self-expression versus attention-seeking for clout, since both are ways of describing the same footage depending on how charitable you're being. I then go further and claim the case shows genuine looping, on the grounds that once "TikTok tics" became a named, publicly discussed thing, with clinicians and commentators describing and debating it, the actual presentation of symptoms narrowed and converged over time into something more recognisable and shareable, which in turn drew in more people who saw themselves in it.

What I'd really like your honest comments on is that last move, and my friend's worry. Is there a genuine difference, on Hacking's own terms, between a case like this and a proper looping effect — something that might turn on whether the people involved are reflectively responding to an official classification and the expert discourse around it, rather than just picking up a behaviour they watched someone else perform? And separately, does the cultural-polarity vector I've proposed actually do the same kind of work that tourism and vagrancy did for fugue, or is that the weakest joint in the whole argument? More generally, I'd welcome any comments on whether the comparison is genuinely illuminating or just superficially cute, and I'd much rather hear now that I've overreached somewhere than post something that only sounds clever.
