I'm prepping a session for my graduate seminar on comparative philosophy of language, built around Gongsun Long's White Horse Dialogue from the Gongsun Longzi. Rather than just lecture on the received readings, I've drafted a short framing note that stakes out a claim of my own, and I'd like your candid comments on it before I bring it in front of the students, some of whom will have read Hansen and Graham more recently than I have.

Here's the note. Two of the dialogue's arguments for "a white horse is not a horse" — the one turning on 'horse' naming a shape while 'white' names a color, and the one observing that a horse of any other color can satisfy a request for 'a horse' but not a request for 'a white horse' — trade, as Chris Fraser's SEP entry on the School of Names concludes after surveying the rival readings, on sliding between two senses of 'is': the 'is' of class-inclusion (white horses are among the horses) and the 'is' of strict identity (the class of white horses just is the class of horses). That diagnosis is not new. What I want to add is that this slide is made unusually easy, and not merely available, by a further fact about Classical Chinese: because its common nouns behave more like English mass nouns ('gold,' 'furniture') than count nouns, and because the language has no articles and no obligatory number-marking, a listener has no cheap grammatical handle — nothing like English 'a' versus 'the' — for keeping the class-inclusion reading and the identity reading apart. On this view, Chad Hansen's mereological "mass noun hypothesis" about Classical Chinese semantics, usually pitched as a rival to the identity/class-inclusion diagnosis, is better read as explaining why that diagnosis's equivocation was so exploitable in Classical Chinese specifically, rather than as a competing solution to the puzzle in its own right. I'm aware Hansen's stronger mereological claims have taken real hits, including from Fraser himself, so I want to rest my point on the comparatively modest, grammar-only observation about articles and number-marking, not on the full "stuff" ontology.

I'd value three things from you. First, a fidelity check: am I representing Hansen's and Fraser's positions fairly, or have I flattened either of them into something they wouldn't recognize? Second, a substance check: is the move from "the equivocation is available" to "the equivocation is unusually exploitable given the grammar" doing genuine philosophical work, or have I just dressed up a distinction without a difference in linguistic clothing? Third, a stress test: can you construct, or assess one I half-remember hearing at a conference ("decaf coffee is not coffee"), an English sophism with the same rhetorical shape as the white horse argument, and tell me whether it falls as flat in English as my account predicts, or lands just as well, which would count as evidence against me?
