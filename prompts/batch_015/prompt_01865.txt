I've spent a fair amount of time over the years following the debate over coherence arguments on LessWrong and the Alignment Forum: the cluster of claims, going back to Omohundro and given their canonical statement in Yudkowsky's "Coherent decisions imply consistent utilities," that any sufficiently advanced agent that isn't leaving free money on the table must be representable as an expected-utility maximizer. I recently went back down this rabbit hole after rereading Rohin Shah's old rebuttal about goal-directedness, the "There are no coherence theorems" post, and Elliott Thornley's work on using incomplete preferences to keep AI systems shutdownable, and I still can't decide who has the better of it. A friend of mine, who is more sympathetic than I am to the "coherence is basically destiny" camp, and I have been arguing about this on and off for weeks without getting anywhere, so I'd like you to do what we couldn't and actually stage the argument properly.

Please write a dialogue between two people who both know this literature cold. One of them, Vera, holds that any sufficiently advanced, resource-using agent that survives long enough will come to act as if it's maximizing some utility function — not because of any single theorem in isolation, but because every proposed escape route seems to either smuggle in a utility function under another name or leave the agent exploitable. She has a ready response to the "incomplete preferences" line of argument, including something like Gustafsson's forcing money-pump for Completeness, and she suspects that agents which dodge that will simply get "trammelled" into completeness anyway once they face a rich enough range of choices. The other, Marcus, thinks this whole picture proves far less than advertised. He points out that being representable as an expected-utility maximizer is compatible with totally non-goal-directed behavior (a thermostat or a twitching robot both technically qualify), that none of the standard representation theorems actually say what people think they say about dominated strategies, and that agents with genuine preferential gaps — say, between trajectories in which shutdown happens sooner rather than later — can adopt simple policies that make them invulnerable to the money-pumps typically used to force completeness on them. For Marcus this isn't just an abstract dispute: it's more or less his best hope for building agents that won't resist being switched off.

Have them actually engage with each other's strongest moves rather than talk past one another. Marcus should press Vera on whether "coherence" is doing any real work once we notice that any policy whatsoever can be rationalized as maximizing some utility function, and Vera should have more to say in response to the preferential-gaps proposal than just restating the money-pump intuition. I'd rather the dialogue end with the actual crux exposed than with either side getting flattened. Set the scene however feels natural to you; I keep picturing this happening at the tail end of a meetup, after almost everyone else has left, in front of a whiteboard still covered in half-erased decision trees, but that's just a suggestion.
