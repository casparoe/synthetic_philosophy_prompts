A friend of mine and I spent most of a long train journey arguing about theories of reference, and I came away only half persuaded of a conclusion that still doesn't sit right with me. We had just finished a unit on definite descriptions in our philosophy of language seminar, and her position was that Russell's theory of descriptions is, for all practical purposes, a dead theory. Her reasoning was that Kripke's Naming and Necessity dismantled it in three separate ways: the modal argument (names are rigid designators, picking out the same individual in every possible world in which it exists, whereas the descriptions Russell would associate with an ordinary name are typically not rigid, so the two cannot mean the same thing); the epistemic argument (if a name were synonymous with some description, identity claims like "Aristotle was the teacher of Alexander" would be knowable a priori, when in fact they are perfectly contingent, empirical claims); and the Gödel-Schmidt case (we go on referring to Gödel with the name "Gödel" even in a scenario where the one description we associate with him, "prover of the incompleteness theorem," turns out to be true of someone else entirely). Since all three arguments show that a name's reference cannot be fixed by an associated description, she concluded that the machinery Russell built in "On Denoting" to handle phrases like "the present King of France" has effectively been superseded, and that logic courses only keep teaching it out of habit rather than conviction.

I couldn't mount a good defense on the spot, but the argument has kept nagging at me since. Assuming Kripke's three arguments are exactly as strong as she says, does it actually follow that Russell's theory of descriptions is obsolete in the way she claims? And if her reasoning overreaches somewhere, I would like to understand precisely where.
