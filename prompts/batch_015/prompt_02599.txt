Kurt Gödel's modal ontological argument is often presented as more resilient than Anselm's or Descartes's versions, since it does not trade on treating existence as an ordinary predicate. Instead, it derives the necessary existence of a "God-like" being from a small set of axioms about "positive properties" within a higher-order modal logic: positive properties are closed under strict implication, being God-like is itself a positive property, positive properties are themselves necessarily positive (positivity does not vary across possible worlds), and necessary existence is a positive property. In a philosophy of religion seminar I'm taking, the professor mentioned in passing that the objection logicians now treat as most damaging isn't Kant's familiar one, but something subtler: combined with Gödel's definition of an individual's "essence," these axioms are generally thought to generate "modal collapse" — the result that every true proposition turns out to be necessarily true, so that the distinction between the contingent and the necessary disappears entirely.

I'd like you to work through this yourself rather than just summarizing the debate around it. First, reconstruct as precisely as you can how the collapse is supposed to follow from the axioms above: which axiom, or combination of axioms, is doing the real work, and why does the essence clause end up being the hinge connecting a being's necessary existence to full-blown necessitarianism about all truths? Once you've laid this out, argue for the following, admittedly unpopular, position: that a committed defender of Gödel's proof should accept modal collapse rather than weaken the axioms to avoid it — that is, treat the necessitarian consequence (everything true is necessarily true) as a tolerable, even illuminating, cost of the argument rather than a decisive reductio of it. Your case should engage with the actual logical mechanics of the derivation rather than resting on a general appeal to Spinoza or Leibniz, and it should directly confront the strongest version of the objection: that necessitarianism seems to make all our ordinary talk of contingency, possibility, and free will simply false.
