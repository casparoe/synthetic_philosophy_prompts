This term I'm taking a philosophy seminar on decision theory, and right after a lecture on Hofstadter's concept of superrationality in the one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, two people in my study group got into an argument about whether the same reasoning justifies voting in elections.

(1) Rhea argued that voting is superrational: your decision procedure isn't unique, so you should ask what happens if everyone who shares your politics and would run this very argument reasoned the same way. If they'd all conclude their vote doesn't matter and stay home, your side predictably loses, so the fact that any single vote is causally inert is beside the point.

(2) Callum replied that this proves too much. You can bootstrap almost any individually costless sacrifice into a duty this way, and that's really just Kant's universalizability principle wearing a game-theory costume, not a genuine decision-theoretic argument, since your vote still doesn't cause anyone else's.

(3) Rhea said the two aren't the same: superrationality isn't the moral claim that you should act as you'd want everyone to act, but the empirical claim that sufficiently similar reasoners running the same computation on the same inputs will reach the same output, so your choice is evidence of theirs, in the way your choice in a Newcomb problem is evidence of the predictor's, without literally causing it.

(4) Callum answered that this only relocates the problem: the argument needs the other people tempted to abstain to actually be running this superrational computation, but most non-voters are just apathetic or busy, not silently reasoning about correlated decision procedures — so the correlation Rhea needs isn't there, and the argument smuggles in the empirical assumption it most needs to earn.
