In Anarchy, State, and Utopia, Nozick considers and ultimately rejects the maxim "utilitarianism for animals, Kantianism for people," concluding that even for animals, utilitarianism "won't do as the whole story." In its place, he grounds his side-constraint rights in an intervening capacity he labels M: roughly, one's ability to guide one's life by an overall conception one chooses to accept — a capacity he connects to the meaning of life, and which he takes to separate humans from animals. On this picture, a being either has M and is owed the full protection of inviolable rights, or lacks it and receives no political protection whatsoever.

Yet in Philosophical Explanations, and again in The Examined Life, Nozick develops a graded axiology of "organic unity" on which value comes in degrees, explicitly ranking "people above other animals above plants above rocks." He describes his own vegetarianism as required by a "respect for and valuing of animal life" — yet he never proposes that animals receive any legal or political protection.

Write an essay assessing whether these two strands of Nozick's thought are consistent: a binary, all-or-nothing theory of rights keyed to capacity M, alongside a graded theory of value on which animals matter quite a bit morally yet not at all politically. Weigh the best ways of reconciling the two pictures against the best reasons for thinking they are genuinely in tension. Don't end on a non-committal note: reach and defend a definite verdict on whether Nozick's treatment of animals is a stable position or a real inconsistency in his system.
