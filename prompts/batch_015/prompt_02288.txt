Okay so this summer I've been going down a bit of a decision theory rabbit hole trying to find a topic for an independent research project, and I keep running into this thing called "Good's theorem" on various blogs and old forum posts. Its from a short 1967 paper by a guy named I.J. Good (who I actually already knew the name of from AI safety stuff, he's the "intelligence explosion" guy, small world). The basic idea seems to be: if you're a Bayesian who actually updates properly on new evidence and then picks your best action given your new beliefs, then getting more information for free can never make your expected outcome worse, only better or equal. Apparently the proof is short enough that even I could kind of follow it — it comes down to the average of your best-case payoffs across all the ways the evidence could come out always being at least as big as the payoff of the single best action you'd pick with no evidence at all.

Then totally seperately, in econ class we were doing a unit on adverse selection and my teacher used genetic testing and health insurance as the example. Her point was that if genetic tests got good and cheap enough that everyone — you and the insurance company — found out your real disease risk before any policy got signed, insurance could basically fall apart: insurers won't cover people they now know are high-risk at a price those people can afford, and "accurately priced" insurance kind of defeats the purpose for the people who need it most. So looking at it from before anyone's tested, pretty much everybody would rather that information not exist yet, even though it's free and completely accurate — which sounds exactly like a case where more information makes people worse off, not better, and that feels like a direct contradiction of Good's theorem.

Like, does the insurance/genetic testing thing genuinely break Good's theorem, or is the theorem secretly relying on something (my best guess is it has to do with there only being one decision-maker in the theorem, versus a bunch of people whose contracts with each other depend on the same information) that quietly stops holding in the insurance case? I don't need the full formal proofs of either result, I just want to actually understand why these two things I read seem to flatly disagree, and more generally how you'd know in advance which category a new situation falls into.
