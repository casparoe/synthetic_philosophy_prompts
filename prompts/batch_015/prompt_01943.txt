A few friends and I run an informal reading group that has been working through classical Indian philosophy this year, and last month's session on Nāgārjuna's Mūlamadhyamakakārikā turned into a long argument I never quite settled in my own head. Two of the group, Jonah and Yuki, kept going after the rest of us had drifted off to dinner, and by the end neither had convinced the other, though both said things that struck me as genuinely right. The argument grew out of the opening chapter's fourfold rejection (the catuṣkoṭi, or "tetralemma") of all four ways a thing could arise — from itself, from another, from both, or from neither — and the way the same fourfold structure recurs in Chapter Twenty-Five, applied first to whether nirvāṇa itself is existent, non-existent, both, or neither, and then to whether the Tathāgata exists after death.

(1) Jonah's opening claim was that this is intellectually dishonest unless you accept that Nāgārjuna is asserting real contradictions: rejecting a claim and rejecting its negation in the same breath is either meaningless or a plain violation of the law of excluded middle, and the only reading that takes the text at face value rather than explaining away the hard cases is the "dialetheist" one associated with Graham Priest and Jay Garfield, on which some things really are both true and false, or neither, and classical logic simply doesn't bind here. (2) Yuki answered that all four options are only rejected relative to a shared, unstated assumption — that the things and the "arising" in question have svabhāva, self-sufficient independent existence — so the fourfold rejection is an ordinary reductio applied four times over, closer to refusing to say whether the present King of France is bald than to asserting "P and not-P"; nobody thinks a failed presupposition commits them to paraconsistent logic in the France case, and she saw no principled reason to treat Nāgārjuna differently. (3) Jonah granted that this reading sits comfortably with Chapter One, where "arising" plausibly only applies to things with inherent existence in the first place, but objected that it strains badly against Chapter Twenty-Five: immediately after refusing all four descriptions of nirvāṇa and of the Tathāgata after death, Nāgārjuna goes on to assert, using the very concepts of sameness and difference he had just declared inapplicable, that saṃsāra is not even slightly different from nirvāṇa — which looks like a substantive claim riding on what was supposed to be a purely negative maneuver. (4) Yuki's response was to invoke Nāgārjuna's own insistence, in the Vigrahavyāvartanī, that he "has no thesis": the whole enterprise operates at a therapeutic register rather than an assertoric one, so it is a mistake to hold the saṃsāra–nirvāṇa line, or anything else in the text, to the standards of ordinary assertoric logic in the first place. (5) Jonah's closing move was that this can't be had for free: either "I have no thesis" is itself a thesis, which is self-undermining, or it genuinely is not an assertion, in which case Yuki owes an account of why the reductio in Chapter One gets to establish something real — that nothing has inherent existence — while the identity claim in Chapter Twenty-Five and the "no thesis" line itself get to opt out of that standard; otherwise, he said, she is simply switching between a "rigorous argument" register and a "not even a claim" register depending on which objection is currently inconvenient.
