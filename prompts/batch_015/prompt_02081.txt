I've been a lurker on LessWrong and the Alignment Forum for well over a decade, and I recently fell back down the rabbit hole of the "coherence arguments" debate. That's the cluster of arguments, tracing back to Omohundro and popularized by Yudkowsky's 2017 essay "Coherent decisions imply consistent utilities," for the claim that sufficiently advanced agents will end up representable as expected-utility maximizers on pain of leaving free money on the table — and, by extension, for the worry that such agents will therefore be dangerously "goal-directed." What pulled me back in was rereading Thornley and Hendrycks's 2023 rebuttal, "There Are No Coherence Theorems," together with the long comment thread underneath it in which Yudkowsky himself shows up to push back. I think that exchange contains the real crux of the disagreement, but it leaves things in a frustratingly half-resolved state, and I'd like some help thinking it through.

The specific piece I can't settle concerns the completeness axiom. Thornley distinguishes genuine indifference between two options from a mere "preferential gap": with real indifference, sweetening one option ever so slightly should flip your preference, whereas a preferential gap can survive small sweetenings and sourings on either side. He then runs a classic money pump for completeness, borrowed from the philosopher Ruth Chang: a three-option decision tree in which the agent strictly prefers A to a slightly worse A-, yet has preferential gaps both between A and B and between A- and B. The standard argument says such an agent can be routed through the tree so that it ends up paying to leave with A- when it could simply have kept A. Thornley's response is that the agent can immunize itself against this pump, and every variant of it, just by adopting the policy of never choosing, at a later node, an option it strictly disprefers to something it earlier turned down — and he argues that following this policy never once requires the agent to act against a preference it actually holds, precisely because the preferential gaps mean it was never required to choose between the relevant options in the first place.

What I'd like help with is twofold. First, could you walk through, in a way I could explain to a friend who only half-remembers what a Dutch book is, why this policy is supposed to work — why it blocks the pump without ever forcing the agent into a choice that its own preferences don't settle? Second, and this is the part I find more interesting: a couple of commenters on the thread (Charlie Steiner and Lucius Bushnaq, if the names are useful) suggested that an agent running this memory-plus-policy setup is behaviorally indistinguishable, across every decision tree you could actually put it through, from an agent that simply has complete preferences to begin with, so that the "incompleteness" survives only as a fact about internal bookkeeping and not as anything visible in the agent's choices. Is that objection basically right? And if it is, does it actually rescue the original coherence argument, or does it just show that completeness was never doing the intuitive work everyone assumed it was doing? Since Thornley's stated motivation for all this is to use incomplete preferences as a route to building shutdownable AI, I'm also curious whether the dispute has real teeth for that downstream project, or whether it washes out to a purely terminological disagreement once you look only at behavior.
