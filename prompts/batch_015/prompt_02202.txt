So I fell down a rabbit hole last week that started with interpretability research and somehow ended on the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy page for "teleological theories of mental content," aka teleosemantics, which is basically philosophy of minds attempt to explain how a thought can be "about" something using evolution instead of just asserting it. The example that stuck with me is the frog whose brain fires the same signal for a fly and for a BB pellet flicked at it - Millikan's version of the theory says the signal really means "fly," not "small dark moving thing," because catching flies is what that detector was selected by evolution to do, so firing at a BB pellet counts as the detector misfiring rather than the concept being some vague disjunction. Apparently this specific frog has an entire literature devoted to arguing about it, they call it the disjunction problem.

Anyway I couldn't stop thinking about how much this sounds like stuff we argue about at work. People constantly describe SGD as "basically evolution but faster" for a network, shaping weights to do well at some task the same way selection shapes an organ over generations. So I brought it up in our team's book club (we're on an AI-philosophy kick right now) and asked whether that means a trained image classifier's activations are "really" about cats, and not some gerrymandered cats-or-that-one-weird-carpet-texture thing, for the exact same reason the frog's neuron is really about flies rather then small dark things in general. Nobody had a real answer, just vibes pointing in both directions.

I'd like you to actually settle this one for me in argument form, not a "there are considerations on both sides" essay. Specifically: please argue that training a single network with gradient descent does NOT give you the kind of history that teleosemantics needs, so that even someone fully on board with Millikan-style teleosemantics for animals should still deny that this move rescues real content for neural nets. Be concrete about which ingredient of the frog story - something about selection acting across a population over generations, heredity, what "normal conditions" would even mean for a training run, whatever it turns out to be - is doing the actual work for the frog and is either missing or works totally differently for a trained network.
