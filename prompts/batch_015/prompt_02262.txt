I'm a PhD student in philosophy, and I've been drafting a short paper that grew out of two seminars I'm taking for unrelated reasons: one on women philosophers of the early modern period, where we read Émilie du Châtelet's 1748 Discourse on Happiness, and one on the philosophy of death, where we read Bernard Williams's "The Makropulos Case." I went in assuming these would stay in separate mental folders, but the more I think about it, the more I suspect du Châtelet's text has something genuinely useful to say to Williams's argument, and I'd like an outside opinion before I develop the idea further.

Here is the argument as I currently have it sketched. Williams contends that an indefinitely extended life would eventually become unbearable, because the "categorical desires" that give a person reason to go on living either stay fixed, in which case they are eventually satisfied and exhausted, leaving only boredom, or they change enough over the centuries that it becomes doubtful whether the resulting person is still the one the immortality was meant to benefit. Du Châtelet, writing two centuries earlier, draws what looks like a relevant distinction in the Discourse: ordinary desires, on her view, are extinguished once their object is obtained, but passions and tastes (her recurring examples are love, gambling in old age once love has become unavailable, and, above all others, the passion for study) are not used up by being acted on; engaging them intensifies rather than depletes them. That looks like exactly the sort of non-terminating motivational state Williams's immortal would need to escape the dilemma. My hesitation is that du Châtelet is equally insistent that passions stay alive only because of privation, uncertainty, and a controlled susceptibility to illusion, such as the hope and fear that make gambling exciting, or the flattering blindness to a beloved's faults that keeps love from souring. None of those seem like resources an agent could keep drawing on indefinitely once enough centuries have passed and enough has been learned. So her account may just relocate Williams's problem by a few hundred years rather than actually dissolve it.

I'd value comments on three things: whether I'm representing du Châtelet's distinction between passions and desires accurately, rather than smoothing over some real complication in the text; whether the objection I raise about privation and illusion as finite resources actually holds up, or whether her account has some resource for handling it that I'm not seeing; and, at a more basic level, whether the pairing with Williams strikes you as a genuine philosophical payoff or just a superficially neat coincidence of vocabulary. I'm working from memory and old notes rather than the texts themselves at the moment, so please flag anything that looks like a misremembered detail rather than treating it as a settled premise.
