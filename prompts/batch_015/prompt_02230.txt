As a PhD student working on the history and philosophy of physics, I have been wondering how far Aristotle's own solution to Zeno actually extends. In Physics VI.9, he resolves the dichotomy and Achilles paradoxes by holding that the relevant infinity is only ever potential: a finite distance and the finite time needed to cross it are both divisible without limit, but neither is ever actually cut into infinitely many completed parts, so a runner is never required to finish an actual infinity of sub-tasks. Now suppose he were presented with Thomson's lamp: off at the start, switched on after one minute, off after another half-minute, on after another quarter-minute, and so on, with the question being whether it is on or off once two minutes have elapsed. Unlike the runner's single continuous motion, each flick here is a discrete, fully actual event, and infinitely many of them will already have occurred before the two-minute mark is reached. Does Aristotle's potential/actual infinite distinction, developed for continuously divisible magnitudes, genuinely get a grip on this discrete case, or does that disanalogy leave him without a ready answer? And reasoning strictly from commitments he actually held, would he conclude that the lamp scenario is impossible, that the question of its final state is ill-posed, or something else?
