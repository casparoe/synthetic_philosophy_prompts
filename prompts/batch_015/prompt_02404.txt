Having spent thirty years in analytical chemistry before retiring, I've taken up philosophy, and I'm currently reading Augustine's treatises on lying, De Mendacio and Contra Mendacium. What strikes me, as someone used to thinking about models and their domains of validity, is that Augustine grades a lie's sinfulness along at least three axes at once — the sanctity of its subject matter, the net harm or benefit to others, and the speaker's motive — which is how he ranks a lie told about religious doctrine as the gravest of his eight kinds of lies, and a lie that merely protects someone's chastity as the least grave.

Construct one original scenario, unlike any discussed by Augustine or his commentators, in which these axes pull apart: subject matter points toward the grave end of his scale while motive and consequence point toward the mild end, or vice versa. Then tell me whether Augustine's own stated principles actually settle the resulting rank — and if so, which axis wins and why — or whether they leave the case genuinely indeterminate.
