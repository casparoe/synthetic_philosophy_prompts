I don't have any formal background in philosophy, but I've spent a lot of time working through Nozick's *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* on my own, and I keep wondering how the Wilt Chamberlain argument applies to something Nozick died too soon to see. He passed away in 2002, well before generative AI existed, yet today's leading AI companies became some of the most valuable firms in the world largely by training their models on huge amounts of text, art, and code that ordinary people had posted online — almost always without payment or explicit permission — and then selling access to the resulting systems to vast numbers of paying users. Reasoning strictly from Nozick's own categories (justice in acquisition, justice in transfer, rectification, and the Lockean proviso), what do you think his actual verdict would have been? Specifically: would he treat the later, clearly voluntary user payments as doing the same "liberty upsets patterns" work that Wilt Chamberlain's ticket sales do, or would the fact that the training data itself was never justly acquired or transferred mean that, on his own terms, no amount of downstream voluntary exchange could launder the resulting fortunes into legitimate holdings? Please commit to a specific verdict rather than just cataloguing considerations on both sides.
