I've spent a long time reading rationalist blogs and forums, going back to the original Sequences, and I still can't referee an argument that broke out recently in a Slack group that grew out of a local meetup. Two of the more technically-minded regulars, Nora and Sam, spent an evening arguing about whether Solomonoff induction actually delivers on its reputation as "Occam's razor, formalized," and I've been turning the exchange over ever since. (1) Nora opened by claiming that Solomonoff induction basically solves Occam's razor: instead of the vague heuristic "prefer the simpler hypothesis," you get a precise universal prior that weights each computable hypothesis by roughly 2^-(length of its shortest program), plus a proof that a reasoner using this prior will end up dominating, up to a multiplicative constant, any other computable predictor. (2) Sam objected that this doesn't solve anything, because the invariance theorem behind Kolmogorov complexity only guarantees that two choices of universal machine give complexities differing by at most an additive constant — and that constant is just the length of whatever compiler translates one machine's programs into the other's, which a sufficiently gerrymandered machine can make enormous. So, Sam said, the theorem tells you that competing simplicity rankings must eventually agree, not which one to use before they do, and "eventually" is cold comfort on the finite evidence anyone actually has. (3) Nora replied that Sam was asking the theory to do a job it was never meant to do: the achievement isn't handing you a ranking for any two hypotheses on demand, it's showing that induction can be grounded in nothing more than treating the environment as computable and literally counting bits, with a guarantee that no computable predictor beats this one in the limit.

(4) Sam countered that "treating the environment as computable" is itself a substantive simplicity assumption, so using it to justify a simplicity-based method comes uncomfortably close to circular: you need something Occam's-razor-shaped just to get the theory off the ground in the first place. (5) Nora answered that this isn't circularity but honesty about terms — any hypothesis a scientist could ever state, check, or use to predict something is, by construction, a finite effective procedure, so restricting to computable hypotheses isn't an extra premise smuggled in, it's just what "hypothesis" already means. (6) Sam's closing point was that Solomonoff induction is provably not computable even in principle, so no real reasoner can run it, or approximate it while keeping the guarantees, which means the whole apparatus is an existence proof about an idealized agent nobody can be, and calling that a "solution" to a two-thousand-year-old problem overstates what has actually been shown.

Neither of them budged, and having sat with it for a while, I don't think either side is simply right. Could you adjudicate the exchange move by move? Then briefly justify each score, and close with a short paragraph on what you think Nora and Sam are each still missing.
