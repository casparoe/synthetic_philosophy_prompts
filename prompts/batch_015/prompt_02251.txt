Okay so this might be a weird question but it's been living in my head for like a week. For my IB Theory of Knowledge class we have to give a presentation on whether math is the one area of knowledge where we can actually be 100% certain, and I went down this huge rabbit hole on David Hilbert while researching it. So in 1900 Hilbert makes this famous list of 23 unsolved problems, and problem #1 is the continuum hypothesis (basically, is there a size of infinity strictly between the whole numbers and the real numbers, or not). Then in 1930 he gives this speech when he retires, in Königsberg, that ends with "wir müssen wissen, wir werden wissen" - we must know, we will know - like his whole life's motto was that every real math question has a definite answer we can eventually dig up, no exceptions, no "ignorabimus." The wild part is that Gödel had quietly announced his incompleteness theorem at basically the same conference the day before, and Hilbert apparently wasnt even in the room to hear it.

Heres the thing that got me though: it turns out Hilbert's OWN problem #1 is one of the things Gödel's work eventually helped kill off. Gödel showed in 1940 that you can't disprove CH from the normal axioms, then Paul Cohen showed in 1963 (both after Hilbert died in '43) that you can't prove it either, so it's just permanently undecided from the axioms everyone actually uses. And apparently set theorists still haven't given up - some are searching for totally new "large cardinal" axioms to settle it anyway (I saw a Quanta article about a guy named Hugh Woodin doing this), while other people are just like, fine, CH has no single true answer, different "universes" of math can disagree about it and that's ok.

So what I really want to know is: what do you think Hilbert would've made of all that? Like, would he have seen it as basically a personal disproof of his "we will know" thing, or does his actual philosophy (formalism, caring more about consistency than some Platonic math-truth floating out there) give him an out, like he could've just shrugged and said "fine, let's add CH or its negation as a new axiom," the same way mathematicians added new axioms to get non-Euclidean geometry? And do you think he'd side with the "keep hunting for the right new axiom" camp, or would he think that whole multiverse-of-set-theories idea is a cop out? I already know the history part, I'm way more interested in your actual best guess about how his specific views would've reacted, not just a recap of what happened.
