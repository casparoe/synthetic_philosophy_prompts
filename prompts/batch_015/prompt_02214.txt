At my aunt's birthday dinner, my cousin mentioned that her grandmother, who has dementia, keeps asking where her late husband is, and the family has stopped correcting her and now just says he's out and will be back soon. Two friends of mine at the table ended up in a long back-and-forth about it that outlasted the rest of the dinner conversation.

(1) Maya said this is obviously fine: it costs no one anything and spares the grandmother from reliving fresh grief every time she asks. (2) Owen, who had recently been reading Augustine on lying, said Augustine would reject this completely: he explicitly condemned lying to the sick or dying about their condition, and defined a lie as a false statement uttered with intent to deceive, irrespective of whether it helps or harms anyone. (3) Maya said a view that forbids comforting a dying woman with dementia is refuted by that implication, not vindicated by it. (4) Owen replied that this move just assumes consequences are what settle the question, which is exactly what Augustine denies: in his ranked catalogue of lies in De Mendacio, one category is precisely a harmless lie that saves someone from serious danger, and he still counts it a sin, just a lesser one -- so "it's kind and hurts no one" was never going to get anyone off the hook on his terms. (5) Maya answered that if the real reason lying is wrong is that it damages the liar's own truthfulness or soul, as Augustine himself argues, that's still a consequentialist reason -- it has just relocated the relevant harm from the hearer to the liar -- so Owen hadn't actually escaped consequentialism, only hidden where it was doing the work. Owen didn't have a ready answer, and it's stuck with me since.

Could you go through this move by move, saying who has the better of it at each numbered step, and address directly whether Maya's closing move in (5) actually succeeds against Augustine's position -- and whether either of them is missing something important?
