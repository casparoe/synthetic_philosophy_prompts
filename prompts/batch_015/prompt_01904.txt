So me and my friend Priya got into it for like an hour last night about Williamson's anti-luminosity argument from Knowledge and Its Limits -- specifically the version that's supposed to sink the KK principle -- and neither of us backed down, so I need a tiebreaker. Quick recap in case its useful: Williamson runs the whole argument through that sorites-y case where you feel freezing cold at dawn (case α_0) and are basically hot by noon (case α_n), with a long chain of barely-distinguishable cases in between. The key premise, call it (1i), says that if you know at case α_i that you feel cold, then you actually still feel cold at the very next case α_(i+1). He gets that out of the safety principle, roughly the idea that knowing p requires your belief in p to be true across every case close enough to the actual one. At some point we literally had the IEP page on the KK principle pulled up so we could argue about premise numbers, which tells you how far gone we were (also alot of wine was involved, to be fair).

Priya's move was the one I've seen attributed to Neta and Rohrbaugh, and maybe Brueckner and Fiocco too -- just deny the safety principle outright using one of those Gettier-adjacent cases, like the person drinking pure water standing next to someone who *would* have poisoned it if she'd lost the lottery, but didn't, because she won -- a case where you obviously still know you're drinking clean water even though your belief technically isn't "safe" in some very nearby possible case. Her conclusion: safety is false as a general principle, so (1i) never gets justified, so the sorites never gets off the ground, and KK survives intact. She also brought up the self-presentation move people like Weatherson make, but we agreed that one only helps with phenomenal stuff like pain, not with knowledge itself, so it's beside the point here.

I think Priya's just wrong, because there's a way to defend (1i) that never touches the safety principle at all: just contrapose it. If you don't feel cold at α_(i+1), and you weren't aware of feeling any different between α_i and α_(i+1), how could you possibly have known, back at α_i, that you felt cold? That argument seems to be about what your own belief-forming process could have been tracking, not about some general "true in every nearby case" modal principle -- so it should survive totally intact even if the water-poisoner case really does kill safety as a general theory of knowledge. So here's what I actually want: make my case for me, as strong as you can. Argue that denying the safety principle does NOT save KK from Williamson's argument, because his argument doesn't actually depend on the general safety principle being true. Feel free to flag weak spots in how I've set this up, but I want the thing to come down clearly on my side, not Priya's.
