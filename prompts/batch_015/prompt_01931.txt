A couple of weeks ago, over dinner, two friends of mine got into a long back-and-forth about whether it's rational to vote in a national election, and I still haven't been able to decide who came out ahead. I'm a backend engineer, not a philosopher, so the opening moves felt like comfortable, quantitative territory to me, but the exchange eventually turned on a move I don't have the philosophical vocabulary to evaluate, and I'd like a more careful referee than I can be myself.

It started when Mara, who works in data science, argued that voting is irrational once you look at the numbers: in a state with several million voters, a rough estimate puts the odds of any single vote actually being the tiebreaker at around one in ten million, so even multiplying that by a large personal stake — say, your preferred candidate winning is worth $10,000 to you — yields an expected payoff of a fraction of a cent, far below the time cost of registering, researching candidates, and standing in line (move 1). Josh objected that she was only counting the payoff to herself: if her vote were the tiebreaker, the resulting policy difference would affect the welfare of everyone in the state, not just her, so the stake that should be multiplied by that same tiny probability is the total welfare difference across millions of people, which could plausibly run into the billions of dollars and would easily clear that bar (move 2).

Mara replied that this proves too much, since almost any act with a minuscule chance of an enormous collective payoff would come out "rational" by the same reasoning, and that in any case nobody can pin down the welfare difference between two candidates precisely enough for the multiplication to mean anything rather than a rationalization dressed up as arithmetic (move 3). Josh conceded the estimate is fuzzy but said fuzzy isn't the same as zero, then shifted tack entirely: he argued that the right question isn't what her single vote causes, since that really is negligible, but what happens if everyone who accepts her reasoning acts on it — if "my vote is causally negligible, so don't bother" is a sound argument for her, it's equally sound for every other voter who thinks the same way, and if all of them abstained the outcome would obviously change, so it's the rule, not the individual act, that should be evaluated (move 4). Mara's comeback was that this was a bait-and-switch: her vote doesn't cause anyone else's vote, so she can't be evaluated as though she were choosing on behalf of a bloc, and reasoning about what a rule would produce if everyone adopted it is just a different, and in her view illegitimate, theory of rational choice, smuggled in to rescue a conclusion the first argument couldn't support on its own (move 5). Neither of them budged. Could you go through moves 1 through 5 in order and say how well each one actually holds up on its own terms, rather than just declaring an overall winner, and then tell me whether there's a standard resolution to this kind of disagreement in the decision-theory or rational-choice literature, and what it suggests that both Mara and Josh are still missing?
