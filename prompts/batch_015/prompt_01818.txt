I'm drafting a novel that riffs on the old fable of the servant who flees Death in Baghdad only to keep his appointment in Samarra, and in one scene a decision-theorist character has to walk another through the choice with real rigor. Digging into how philosophers actually formalized this, I found that Gibbard and Harper's version relocates the story: a man in Damascus is warned of a midnight appointment with Death, an excellent predictor of where he'll be, and must choose between staying put or fleeing to Aleppo. Causal decision theory notoriously finds that neither pure choice is self-ratifying here—committing to either city makes that very city look like the more dangerous one to be in—so the standard fix has him randomize with a coin instead, since only that mixed strategy survives its own scrutiny. For my notes, I'd like an essay that actually settles whether this coin-flip resolution genuinely vindicates causal decision theory or tacitly concedes that something is wrong with its notion of rational choice, weighing that position against evidential decision theory and against any more radical alternative you find more persuasive. Don't leave the question open: commit to and defend a specific verdict on what the man should actually do.
