hey, i'm working on a diss chapter on the principle of indifference and want to sanity check an argument before i commit it to paper. here's the rough idea, tell me if it holds together or if i'm fooling myself.

quick setup: Bertrand's paradox asks for the probability that a "random" chord of a circle is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle. depending on how you generate the random chord you get three different "correct" answers -- 1/3 if you pick two random endpoints on the circumference, 1/2 if you pick a random radius and then a random point along it, 1/4 if you pick a random point in the disk as the midpoint. same amount of "ignorance" going in, three incompatible uniform distributions coming out, which is basically the whole problem with naive principle-of-indifference reasoning: "equally likely" isn't well defined until you've already picked a parametrization, and "i have no information" doesn't tell you which parametrization to use.

Jaynes' fix (the 1973 "well-posed problem" paper) was to stop treating it as a pure ignorance problem and treat it as a physical limit instead -- straws tossed onto the circle from far away -- then require the answer be invariant under rotating, translating, and rescaling the circle. that requirement picks out a unique distribution (the "random radius" one, 1/2) even though nothing about "i don't know anything" alone singles it out. so the move is: swap "what am i ignorant of" for "what symmetries does the actual physical setup have" and let the symmetries fix the measure instead of vibes. (apparently even this is contested, theres a foundations of physics reply plus a 2015 paper by drory arguing the invariance requirement doesnt pin things down as uniquely as jaynes claimed once you're explicit about the generating process -- but grant him the result for now.)

here's the bit i actually want to argue: this symmetry move rescues POI-style reasoning exactly where we need it least -- physical randomizing setups, Jeffreys priors, stuff with real causal symmetries -- and does nothing where we need it most, namely anthropic/self-locating cases like Sleeping Beauty, the doomsday argument, or Bostrom's presumptuous philosopher. there the ambiguity is over how to individuate "observers" or "observer-moments," and there's no physical process whose rotations/translations/rescalings you can point to, so the Jaynes trick has nothing to grab onto and we're stuck with bare, unmotivated priors over reference classes.

does that seem right, or is there some clever way to smuggle an invariance argument into the anthropic cases that i'm missing? also poke holes in the jaynes recap if i've mangled it, and be honest about whether this framing is actually novel enough to hang a chapter on or if someone's obviously already made this exact move and i just haven't found the paper yet.
