I teach a graduate seminar on higher-order evidence, and for several years I have leaned on a piece of classroom scaffolding that I have come to suspect is doing far more argumentative work than it deserves.

The picture is that of the standby instrument. A pilot who has reason to distrust her primary altimeter does not stop flying: she cross-checks it against the standby, forms a view about how far the primary is off, and discounts its readings by that amount. The primary instrument plays the role of first-order evidence; the standby reading is higher-order evidence about the reliability of the first-order source; and rationality requires integrating the two rather than continuing to take the primary at face value. Almost everything I say about higher-order evidence gets cast in these terms, and I have noticed that a colleague of mine, who is a thoroughgoing conciliationist, now uses the picture to license two claims I am no longer confident it can carry. The first is that there is no such thing as rationally permissible epistemic akrasia: believing that p while believing that this very belief of yours issues from an unreliable process is, on the picture, exactly like trusting a faulty altimeter because you happen to like the number it displays, and is therefore never rational. The second is that the rational response to higher-order evidence is always to move one's first-order attitude in the direction the higher-order evidence indicates, by a determinate amount: discount in proportion to how badly the higher-order evidence impugns the first-order source. Call these the no-akrasia verdict and the discount-and-descend verdict.

What makes me suspicious is a distinction I have started drawing in my own notes. Much higher-order evidence is self-exempting: the impugning evidence reaches you through a channel independent of the competence it impugns, as when a lab report tells you the drug you took six hours ago degrades arithmetic judgment, or a certificate states the standby instrument's own error bounds. But some higher-order evidence is self-consuming: it is delivered by the very faculty it tells you not to trust. The mathematician who works through a long proof at two in the morning and concludes, by exactly that reasoning, that her judgment about such proofs is now untrustworthy is in this position; so is anyone who reads an argument, deployed in full generality, for the conclusion that we are systematically unreliable about some question, and applies it to itself. Peer disagreement looks like an intermediate case, since the impugning evidence comes through a channel that is partly independent of you and partly shared with you.

So I would like an audit of the standby-instrument picture, and I do not want a map of the literature or a reading list; I want the audit itself.

First, please set out the correspondence explicitly, as a table: what plays the role of the primary instrument, of the standby, of the true value, of the known error distribution, of the discount factor, of the pilot herself, and of the further action she takes. Mark, for each row, whether the mapping is forced by the picture or is a stipulation my colleague has quietly made.

Second, the places the mapping breaks. Candidate disanalogies I would like you to consider include: the independence of the two channels, which the engineer guarantees and the agent cannot; the availability of a stable error distribution or calibration base rate, which the instrument has and the agent generally does not; the existence of a third and authoritative source against which both instruments can be checked, such as the ground or the control tower, for which the agent has no analogue; the difference between a scalar reading and a propositional attitude; the fact that the pilot's discounting is performed by an unimpaired pilot, whereas the agent's response to the higher-order evidence is itself an exercise of the impugned faculty; and the fact that in the instrument case the true value is fixed independently of both readings, while the question of whether you are reliable is in some sense settled by the very judgments in question. Please add the ones I have missed, and separate the disanalogies that are load-bearing for the two verdicts from those that are merely decorative.

Third, say what the picture imports into the target that the target lacks. My own list would include: that trust is a continuous scalar one can dial rather than an all-or-nothing stance; that there is always a determinate amount by which to discount; that first-order and higher-order states can be treated as probabilistically independent given the truth of the target proposition; that total evidence always factorises into a readout and a calibration certificate; and that the correct response is reached by a single application of the discount operation.

Fourth, and this is where I would most value your help: suppose we restate the discount-and-descend verdict as a fixed-point requirement, so that the rational state is a fixed point of an operator that takes your current attitudes together with the higher-order evidence and returns revised attitudes. For self-exempting evidence, does such an operator have a unique and reachable fixed point? For self-consuming evidence, does it have one at all, and if it has several, does the picture say which to select? And please test the whole apparatus on the case where the higher-order evidence says your reliability is below chance, so that the right response would seem to be to invert rather than merely to damp your confidence: can a faculty that is anti-reliable carry out its own inversion, and does the instrument model have anything to say here, or does it fall silent?

Finally, given all this, tell me whether each of the two verdicts survives, whether it survives only for the self-exempting cases, or whether it was the analogy carrying it the whole time; and if you think there is a better analogy for the self-consuming case — something like a scale that must weigh itself, a rule that expands with the temperature it is measuring, or a court of last resort that is also a party to the dispute — give it to me, together with what it gets right that the standby instrument gets wrong.
