Topic is adaptationism and the Gould–Lewontin spandrels critique.

Here's my problem. Every version of this question I've written gets answered by a perfectly competent recapitulation. The student says: Gould and Lewontin accuse adaptationists of telling just-so stories and of mistaking architectural byproducts for adaptations, the spandrels of the cathedral vault being the flagship example; the adaptationists reply that byproducts get co-opted later and that optimality models make genuine, testable predictions; therefore the whole thing is an empirical dispute that has to be settled case by case. All of that is true. All of it is worth a B. And none of it tells me whether the student actually understands where the disagreement lives. I want a question where that paragraph alone gets you maybe 12 out of 25.

So: pretend you're a professional philosopher of biology who's taught this material a bunch of times and knows the adaptationism literature from the inside. Before you write anything, brainstorm for me. Give me a few paragraphs where you sketch three candidate questions, reject at least two of them by name with an actual reason (this one is answerable by recitation, that one turns on some fact about a real taxon that nobody could reasonably be expected to have memorized in an exam room), and keep the one that survives. I want to see the rejects, not just the winner.

Some constraints on the version I keep circling back to, in case they're useful — feel free to change the details, but please keep this level of specificity, because I want the question to be about reasoning through something, not about recall:

The question should be built around a made-up case study. My rough shape: a lineage of burrowing lizards on a high volcanic plateau that ram through compacted ash with a heavily reinforced, fused braincase. The reinforcement is uncontroversially an adaptation for ramming. But the bony struts that brace the skull pinch the canal the optic nerve runs through, so the adults have terrible visual acuity and a much reduced visual midbrain, while their soft-soil sister taxa, which don't ram, have unfused skulls and excellent vision. Meanwhile these lizards have flamboyant species-specific throat fans that they use in close-range courtship, and females demonstrably prefer the brighter ones.

Now the wrinkle I want injected, which is the part that does the real work: the visual system has clearly been re-tuned around the low acuity. The surviving visual tissue is hypersensitive to exactly the wavelengths the throat fan reflects, and the olfactory bulbs have ballooned. So the animal you're actually looking at has a well-designed, low-acuity, fan-detecting visual system, and the original architectural accident has been partly compensated away — the historical signature that would let you tell a spandrel from an adaptation has been overwritten by later selection. To make it worse, the stratigraphy is unhelpful: in the two closest outgroups, skull fusion and acuity loss show up in the same beds, so there's no time gap to argue from. Call this the erosion problem, or whatever you want to call it.

Given that setup, the question should force a real choice. Is the claim that the poor acuity is a spandrel a claim about causal history that we've simply lost the ability to test? Or is it a claim about which kind of explanation is the appropriate one, so that it survives the erosion problem untouched? Or does the origin-versus-maintenance distinction just eat the entire dispute — and if so, is that dissolution a neutral clarification or is it quietly handing the adaptationist the win? Students should have to pick one and defend it, not list the options.
