I run a small philosophy-of-science discussion group at my local library: nine to eleven people, mostly retired engineers and teachers, nobody with philosophy training and nobody with much maths beyond school level, two hours on a Tuesday evening. We have been working through popular books on relativity, and on my own I have been reading about the hole argument. My rough grasp is this: if you think of spacetime as made up of points that are the points they are independently of the matter and fields in them, and the theory lets you slide which point counts as which inside a region where nothing at all is happening, then you can generate two histories that differ only about which bare point is doing what, and no possible observation could ever tell them apart. So either the theory fails to determine what happens, or the points were never individuals in the first place. I do not want to tell them this; I want them to walk into it.

Please design a two-hour session for them, a game with boards, counters, roles and a scoring scheme rather than a seminar discussion, in which they first commit, in writing and in advance, to predictions stated in terms of named points, and then discover for themselves that the only predictions that can actually be scored are relational ones (next to the red counter, two steps from the landmark), because during a run of rounds in which a designated hole region is empty the points inside it are quietly relabelled. Arrange it so that the group, and not I, is the one to say what has to be given up. Give me the rules, the materials, the payoff scheme, a timeline, and a debrief that maps what happened at each stage onto the corresponding piece of the argument. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, my own worry being that they will come away thinking only that labels are arbitrary, which is the boring reading, and be frank about which part of the argument the game cannot reproduce and which I will have to state myself. In the debrief notes, please separate what is settled among people who know this material from what is genuinely contested: especially whether the argument really does overturn the view that spacetime points are individuals, and whether the breakdown of determinism is a discovery about the world or an artefact of how determinism was stated. I would not want to tell the group that something is agreed when it is not.
