I'm a journalist working on a long piece about organizations that write rules governing how their own rules get changed, and I've been sitting in on the governance working group of an AI research institute whose ten-page charter can be amended by a two-thirds vote of its assembly — call that Rule A — as well as reading through the procedural sediment of a scientific society whose handbook has been amended more than two hundred times. My brother-in-law, who builds distributed systems, keeps telling me it's just a consensus problem in a funny hat; a friend who does mathematical logic says it's all the recursion theorem again. What has actually thrown me is that I have very firm gut reactions about which maneuvers take and which are somehow cheating, and the reactions refuse to line up. I am fine with the assembly's providing that a particular section on data retention may be amended only by a three-quarters vote; I am also fine, though I hesitate a beat, with its raising the bar for amendments to Rule A itself. I am fine, with more hesitation, with a clause providing that this section may not be amended or repealed at all. But I balk at an amendment, properly passed two-thirds, providing that no provision of the charter may ever again be amended: that strikes me as a kind of fraud on successors, even though it is only the maximal version of the self-entrenching clause I just accepted. And I balk, differently, at a ratchet amendment that raises the threshold for amending section X each time X is amended — two-thirds, then three-quarters, then four-fifths, and so on — where every individual rung looks exactly like the entrenchments I accepted and yet the ladder as a whole looks like a trick for making X unamendable by installments. I have no hesitation at all about one gambit the working group floated: an amendment that redefines the assembly, for the purpose of all future amendments, to consist of those who voted for this amendment. That is plainly cheating, and yet it complied with every procedure in force when it was passed. I feel the same way, though less confidently, about the continuity clause someone proposed: it declares the charter to be the same entity as the one adopted in 2019 and provides that any amendment touching sections one through three converts it into a different charter, which shall have no authority — which looks to me like entrenchment smuggled in as a definition, but I can't say why it differs from the suicide amendment except in rhetorical packaging. The case I go back and forth on is the one where two amendments pass at the same sitting, each by two-thirds, one saying that any provision may be amended by a two-thirds vote and the other saying that no provision enacted at this sitting may be amended: my gut produces no stable verdict, only the sense that one of them has to lose and no way of choosing.

My own tentative principle is thinner than I'd like: an amendment is valid if and only if it was enacted in compliance with whatever amendment procedure was in force immediately before it took effect, and once valid its content — including its content about what later amendments will require — binds every later assembly without substantive limit. I can see that this principle cheerfully validates the suicide amendment and the ratchet, and so forces me to drop two verdicts I don't want to drop, and that it has no opinion at all on the same-sitting collision, and that it may even validate the quorum trick I find indefensible. Can you test it against my cases and repair it, or replace it with two or three candidate principles stated precisely enough that I can see which clause is doing which work — with the motivating case named for each clause, and an honest account of which of my verdicts each candidate cannot accommodate? In particular I'd like to know what each candidate says about three cases that aren't in my list: an amendment providing that the amendment shall be conclusively presumed to have been validly enacted whether or not the votes were properly counted; an amendment providing that any purported repeal of that amendment is itself to be treated as an amendment of it and shall therefore require unanimity; and a charter under which amendments take effect only when certified by the Secretary, with an amendment repealing the certification requirement that the Secretary has refused to certify.
