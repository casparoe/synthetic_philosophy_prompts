I took a seminar in value theory last spring and have been trying on and off to turn a short paper out of a question I can't let go of: whether better than can fail to be transitive, and if so where exactly the failure is located. I keep stalling at the point where I have to say what my own verdicts add up to, so let me just record them. (1) Four hundred takes of a pianist recording a nocturne, ordered so that an expert I trust says each take is very slightly better interpretively than the one before, with none of the differences perceptible to me: I say each take is better than its predecessor, and I also say the last take is better than the first, so the chain transmits. (2) A sequence of forty-one flats: number 1 is $900 a month with a 45-minute door-to-desk commute, and each successive flat costs $25 more and cuts 45 seconds off the commute, so the last is $1,900 with a 15-minute commute. At every step I would take the next flat and I judge it better than the one before, but the first and the last I judge to be on a par: neither is better, though they are genuinely comparable rather than incomparable. (3) The very same two endpoint flats reached in four moves instead of forty, each move adding $250 a month and seven and a half minutes: again I would take each step, but here I do judge the last flat better than the first. Cases 2 and 3 have identical endpoints and, as far as I can tell, the same local trade at every step; the only difference is grain, and I dislike that my verdicts move with it. (4) Sixty work schedules, each paying $60 a month more than the last and costing four minutes more sleep a night, starting from eight hours: I would take each step, but the last schedule I judge to be worse than the first, not merely not better, and the difference from case 2 seems to be that somewhere around six hours a threshold gets crossed. (5) Three job offers in a cycle, where A beats B on salary and colleagues, B beats C on colleagues and commute, and C beats A on commute and salary: asked about any one pair on its own I will say the first is better, but I will not stand by all three verdicts together, and I take the cycle to show that I was shifting which respect mattered between comparisons. (6) A chain of versions of a story, each three pages longer than the last and, by my own judgment, a better piece of writing, running from a three-page short story to a three-hundred-page novel: I judge the endpoints incomparable rather than on a par, which worries me, since I accepted every local comparison and so seem to have given up the transitivity of comparability as well as of betterness.

The principle I keep trying out on myself is roughly this: unqualified betterness is always betterness relative to a weighting of the respects and to a reference point, and a chain transmits betterness from its first item to its last only if every comparison in the chain is made from a common anchor, that is, with the same weighting from the same reference point; where the weighting or the reference point drifts between comparisons, the chain transmits nothing at all, and the endpoints may be on a par or incomparable. That seems to me to handle 5, and to explain 2 by saying that with small steps my reference point slides along with the flat I am currently considering, so that no two comparisons in the chain are made from the same anchor. But it sits badly with 3, since sliding should if anything be easier with large steps, it says nothing about 1, where there is only one respect and so no weighting to fix, and it does not by itself deliver the reversal in 4, where the threshold verdict looks like a verdict about the schedules themselves rather than about how I was comparing them. A friend also says my verdict in 2 is just status quo bias or loss aversion, that I am reporting what I would choose step by step rather than what is better, and that I should drop it. I would rather not drop it, but I want to know what keeping it costs me.

So: can you state precisely a principle about the conditions under which better than is transitive, or a short list of two or three candidate principles, telling me which of my six cases motivates each clause and being explicit about where you are overturning one of my verdicts rather than accommodating it? I would especially like to know what your candidates predict about three cases I have not made up my mind on: a forty-step flat chain that crosses a threshold in the traded respect halfway along; a forty-step chain in which the trade rate itself drifts, so that I demand more money for the same saving as the commute gets shorter; and the recording case from (1) run at coarse grain, four takes instead of four hundred. And please tell me honestly which of my verdicts no principle you can construct will accommodate, and why that one rather than another is the one that has to go.
