I have a deadline this week for a three-page prospectus for a twelve-month funded research project — roughly ten thousand words of output, plus a small workshop at the end — and I need to turn a half-formed idea into something a committee will take seriously. Where the idea came from: I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about whether the no-free-lunch theorems in computational learning theory and optimization represent a genuine advance on Hume's problem of induction, or only a formal restatement of it in the vocabulary of algorithms. Neither of us moved the other, and by the end I thought the argument had stalled for two reasons: we were both using the phrase no-free-lunch result for two different claims, and neither of us could say which substantive assumption in the derivation is actually doing the skeptical work.

My half-formed idea, which I would like you to sharpen rather than replace: the standard results average an algorithm's performance off the training data over all possible target functions — uniformly, over a space closed under permutation of the inputs — and get the same expected performance for every algorithm, so that success on some problems is exactly paid for by failure on others. I suspect the skeptical bite comes almost entirely from that uniform averaging, which is in effect a principle of indifference over target functions; and I suspect that once you ask what justifies that measure, the no-free-lunch argument collapses back into the older dispute about whether there is any non-question-begging way to be uncertain about the unobserved. If that is right, the theorems are best read as relocating Hume's problem onto the justification of a prior rather than as solving or strengthening it, and the live question is whether that relocation is illuminating — because it makes the work done by the prior explicit and lets us ask precisely how much structure a prior needs in order to break the symmetry — or merely cosmetic.

What I need from you is the prospectus itself:

- A precise statement of the question, sharper than the paragraph above, framed so that it has a determinate answer within a year. I would rather the document contain one well-defined question than three suggestive but vague ones.
- Why the obvious ways of settling it do not settle it. The three I keep running into are: that these theorems are just Hume in new notation and there is nothing further to say; that they quantify over grue-style gerrymandered target functions that no sane prior would take seriously and so tell us nothing about actual induction; and that in a Bayesian framing the prior was always doing all the work, so the theorems add nothing. Say what each gets right and why none of them is sufficient.

I do not want the proposal to rest on my proving a new theorem — I am not a mathematician, and the committee will know it.

If the honest answer is that it fails in case I end up saying only that everything depends on what one means by a neutral prior, tell me that, and tell me how to re-scope the project so that it does not.

Please write it in a formal register suitable for a funding committee, at about three pages.
