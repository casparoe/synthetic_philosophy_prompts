I'm a retired atmospheric chemist (well, flux work mostly, and in the last decade a great deal of committee work, which is not the same thing), and for a couple of years now I've been sitting in on a philosophy reading group at the university here. I mostly keep quiet, because I often can't tell the difference between a position being genuinely crazy and me simply not having the background. But I've started writing up short notes after each meeting to see whether I can get any grip on things.

Last week the group did consciousness, and one of the popular books that was recommended — I won't name it, the author is a serious science writer and I may be doing them an injustice — had a chapter on something called the meta-problem of consciousness, which I'd never heard of. I don't have the book to hand, so what follows is reconstructed from my notes: paraphrase, not quotation. Roughly, with the sentences numbered so they're easier to take apart:

(1) Chalmers distinguishes the hard problem — why any physical processing in the brain should be accompanied by subjective experience at all — from what he calls the meta-problem, which is the problem of explaining, without helping yourself to consciousness, why we say and think the things we do about consciousness. (2) Why we insist we have experiences, why we find philosophical zombies conceivable, why the whole business feels like a mystery rather than like digestion. (3) The meta-problem is hard, but hard in the ordinary scientific way rather than the metaphysical way, because what has to be explained is just more behaviour: verbal reports, judgments, our willingness to go on about what red looks like. (4) That is what makes it the useful one. (5) The point of it — or at least Chalmers's point in raising it — is that it gives the materialist a way out: if there is a purely functional story about the mechanisms that make us talk about qualia, then a creature physically identical to us but with no inner life whatsoever would talk in exactly the same way. (6) And that means the only evidence anyone has ever had for consciousness, essentially our own reports, is evidence that would look the same whether or not consciousness existed — so illusionism, which is really just a polite way of saying that consciousness doesn't exist, follows more or less automatically. (7) The realist's reply, that the simplest explanation of why we report having experiences is that we do have them, is just the hard problem restated with extra steps, and gets them nowhere. (8) And in fact once the meta-problem is solved there isn't much of the hard problem left, since the hard problem was always only the puzzle of why we find consciousness puzzling.

Now, some of that struck me as exactly the sort of thing a philosopher would say, and some of it struck me as too convenient. The idea that you can dissolve a metaphysical problem by explaining why people go on about it smells like a move I used to see in arguments over whether the measurement problem is really a problem, where somebody explains the history of the angst and thinks they've thereby answered the physics. My own hunch is that the meta-problem is either the hard problem in a cheap disguise, or else just cognitive science with a grander name, and that either way there's nothing here to be impressed by. But my hunch is also suspiciously comfortable for a scientist, and I'd of thought the people in the group would have said so if the chapter were simply wrong.

So: could you go through it claim by claim and tell me what's fair? Which sentences would someone who actually works on this accept, which would they wince at, and which are straightforwardly wrong? I'd particularly like to know whether the meta-problem is really supposed to be *easier* than the hard problem in the way sentence 3 puts it, or whether that's the author's gloss; whether it's true that Chalmers raised it as a weapon for the materialist side (that surprised me — I'd have guessed the opposite, given his reputation); and whether the claim in sentence 6 that illusionism "follows more or less automatically" is something an illusionist would actually want to say, or whether it's an opponent's version of their view. And if you strip out the parts that aren't right, does the interesting conclusion — whatever it properly is — survive correction, or does the whole chapter turn on the bits that are wrong?

One more thing: does Chalmers, or anyone, have an actual answer to the "simplest explanation is that we're conscious" move in sentence 7, or is that really just the hard problem wearing a costume? If it's the latter I'd like to be told plainly, since that's the part I find hardest to argue with.
