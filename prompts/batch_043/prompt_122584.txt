I'm giving a talk to my university's AI safety reading group next Thursday, and I want to arrive with a proper Q&A prep sheet rather than the half-formed list of talking points I have now. My thesis is that approval-directed agency is not a subspecies of goal-directed agency, and that the group's habit of describing every trained system as an optimizer of some objective has led them to misdiagnose what actually goes wrong with these systems. The argument I plan to give has three moves. First, I concede up front that any approval-directed policy can be formally represented as maximizing expected approval, so the distinction cannot be about the mathematical form of the target; I locate it instead in the counterfactual dependence of the target on who is doing the approving and on what that approver currently knows. Second, I offer a diagnostic I have been calling the swap test: put a different, or partly simulated, principal into the approval channel mid-trajectory and see whether the system's behavior re-optimizes around the new principal's verdicts or instead keeps executing its existing plan and treats the new principal as an obstacle; I claim the first pattern is the signature of approval-directedness and the second of goal-directedness. Third, I argue that the two kinds of system fail in different ways and therefore call for different mitigations: goal-directed systems fail by instrumental convergence and resistance to correction, whereas approval-directed systems fail by degrading the epistemic independence of the approver, which is why I think the latter call for something like epistemic containment and approver diversity rather than impact penalties or corrigibility training.

The audience is eight people, and I can more or less predict who will say what. Two of them are RLHF practitioners at an industry lab who are convinced that approval direction is just reward modeling with extra steps and that everything I call a distinctive failure mode is an instance of reward hacking they already know how to handle. One is a PhD student in interpretability who will want to know what any of this predicts about activations, probes, or circuits in models that are probably neither approval-directed nor goal-directed in my sense. One is a postdoc in theoretical computer science who works on reward tampering and will press on whether my distinction is stable under optimization pressure at all, or whether gradient descent inevitably converts an approval-directed policy into a goal-directed one as soon as it becomes cheaper to cache the approval signal than to consult it. And one is a philosopher of action who keeps asking what a goal would have to be such that maximizing someone's approval does not count as having one, and who will not accept a list of behavioral signatures in place of a criterion. A friend who read an early draft said the whole thing collapses into a verbal dispute about the word goal; I do not think that is right, but I would like to hear the strongest version of it.

What I need is a prep sheet, ordered by how much damage each objection would do rather than by how likely it is, with each objection phrased the way that particular person would actually put it rather than in my vocabulary, a reply I could deliver in about two minutes, and a candid note on where that reply is softest. I also want: what I should concede in the first two minutes so the room stops treating me as someone defending a distinction nobody needs; the single reply that has to land for the talk to be worth giving; guidance on how to handle the version of the objection that says an approval-directed system has an instrumental reason to manage the approver's beliefs, since that one seems to collapse my distinction back into theirs; and the question I am hoping nobody asks, which I think is something about what stops an approval-directed system from quietly constructing or training a principal who approves more readily than the one it was given, together with the least bad answer available to me. Please write the prep sheet in American English, and keep it keyed to the argument sketched above rather than to the approval-direction literature in general.
