I work as staff to a city infrastructure design review board. Our enabling ordinance is thin on the point that matters: any design feature that is *political* — one that distributes power, access, or standing among residents — has to go to a public hearing before adoption, while anything else can be cleared by staff. Most of our work has therefore gone into deciding what counts, and over four years we have accumulated a set of rulings. I now have to write the recommendation on a new one, and I would like the reasoning rather than just the answer.

The precedents, as I would summarize them for the board:

1. **Shelter redesign.** Benches in a transit shelter replaced by narrow leaning rails; the overhead light repositioned so the rear corner is dark. Staff cited maintenance costs and vandalism. Ruled *political*, hearing required.
2. **Parkway overpasses.** A reconstruction sets bridge clearances low enough that transit buses cannot reach a lakefront park, though cars can. The engineers cited road grading and sight lines; the record also shows that a bus-only ramp option was dropped as too expensive. Ruled *political*; our findings said explicitly that we did not need to decide why the clearance was chosen.
3. **Smart water meters.** Replacing all residential meters with units that report hourly and permit remote shutoff for nonpayment. Ruled *political*; the findings stressed that the city would acquire a capacity it had not previously had, together with a standing question about who gets cut off and under what rule.
4. **Road diet.** Removing a lane of on-street parking to add a separated bike lane. Staff flagged that drivers lose parking and cyclists gain a protected lane. Ruled *not political*, on the reasoning that allocating a street among travel modes is ordinary transportation policy rather than a distribution of standing.
5. **Online permits.** Moving all permit applications to a web portal open at all hours, with in-person counters retained at two locations and telephone filing retained for anyone who asks. Roughly a tenth of residents lack home broadband. Ruled *not political*, because the alternative channels were still available.
6. **App-opened bins.** A pilot of solar compacting bins whose lids unlock only through a resident registration app. Ruled *political*: it makes use of a public amenity conditional on having a phone, an account, and a willingness to be enumerated.

The new proposal is a signal retiming. Nine downtown intersections would move from push-button-actuated pedestrian phases to fixed intervals: seven seconds of walk indication plus a clearance interval computed at 4.0 feet per second, up from the 3.5 feet per second previously used, with the whole corridor coordinated into a green wave for vehicle progression and tied to a state target for reducing idling emissions. Consequences, from the department's own model: pedestrians who travel slower than about four feet per second — many residents over seventy, people using canes, walkers, or wheelchairs, young children — will not reach the far curb inside the interval, and roughly a quarter of crossings by residents over seventy would still have someone in the roadway when parallel traffic is released. The push buttons that previously extended the interval would be removed, as they are incompatible with corridor coordination. Nobody has proposed this in order to exclude anyone; the stated aims are throughput and emissions, and the model notes the pedestrian effect without proposing mitigation. Two aggravating facts: five of the nine intersections are the only crossings of a rail corridor within about half a mile, with the nearest alternatives involving a steep grade or a staircase; and the city increased enforcement of crossing against the signal last year.

What I want from you: a prediction of how a board faithful to its own record should rule, and the rule it appears to be running — something like an intent-or-function test, a foreseeable-patterned-effect test, a test about what social apparatus the artifact requires, or a test about whether an option is foreclosed rather than merely burdened. Then, the other rules that fit all six verdicts about as well, what each of them predicts about the retiming, and which of the six does the most work as an analog — I can see a case for the overpasses and a case for the road diet, and the online-permits ruling cuts whichever way you read its reasoning. If our precedents genuinely underdetermine this, say so, and state what principle we would be adopting with each choice. Finally, give me the further case that would most cleanly separate the surviving rules — the variant of this one, or some different proposal, that would make them disagree in opposite directions — and tell me what would change your mind: which argument, or which additional fact about the intersections, the affected pedestrians, or the availability of alternatives, would lead you to the opposite recommendation, and where you think your reasoning is most likely to fail.
