I have no academic background — I do scheduling for a freight company and read about this in the evenings — and for about two years I have been going back and forth on a forum with two people whose views I respect and cannot shift. One holds that the only honest test is the standard legal one used by asylum systems: you qualify if you have a well-founded fear of being persecuted because of who you are — your religion, your politics, your ethnic group, your membership of some targeted social group — and that destitution, however severe, is not persecution and does not get you in. The other holds that what matters is how badly off you are and how much the receiving country could help at acceptable cost, so that a starving family has a stronger claim than an uncomfortable dissident. I think both are leaving something out, and the something is this: a country can have a duty to take someone in because it is *responsible* for the fact that they had to leave, even when it did not persecute them and even when they are not among the worst off. I want to know whether that idea can carry a case on its own.

So please build me a case to order, not an illustration of mine. I will use it in that argument, which means the verdicts have to be generated by the principles rather than written into the story.

Specifications:

1. One paragraph, 150 words at most. Setting: an ordinary decision-maker's desk — an asylum office, a consulate, a resettlement intake. No science fiction, no siege, no torture, no camp.
2. Three tests must be applied and must split like this: the persecution test says reject; the urgent-need or priority-to-the-worst-off test also says reject; the causal-responsibility test says admit. Show the inference for each, including the step where the first two come out negative — do not just announce it.
3. The claimant should not be someone an ordinary reader instinctively roots for. Not destitute, not ill, no children in the story, some savings and some marketable skill, and at least one genuinely available alternative to claiming asylum in this particular country — just a worse one. If the reader's sympathy is doing any of the work, the case has failed.
4. The receiving country's contribution must be: deliberate policy rather than accident; lawful and democratically authorised; a net benefit to that country's own voters; counterfactually fragile, in that another country would have done substantially the same thing within a year or two if this one had stood aside; and mediated through enough steps that no official ever aimed at displacing this person or anyone like them. I want to see whether overdetermination and distance defeat the causal claim or merely complicate it, and I want your honest view on that.
5. The case must not quietly reintroduce what it is supposed to do without: no sympathetic destitution smuggled in under another description, and no persecution by proxy. Please check your own scenario against this explicitly.

After the scenario and the walkthrough: name the single feature of the case that is doing the work, and run the collapse test — remove that feature and tell me what each of the three tests then says. Then give me the strongest thing a defender of the test that comes out worst could say in objection, in their voice, and the best reply you can manage. If all five specifications cannot be jointly satisfied, say so plainly, say which one you had to bend, and build the nearest case that works.
