I'm the TA for a graduate seminar on rational choice and the recent literature on transformative experience — the stuff built around the now-standard "becoming a vampire" case — and I have to run next week's discussion section on classification: which experiences count as transformative in the sense that matters for decision theory, and why. Our professor has been impressively consistent in her verdicts but has never stated the rule she is using, and my study group has been trying to reverse-engineer it from her case notes. Here is her track record, with the one-line reasons she gave in the margins: first-time parenthood, yes ("the paradigm case"); undergoing a vampire transformation, yes ("you cannot know what it is like in advance, and the one you become would not consent to the reversal"); the same vampire transformation with a guaranteed, costless way to reverse it after a year, yes ("reversibility tells you what to do, not what kind of experience this is"); adult conversion into a religion, yes; first-time hearing through a cochlear implant in someone deaf from birth, yes ("not just new sensations — it changes what she can do, who she can be with, what she ends up caring about"); a pill that permanently reorganizes your political or religious convictions, even when earlier takers have published detailed, accurate first-personal descriptions of the shift, yes ("testimony does not hand you the phenomenology"); hearing a piece of music in a musical idiom entirely new to you, no ("surprise is not transformation — her ends survive the concert intact"); a year-long sabbatical in a country whose language you don't speak, no ("disorienting, but your ends travel with you"); general anesthesia for surgery, no ("there is nothing it is like"); a second child five years after the first, no — though her note added that this one "is closer than the literature lets on."

The case I want to put to her, and that I want to be able to defend in section, is this: Ruth was raised from birth to the age of twenty-one in a strict, insular religious community, left it, and has spent the last sixteen years as an anthropologist of religion and a convinced atheist. At thirty-seven she is invited to return permanently and in good standing, which would require abandoning her scholarly life; leaving again afterwards would cost her her family. Two stipulations matter. First, there is no epistemic gap about the form of life itself: she lived it for twenty-one years, the community has changed very little, and she can predict the texture of the days as well as anyone can predict anything. What would be new is only what it is like to return to it as an adult who has chosen it, which may or may not be the same thing. Second, the value change is the whole issue: former members who have gone back as adults reliably report that within a few years they come to want what the community wants, that the pull of inquiry and autonomy fades, and that they do not experience this as a loss.

So here is what I'd like from you. Set out the two or three rules that fit her ten verdicts equally well — as I see it, something like an epistemic-access rule, a preference-or-identity-change rule, and some conjunction of the two, but tell me if there's a better candidate — and run each of them across the whole list, flagging which verdicts each one handles awkwardly (the cochlear implant and the second child are the two my group keeps stumbling over). Then predict her verdict on Ruth, say which rule she is implicitly running and why that reading of her is preferable to the alternatives, and give me your confidence. Finally, name the further case, or the further stipulation on Ruth, that would actually settle it: if you could ask her one more question before I run section, what would it be? One sub-question I can't resolve on my own and would like addressed: Ruth is only about sixty percent confident that the value change would happen to her at all — some returnees don't change. Does that bear on whether the experience is transformative, or is it a separate expected-value problem that classification shouldn't touch? I've already lost one bet guessing what she'll say, so I'd rather have the reasoning than a prediction I can't defend.
