I'm writing a novel — the murder happens in the first chapter, twenty-two years before the rest of the book, and most of the book is my guy's fourth parole hearing. The jurisdiction is invented but not strange: the board weighs risk, remorse, the usual. The one thing I made up is that the board has to make a written finding on whether the applicant is, quote, substantially the same person who committed the offense, and has to treat that finding as a reason for release or denial that's independent of whether he's dangerous. I put that in because I wanted the hearing to be about something other than recidivism statistics, but now I have to make the board chair say something true, and I cant work out what would be true.

The thing I keep bumping into is Galen Strawson's Basic Argument. As I've got it: you do what you do because of the way you are; to be genuinely morally responsible for what you do you'd have to be responsible for the way you are; and you can't be, because any act by which you made yourself that way would itself have to come out of a way you already were, and so on back forever — so nobody is ever responsible in the sense the practice seems to need. If that's right, the whole hearing is theater.

But the hearing isn't the only place my legal system seems to have half-swallowed the argument already, and the halves dont match. At sentencing, a brutal childhood, a violent father, a head injury, fetal alcohol exposure — all of it gets in as mitigation, and the shape of the argument is always: look at the causes, he didn't build himself. And nobody ever gets extra time for having had a soft, lucky, well-resourced childhood, even though that's a cause too, and equally unchosen. So causation counts in one direction only. On top of that, in my jurisdiction (as I gather in real ones) you can only introduce that causal material up to the moment you turned eighteen; after that everything you did is treated as you, full stop, as if the causes switch off at midnight on your birthday.

Then twenty years later the same system turns around and asks the guy to show he's changed, and treats his having changed as a reason to let him out early — which seems to presuppose that somewhere along the line he became the author of his own character, the one thing the Basic Argument says nobody ever manages.

So what I need straight, and I'd rather have the real structure than something that just sounds good in dialogue:

1. What does mitigating-for-background actually presuppose? Does it strictly need some baseline view — that there's a normal, adequately in-charge agent, that falling below it excuses, and that being above it is just the default no one gets credit for — or is that only how it happens to get argued in courtrooms? Would several different theories of responsibility license the same one-directional asymmetry, in which case my sentencing scene is just underdetermined and I should stop trying to make it mean exactly one thing? And does mitigation even survive if you accept the Basic Argument, or does it become an orphan — something the system goes on doing with nothing left holding it up?

2. What does my made-up same-person finding presuppose? Is it really a claim about personal identity over time, or can you get the same hearing out of something thinner — that the applicant no longer endorses what he did, that punishment has a kind of expiry date, that we're tracking change for reasons that have nothing to do with blame at all? And if the board finds he's changed for the better, does that make him less blameworthy, or only less frightening, or does it do some third thing I'm not seeing?

3. If a jurisdiction took the Basic Argument seriously all the way down, what would have to change in these two practices? Would sentencing and release collapse into one purely forward-looking risk assessment, or is there a version of blame that survives and still looks more or less like what we do now?

I'd especially like to know where I'm confusing two different things, since I suspect I definitely am: my board chair keeps wanting to say we're not in the business of ultimate responsibility, we're in the business of whether you can be trusted with a key — and I can't tell whether that's a dodge or the whole answer.
