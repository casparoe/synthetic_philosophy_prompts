I'm in a small reading group — four of us, all of whom came to philosophy by way of the rationalist blogs, so the house instinct is that any dispute that can't be cashed out in a bet is probably verbal somewhere. We've been stuck for two weeks on whether genuine moral dilemmas are possible. One member, who leans consequentialist and has a low tolerance for what he calls spooky normativity, thinks he can dissolve the question rather than answer it. His argument doesn't just conclude that dilemmas don't happen; it concludes that the phenomenology of being in one is always either self-deception or confusion, and he's cheerful about that — he calls it the Flatness Result, and he thinks flatness is what you should expect once you stop reifying the balance of reasons into a set of ghosts that outlive it. I've written the argument out as cleanly as I can, because I want the diagnosis rather than the verdict:

(1) All-things-considered verdicts are exhaustive: when the balance of reasons settles that you ought to do A, every consideration that told against A has been fully spent in arriving at that verdict, and no normative significance is left over.
(2) Moral residue — remorse, guilt, the sense that something is owed and has gone unpaid — is warranted only where some claim on you survived your action unexhausted.
(3) So if you do what you have most reason to do, no residue is warranted; whatever you feel afterwards is either regret that the world is bad, which isn't a moral response, or discomfort at your own uncertainty, which isn't residue.
(4) The cases everyone calls dilemmas — Sophie's choice, the two children in the water, the promise you can keep only by betraying a confidence — are exactly the cases in which agents report residue that seems not merely painful but fitting.
(5) In such a case, either the agent knows which action the balance of reasons favors, or she doesn't. If she does, and does it, then by (3) no residue is warranted. If she doesn't, what she feels is anxiety about her own ignorance, and calling it residue just dramatizes uncertainty.
(6) So the residue that was supposed to be the signature of a genuine dilemma is noise either way. Strip it out and there is nothing left to distinguish a real dilemma from a merely hard choice, so there are no genuine moral dilemmas.

What bothers me is that the thing works too well. Run (1) through (3) on a case involving no conflict of requirements at all — the lorry driver who kills a child through no negligence of his own, or an official who must do something morally ugly to avert something worse, where nobody denies there was a single thing she ought to have done — and the argument returns the same answer: no residue warranted, because the verdict was exhaustive and she acted on it. But there the conclusion looks flatly wrong. The driver's remorse and the official's sense of having dirty hands are paradigms of fitting response, and the literature even has a special term, agent-regret, for the first. So the argument isn't really about dilemmas; it's an argument against warranted residue anywhere, with the no-dilemma conclusion as a corollary, and I don't think my friend has noticed that he has paid for the corollary twice over. At the same time I don't want to stop at the absurdity, because the argument looks valid to me and something in it is doing real work — I'd rather be told which step is bearing the weight than be told the conclusion is false.

So: name the step you reject, say why it looked innocent before you pulled on it, and then price the alternatives, including the ones you didn't pick — what does giving up (1) cost you about the settledness of all-things-considered judgment, what does giving up (2) turn residue into, and if your answer is to reject (5), what exactly is the third option it leaves out and what machinery do you need to state it? One caveat on my own instincts: if forced I'd sooner give up something in the first three premises than give up (4) or the claim that dilemmas are real, but I don't trust that ordering, since (4) is the premise most likely to be contaminated by whatever theory I picked up from the cases themselves.
