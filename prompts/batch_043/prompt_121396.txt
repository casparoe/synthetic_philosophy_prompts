I'm retired now — I spent my career in ion channel biophysics, and since I stopped I've been auditing a philosophy of mind seminar at the university, mostly as the annoying person at the back who keeps asking what the operational definition of a term is. A few weeks ago we had a session on machine consciousness / AI welfare and I went home irritated, and I've been scribbling ever since. What came out is about a page of notes toward a short essay saying that the "reward is valence, so reinforcement learners might be suffering" move rests on a category mistake — and not for the reason people usually give (carbon chauvinism, or the claim that the computation is too simple to matter). My version of the worry is structural: the framing is set up so that the evidence can never be evidence. I tried the idea out on some former colleagues at the departmental Christmas thing and got nowhere, because half of them thought I was saying machines definitly can't suffer and the other half thought I was saying they obviously can, and I'm not sure either reading is what the notes say.

Here are the notes, more or less as they stand:

- Main claim: a reward signal is a *shaping* quantity. Whatever you pick as R is, by construction, what makes the agent behave as it does. So "look, it avoids the thing that lowers R, the way an animal avoids a shock" is circular — of course it does, that's what R is for. To get from reward to valence you need a handle on valence that isn't already saturated by facts about behavioural disposition. I've been calling this the calibration problem, but I've no idea whether it's new or whether everyone already has a name for it.
- Candidate independent handle: valence can come apart from avoidance. People seek out states they'd describe as unpleasant (grief, horror films, very hot curry, revenge that leaves you feeling worse afterwards). Define valence as strength of the disposition to avoid and you lose all of those; define it as something over and above that, and you owe an account of what — and you don't get to just help yourself to "the feeling."
- Second thread, the one I think is load-bearing: call it the online intrusion condition. In animals, aversive states seem to do a distinctive job — they globally interrupt whatever the animal was doing, they grab control, they outlast the stimulus, and they're what makes the animal work to change things *now* rather than merely learn for next time. A gradient update doesn't do that. It happens after the episode, off to the side, while the policy carries on executing; nothing competes for the same resource. So the functional role we actually care about when we worry about suffering is unoccupied. (Objection I can't shake: that's a contingent fact about current architectures, not a principled barrier. Probably true — but then the essay should be about actual and near-term systems and not about RL in the abstract, and I should say so up front.)
- Third thread, my favourite and also the one I trust least: the reward-prediction-error business. As I understand the neuroscience, the signals that look like "pleasure" actually track surprise rather than goodness — biggest response when something unexpected happens, shrinking as the outcome gets predictable. So if you read R, or the TD error, straight off as felt valence, you get a system whose most intense experience is the moment its model of the world breaks, and whose pleasures fade with familiarity. That's backwards from any notion of valence I recognise, and it suggests the mapping isn't even the right shape. Please tell me if I've got the biology wrong here, it's the part I'm least sure of.
- Two things that worry me about my own argument. (1) Am I just reinstating the other-minds problem for machines and dressing it up in systems neuroscience? If the calibration point works it looks like it proves too much — I can't run that test on you either. (2) If the intrusion stuff is the load-bearing part, there's an uncomfortable consequence I should probably face: someone could just go and build the intrusion in, and then my argument gives me no reason at all to say that system is fine. Bug or feature?

What I'd like from you, if you're willing: read the notes as a referee would. Is there a real argument in there or have I reinvented the wheel under a new name? Where exactly does it break, and which of the three threads should I promote to the main line and which should I cut, given that I probably have about 2000 words in me? Is the "stake that isn't exhausted by dispositions" idea a genuine distinction or just a relabelling of "what it's like"? And what's the strongest reply I haven't considered — I'd rather hear the objection now than after I've written it up. Don't bother with a reading list or telling me which papers to look up: I've lost my journal access and I'd much rather you just say the thing in your own words. Same goes for the machine learning — if I've got the mechanics of policy gradient or TD learning wrong, say so plainly, that's the part where I'm most likely to be talking nonsense.
