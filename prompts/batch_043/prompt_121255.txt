I'm finishing a thesis chapter on gradations of moral status — the chapter is supposed to run, continuously, from insects down to thermostats — and before I defend it I would like an audit of the package of commitments I have assembled. I have listed them in the order in which I find myself arguing for them, not in order of confidence.

1. Gradualism about the base property: whatever it is in virtue of which a system has moral status, it comes in degrees, and it varies continuously with the complexity of the system's processing. There is no biologically principled discontinuity anywhere on the scale, and in particular no bright line to be drawn at vertebrates.

2. A genuine zero: below some level of organisation — thermostats, and I would argue anything lacking integrated, valenced, globally available states — moral status is exactly zero, not a very small positive number. I take no comfort in panpsychism and reject the idea that everything registers a little. I have been reassuring myself that (1) and (2) are compatible because moral weight can go to zero continuously as one approaches the threshold from above.

3. Welfare is valence all the way down: what makes a state better or worse for its subject is solely how it feels. Frustrated preferences that are nowhere registered in experience, opportunities never taken, dignity, autonomy and proper functioning contribute nothing beyond whatever experiential difference they make.

4. But per-moment weight also seems to me to scale with how much experience there is: holding felt intensity fixed, I want to say that a creature with a large and richly differentiated phenomenal field has more at stake in a single moment than one whose entire field is a single undifferentiated twinge.

5. Intensity does not track complexity: I can find no argument from cognitive complexity to felt intensity, and one argument in the opposite direction. Complex minds bring top-down modulation, anticipation, distraction and retrospective sense-making, all of which look as though they blunt or buffer what is felt, whereas a very simple creature may undergo its pain with nothing at all to buffer it.

6. The case verdict I am committed to: insects fall on the positive side of the threshold but well down it, with a per-unit-time moral weight two to four orders of magnitude below a mammal's.

7. Grain neutrality: I aggregate over successive phenomenal moments, and I stipulate that each moment's contribution is proportional to its duration. I do this because insects appear to resolve temporal change far faster than we do, and if moments counted equally then their much finer grain would multiply their weight per second.

8. Aggregation and uncertainty: I sum linearly across individuals, with no floor beneath which a being drops out of the sum; and because my uncertainty about nearly everything above is deep, I want to use expected values computed with my actual credences, while also allowing a precautionary tilt where the stakes are astronomically large and the probability is not negligible.

9. Replaceability: for creatures that lack the kind of diachronic unity that makes a future something they can have, a painless death is no loss to them, so I count experiential welfare only and ignore deaths entirely in the ledger.

10. A negative desideratum: I do not want the view to collapse into the panpsychist's numerology. It should not turn out that the bottom of the scale, which is occupied by vastly more systems than the top, swamps everything above it.

The symptom that prompted this: when I combine (6), (8) and the population facts, expected insect suffering appears to dominate every other item in the ledger by many orders of magnitude, and I cannot tell whether that is a discovery or a reductio.

What I want from you: go through the ten and tell me which pairs are in real tension and which only look as though they are, and rank the real ones by severity rather than flagging them all equally. For each real tension, say precisely which commitment does the work, what would have to be given up, and what the cheapest available revision is and what that revision costs me elsewhere in the set. For each apparent tension, say in a sentence or two why it dissolves. Then tell me whether any single revision would clean up more than one of the serious cases at once, and whether there is anything in the list that is doing no work at all and could simply be deleted.

Two qualifications. First, my own suspicion is that the trouble is concentrated in the categorical items — (2) and (9) — sitting inside an otherwise scalar picture, but my supervisor says those are the easy ones and that I have misidentified the problem, so please do not simply take my word for where the difficulty lies. Second, I would rather not be given a tour of the literature on this: I need to know what my own ten commitments commit me to, stated in terms of them, so that I can decide which one to drop before the defence.
