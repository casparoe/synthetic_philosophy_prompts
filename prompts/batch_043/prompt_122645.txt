The exam question (third-year epistemology, take-home paper, 600-word limit):

In the notebooks published as On Certainty, Wittgenstein suggests that our questions and our doubts rest on propositions that are exempt from doubt, and that these work rather like hinges on which a door turns. He also remarks that the river-bed of thought may itself shift, and that there is no sharp division between the movement of the waters on the bed and the shift of the bed. Call the position built from these remarks hinge epistemology, with the following stipulations.

A hinge commitment H of a subject S is arational: S does not hold H on the basis of evidence or argument, and H is not the upshot of any inquiry. Rather, H is what makes some practice of giving and asking for reasons possible. H is cross-cutting if doubting it would disable doubt and justification as such, in every domain (traditional candidates: that there is an external world, that there are other minds, that I am not now dreaming). H is domain-local if doubting it disables only one practice (traditional candidates: that physical objects persist unperceived, for perceptual reporting; that the Earth long predates my birth, for history and testimony; that my memory is broadly reliable, for remembering). A domain-local hinge can in principle be assessed from a neighbouring practice that does not presuppose it; a cross-cutting hinge, by hypothesis, admits of no such external vantage point.

This gives rise to the fixture problem. A hinge, literally understood, has to be fixed to something; a domain-local hinge is held in place — challenged or corroborated — from within a neighbouring practice. But what holds a cross-cutting hinge in place, given that every practice already rests on it? The self-anchoring response says: the very practice that the hinge makes possible. The practice, in being exercised, fixes the hinge that makes it possible, and nothing external is needed. Critics press the idle-hinge objection: if the practice fixes the hinge, the anchoring is circular, the hinge turns out to be doing no independent work, and any practice whatsoever would anchor itself equally well.

Assess the self-anchoring response. Your answer must (a) state precisely what the self-anchoring theorist has to show if the response is to answer the fixture problem rather than merely restate it; (b) say whether, on this view, a cross-cutting hinge could ever be lost or eroded, and reconcile your answer with Wittgenstein's remark about the shifting river-bed; (c) explain how the resulting position differs from classical foundationalism's response to the regress of justification — in particular, whether the self-anchoring hinge is simply a basic belief under another name.

Candidate 4071's answer (508 words):

Hinge epistemology gets its bite from the claim that justification comes to an end not in a special class of reasons but in something that is not a reason at all. The fixture problem is serious only for cross-cutting hinges. A domain-local hinge, such as the broad reliability of memory where remembering is concerned, can be checked from a neighbouring practice that does not rest on it: I test memory against perception and perception against memory, and each can correct the other. A cross-cutting hinge, such as the existence of the external world, is presupposed by every practice, so no neighbouring practice is available, and there is no vantage point from which it could be supported or impugned. That is why the fixture problem bites.

(a) The self-anchoring theorist has to show that being held in place need not mean being supported by reasons. What must be shown is that the exercise of the practice that the hinge makes possible is not merely evidence for the hinge but constitutive of the hinge's holding at all: there is no gap between our going on in this way and the hinge's being in place. If the reply instead offered the practice as a reason for the hinge, it would collapse into bootstrapping, which is just the idle-hinge objection, since the practice can count as successful only by presupposing the very hinge it was supposed to secure. So the self-anchorist must insist that the anchoring relation is non-epistemic.

(b) Could a cross-cutting hinge be lost? I think not. Because hinges are arational, they are neither supported by evidence nor answerable to it; no possible experience could dislodge one, and since a cross-cutting hinge is presupposed by every practice, no change within any practice could displace it either. This is consistent with the river-bed remark: what shifts is the water, the ordinary empirical propositions, while the bed, which is the hinges, remains. And it is good news for the self-anchorist, since if hinges could never come loose, there is no fixture problem for them in the first place.

(c) The view is therefore not classical foundationalism under another name. Foundationalism answers the regress by positing basic beliefs that are justified non-inferentially and, in its classical form, are certain and incorrigible; a hinge is not justified at all, and so it cannot be non-inferentially justified. Still, the anchoring relation is genuine: the practice, in being exercised, supplies its own justification for the hinge. My successful perceptual judgments confirm that there is an external world, and the circularity here is benign rather than vicious, because the confirmation is global — it comes from the total practice rather than from any single judgment that already presupposes the hinge. So the hinge is both fixed by the practice and confirmed by it, and the fixture problem is answered.
