I wonder whether you would be willing to help me with something that has been troubling me since I took up tutoring A-level mathematics in retirement. My own working life was spent in experimental chemistry — nothing remotely connected with logic — and I have come to philosophy late, so I should be grateful if you would forgive any clumsiness with the vocabulary and correct it where you can.

The practice I want to ask about is the marking of school mathematics. Candidates are told to 'show your working', and the mark schemes are built out of two distinct currencies: method marks, awarded for the steps of a derivation, and accuracy marks, awarded for getting the right answer. Several features of this practice strike me as hard to reconcile with one another. A candidate who writes down the correct answer and nothing else receives almost no credit, however right the answer is. A candidate who makes an early slip of arithmetic and then reasons impeccably from the wrong number still collects the later method marks — the examiners call this 'follow-through' — so the inference is judged against what the candidate actually had in front of him rather than against the truth. And in a 'show that' question, where the result to be proved is printed in the paper itself, what is being examined can only be the derivation, since the conclusion has already been settled by the authority of the exam board.

Yet the same practice penalises a candidate who tries to *state* the licence for his step: one who writes, alongside P and if-P-then-Q, the further line that if P and if P then Q then Q, is given nothing for it and is, in my experience, quietly marked down for failing to understand what a proof is. Much the same happens with the word 'obviously': from a strong candidate it passes as a step, from a weak one it is treated as an unlicensed leap. And examiners will sometimes reject an inference for using a technique outside the syllabus — a calculus method on a paper that forbids it — even when the reasoning itself is impeccable, which suggests that what is being policed is whether a rule is *authorised* rather than whether it is *true*.

This looks to me like Carroll's little dialogue about Achilles and the Tortoise turned into an institution, and I cannot tell what the institution thinks it is doing. So my question is: what view of what makes an inference justified does this practice presuppose?

In particular, I should like to know the following. Does it strictly presuppose that the justification for a step is *rule-shaped* rather than *reading-shaped* — that the Tortoise's regress terminates in something which is not another proposition from which the conclusion might be drawn — or does it presuppose only the weaker, pedagogical claim that it is useful to keep the licence implicit? Are its parts mutually consistent: can a practice that follows through from a false premise also deduct accuracy marks for the same falsehood, and can it insist that every step be shown while penalising the candidate who shows the rule? Where is it underdetermined — could the truth-preservation view (a step is valid when it can never take you from truths to a falsehood), the view that rules are norms of a practice one is trained into, and the view that the laws of logic are merely the best-supported of our theories and revisable like any other, all keep exactly this mark scheme, and if so what is the smallest commitment common to them? And what would actually have to change in the marking if the first of those views replaced the one implicit now: would examiners check interpretations rather than steps, would a proof that the rules preserve truth become relevant to them, and would a scheme offering a bonus mark for every rule explicitly stated collapse into the regress?

I have a tentative idea of my own which may be the wrong sort of thing altogether. In the laboratory we distinguished a reading from a calibration: you calibrate a balance against a standard weight, the calibration is not itself one more reading, and you cannot calibrate an instrument using only itself. The Tortoise, it seems to me, is demanding a calibration in the shape of a reading. Is that a useful way to put it, or does it merely relocate the mystery — and if the rules of inference really are to steps as a standard is to readings, what sort of thing is the standard, and should a mark scheme care whether it makes for true conclusions or only whether it is the one in force?
