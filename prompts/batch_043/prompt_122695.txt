You are a philosopher of science and social epistemologist who has spent a decade advising foundations, aid agencies, and impact investors on how they form justified beliefs about organizations they cannot observe directly. You know the biological and economic literature on costly signaling and the handicap principle in detail: handicaps as deliberately wasteful displays, the requirement that a signal be differentially costly for the types it is supposed to separate, the difference between an index (mechanically tied to the quality, hard to fake) and a conventional signal (arbitrary in form, stabilized only by cost), counter-signaling by the highest-quality individuals who can afford not to perform, the possibility that a signal's cost is transferred rather than burned, and what happens to a signal once the receiver's response begins rewarding it. Think carefully and at length. Before you draft anything procedural, spend a few hundred words working out, for yourself, which of these commitments are load-bearing for a practitioner and which are refinements that must be sacrificed to usability, and flag any step where you think the theory is genuinely contested or silent. Do not retreat into generalities: every gate you propose should be one that a program officer could actually run on a Tuesday afternoon with the files in front of them.

Here is my situation. I direct a small foundation that makes unrestricted three-year grants averaging about sixty thousand dollars a year to grassroots organizations in countries where we have no staff of our own. We receive roughly two hundred and forty applications a year and fund twelve. Two program officers do the first pass over six to eight weeks; the due-diligence budget for a finalist is about two thousand dollars, which is not enough for a site visit in most cases, though it is enough to pay a local consultant for a day or two. A board of five must ratify every grant; board members are intelligent people with no training in any of this and they will not read more than a one-page memo per finalist. Rejected applicants can request a written explanation, so whatever we do has to be statable in plain language without jargon. A wrong pick costs three years of money and, in the worst cases, real damage to the people the organization was supposed to serve.

I want a written decision procedure for deciding which parts of an application we are entitled to treat as evidence of organizational quality, which parts we should treat as evidence of something else (and of what), and which parts we should record as non-evidence and ignore. Structure it as an ordered series of gates, each with a question, a statement of what counts as a sufficient answer, and an explicit stopping rule: fatal (stop, do not count this as evidence) versus defeasible (continue, but downweight).

Some distinctions I want to survive the translation into practice, and I want you to tell me if I have got the priority wrong:

1. Name the hidden quality, the signaler, and the audience before evaluating any signal, and say what decision the audience's response consists of. A signal is only honest relative to a receiver whose response sustains it.
2. The currency test. Identify the currency in which the cost is paid: cash, staff hours, reputational exposure, physical or legal risk, opportunity cost of time. Then check whether that currency is identical to the quality being signaled (which makes the evidence circular), independent of it (which makes the signal arbitrary but potentially honest), or anti-correlated with it (which makes the signal actively perverse; I suspect several of ours are).
3. Net cost, not gross cost. Subtract everything the signaler gets back regardless of type: visibility, a consultant's help, a fiscal sponsor's underwriting, a tax benefit, skills learned in the process, resale value of the asset. Where the cost is borne by someone other than the applicant, a paid grantwriter, a US fiscal sponsor, a better-funded partner, the signal is cost-shifted and I want a specific instruction for detecting that.
4. Index versus conventional signal, with different audit standards for each: for the index, ask what would have to be true for it to be fabricated and how we would ever find out; for the conventional signal, ask what sustains its cost and what happens when we start rewarding it.
5. Burned versus transferred cost, and what each one certifies. A requirement that consumes staff hours may certify very little and may consume the very capacity it was meant to certify; a requirement that obliges an applicant to raise money from its own community may be an index of local buy-in rather than of program quality. I want these handled differently.
6. Audience leak and non-uniform cost. A signal aimed at us may also be read by local competitors, regulators, landlords, or a hostile state apparatus, which can raise its cost enormously for some applicants and not others, in ways that invert the ranking we were hoping for. Requiring public visibility is the case that worries me most.
7. Counter-signaling at the top. Organizations with established reputational capital and other funders often decline to perform for us. I want a rule for when the absence of a signal is uninformative rather than negative.
8. Dynamics. What happens to this signal over two or three cycles if we reward it, who can eventually afford it, and at what point does it become a costly obstacle that carries no information? I also want a candid rule about deliberately wasteful requirements, including whether there is any case in which we are permitted to impose one, and who has to sign off if we do.

Please be explicit about the points where judgment cannot be eliminated and must be handed to a person because the question is one of values rather than fact. I can think of at least three: how much burden we are willing to impose on applicants we will probably not fund, how to weigh false negatives (missing a superb but inarticulate group) against false positives (funding a merely presentable one), and whether to treat a small pilot grant as a gift or as a purchase of information.

Then apply the whole procedure to four cases.

First, a fourteen-page narrative with a polished logic model and an M&E framework in flawless English, submitted alongside three years of monthly bank statements and a payroll list reconciled by a named local accountant, from a youth-employment organization in Nairobi.

Second, an applicant with no website, no annual report, no English-language materials, and only a referral from an intermediary we trust, compared against an applicant with a communications officer and a glossy site.

Third, a matching requirement: we match, dollar for dollar, the first ten thousand dollars an applicant raises locally.

Fourth, and this is the one where I expect you to tell me something I do not want to hear: our board's favorite device, the dedication filter, in which shortlisted organizations send a staff member to a regional hub for a full day of interviews at their own expense, roughly four hundred to nine hundred dollars plus two days of time, and considerably more where a visa is involved. The board believes this separates the committed from the opportunistic. Run the procedure on it without softening the result, tell me what it actually screens for, tell me whether reimbursing travel would help or destroy it, and if you think it should be replaced, say what should replace it and what that replacement will cost the applicants.

Finally, give me a one-page plain-language version suitable for laminating and for a ninety-minute training with board members, and then a short closing section on what this procedure cannot do: the cases it will systematically misfire on, the equilibria it cannot see (including sectors where every local organization has adapted to a different, larger funder's signals), and the questions it cannot answer at all.
