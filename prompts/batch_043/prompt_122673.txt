I help run a small film club in a town whose main street has been hollowed out by a big-box store, and every December we screen It's a Wonderful Life. I also spent last winter reading about luck egalitarianism on my own, after a friend lent me a book about it and I got interested in the question of which of our misfortunes we should be asked to carry ourselves. Now I can't watch the film without seeing brute luck everywhere in it: the $8,000 that Uncle Billy folds into a newspaper that ends up in Potter's hands, the Depression-era run on the Building & Loan, the ear George loses saving his brother, the whole apparatus of a man who keeps nearly making it out of town and keeps getting pulled back. I'd like you to read the film through luck egalitarianism — not as an illustration of a theory I already understand, but as something that might press on the theory. Which of George's misfortunes does the view classify as brute luck and which as the upshot of gambles he chose to take, and is that classification one the film would accept?

Two half-formed ideas of my own have been nagging at me, and I'd like you to test them against both the film and the philosophy rather than just agreeing with me. The first is that the Building & Loan itself is the real moral agent of the film: it is a device for pooling the town's housing risk against a man who makes his money by buying people up when they are uninsured, and Pottersville is what Bedford Falls looks like when that pooling institution has never existed. On that reading the film isn't really about one man's bad night; it's about the difference between a community that has built an institution for absorbing brute luck and one that hasn't, and that strikes me as much closer to what luck-egalitarian justice would look like on the ground than any individual transfer of money would be. The second idea I've been calling absorbed luck: George repeatedly and voluntarily takes on other people's brute luck — Uncle Billy's mistake, the depositors' panic, his brother's drowning — in situations where the alternative was not really available to him, since you cannot let your brother drown or let the depositors lose everything. Standard luck egalitarianism seems to me to treat that as option luck and therefore to let the loss stay with George, and that feels like exactly the wrong result. Does the distinction I want between insurance (a risk I choose to carry at a known price) and absorption (a risk that is dumped on me because walking away was not a live option) hold up, or does it collapse into the familiar objection that luck egalitarianism is harsh to people who make bad gambles?

I also want the parts where the reading fails, because I don't want to stand up in front of forty people and pretend the film is a philosophy paper. No one in the film claims George is owed anything, including George: the money arrives as gratitude and the ending is a gift, which is close to what one critic of luck egalitarianism says is wrong with the whole approach — that it turns the unlucky into objects of pity rather than equals who can make claims. Clarence's counterfactual seems to answer a different question altogether, about whether a life matters, not about what anyone is due. And the community the film is so sentimental about is a narrow one — largely white, largely homeowners — so I wonder whether a theory focused on individual luck shares the film's blind spot about who was never in the risk pool to begin with. If you were leading the discussion after the screening, which two or three scenes would you point us to, where does the film actually add something to the debate that the arguments alone don't give you, and where would you say plainly that it is dodging the question?
