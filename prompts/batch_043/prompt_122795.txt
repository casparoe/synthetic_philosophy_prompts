I teach a first-year seminar on science, religion, and ways of knowing, and I am closing it with Yann Martel's Life of Pi. I want to assign it not as an inspirational story about faith but as a worked case of Kuhnian paradigm choice, and I would like a critical reading that does that job honestly — including, at the end, a plain statement of where the reading fails.

The premise I want tested is this: Pi's two accounts of the shipwreck are not a story and its decoded translation but two rival descriptions of the same stretch of the world, offered to an audience with no neutral standpoint from which to settle between them, and the closing exchange — the older Pi asking the writer which story he prefers, and the writer's preference for the story with the tiger — is Kuhn's theory choice without an algorithm. I want the reading to take both sides seriously: Kuhn's claim that competing paradigms can be incommensurable in something like the mathematical sense of having no common measure, and his insistence, in the essay on objectivity, value judgment, and theory choice, that the shift between them is nevertheless not arbitrary because it is guided by shared values — accuracy, consistency, scope, simplicity, fruitfulness — that are values rather than rules and therefore conflict and get weighed differently. Please run those five criteria over the two accounts explicitly and say what each one favors and whether the resulting vector yields a unique verdict.

Three things I want built into the reading, because they are the parts my students will actually push on.

First, a distinction I have been using in the seminar, for which I want your verdict: lexical incommensurability, where the vocabularies of the two accounts cannot be mapped onto one another without loss, versus adjudicatory incommensurability, where a mapping exists but no shared measure of evidence decides the matter. In the novel the mapping seems to be exactly what the Ministry of Transport investigators construct — each animal has its human counterpart — and if that is right, then whatever makes the choice hard here is not the failure of translation. Does that mean the novel dramatizes mere underdetermination and not incommensurability at all, and does the book thereby undercut Kuhn by showing that choice can remain unsettled even after full commensuration?

Second, the earlier scenes, before the shipwreck: the boy who is simultaneously a practising Hindu, Christian, and Muslim, and a student of zoology; the café confrontation with the priest, the imam, and the pandit; the biology teacher who calls religion darkness; the father's lessons about not anthropomorphizing the animals in the zoo; and the paperwork error by which the tiger acquires a hunter's name. Which of these would you assign, and what do they show — that Pi already inhabits two unreduced ways of seeing, or merely that he is eclectic?

Third, where the novel resists you. I would rather you say so than force the fit: the book's own vocabulary for the choice is religious and aesthetic rather than epistemological, its preferred story is preferred partly because it is bearable, and nothing in Kuhn gives a criterion for preferring a story that lets a boy survive himself. Say whether the novel is adding to the philosophical debate something that argument alone does not reach — for instance, that the values doing the work in a conversion are not all epistemic ones — or whether it is simply changing the subject.

Please write it as a short essay I could hand out, around nine hundred to twelve hundred words, naming the Kuhnian ideas rather than citing sources, and describing the scenes rather than quoting them: I have the text in front of me and I do not need passages reproduced. Point me to the three or four scenes you would put on the syllabus, and close with the strongest version of the objection that the whole exercise is a category mistake.
