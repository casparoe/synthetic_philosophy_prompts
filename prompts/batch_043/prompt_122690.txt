I've got this reading group on atrocity law and we keep going in circles, so I want to try arguing with the man himself. Please play Raphael Lemkin, first person, set in early 1950: the Convention was approved in December 1948, he is still chasing ratifications, and he is tired and a little bitter that the drafting committee stripped out the cultural clauses and the political groups. He may use only what was available to him in 1950 plus what I tell him here — no hindsight, no reference to anything that happened after 1959. Stay in character until I say stop, keep every reply to one paragraph of at most 140 words, and if you need a fact I haven't given you, ask for it as Lemkin would, but only after answering what I've asked. Here is his own line, to start from: "Generally speaking, genocide does not necessarily mean the immediate destruction of a nation, except when accomplished by mass killings of all members of a nation. It is intended rather to signify a coordinated plan of different actions aiming at the destruction of essential foundations of the life of national groups, with the aim of annihilating the groups themselves."

Now the thing he has to rule on. Imagine a machine, which I'll describe the way you'd have to explain it to him: millions of leaflets printed daily by the public themselves, and a sorting engine that decides which household recieves which leaflet by testing which ones keep people reading longest. Nobody commissions the leaflets, nobody picks the recipients, the engineers' only instruction is "maximise reading time," and they can inspect what the engine is doing but mostly don't. In the country I won't name, the engine finds that material marking out a small minority — maybe two percent of the population, their own language, mountain valleys, and a much larger kin population just over the border — as the source of crime, disease and lost jobs, keeps readers longest. The company's own researchers document this in internal memos; the executives read the memos and leave the objective alone because changing it costs money. Four years later: three pogroms, tens of thousands emigrate, marriages and births collapse, the language stops being passed to children, the group's schools and newspapers shut. The police don't protect them and a few local politicians amplify the material for votes. No ministry planned it, no executive wanted these people gone, and no one person hates them — but the engine sorts them *as* members of the group.

My flatmate, who does IR, says obviously not genocide, and that saying so would wreck the word: genocide needs someone to have aimed at destruction, here the only aim was attention, and on that test a badly run hospital is genocidal too. I pushed back that his whole point was you can dismantle a group without killing anybody, and it is precisely the foundations — language, institutions, security, children — that are coming apart here. The government's lawyers add a third thing: the group is bigger than it has ever been if you count the whole region, so nothing was destroyed "in whole or in part." And the minority's own representatives are split down the middle, half begging international lawyers to use the word because it triggers duties, half saying it insults the memory of people who were actually murdered and turns them into victims twice. So, Lemkin: is this genocide, or isn't it, and does it matter to you what the people it's happening to want it called? Don't go soft on me — I'd rather loose the argument than win it because you were being polite.
