Hey — I'm putting together a discussion starter for a small training module on governance projects, and I could use a dialogue. I've been arguing about this with two friends for weeks and we keep talking in circles, so I'd rather hand people the disagreement properly staged than my muddled summary of it.

Setup, all invented: in the districts of this made-up country, whoever wins a road-maintenance contract pays what everybody locally calls the levy — a rough slice of the contract value, handed to the district chair, spent on funeral costs, school fees, drought relief, and, in the run-up to elections, on household gifts. None of it appears in the procurement code; reciepts are handwritten when they exist at all. A donor-backed Open Procurement Initiative wants to criminalize both paying and receiving it, plus fund a whistleblower hotline. A group of local reformers has a counter-proposal they call the public counter-gift: keep the levy but make it a statutory surcharge at a rate the council sets and publishes, pay it into an audited district fund, and — the part they insist on — strip the chair of all discretion over individual payouts, so disbursement follows a published rule and nobody ends up owing anybody a personal debt.

What I want is a dialogue between two people who both know this world well and both want less predation in it: say, a governance adviser with the donor (trained as a political theorist before she went into consulting) and a political theorist at the local university who has spent years documenting how the levy actually operates. Neither should be a strawman and neither should win by assertion — I want them to answer each other's real arguments. The adviser has to genuinely engage with the point that the levy is doing the work of a welfare state that doesn't exist, and that banning it without building fiscal capacity mostly just drives it underground and raises the price; the local theorist has to genuinely engage with the point that un-audited, relationship-dependent discretion systematically benefits whoever is closest to the chair, prices out small contractors who aren't in the network, and that appeals to local tradition are exactly the line the people actually looting the country use. Please have them get past the opening exchange and converge on sharpening what the real crux is: whether what's wrong here is the transfer itself or the discretion and the personal obligation it creates — the converting of something people should be entitled to into a favor someone grants them — and therefore whether making the transfer public and rule-governed fixes the thing or quietly destroys the thing that made the levy work (is a legally mandated, rule-bound gift still a gift, or just a tax with extra steps?). I'd also like them to tangle with whether there's some category of thing that shouldn't be tradeable even with full publicity and consent — a judge's ruling, a procurement ranking, a vote — and who gets to decide which things those are, and to run the awkward case of the minister who takes money from contractors and spends every cent of it on the constituency, while the donor's own by-the-book procurement rules reliably steer contracts to large foreign firms.

After the dialogue, add a short out-of-character coda (a paragraph or two) saying which of the disagreements you staged are live disputes among people who actually work on this and which you think one character is simply getting wrong, and flagging anywhere the argument leans on a contested empirical claim — like whether criminalization really does make things worse on net — rather than on something settled.
