A friend sent me a blog post about acting for the right reasons and I've been going back and forth with him about it for like a week, and I'm out of my depth. I write backend services, I have zero philosophy training, so if one of us is saying something dumb it's probably me.

The post summarizes the view roughly like this:

"Kant's idea is that what matters morally isn't what you do, it's why you do it. An action has 'moral worth' only when you do it purely out of duty — if you do the right thing because you feel like it, or because it makes you happy, or because you'd feel guilty otherwise, then the action is still the correct one but it has no moral worth, you get zero credit for it. So doing the right thing for the wrong reason is morally worthless, and duty is the only reason that counts. Taken seriously this means the person who has to drag themselves to the hospital to visit a sick friend, hating every minute of it, has more moral worth than the person who goes because they actually want to see their friend. Which is obviously absurd, and that's basically why nobody defends this position anymore."

My gut says the last bit is a strawman, but I can't explain why, and I also can't tell which of the earlier sentences are the real view and which are the blogger sliding something in. Can you go through it more or less line by line and tell me what someone who actually holds this kind of view would accept, what they'd wince at, and what's just wrong? And whether the "obviously absurd, nobody defends it" conclusion survives once you fix the misreadings — or whether the hospital thing really is a problem for them.

Also, two things I came up with on my own that I want to know are real distinctions or me inventing them:

First, is "acting for the right reasons" the same as being able to state the right reason? My colleague said no, that's the difference between a justification and the thing that actually caused you to act, like the difference between the reason in a commit message and the reason the code was actually written. That sounds right to me but it might be a distinction I made up.

Second, the part that really gets me: if your desires were shaped by training — say you were raised, or reinforced, into genuinely wanting to help people — does that make you like the person with the nice disposition, i.e. no moral worth? Because if so the view seems to punish you for having been well-trained, which feels backwards. And by the same logic, if I write a cron job that donates money to charity every month, is that the most Kantian agent possible or the least?
