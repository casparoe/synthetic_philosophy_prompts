I write literary fiction — third-person, past-tense novels that slip in and out of free indirect style — and my copy editor and I keep losing arguments about conditional sentences. My last manuscript had nearly a hundred of them, and on about a dozen we flatly disagreed: is the thing in the "if" clause being presented as something that happened, as something that did not, or as something still open? And whose state of knowledge settles that — the narrator's, or the character whose head we are in? In free indirect style that is precisely what I often don't want pinned down. A friend who reads linguistics tells me this is the territory of indicative versus counterfactual conditionals, which suggests there is more structure here than my ear supplies.

What I need is a procedure my copy editor can run on each conditional in a manuscript, in order, with no training in linguistics: the questions to ask, what counts as an adequate answer to each one, the point at which the procedure says "this has to be rewritten," and the point at which it hands the question back to me because it is a matter of what I'm trying to do rather than of correctness. It has to be runnable in roughly ten seconds a sentence, it has to say how to handle nested conditionals and "even if," and it should be explicit about which of its questions are judgment calls wearing procedural clothing. I sign off on whatever it flags, and I need it to be honest about where it will misfire. The stakes are trivial per sentence and real per book: a conditional that quietly settles something I wanted left open can spoil the last fifty pages.

Please run it on these three sentences from the novel I'm drafting, one of which I am attached to and would rather not change — and tell me straight if the verdict is that it has to change anyway:

1. "If he had known about the letter, he would have taken the morning train."
2. "If she was lying about the fire, then everything he had built on her account was already ash."
3. "If he died in the fire, he died believing she had left him."

For each, say what the sentence commits my fictional world to, what it conveys about the mind we are in, and where the procedure cannot settle the matter. Please write the answer in American English.
