Right, so I'm three drafts into a novel about three siblings who co-host a podcast about the culture war — one of them does moral psychology at a mid-tier university, one writes speeches for a party I'm not naming, and the third is the sort of person who reads the comments — and I've noticed that whenever they argue about whether the psychology of left and right tells you anything about who is actually right, they end up saying some version of the same thing: that the other lot are reasoning backwards from a gut feeling. I want them in genuinely different positions rather than three shades of tu quoque, and I thought the way to get there was to build the logical space first and then drop each of them into a cell. So: give me a taxonomy of the positions on whether the moral psychology of left and right is evidence about who is right, built as a tree where every branch is labelled with the question that does the splitting, phrased so that someone who has never heard the name of any of the positions could still answer it. Build it from the logical space rather than from a list of views — the questions first, then the combinations of answers they permit, then the views actually held placed in the cells they occupy.

Some constraints, because these are the bits my characters actually row about. Let the entry commitment be one all three of them already accept: that people differ in how much weight they give to care, fairness, loyalty, authority and sanctity, and that those differences predict where they land politically. The first real split should be about what kind of disagreement they take themselves to be having — whether left and right differ over how much weight each foundation deserves, over whether a given foundation applies to this case at all, or merely over the facts on the ground (does the policy really harm anyone, is there really a group there to be loyal to) — because if it's mostly the last one then the psychology starts to look definately beside the point. Then there's the case I keep coming back to, the swapped-foundations one: two populations with identical profiles who end up with mirror-image politics because their parties happened to inherit opposite stances on some issue a century ago. If that's possible, then having the profile you have can't be what makes your politics correct, and I want to know which node that thought belongs at. Same for the heritability case: if the profiles turn out to be substantially heritable and also shift with a framing cue, does that debunk both sides at once, neither side, or just whichever side you were already sceptical of. And if the only difference left is that one side's concern reaches further out — more people, more animals, more of the future — is that breadth a reason to think they're right, or just more output from the same machinery.

Give it to me as exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, with no introduction and nothing summing up at the end: first the entry question and the leaf each answer drops you at; then the three branch questions that have to come after it, in the order they have to come, each with the answers it allows, the leaf each answer leads to and one line on that leaf's most awkward commitment; and last, the cells the tree permits but that nobody seems to hold, with a word on whether each is incoherent, merely unattractive or just unclaimed, plus any two leaves your tree can't really separate — flag those rather than quietly merging them. If you can squeeze into that last item one sentence that only one of my three characters would ever say, that'd be perfect, but dont force it.
