I host a small interview series on practical ethics — one argument, one hour, no soundbites — and my next guest is a philosopher who has spent roughly fifteen years defending Benatar's asymmetry argument in print. Please treat her as a composite of the strongest available version of that position rather than as any particular living person: refer to her as "Dr. M.," don't attribute biographical details to her, and reconstruct her replies rather than quoting anyone. The timing is awkward in a productive way: my wife and I have just started IVF and are genuinely undecided about whether to have a first child, so I have no interest in a conversational warm-up or in questions that would work just as well for any guest. I want questions that only make sense to someone who knows how the argument is actually assembled — the four-cell table (presence of pain is bad; presence of pleasure is good; absence of pain is good even when there is nobody there to enjoy it; absence of pleasure is not bad unless there is someone for whom that absence is a deprivation) — and that press exactly where the assembly is thinnest.

You are a professional philosopher working in population ethics and the ethics of procreation who also coaches junior scholars for hostile Q&A. Before drafting anything, brainstorm: spend a paragraph listing the ten or so strongest objections to asymmetry-based antinatalism and the stock reply to each, then select the eight to ten that will actually make her work, and put them in the order you think a good interviewer should ask them in so the pressure escalates. No citations, reading lists, or references to specific papers and years — I want your own reconstruction of the dialectic, since I can't check sources anyway. Here is what I need covered, at minimum: (1) whether each of the four cells can be motivated independently, or whether they are only plausible as a package and whether any one cell is secretly doing all the work; (2) the charge that the table is gerrymandered — if absence of pain is good, why isn't absence of pleasure good, or at least bad-making? — followed up on her likely reply that the relevant terms are being used non-comparatively in one cell and comparatively in the other; (3) the scope problem: if nobody is deprived by not existing, why doesn't the same reasoning drain the point from long-term policy aimed at future generations, and why isn't human extinction not merely permissible but costless; (4) the fixed-number case that IVF puts in front of me — when some embryos will not be brought to term and my wife and I will have a child either way, does the asymmetry obligate us to select the best-off possible child, and is the "would have existed anyway" clause really doing the work, given that which child exists is identity-affecting; (5) risk and uncertainty — is there any probability of a bad outcome below which creating a child becomes permissible, and if the answer is that any nonzero risk forbids it, is that a reductio; (6) whether the reason against creating is decisive or merely pro tanto, given that a created child's life could be good and the parents' lives might be transformed for the better — and if it is merely pro tanto, whether the whole public debate has been about the wrong thing (permissibility rather than the existence of a reason); (7) the implication that people who want to parent owe it to no one in particular to adopt rather than procreate, and whether that is a consequence to embrace or a sign the argument has detached from ordinary moral thought; and (8) how much of the case rests on empirical pessimism about how good human lives actually are — adaptive preferences, optimism bias, and so on — given that I believe my own childhood was, in fact, good.

For each question, give me: the question itself, one line on what it is testing, the reply you expect her to give (in your own words as a reconstruction, explicitly not as a quotation), and the follow-up that takes that reply away from her — the move that makes her second-order resources, such as scope restrictions or distinctions between what the asymmetry is about at bottom and what it merely entails, progressively harder to reach for. Close the sequence with a single question about what could count as evidence against the asymmetry, framed so that "most people would agree with me if they reasoned correctly" is not an available answer.
