I run investments for a small endowed foundation — roughly $140M, four staff, and an investment committee of four lay trustees who sign off on every position in a one-hour meeting once a month. Last night I had a three-hour argument with one of those trustees, an applied mathematician, and I lost most of it. Her claim, as I now understand it, was that our sizing decisions should be Kelly sizing: maximizing expected wealth is a sucker's game because the expectation is dominated by the one path where everything works, whereas maximizing expected log wealth maximizes the growth rate and, over enough repetitions, beats any other rule with probability approaching one. She also said two things I'd rather have checked than assumed: that half Kelly buys you about three-quarters of the growth rate for roughly a quarter of the variance of the log outcome, and that past twice Kelly the growth rate turns negative, so overbetting is worse than having no edge at all. What she could not tell me is what any of this means on a Tuesday, for a two-page memo on an illiquid commitment, in front of a committee that reads expected terminal value and hates drawdowns.

So here is what I need: a reusable sizing procedure, in words, that my analyst — a sharp generalist with no graduate mathematics — can run in about twenty minutes from a standard two-page memo, and that the trustees can audit afterward. Our situation: thirty to forty sizing decisions a year; most opportunities illiquid, overlapping, and correlated with what we already hold; rebalancing rare and slow; and a wind-down clause in the founding document stipulating that if real assets fall below $90M in today's dollars the foundation must spend out and dissolve. That last feature is where the argument ran out. My trustee's instinct was that near the floor we should take more risk to climb back out; mine was the opposite. I want the procedure to settle what quantity the fraction is a fraction of, and to say plainly which of us was wrong.

The procedure has to handle, at minimum: a screening gate for whether an opportunity is even the kind of thing this reasoning applies to — I want the analyst obliged to name the reference class of similar future opportunities and say roughly how many draws are left in it, and I want the procedure to refuse to produce a number when that class has fewer than about ten expected remaining instances, routing the deal to a fixed exploration budget instead; how to define stake, odds, and edge for a messy real commitment; what to do when the edge estimate rests on a thin or flattering track record, since we have been badly overbet by model error before; the fact that thirty independently sized positive-edge deals will over-lever us, so there must be an aggregation step with some shared growth budget and a rule for ranking deals once that budget is used up; and the point that the per-deal multiplier — full, half, quarter — is not something the theory hands you, so it should be fixed once by the committee with stated reasons (parameter uncertainty, illiquidity, inability to rebalance, drawdown tolerance) rather than tuned deal by deal. For each gate, mark whether a failure there is a hard no or a place where the underlying question is one of values and has to go to a human.

Then run it on three cases: (a) a liquid, genuinely repeatable opportunity in listed markets with fifteen years of daily data and a well-understood edge; (b) a one-off illiquid commitment in an asset class we have no track record in and no obvious sibling deals for — my suspicion is that the honest answer is that the procedure will not size it at all beyond the exploration budget, and I want to see that argued rather than papered over; (c) a deal whose standalone number is enormous but that sits almost on top of exposure we already carry, at a moment when we are not far above the wind-down threshold. I expect the committee to dislike at least two of these answers; say which, and why.

Close with a short section on what the procedure cannot do: whether log growth is even the right objective for an institution with a spending rule and a legal death at $90M, what happens if the regime generating the edge changes underneath us, and why no backtest we can run will settle any of this.

Hard length limit: 1,400 words for the whole thing. No more than twelve gates, each stateable in one plain-English sentence a trustee without mathematical training can follow, then the three worked cases at roughly 120 words each, then the closing section. One or two formulas may appear if they earn their place; no derivations.
