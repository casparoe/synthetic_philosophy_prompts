I'm writing the handout for my philosophy of physics seminar on whether a framework like string theory or eternal-inflation cosmology can be confirmed without empirical evidence, and I'd rather have a decision tree than a survey. Start it from the single premise nobody in the room contests — that we have no direct empirical access either to Planck-scale physics or to other universes — and branch only on questions that actually separate the positions: whether confirmation needs to be empirical at all; whether an argument can count as evidence if you cannot say what would have been the case had the theory been false; whether the usual non-empirical arguments (no alternatives, meta-induction from the track record of theories that were likewise uniquely promising, unexpected explanatory interconnections) carry weight separately or only as a package; whether the anthropic explanation of the observed value of the cosmological constant is a prediction or only an accommodation of a number we already knew; whether a framework that assigns no well-defined measure over its own outcomes can be confirmed by anything at all; and whether the multiverse is something the theory gets credit for predicting or a symptom that it predicts too much. Build the logical space first — the questions and which combinations of answers they permit — and only then place the actual positions in it, marking the cells nobody occupies and saying briefly why each is empty: incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Aim for eight to ten leaves, and phrase every node so a student could answer it without knowing which view it leads to.

Two further constraints. First, each leaf should say not only what it claims about confirmation but what it licenses in practice: full belief, continued investment of research effort, or merely a refusal to rule the framework out — my suspicion is that several of these views collapse into one another once you ask what their holder would do. Second, please flag the pairs your tree cannot separate, particularly if Bayesian non-empirical confirmation and plain research optimism turn out to be indistinguishable in what each would do differently given a decisive negative result.
