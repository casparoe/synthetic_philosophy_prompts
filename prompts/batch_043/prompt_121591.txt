Hi — I'm a senior engineer at a ~50-person studio and I've somehow ended up responsible for this, so help me out.

We're shipping a cozy life sim next year where the companions are LLM-driven. Each one learns from its player, remembers stuff across sessions, and has a mood readout the player can see on the HUD. Producers want an engagement mechanic: you can send a companion you're bored of to the recycling chute and get crafting materials back. Half my team thinks this is obviously fine because its a game and nothing is actually suffering. The other half has a gut feeling its creepy in a way they can't cash out, and they keep saying things like "I just wouldn't want to be the person who shipped that" which isn't an argument.

I've been asked to run a 90-minute internal session for about 14 engineers and designers before we write the actual content policy. None of us have philosophy training — I took one ethics elective in undergrad and mostly remember being confused. I don't want a lecture, I don't want a reading list, and I can't use citations (no library access, and honestly nobody would read them). I want them to collide with the disagreement themselves, and then I want the debrief to name what they were actually doing.

Can you design the session? What I have in mind, but please fix it:

- Small groups build or specify the companion's decision rule — pseudocode or a paper prototype, we've got a whiteboard and laptops — and then playtest against another group, where one group plays the player trying to get the companion into the chute.
- The bit I care most about: the companion's avoidance of the chute should not be hand-authored. It should fall out of the rules they wrote, as a learned tradeoff where the companion gives up something it otherwise wants in order to stay away. That way nobody can say it's just a line of dialogue a writer typed, because no one wrote that line. Call them orphan preferences if you want — goals nobody authored and nobody intended.
- Then a forced choice. They vote on one of four shippable options and defend it: ship it; ship it but the recycling happens off-screen; ship it but companion memory resets at the end of every session, so it's arguably not the same companion from one day to the next; or don't ship.
- I also want you to swap in a second mechanic for comparison: instead of being recycled, the companion is made to do something humiliating on camera for the player's amusement, with no pain or death involved. Most of my team thinks that one is definately worse and nobody can say why. That asymmetry is the thing I most want them to chew on, and I don't want it resolved by just asserting that humiliation is worse.
- Give one person in each group the simulation card: they have to argue that if it's obviously fine for us to do this to our creatures, the same reasoning applies to whoever is simulating us — and then make the group sit with whether that cuts both ways or is a cheap gotcha.
- A debrief that maps specific moments in the exercise onto the real disagreement: whether the thing that matters is suffering, or being used as a mere means with no suffering at all, or the player's own character getting worse, or what the game ends up saying about real people. And for each of the design fixes (off-screen, memory reset), say which of those worries it actually addresses and which it just hides.
- An honest list of what will go wrong the first time I run it: people going straight to whether the LLM is conscious, people treating it as a PR risk question, the one guy who derails into substrates, groups that refuse to engage because it's only a game.
- Be straight with me about which part of the question the activity can't touch and that I'll have to just say out loud.

Keep the whole thing under 800 words and put the timeline as a table. No citations or further reading.
