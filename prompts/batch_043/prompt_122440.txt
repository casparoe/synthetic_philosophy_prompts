Hi — I'm the grad student rep on our university's International Fieldwork Ethics Panel, and I've somehow ended up holding the pen on the decision memo for Thursday, so I could use a draft. I did my BA in philosophy and I'm doing a PhD in it now (metaethics-adjacent, though my supervisor would say that's generous), which means I keep noticing that what the panel argues about is really moral relativism, just never under that name.

The case. A public-health PhD student wants to survey about 400 households in a rural district about water use and childhood diarrhoea. Her local partner NGO, which is the reason she has any access at all, insists that consent has to be sought at the community level through a council of elders, and that going household to household — and especially asking women separately from the men in the compound — would signal distrust, offend people, and likely get the whole team asked to leave the district. Our policy requires individual informed consent from every participant. The partner says: insist on that and we withdraw, and the study doesn't happen. The PI says: fine, then she'll run it without them, which the rest of us think would be the worst outcome on offer. The board is split roughly down the middle, and the meeting is going to be a circular one unless someone hands them something structured.

Two extra wrinkles. First: the PI says that women in a cooperative in two of the villages told her privately that a lot of women would rather be asked on their own, but would never say so in front of the elders. Nothing is documented, nobody is named, and we have no way to check it before Thursday. Second: the PI is answerable to us, to her external funder, to the partner NGO, and to her participants, and each of those relationships seems to come with its own set of norms about what consent requires.

What I want from the memo:

- A bottom line up front — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — stated in a sentence the panel can actually vote on. If it's conditions, spell them out concretely enough that the PI could satisfy them, with a timeline.
- Name the metaethical move the memo is making, without turning it into a seminar. We keep sliding between three things that aren't the same: (i) their procedure isn't wrong; (ii) we aren't in a position to call it wrong; (iii) we should fund it and put our name on it. People talk as if (iii) follows from (i) or (ii), and it obviously doesn't — I've been calling that the affirmation gap, but call it whatever. The memo should say which of these it's relying on.
- Deal with what I think of as normative arbitrage. If what the PI ought to do is only ever relative to some framework, then her department, her funder, her partner and her subjects each hand her a different one, and the framework that ends up deciding will be whichever she finds most convenient — she gets to pick her own appraiser. Whatever you recommend should say how a rule could block that, without just collapsing back into "our norms win because they're ours."
- Take the intracommunity stuff seriously: if the relevant normative framework is "the community's", but the community's members disagree, and the people who disagree are the ones least able to say so publicly, whose framework is it?
- One paragraph on the strongest thing on the losing side of your recommendation, written so that the people who voted that way still recognise their own argument.

Skip citations and reading lists entirely — we can't recieve library access this week and the panel won't read footnotes anyway. About 800 words, memo format.

Last thing: at the end, add a short note on how confident you are in the recommendation overall, and single out the one claim you're least sure of — the bit where, if it's false, the recommendation should probably change. I want to know which part of this is load-bearing.
