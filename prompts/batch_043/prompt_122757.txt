I run a tiny podcast out of my spare room — me, a mate, and a cheap mic — where I get one guest to explain one argument to someone with no training in their field (me; I drive a delivery van during the week). Next week's guest is a philosopher who thinks the lesson of Lewis's triviality results is that indicative conditionals don't have truth conditions. The way I've got it in my head: if you insist that how confident you are in "if A then B" just is how confident you are in B given A, and you also treat "if A then B" as a claim with a probability of its own, the maths drives you to the result that A and B are probabilistically independent for any A and B you like, which is absurd — so what goes is the claim, not the equation.

I know the basic moves already, so don't give me anything like "what got you into this topic". Draft me six questions, each with the follow-up you'd expect given the answer they're most likely to give:

- which premise they actually drop — the equation, the idea that the conditional is a proposition with a probability, or the ordinary rules for probability — and why that beats the option of keeping truth conditions and saying the equation only holds in a special range of cases (when the antecedent is somehow independent of the consequent, or whatever the usual carve-out is);
- embedded conditionals: what am I even doing when I say "if A, then if B then C", or shove a conditional into the if-part of another one, if the inner sentence doesn't state anything? I expect something like "you suppose A, then suppose B inside that" — push on what happens to the inference that lets you move the antecedent around (the going back and forth between "if A and B then C" and "if A then if B then C"): does it come out valid, invalid, or valid for a reason nobody signed up for?
- whether the argument proves too much: run the same style of reasoning on "it will probably rain" or on disjunction and it seems to show those have no truth conditions either. If they swallow that, ask what's left of the thought that our sentences say how things are; if they choke on it, ask what's supposed to be different;
- conditional bets: I pay you a pound for a bet on B that gets called off and refunded if A doesn't happen, so my fair price is my confidence in B given A. Doesn't that look like the equation getting its grip from something proposition-shaped? I expect "the bet doesn't need a proposition to be settled against", so press on whether the betting story really delivers the equation or only something close to it;
- what would count against them: suppose it turned out that across a big class of ordinary cases people's confidence in "if A then B" tracks their confidence in A-and-B together rather than their confidence in B given A. Is that evidence about what the sentence means, or just about how people fumble it?
- and one to close on what the view can't say: if conditionals don't state anything, what's going on when one person says "if A then B" and another says "if A then not B" — what's the disagreement about, and is it the same kind of thing as a disagreement about whether it will rain?

Keep them in plain English where you can, since I'll be reading them out loud and stumbeling over them.
