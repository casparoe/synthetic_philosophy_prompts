I work as a software engineer and in my spare time I have been building a small case-based training tool for a hospice charity: a scenario bank where each case describes a patient, asks whether anything is bad for that patient and whether we ought to treat it, and stores the verdict of the charity's clinical ethics adviser, Dr. Nadia Rahimi, so that trainees can be shown a run of her earlier verdicts and asked to predict the next one. I have seven of her verdicts logged that seem to bear on the case I am about to add, and before I write the answer key I would like a second opinion, because I genuinely cannot tell whether my new case is a new case or just a relabelling of one she has already decided. Her verdicts so far, in compressed form: post-operative abdominal pain in a frightened patient who asks for morphine — bad for her, treat it; pain asymbolia after a right-hemisphere stroke, where the patient localises and intensity-rates the pain correctly, calls it pain, and says it hurts, but shows no distress, never seeks relief, and does not mind it when pressed — still bad for him, and still treat it; a masochist in intense pain he actively wants, enjoys, and seeks out again — nothing bad for him in that episode, she said, and she would not take it away, though I do not know whether she meant a small residual badness that is outweighed or no badness at all; phantom limb pain with no tissue damage, in a patient who knows there is no tissue damage — every bit as bad as ordinary pain, treat it; a woman in labour who refuses an epidural for reasons she endorses and who a year later is glad she felt it — the pain was bad for her at the time, and not regretting it does not retrospectively cancel that; a man with chronic back pain at six out of ten for years who has adapted to it, says it is part of who he is, and does not want it gone — still bad for him, but its badness does not by itself license us to override his refusal, so treat only if he asks; and a woman with advanced dementia who cannot speak but who grimaces, withdraws, and shows autonomic arousal during wound care — yes, bad for her, treat it, and she added that we do not need her to be able to tell us.

The case I have drafted, which I am calling lucid analgesia and which is entirely hypothetical rather than something I think anyone has actually done, is this: a targeted neuromodulation that leaves the whole sensory-discriminative profile of a patient's pain untouched — same site, same deep-aching quality, same eight-out-of-ten intensity rating, unchanged performance on sensory discrimination tasks, and she still classifies what she feels as pain — but abolishes the hurtfulness, so that she reports the intensity as unchanged while insisting that it no longer feels bad, like a very bright light that has stopped glaring. She shows no distress, makes no attempt to escape, declines analgesics, asks that the intervention be left on, and has now experienced the pain both with and without it and prefers it on. Her withdrawal reflexes and autonomic responses, however, are exactly as they were, and to keep the case clean I have stipulated that the underlying condition is unaffected either way, so analgesia would do her no medical good beyond changing how things feel for her.

What I want is a prediction and a diagnosis of the rule behind it. What will she say about whether anything is bad for this patient, and about whether we have any reason to treat the pain or switch the intervention off? Which rule is she implicitly running, and which other rules fit all seven of her earlier verdicts just as well while disagreeing here — I assume her asymbolia verdict is doing more than one kind of work, since it is not obvious to me whether that case involves pain without hurtfulness or merely pain without suffering, and I suspect the two readings come apart exactly on my new one. Give me a ranked best guess with the reasoning, tell me honestly whether cases two and eleven are the same case twice or a real difference, tell me how much weight her verdict on the non-verbal dementia patient suggests she would put on bare behaviour against a patient's own report, and finally — since I can add a twelfth case — describe the further case that would separate the surviving rules most cleanly. I would rather ship an answer key that is explicit about which principle it assumes than one that is confident and quietly wrong.
