I'm TAing an intro formal epistemology course this term and I built what I thought was a clean argument for why Judy Benjamin shouldn't change how confident she is that she's in friendly territory. Now I think it proves way too much and I cant find where it breaks.

Setup, so we don't argue about numbers. Judy's plane goes down somewhere over an area split into Blue (friendly) and Red (enemy), and each half is split into an HQ company area and a Second Company area. Her prior over the four cells is

Blue & HQ: 4/9
Blue & 2nd Co: 2/9
Red & HQ: 1/9
Red & 2nd Co: 2/9

so she starts at 2/3 on Blue, 5/9 on HQ, and 1/2 on Red given 2nd Co. She radios the scout, there's static, and all she makes out is: if you are in Red territory, you are in the Second Company area. Assume she learns nothing else — nothing about why he phrased it that way, nothing about what he would have said if he'd known more, no assumption that he'd have named the exact cell if he could. Just the conditional.

My argument:

(1) What Judy acquires is a conditional C whose antecedent A is that she's in Red territory.
(2) In every situation where A is false, C is trivially satisfied, so it puts no constraint at all on the Blue cells.
(3) So the only cell C eliminates is the A-and-not-B one: Red & HQ, worth 1/9 of her prior.
(4) A message that constrains only what is the case if A cannot change how likely A is. All it can do is move probability around inside the A region, so the freed 1/9 gets redistributed among the surviving Red cells.
(5) Same on the other side: since the content never touches the Blue cells, the ratio between them stays 2:1.
(6) So her posterior is 4/9, 2/9, 0, 1/3: still 2/3 on Blue, still 1/3 on Red, now certain of 2nd Co given Red. In particular her confidence that she's in friendly territory doesn't move, which is the bit I wanted — it would be wierd for a message that only describes the bad case to cheer her up.

Here's what's bugging me. Two things.

First: C is logically equivalent to its contrapositive, if I am in an HQ area then I am in Blue territory. Run (1) through (6) on that version and the antecedent is now HQ, so the message can only shift probability around inside the HQ region, the 1/9 lands on Blue & HQ, and Judy ends up at 5/9, 2/9, 0, 2/9 — that's 7/9 on Blue. Same message, same content, two different posteriors depending on how you write the conditional. My argument looks like it's reacting to the phrasing rather than the content, and I don't think it's allowed to do that.

Second, the general form looks worse. Take any ordinary statement S and rewrite it as: if not-S, then something impossible. Its antecedent is not-S, so by the reasoning in (4) learning it cannot change P(not-S) — but it's equivalent to S, so of course it drives P(not-S) to zero. So either the principle in (4) is flatly inconsistent or it was never really about conditionals, and I can't tell which.

The distribution I keep coming back to is 5/9, 1/9, 0, 1/3: it's the only one that zeroes Red & HQ while keeping P(Blue) at 2/3 and P(HQ) at 5/9, so in a loose sense it changes least. But I can't derive it from anything, and it has a side effect I don't like — it pushes her from 1/2 to 3/4 on Red given 2nd Co, so being in Second Company territory becomes evidence that she's behind enemy lines. Plain conditionalization instead gives 1/2, 1/4, 0, 1/4, which leaves that conditional alone but makes her 3/4 on Blue.

So: which step do I reject? I'd most like to keep (2) and (3), and I'd also like to keep the assumption that the scout's informative behaviour carries no extra evidence, since the whole point of the case is that the conditional is all she gets. If (4) has to go, tell me what replaces it and whether the replacement lands near 5/9, 1/9, 0, 1/3 or near 1/2, 1/4, 0, 1/4; and whatever you recommend, tell me how to answer the student who says no message about what holds if she's in enemy territory should make her more confident she isn't. Name the step, say why it looked fine, and price the alternatives you're not taking.
