I work in applied Bayesian statistics, mostly on prior specification and prior sensitivity, and I spent last evening arguing with a philosopher friend about whether the no-free-lunch theorems just are the problem of induction, stated properly. He said yes, I said the resemblance is superficial, and we got nowhere because neither of us could say precisely where the analogy breaks. I would like that done properly, with the failures reported, not another round of assertion.

Take Hume's problem of induction: the argument that inference from observed to unobserved cannot be justified without a premise about the uniformity of nature, and that this premise can be established neither a priori nor a posteriori without circularity. Add the main replies I remember from the reading: that the uniformity assumption is a brute but unavoidable presupposition of any empirical reasoning; that induction can be vindicated pragmatically, as the policy whose success any rival policy must itself presuppose in order to be proposed; and that the warrant for inductive inference might come from local empirical facts about the kind of system under investigation rather than from any global principle. Restate that whole exchange in the vocabulary I actually use: hypothesis classes and their closure, priors over hypotheses, hyperpriors over model classes, expected off-training-set error, the NFL result that all algorithms have identical expected error when averaged uniformly over target functions, distribution-free versus distribution-dependent guarantees, and the fact that in real problems the true generator is usually not inside any class I have written down.

Then the residue report, which is the part I care about. First, what translates cleanly: I suspect the claim that you cannot get an out-of-sample guarantee without assuming something about the world is exactly, and only, the NFL result, and I want you to confirm that or say why it is more than that. Second, where the translation has to cheat. My worry is that NFL theorems are a priori results about a formal average over a closed, fully specified function space, whereas Hume's question is whether anything could justify the assumption one adopts, and my vocabulary has no way of asking that because priors are inputs to the analysis rather than claims within it. Tell me plainly if the translation is smuggling in my framework's treatment of priors as exogenous, and then say whether that is a defect of my framework or evidence that Hume's demand is the incoherent one. Third, what has no counterpart at all. The move of my friend's that I could not answer: he said the NFL framing presupposes a fixed hypothesis space, and that the real difficulty is one level up, since no body of evidence could tell you that you are working with the wrong space entirely, because a space you have not conceived of is not something you could have assigned a prior to. I cannot tell whether that merely opens the regress one level or names something my vocabulary genuinely cannot express. If it is a genuine gap, say whether any machinery on the ML side comes close to expressing it, and if not, sketch roughly what a formal version would have to look like.

Be explicit about which parts of your translation are imports from the statistical side rather than consequences of the philosophical claims. Close by stating how confident you are in the overall verdict, and single out the one claim in your answer you would be least surprised to have wrong.
