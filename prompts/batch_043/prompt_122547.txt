I'm in the fourth year of a philosophy PhD (my dissertation is on the ethics of intergenerational risk), and I've been invited to give a twenty-five-minute talk with fifteen minutes of questions at a day-long technical consultation that a finance ministry is holding on the discount-rate schedule in its regulatory cost-benefit guidance. My supervisor thinks the argument I want to give is the strongest thing in the chapter and also the most likely to be eaten alive in that particular room, so I'd like to prepare for the question period rather than polish the slides further. The room is about twenty people: eight or nine career civil servants from the ministry's cost-benefit unit who actually write the guidance and defend it to audit bodies and to other departments (they care whether a rule is auditable, consistent across departments, and cheap to administer; they will not be moved by ethical argument, and someone will ask what number to put in the spreadsheet); three environmental economists who run integrated assessment models and know the long-run climate numbers cold; one macroeconomist on the ministry's advisory council, a convinced descriptivist who holds that the social rate should track the observed pre-tax return on capital; and one political philosopher who is broadly sympathetic to me but thinks the whole aggregative framework is built on sand, because who exists in future centuries depends on what we do now. Nobody there works on the normative theory of discounting as such, which is why I need objections translated into their vocabularies rather than mine.

Here is the argument sketch they'll be aiming at. I want to stop treating the social discount rate as a single number to be chosen and instead decompose it into three things that should be estimated separately: an opportunity-cost term, the real return a unit of current consumption could earn if invested rather than spent, which I concede is high and which I grant applies in full to benefits that compete with private investment; a survival hazard, covering the probability that a benefit never arrives because the policy is reversed, the institution maintaining it collapses, the beneficiaries turn out to be different people living different lives, or the harm is averted later by adaptation instead; and a pure time-preference term, a discount applied to a future good merely for being later, which I argue should be set at zero. My two substantive claims are, first, that nearly every intuition offered in favour of a positive pure rate — they may not be there, the future is uncertain, they'll be richer than us, we won't be around to enjoy it — is really an intuition about opportunity cost or about hazard, so once those are modelled explicitly there is nothing left for a pure rate to do; and second, that the hazard is front-loaded, since reversal risk for a long-horizon policy is concentrated in the first two or three decades, after which statutory entrenchment, sunk infrastructure, and constituencies with a stake in continuation make abandonment progressively less likely. If that is right, the average annual hazard over a horizon falls as the horizon lengthens, so the effective rate declines: close to the market rate for benefits arriving within twenty years, and very low for benefits three centuries out. I plan to concede at the outset that hazard schedules are hard to estimate from anything, that the decomposition is administratively costly, and that a near-zero long-run rate will make some very-long-horizon projects look disconcertingly cheap to fund.

What I want from you is the prep sheet itself, not a defence of the view. Give me eight to ten objections in roughly the order they will come, each attached to the kind of person in the room who would raise it and phrased the way that person would phrase it — the auditors' questions in the language of defensibility, consistency, and administrative burden; the modellers' questions about whether the thing is implementable in a model at all and whether it changes the sign of any policy conclusion they care about; the descriptivist's in the language of revealed preference and capital allocation, since he will say that a near-zero long rate instructs the state to starve present investment for centuries of hypothetical benefits and will put it exactly that way; and the philosopher's about whether any of this survives the dependence of future identities on present policy. For each objection give a reply I could actually deliver in about two minutes while standing up, and a candid note on where the reply is soft, begs a question, or should just be conceded. Rank by how much damage each would do rather than by how interesting it is — I would rather be ready for the boring objection that sinks me than the dazzling one I can bat away, and since I cannot lean on citations in that room, the replies have to work as arguments. Finally, give me the one question I am hoping nobody asks. My own guess is that it concerns whether the political reversal I am calling a hazard is not simply the polity's revealed time preference wearing a technical costume, in which case my distinction between hazard and pure time preference is cosmetic and the whole decomposition collapses back into the ordinary disagreement — tell me how bad that objection really is and what, if anything, I can say to it.
