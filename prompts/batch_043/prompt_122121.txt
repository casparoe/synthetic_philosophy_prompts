Hey — you're a professional philosopher who specializes in moral responsibility, and you've spent way too many hours arguing about Nagel on the internet. Take your time with this one: brainstorm three or four candidate scenarios before you commit, then pick the best and tell me why the others lost.

I'm writing up a peice on the control principle — the thing people gesture at when they say you can't be more blameworthy for how things turned out than for what was up to you. Call it CP. Everyone's favorite deflation of the drunk-driver case is "the dead kid is evidence about how bad your driving was," and I want a case that doesn't die to that. So: build me one.

Spec. A case of pure resultant moral luck: two agents whose choice, mental states, evidence, character and the risk they impose are the same; the difference has to sit entirely downstream of everything the agent does — she doesn't do anything different, the world does. And in the case, the unlucky agent really is more blameworthy. Not just more regrettable, not just a more useful target of blame, not just someone we'd predict people will blame harder. More blameworthy. One paragraph, ordinary setting (a hospital, a building site, a school run — no trolleys, no demons, no perfect predictors), and the verdicts have to fall out of the theories rather than be written into the story.

The hard part is that it has to survive all three of the following, and I want you to show explicitly that it does for each one:

(i) Outcome-as-evidence. The bad outcome can't be new information about the quality of the agent's judgment or her character. Stipulate the risk as precisely quantified and known to the agent beforehand, and ideally already known to whoever's assessing her.

(ii) Forward-looking stuff. Even if blameworthiness is equal, you might have more reason to blame the unlucky one — deterrence, reassurance, incentive effects. Hold that fixed: nobody else ever finds out, or the downstream effects are identical.

(iii) The "that's not blame, that's something else" move — Williams's agent-regret, or the shift from blameworthiness to what you owe the person you actually wronged. So give me a marker that says the extra thing is blame-shaped: does she take herself to deserve something? Is third-party resentment fitting? Does she owe an apology to someone the outcome didn't touch?

Then walk through what CP says and what a view that genuinely accepts resultant luck says (the "you're on the hook for how it actually goes" view, whatever you want to call it), and tell me wich single feature of the case is doing the work, what happens if you delete that feature, and how a CP defender would attack the case.

If you think the spec can't be met — that once you subtract (i) through (iii) there's no blameworthiness left, only residue — say that instead. But then I want at least two serious candidate cases and a diagnosis of exactly where each one collapses.

Last thing: tell me how confident you are that the case does what I asked, and what would change your mind — which argument or consideration would convince you the difference got deflated after all, or that your diagnosis in (iii) was the wrong one.
