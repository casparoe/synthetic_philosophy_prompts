I write a column on science and ideas for a Sunday paper, and I've been sitting on a line a hedge fund guy dropped at dinner after a conference panel on the epistemology of markets. We'd been arguing about whether betting-market odds should count as better evidence about an outcome than a panel of specialists — someone brought up a small-cap biotech that doubled on a trial readout, someone else brought up election betting — and he ended it with: the market is always right. When I pushed back he said its basically what the no-trade theorem says: Milgrom and Stokey's 1982 result, rational traders, common knowledge, nobody trades because any offer already leaks the offerer's information. So the price already contains everything, so the market is right.

That feels like three or four different claims stapled together, and I want to pull them apart for the column. Specifically:

- Give me a menu of the precise claims that could be hiding in the market is always right, ordered from the near-trivial to the genuinely wild. Skip the dictionary version (prices are whatever trades happen at) — start past that. I'd guess the menu includes something about prices incorporating public information, something about aggregating dispersed private knowledge that nobody has in one head, something about the price being the best available estimator of the truth, something about it being impossible to systematically profit, and some stronger version where the price is the only thing there is to be right about. Add the ones I'm missing.
- For each: platitude, contentious but defensible, or just false? And the part I actually need: which ones do the work he wanted? He was using it to (a) say my friend who thinks the biotech is overpriced is kidding herself, and (b) say we should defer to the odds over the specialists. Those are different jobs and I suspect different readings are needed for each. Tell me which readings earn which conclusions and which ones dont get him there at all.
- Does the no-trade theorem support any of the readings, or is he just borrowing the prestige of a proof? My sense is its closer to a claim about when trade fails to happen than a claim about prices being right, and that if anything it cuts against him: the result says no trade under those conditions, but there is obviously a colossal amount of actual trading, so whatever explains the volume — noise traders, differing priors, people trading for reasons that have nothing to do with information — is also what decides whether prices mean anything. Am I close? Say where I'm going wrong.
- One more thing I want to use. I've started calling it epistemic arbitrage, profiting off the gap between what a price implies about the world and what the world is, and I want to know if that's coherent or whether it collapses into plain arbitrage the moment you cash it out. Because on the strong reading of the slogan there is no such gap and my phrase is empty, and on the weak readings I don't know what right is doing in the sentence at all.

Don't bother with citations or papers to read — I want the argument, and I can't check sources from a hotel bar anyway. And please don't spend six paragraphs telling me it depends what I mean by right; give me a verdict on which readings survive, and your best guess at which one he definately meant (he's a macro guy, if that helps).
