Hi there. I'm a novelist — four books, you've definately never heard of them — and for about a year now I've been keeping a notebook on my own bullshit, mostly how often I know what's going on in my own head versus how often I'm just narrating. I've ended up with a pile of verdicts and no principle, and it's starting to bug me while I work.

Here's what I think, case by case.

1. A reader writes to say my character Lena slaps her sister because she's ashamed of being pitied, not because she's jealous. I'd have said jealous, no hesitation. Reading his email I get this hot click of yes — but I also know I'd have got the same click if he'd said the opposite. Verdict: before the email, my report on my own character's motive wasn't knowledge in any useful sense. After the email I'm not sure it is either.

2. A friend says I'd stop writing if the money stopped. I say no, instantly, total certainty, and I can't produce a single reason. Verdict: trustworthy. There's no evidence for it and I still trust it.

3. I told an interviewer the ending of book two came from a dream about my grandmother. Notebook says: no dream. Three drafts of that ending, all after a phone call with my agent. Verdict: worthless. I confabulated a source, and I believe I'd have passed a lie detector on it.

4. Whether a sentence feels wrong as I'm writing it. I trust this completely — it's the only thing I actually revise on — and I can't say what the wrongness consists in. Verdict: real and authoritative.

5. The pair I keep going in circles on. (a) I say I don't care about reviews. My chest goes tight when a certain critic's name appears in my inbox. I still want to say my I don't care is true — the tight chest is plumbing, not attitude. (b) My character Marta insists she is not in love with the married man, and keeps inventing errands that take her past his house. As her author I say with total certainty that her denial is false. These two are the same shape and I want opposite verdicts on them. Probably I'm special-pleading for myself.

6. Whether I was aware of the fridge humming while I wrote chapter nine. I can't tell now and I don't think I could have told then either, even mid-sentence. Verdict: not a question introspection gets to answer at all.

7. At nineteen I said, in letters I still have, that I was happy. Now I say I was miserable and didn't know it. Verdict: present me beats past me, even though past me was the one standing there.

8. My own term for it, the green-room effect: once I've read a passage aloud to myself a few times it starts to feel inevitable, like it couldn't be any other way, and after that I can't tell whether it's any good. So I've started distrusting my judgment on exactly the passages I've rehearsed most. Verdict: the act of checking wrecks the very signal I rely on in case 4.

9. A grad student sent me a Lacanian reading of my second book and I promptly remembered that I'd intended the wallpaper in chapter four as womblike. Verdict: contaminated. I think the memory was manufactured by reading his essay and I don't think I can recover what I actually intended.

10. Whether a line of mine was meant ironically. I say yes, ironically. Everyone reads it straight. My editor says cut it. I mostly back myself here, but only because irony feels like case 4 — a feel — rather than case 3, a story about where it came from.

So: is there one principle that sorts these, or do I need two? My own guess is something like — I'm authoritative about what it's like for me right now and about whether a thing feels finished, and I'm just another interpreter, with bad data and a theory, when it comes to causes, motives and my own past. But that guess doesn't obviously handle 5(b), where I'm not the subject at all and yet claim certainty, or 8, where the thing I'm authoritative about gets destroyed by the checking.

What I'd like: state the principle precisely enough that I could apply it cold to a case I bring you next month. Then run it on the two I don't have verdicts on yet — (i) there's a paragraph in the new manuscript I have no memory of writing, none at all, and my daughter does sometimes mess with the file: do I know anything here, and what? (ii) my narrator: do I know why I switched him from first person to third, or is that just case 3 with better tailoring? And please tell me straight which of my ten verdicts your account can't save and has to overturn, and why that one and not another. Don't be polite about it, I'd rather have it clean than have it flattering.
