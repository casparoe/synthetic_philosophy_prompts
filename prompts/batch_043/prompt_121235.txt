Hi — I'm a self taught programmer, no philosophy or any academic background. I got into this reading blog posts about AI safety a while back and I've been chewing on one corner of it on my own, and I'd like someone to tell me where I'm kidding myself.

The bit I care about: you give an agent a narrow goal (fetch the coffee) and it wrecks other stuff on the way (knocks over the vase), because nothing in the goal says the vase matters. The standard thing people say is that the fix requires a full specification of human value, which nobody has. I think that's giving up too early. My position: you can get "avoid side effects" out of an agent without specifying what's valuable, by penalizing it for *changing things*, where change is measured structurally rather than in welfare or preference terms.

The version I actually hold, so you're attacking this and not a cartoon of it:

- Compare the world after the agent acts not against "the agent did nothing" but against a counterfactual where it did the minimum that still counts as doing the task. Otherwise you penalize it for doing the job at all.
- Score the difference by how much the agent's own future reachable set of world-states changes — roughly, how many things it can still bring about and which ones — not by whether anything anyone cares about got worse.
- Give it an impact allowance that scales with how big the ask was: "make coffee" gets a small budget, "cure cancer" gets a big one, and it spends budget on impact rather than facing a flat cap.

What would count against this? Please skip "it's computationally intractable" and "you'd need to solve embedded agency first" — I want the stuff that actually breaks it.

1. My biggest worry: an agent penalized for change has a reason to prevent *other* changes, including the human's. Freeze the world, lock in the status quo, keep everyone away from the table so the vase can't fall. Is that avoidable by how the measure is set up, or is it baked into the whole aproach? If it's avoidable, what exactly has to be true for it to be avoidable?
2. The "you've hidden a value function inside the distance metric" objection. Any way of measuring how much the world changed has to treat some changes as signal and others as noise, and that looks like a value judgement. Is there a version of this that says more than "every objective is a utility function if you squint"? If so, what does it say, and does it kill my view or just my choice of metric?
3. Baselines. An inaction baseline preserves whatever would have happened anyway, including disasters the agent could have prevented. My minimal-task baseline needs to know what "minimal" means. How much value am I smuggling in there, and is there a non-arbitrary way to pick a baseline at all?
4. Related: should the penalty be on how much the world changes, or on how much the agent's own power and options grow? I genuinely can't tell whether that's a different question or the same one in disguise, and the answer seems to matter for whether any of this works.
5. My home-made worry: every action has enormous downstream effects, so the score is dominated by butterfly-effect noise unless I coarse-grain, and coarse-graining is exactly where the value leaks back in. Is there a formal version of this — some kind of no-free-lunch result about impact measures — or am I inventing a bogeyman?

Also: which of these would people who actually work on this concede counts against them, and which would they wave off as a strawman? Rank whatever defeaters you find from fatal down to merely awkward. And be honest with me about whether my position has quietly become "you need a little bit of value, just much less than the pessimists claim" — and if it has, whether that's still a real position that risks something, or whether it's been qualified until nothing could refute it. Honestly, the thing that would move me most is one small concrete example, checkable by hand, where my measure gives a clearly wrong answer.
