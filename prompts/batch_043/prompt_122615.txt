So I'm finally filling out my own advance directive paperwork — I'm an infrastructure engineer, no philosophy background beyond some late-night podcast listening — and I got nerdsniped into trying to write down what I actually think about this stuff. Partly it's my mom: she's in the later stages of Alzheimer's and last month we had to decide whether to treat an infection that the form she signed years ago said not to treat. We treated. I've been going back and forth on whether that was consistent with the rest of what I believe, and I'd like somebody to audit the whole set rather than answer one question about it.

Here's roughly where I've landed. (1) A competent person's written instructions should govern once they can't decide for themselves, even when the family is begging us to ignore them and even when following them makes the person distressed or uncomfortable right now. (2) The reason isn't some spooky authority of a "past self" over a different later person — the person in the bed just is literally the same individual who signed the form, so this is ordinary self-determination stretched over time. (3) Ulysses-type setups are fine: a friend of mine with bipolar disorder competently asked, in advance, to be held to his medication plan during a manic episode, including being overridden when he says no at the time, and I think we should override him and I'm comfortable with that. (4) But a competent-now refusal always beats the paperwork: if she's lucid and says stop, we stop, whatever the form says. (5) In advanced dementia, though, I want the directive to stop governing altogether — my mom has no grip anymore on the projects the form was written to protect, forcing that old regime on her serves nobody, and I'd treat the infection; though I also don't think her current preferences are doing much work in the argument either, she likes the game shows now and I don't think that's a reason for much of anything. (6) What actually gives a directive authority is that it expressed values she'd still endorse on reflection, not merely that she was competent when she signed — a form filled out during a bad few months, or mostly to get a daughter off her back, shouldn't bind. (7) And I've build my own paperwork with an expiry mechanic, the way we do leases and TTLs at work: my instructions go stale unless I reaffirm them every couple of years, and if I ever stop reaffirming — for any reason, including that I've become incapacitated — the document lapses and authority falls back to a named human rather than the paper going on speaking for me.

What I want from you: go through these and tell me which ones are genuinely in tension with which, and rank those tensions by severity, worst first — none of this "everything is in tension with everything" stuff. For each real tension, say what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me, and be specific about which commitment can't survive. I suspect a couple of pairs only look like they clash; if so, say why the clash dissolves instead of flagging it. Then tell me which single revision to the set would clean up the most, and what I'd be stuck with if I made it. I'm not attached to any of these, so don't try to figure out which one I secretly want to drop — im asking you to find the weak joint, not to guess my answer. And please don't send me to books or papers or tell me who argued for what; I don't have access to any of that and I want the reasoning, not a reading list.

One format thing: write it as continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — basically how you'd write it in an email to a friend who asked.
