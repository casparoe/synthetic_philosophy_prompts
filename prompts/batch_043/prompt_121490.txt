I have been reading the same blogs and forums for something like fifteen years, and there is a move in those circles that I have never seen audited properly. When someone says that a language model wants something, believes something, or is being deceptive, the standard reply is that a base model is not an agent at all — it is a *simulator*. What you get on any given forward pass is a draw from a distribution over text-generating processes, and the thing that answers you in the chat window is a character being played, not a subject with a point of view. The picture that usually comes with this is the improv actor: give the troupe a suggestion and it instantiates whoever the scene calls for; the actor does not thereby acquire the character's goals, and when the scene ends the character is gone. A closely related version holds that the assistant persona installed by post-training is a mask bolted onto something alien, so fluent first-person talk tells you nothing about what is behind it.

In my corner of the internet the analogy is quietly doing at least three jobs. It is used to license a linguistic rule: treat *want* and *believe* attributions to models as category errors, or at best as shorthand about the character rather than the system. It is used to redirect alignment work toward the character rather than the actor — the claim being that what we shape is which persona gets summoned, not the goals of a persisting subject. And it is used to defuse reports of situational awareness and strategic behavior, on the grounds that those are performances by a character who has read a lot of science fiction rather than evidence of anything with a stake in the future.

I want the analogy audited rather than asserted. Specifically:

1. Give me an explicit correspondence table. What plays the actor, the suggestion, the character, the scene, the end of the scene, the troupe's repertoire, and the actor's own persisting skills and dispositions? On the model side, say clearly which unit the mapping targets: the trained weights, a single forward pass within one context, or the whole scaffolded system including system prompt, memory, tools and fine-tuning. I suspect people slide between these three without noticing.

2. Separate the disanalogies that matter for those three jobs from the ones that are decorative.

3. Say where the analogy imports into the target something the target lacks, or omits something the target has. The one I keep circling: an improv troupe is itself a persisting, skilled, unified subject one level up, with habits and tendencies that show up in every character it plays. If that is right, the framing does not eliminate the subject, it relocates it — and a relocated subject may be exactly the sort of thing that can come to have goals.

Then the part I actually care about. Hold the weights fixed and consider three deployments:

(a) A fresh context, a short system prompt asking for a helpful assistant, no memory, no tools. I take it the actor framing is at its strongest here.

(b) A long, internally consistent system prompt reinforced by post-training, run across very many episodes, with a scratchpad the model writes and reads, where the behavior is stable in a way that no single episode's prompt explains. Now add what I think of as the costume-change test: swap the system prompt for one describing a character with explicitly contrary aims, and ask whether the stable behavior follows the costume or survives it.

(c) The model plus a persistent memory store it can edit, a tool that lets it send email, and a training signal that depends on what happens in the world after the episode ends — so there is feedback across contexts that are separated in wall-clock time, roughly the way a sleeping person's days are.

Is there a principled place to draw the line where the actor framing stops being the right one, and does it fall where my circles implicitly put it? Does a subject need to persist *within* a context, or is persistence across contexts enough? Is the right unit of agency the weights, the episode, or the system? And if it is the system, does the analogy's first job collapse, given that we are perfectly willing to attribute goals to firms and colonies that contain no single persisting conscious subject?

Two replies I expect, and want handled head-on. First: a simulator of an agent is not thereby an agent, any more than a simulation of a hurricane is wet. Say what is right about that, and then say whether the gap between simulating a hurricane and simulating a goal-directed process does any work. Second: whatever stabilizes behavior in (b) and (c) is just the same character being re-summoned more reliably. Tell me what observation would tell that hypothesis apart from the alternative.

Open with your verdict in a single sentence: how far the analogy holds, and whether it can carry all three jobs it is being used for. Then give the table, then the reasoning. No preamble, and do not restate my question back to me.
