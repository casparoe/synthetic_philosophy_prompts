I am putting together a lecture for an undergraduate course on genes, environment, and society, and I would like to use Andrew Niccol's *Gattaca* (1997) to teach something more exact than the slogan that genetic determinism is false. The concept I want to teach is heritability, and I want the film read through the concept rather than used as a decorative illustration of it.

The distinction, as I understand it and would want corrected if I have it wrong: heritability is a population statistic — the proportion of the phenotypic variance observed in a defined population, in a defined range of environments, that is statistically attributable to genetic variance in that population. It is not a measure of how genetic a trait is in an individual; it is not a measure of how fixed, how unresponsive to intervention, or how unavoidable a trait is; and it carries no implication about the causes of differences between populations. A trait can be highly heritable and still be transformed by a change in environment. My sense is that almost every misuse of heritability in public argument is one of these three slides, and I want a reading of the film that says precisely which slide its society commits.

The reading I am tempted by, and suspicious of, goes like this: Gattaca's world treats a genotype as an individual destiny — Vincent Freeman is handed a statistical life expectancy at birth and then lives inside it — and the film refutes that with counterexamples. But I do not think the film is saying that genes do not matter. The engineered capacities on display are real; the twelve-fingered pianist plays a piece no ordinary hand can play. So the film is not a denial of genetic causation, and if I use it as one I will have miseducated the class. What exactly is the film denying, then: that population-level probabilities apply to individuals, that high heritability implies immutability, or something the concept of heritability does not itself cover — for instance the moral claim that a person should not be reduced to a credential?

Two thoughts of my own that I would like tested against the film rather than confirmed. First: if genetic enhancement were universal and standardised, the genetic variance for the enhanced traits would approach zero, and so the heritability of those traits would become undefined or near zero — at the very moment when the traits had become as genetic as it is possible for a trait to be. Heritability would collapse while genetic control was total. Is that a fair point, and does anything in the film register it, or would pressing it be importing a technicality the film cannot carry? Second: in a society where opportunity tracks genotype, genes and environment are correlated, so the heritability of success in Gattaca's world would be measured as higher than it would be in a society where opportunity were distributed independently of genotype. The statistics would flatter the hierarchy that produced them. Does the film give a critic anything to work with here — Vincent's menial employment, Irene Cassini's disqualification for a heart risk, Dr. Lamar's son, who was selected and is not what was promised — or am I reading a postulate of quantitative genetics into a dystopia that has no statistics in it at all?

On the scenes: I would like your judgement on which do genuine philosophical work under this lens. The two games of chicken, and Vincent's explanation of why he won; Jerome Eugene Morrow, who took silver and then threw himself in front of a car, and whose genotype promised what his life did not deliver; the daily ritual of scrubbing and of borrowed samples, where the identity that gets Vincent through the door is a social artefact rather than a biological fact, which may mean the society's failure is one of credentialing and essentialism rather than of statistical inference at all; and Dr. Lamar's decision at the final test, where a man who runs the system sets it aside for reasons the system cannot represent. I want to know where the concept illuminates a scene, and where the scene resists the concept and the honest critic has to say so.

Finally, the objection I expect from a good student: Gattaca's premise — a whole genome read as a prophecy at conception — is not our science, and using a fantasy to teach a distinction about real population statistics may teach the fallacy rather than the correction to it. Is that right? What I would like in the end is a reading faithful in both directions: which scenes I should screen in a fifty-minute session, what the film gets about heritability that my lecture's definitions cannot say, and what it quietly gets wrong.
