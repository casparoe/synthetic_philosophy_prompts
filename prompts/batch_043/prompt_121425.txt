I teach a cross-cultural communication module at a business school in Rotterdam, usually to mixed groups of Dutch and Japanese participants, and I am putting together a short glossary of the things we keep stumbling over. There is one I have no vocabulary for, and my students argue about it every term in a way that suggests they sense a real distinction but cannot state it. Here are two instances. A Japanese host, at the end of a visit that has run long, says warmly that they really must do this again soon and will be in touch about dates; the Dutch guest says, good, shall we say the fourteenth; the room goes quiet; the host is now trapped between confirming a date they do not want and visibly retracting, and the guest looks either naive or aggressive. Notice three things: the host would be dismayed if the guest actually came to believe a date was coming, so this is not a matter of not caring whether it is true; the blame for the awkwardness seems to fall on the guest, who took the words at face value; and the host cannot clean it up by saying what they meant, because saying it would be the real offence. The second instance is the one I find harder. A Dutch manager, asked for a reaction to a junior's draft, says flatly and in front of the team that the proposal is weak and should be rewritten from scratch. The Japanese junior is not surprised by the assessment — they had already worked out that the draft was weak — and is not, I think, hurt by the content. What upsets them is that it was said out loud, in that form, there. When I ask the Dutch participants to soften it, they protest that softening would be the deeper insult, since it would imply the junior cannot take the truth. So here the surface form is a blunt truth rather than a transparent falsehood, and yet it seems to have the same three-part shape: what is to be taken up is not what the words literally offer, the hearer who takes up the wrong thing gets blamed, and repairing things by making them explicit somehow makes them worse.

What I keep circling is this: an utterance whose literal content is not what is meant to be taken up, a convention that fixes the correct uptake, blame falling on whoever takes the literal content at face value, and a version in so many words that is itself an offence — so that what is intended can travel only by indirection, and remains oddly unavailable to be said. I have tried to borrow from things I have read about and none of them fits. It is not lying in the ordinary sense, because nobody is supposed to be deceived. It is not what the much-discussed essay about speakers who are indifferent to whether what they say is true describes either, because these speakers care a great deal and would be upset to be believed. It is not the sort of case philosophers like, where someone says something everyone can see is false in order to show they can get away with it, because nothing is being asserted over anybody here. And it is not just the ordinary indirection of the can-you-reach-the-salt variety, because that decodes automatically, carries no sting, and has a perfectly acceptable explicit paraphrase, whereas here the explicit paraphrase is the one thing you may not produce.

So: is there an established concept, or a literature, for this configuration — and if the nearest concepts only fit imperfectly, I would rather have the comparisons than a name, since what I need for the glossary is the carving rather than the label. In particular I would like to know what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where it stops. Two boundary cases I cannot place myself: a hearer who does not share the convention at all, such as a visiting American, a ten-year-old, or a machine transcript that will simply store the sentence — does the utterance become a lie, a failed ritual, or something else, and does the answer depend on whether the speaker knew the hearer was outside the convention? And the half-hearted case, where the speaker would in fact be mildly pleased if the invitation were taken up but does not expect it to be: at what point does the phenomenon dissolve into an ordinary, if vague, invitation? A third case I would like put on one side or the other is the written one, where a warm we should certainly continue this conversation in an email is later read literally by a third party who has no stake in the convention, say in a contract dispute. I am not after references or a reading list — I cannot chase them from here, and a label I cannot define for my students would not help. What I want is a definition sharp enough that I can tell, for each of those three cases, which side of the line it falls on and why.
