I've just come away from a long argument with a friend about weakness of will that neither of us won, and I would like to turn it into something more useful than it was. Next week I am recording a forty-five-minute episode of a podcast I host, and my guest is a philosopher who has just published a book-length dissolution of the topic. Her claim is that there is no such phenomenon as acting freely and intentionally against one's own all-things-considered better judgment, in the sense that has puzzled philosophers since Socrates denies the possibility in the Protagoras. What there is, on her account, is temporal fragmentation: agents weight near-future outcomes disproportionately, so that as the smaller, sooner option draws close their preferences genuinely reverse; the earlier better judgment was a verdict delivered under a different weighting, and the feeling of having been overpowered is simply what such a reversal feels like from the inside. The machinery is the discount function, and no divided self, no special connection between evaluation and action, and no synchronic failure of will needs to be added. Whenever I pressed her in correspondence, she fell back on a single move: that my worry belongs to the phenomenology of the reversal, while the mechanism is in the discount function.

Draft me eight to ten questions, each accompanied by the answer she is most likely to give and then the follow-up that turns on that answer. No warm-ups and nothing that invites her to restate the view. I want at least one question on each of the following, and I want the sequence arranged so that her fallback move becomes progressively harder to reach for.

First, the suspicion that a discount function relabels rather than explains: if the entire content of the model is that the nearer option looms larger as it nears, in what sense have we done more than rename the fact that the tempting option is tempting now? She will reach for the quantitative predictions and the choice data; the follow-up should test whether any of that data discriminates between her account and a merely verbal one.

Second, the flat-delay case that finally made my friend stop talking: an agent whose two options and their payoffs are both squarely in the present, so there is no differential delay for the discount function to operate on — keep scrolling or close the tab, say the cruel thing now or stay silent now. She will most likely reply that the relevant distance is not calendar time but something like distance from the last moment of deliberation, or descriptive or attentional distance. The follow-up should press the consequence: if the curve is really a function of salience or availability, it is no longer a temporal-discounting model, and the money-now-versus-money-later evidence no longer supports it.

Third, normativity. Under uncertainty about whether a delayed reward will arrive at all, steep near-future weighting can be the payoff-maximizing policy, so some steep discounters are not irrational by their own lights. But akratic is a term of criticism. Does her account entail that some paradigm cases of weakness of will are rationally impeccable, with the disapproving element imported from somewhere else? If she bites that bullet, ask what remains of the traditional claim that the agent acts defectively.

Fourth, the persistence of the better judgment during the action itself: many agents report judging the better option better while doing the other thing, with no lapse in the judgment. If the reversal is a real change of preference, there is no conflict left at the moment of action, and the guilt, the self-blame and the I knew better all need an error theory. If she instead concedes that the judgment persists and is motivationally inert, she has readmitted a divided mind and surrendered the parsimony that was the selling point — the follow-up should make her choose.

Fifth, the mirror-image case my friend raised late in the evening: someone who judges it better all things considered to take the easier, pleasanter option, because she has earned it and because restraint has its own costs, and who nevertheless cannot permit herself to and does the harsher thing. There is no tempting near reward here at all. If she answers that this is simply not weakness of will but some other condition, ask what principled taxonomy sorts the cases — and whether that taxonomy was built to protect the model.

Sixth, the remedy. The picoeconomic literature recommends that agents combat reversal by bundling choices into personal rules, and by devices such as a timed lockbox. Ask what the model says about the person who locks the biscuits away and then spends twenty minutes trying to break the box open: the struggle appears to have been relocated rather than dissolved. And if adopting a personal rule has to be reaffirmed at each moment, is that not exactly the synchronic exercise of will the account said was never needed?

Seventh, akrasia outside action altogether: the case of someone who continues to assert or rely on a claim she has judged to be unsupported, where there is no reward and no delay structure.

Close with one question about what evidence could count against the account — in particular, what it would take for measured steepness of discounting to come apart from real-world failures of will, or for interventions that flatten the curve to leave the behavior untouched — and then one final question that gives her the floor to say what the account is for, and what we would lose if we simply gave up the idea of a synchronic failure of will.
