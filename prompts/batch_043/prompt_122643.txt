So I'm in this experimental philosophy seminar this semester (it's cross-listed with psych, which explains the vibe) and we spent basically all of last week on the Knobe effect — the chairman who says he doesn't care about the environment and gets described as intentionally harming it, while his twin who doesn't care about helping it gets described as *not* intentionally helping it. My professor keeps insisting the interesting question is whether the asymmetry is really about intentionality at all, or whether it's blame and norm-violation leaking into what's supposed to be a descriptive judgment, and I get it in the vignettes, but the vignettes feel rigged to me — one guy, one sentence, one consequence, no history. So for my final paper I want to run it on something real, and the case I keep circling back to is Purdue Pharma and OxyContin: the mid-90s launch, the massive marketing push to prescribe opioids for chronic non-cancer pain, the way the company rode (and partly funded) the pain-is-undertreated campaign, addiction and diversion following, the federal misbranding plea in 2007, the enormous settlements in the 2020s, the Sackler name coming off museum wings. I don't need exact figures and if I've got a detail slightly wrong just go with the rough shape of it. What makes it irresistible to me is that it has *both* side effects built in, the good one (people in real pain getting treated) and the bad one (addiction), produced by the very same campaigns, which is a natural mirror of the Knobe pair that nobody designed on purpose.

Can you do the analysis in both directions? First the disciplined mapping: who is the agent here — because a corporation isn't a person, and I'm not sure the Knobe subject is even the right kind of thing, so tell me what happens to the effect when you swap in a firm with hundreds of decision-makers and a collective-knowledge problem; what counts as the consequence; what the evidence base for foresight is; what the relevant contrast class is. Then be honest about where the vignette structure can't stretch: it's a single identified victim versus a statistical, population-level harm; it's one shot versus a decade-long loop where the agents got feedback (diversion reports, prescribing data) and kept going; and — this is the part I haven't seen anyone test — the moral valence *drifted* historically, since aggressive opioid prescribing was described as compassionate in 1996 and as the cause of an epidemic by 2015. So does the asymmetry track valence-at-the-time-of-action or valence-at-the-time-of-judgment? For a concrete handle, imagine a valence-flip deposition: one witness, one decision (say, the choice to keep pushing after the early diversion reports), asked in the same breath and in randomized order whether Purdue intended to relieve pain and whether Purdue intended to get people addicted, with a jury instruction demanding consistent answers. Predict the split and say what it would actually show.

Then turn it around: what does the episode do to the theory? My roommate, who's pre-law, says the whole thing is just a mens rea question — the law already distinguishes acting purposely from knowingly from recklessly — and I don't think that's right but I can't say why, so please settle it: if the asymmetry really tracks blame rather than control or reasons, is that an argument for junking the purpose/knowledge distinction in corporate intent, or for keeping it precisely because it's insulated from valence? Does the fact that nobody, including people who are sympathetic to the company, wants to say Purdue intentionally relieved suffering count as a genuine prediction of the theory or is it cheap? And give me the one thing the case exposes that the vignette literature structurally couldn't — then a one-sentence verdict on whether the Knobe effect is doing real work here or whether this is just a blame story wearing intentionality's clothes.
