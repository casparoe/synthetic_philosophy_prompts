I spent a career in experimental physical chemistry — reaction kinetics, mostly, with a long detour through spectroscopy — and since retiring I have been sitting in on a cognitive science reading group. We have just finished Hofstadter and Sander's *Surfaces and Essences: Analogy as the Fuel and Fire of Thinking*, and one of the graduate students pointed me toward the structure-mapping side of the literature, where analogy is scored by how well relational structure aligns, with a principled preference for connected systems of relations over isolated object attributes. The two pictures feel to me as though they are answering different questions, and I want to know whether they are really in conflict or whether I have simply failed to see how one subsumes the other. My way in is a question about failure, because that is what I was trained on: in my old field a model that fits for the wrong reason was not a model that fit.

Let me set out the distinction I have been toying with. Call the first failure mode *surface capture*: one or two vivid, perceptually or notationally salient features of the source seize the mapping, and the relational structure is dragged along behind them, so the analogy inherits whatever happened to hang off the striking features rather than what mattered. Call the second *structural overreach*: the relations map cleanly and even elegantly, the systematic structure lines up, and the analogy then keeps yielding confident consequences in a region where nothing licenses them, because the causal or mechanistic warrant for transporting inferences was never carried across — only the shape was. The two are meant to be independent: a mapping can be structurally faithful and inferentially empty, or superficially driven and accidentally fruitful.

Here is my worry, and it is what I would like explained. On Hofstadter and Sander's account, as I read them, categories are not fixed in advance of the mapping; concepts slip and re-form in the act of analogizing, and to categorize is itself to make an analogy to a remembered exemplar. On the structure-mapping account there is, at least tacitly, a relational description on each side that is what it is prior to alignment, and the preference for systematic structure is what tells you which alignment to prefer. If the first picture is right, my two failure modes may be unstable: what I want to call surface capture might just be the source concepts slipping under pressure from the target, which on their view is not a defect but the normal mechanism; and what I want to call structural overreach might be, for them, an analogy simply going through, since there is no fixed correct representation against which to say it went too far. So the question is whether the categorization-as-analogy picture has the resources to say that an analogy *misfires* without helping itself to exactly the fixed representation it denies — and, if it does not, whether that is a genuine explanatory gap or merely the price of a theory that refuses to grade analogies except after the fact.

To make it concrete, take the case I know best historically: reasoning about heat engines by analogy with a fluid falling between two levels, the water-wheel picture that Carnot used and that survives in the way every physicist still talks about heat flow. There is no conserved heat substance, so the mechanistic warrant for the picture is absent; yet that reasoning delivered the right result about the maximum efficiency of an engine depending only on the temperatures of the two reservoirs and not on the working substance. I would like you to explain how each of the two accounts diagnoses this, and then tell me which of my two failure modes it instantiates — or whether the right answer is that it is not a failure at all, because at the right level of coarse-grained description the extracted essence really is the potential-difference-drives-a-flow structure, and the inference was licensed after all. Crucially, I also want the discriminating test: which features of the case would have to be different for it to fall into the other category, so that the taxonomy is doing real work rather than redescribing whatever happened.

Please write this for someone fluent in thermodynamics and new to cognitive science: explain the machinery you rely on, and do not spend words on references, further reading, or bibliographic suggestions — I can get those myself. Roughly eight hundred to a thousand words. Conclude with a single machine-readable label as the last line of your reply, and nothing after it:

\answer{surface-capture} if the case is best described as a salient surface feature dictating the mapping; \answer{structural-overreach} if it is best described as relational structure carried over without causal warrant; \answer{both} if it is genuinely and non-trivially both; \answer{neither} if neither fits, in which case your positive diagnosis belongs in the prose above.
