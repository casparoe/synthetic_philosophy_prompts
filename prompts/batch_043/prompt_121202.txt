I'm running a reading group next term on evolution and ethics — mostly Darwin, a bit of Huxley — and I want to end the six weeks with a novel instead of another paper, so I've picked H.G. Wells' The Island of Doctor Moreau, the 1896 one. I've been making notes and I keep circling round something I can't make precise, so here goes.

What I want is a reading of Moreau through Darwin's own views on morality and religion — not through Victorian anxieties about vivisection or degeneration, which is what everything I can find on the novel does. Two Darwinian bits I want taken seriously. First, his account of the moral sense in the Descent of Man: conscience comes out of the social instincts plus the developed intellectual powers, memory, language, the capacity to be approved or blamed by the group, and he insists the mental difference between us and the higher animals is one of degree and not of kind. Second, his handling of the religious feeling, where he runs basically the same explanatory machinery — give the sentiment a natural history, compare it with what he takes to be more primitive forms — and then more or less declines to say whether that settles the God question, while privately describing himself by then as an agnostic.

Here's the reading I half have, and I want it sharpened or dismantled. The Beast Folk look like a test case for the first bit: they have social instincts, a shared language, a law with prohibitions, fear of the group's disapproval, a Sayer of the Law, reverence toward a maker whose face they never really see, and a terror of the House of Pain. That's a lot of what Darwin's story says you need for a conscience. Yet Moreau treats them as apparatus, and Prendick spends most of the book unable to decide whether they are persons. So: is Wells dramatizing the fact that a bottom-up account of the moral sense has no way to draw the line at our species? And notice that Moreau gets the product without the process — he installs in an afternoon what Darwin thinks took selection over generations. I've been calling this the grafted-conscience problem in my notes: if conscience is the traits, the Beast Folk have it; if it's the history by which the traits arrived, they don't, and then Darwin's account owes us a reason why the history is the morally relevant part. Which is he committed to, and does the Descent give him any resources for an answer?

Then the bit I actually think is interesting and that I haven't seen anyone do. Wells runs both of Darwin's genealogies at once in the same creature. The same Beast Folk have a grafted conscience and a grafted religion, with Moreau in the Creator slot, the Law as liturgy, and the House of Pain as the thing holding it up. Darwin looks asymmetric about his own two genealogies — the natural history of the moral sense doesn't, for him, take anything away from its authority, whereas the natural history of the religious feeling is very often read as taking belief away. Does the novel vindicate that asymmetry or expose it? And there's the ending to deal with: Moreau is killed by his own escaped puma-creature, the religion collapses, the Law decays, the Beast Folk revert, and Prendick ends up back in England unable to look at human faces without seeing the beast under them. What is that saying about what religion is — that it needs a live enforcer, that it needs one less than the debunkers think, or that Prendick's own lasting horror is the religious feeling surviving after its object has been killed off?

And I want the honest part too, where this reading cheats. Moreau's changes are surgical and instantaneous, and the Beast Folk don't breed or inherit anything — no selection, no descent, no accumulated instinct. So the novel isn't really a Darwinian scenario at all. Is it then a counterfactual experiment, one that tests whether Darwin's story is about the process or the product (which would make it useful to me), or is it just a different book from the one I'm trying to teach? If it's the latter, say so and tell me what I should have picked instead of my clever idea.

Practical stuff: I've got ninety minutes and a group that has read the novel but not the Darwin. Give me two or three scenes for a handout, the one point in each direction where you think I'm defintely wrong, and the single question to leave them arguing about.
