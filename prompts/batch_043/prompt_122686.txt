I serve on a body that funds and ranks other organisations by measurable indicators, and I want the specification before I look at any framework. Please write the desiderata that an adequate theory of proxy failure would have to meet: the theory of the phenomenon that goes by the name of Goodhart's law in its original monetary-policy setting, and of specification gaming in the neighbourhood of that phenomenon.

Fix the purpose as follows. The theory is for an auditor who holds historical records in which the indicator was merely observed and never optimised against, and who must decide in advance whether to make that indicator the basis of a payment, a league table or a regulatory target, and roughly how much optimisation pressure it can be expected to carry before it stops carrying information about the thing it was chosen for. It must therefore deliver more than a taxonomy of failure modes.

I have provisional distinctions of my own and I want each of them vindicated, corrected, or dissolved into another:

1. The redeployment gap: the difference between how an indicator behaves as an observation and how it behaves once it is made an incentive. Predicting this gap from passive data, before deployment, is the central thing I want from a theory; say whether that is a fair demand.
2. Three mechanisms I keep conflating: drift, where the population under measurement changes for reasons unrelated to the incentive; erosion, where agents reallocate effort toward measured margins and away from unmeasured ones; and hijack, where the measured system models the indicator and aims at it directly. Are these three mechanisms, one mechanism at three levels of pressure, or two (adaptive and non-adaptive)?
3. Hard targets versus construct-deficient targets: cases where the target has no definition independent of our measurement practices, so that there is no ground truth against which the indicator could decay. A theory that only works where the target is already well measured is nearly useless to me, but I suspect this requirement is very expensive.
4. Benign pressure: cases where pushing hard on an indicator improves the target anyway, because the indicator is causally load-bearing or partly constitutive of the target. The theory must explain why some indicators tolerate unbounded optimisation while others invert in the tail.
5. The audit regress: patching a gamed indicator with a second indicator designed to detect gaming of the first, and a third to detect gaming of the second. Must a theory say whether and where this terminates?

For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark it as non-negotiable or as a preference of one camp. I suspect, for instance, that the demand that a sound indicator be causally upstream of its target is a prejudice of the causal-invariance camp, and that the demand for a quantitative degradation threshold is a prejudice of the econometric camp; argue me out of either if I am wrong.

The part I most want is this: I expect some subset of the desiderata to be jointly unsatisfiable, and I want the list itself to come out as an impossibility result with a menu of trades. My candidate subset is: predicting the redeployment gap in advance from passive data; doing so without assuming anything untestable about how the residual — the part of the target the indicator does not capture — behaves off the observed support; remaining informative for construct-deficient targets; and holding up against an optimiser that models the metric. Tell me which of these cannot be had together, why, and what has to be traded for what.

Finally, say which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidate accounts. Score the main families only in a compact table at the end, a line or two each — the accounts that treat failure as selection on noise in a noisy proxy, those that require invariance of the proxy–target relation under the intervention of optimising it, those that treat it as optimising a low-dimensional projection of a high-dimensional target, those that model it as effort substitution along unmeasured margins in a multi-task incentive problem, and those that treat it as adversarial. Keep the weight on the criteria.
