Before I retired, I spent thirty-two years in a combustion laboratory, most of that time on the numerical side: reacting flows, stiff systems, the usual fight with error growth. Three years ago I began reading philosophy seriously — philosophy of science at first, then, by accident, philosophy of history — and I now attend a small reading group that meets monthly. Our last session was devoted to the old nail rhyme: the horseshoe lost for want of a nail, the horse for want of the shoe, the rider, the battle, the kingdom. The question on the table was whether the rhyme records a real structural feature of historical causation or is merely a piece of folklore with a pleasing shape. I went home and tried to settle it with the only apparatus I trust, and what I produced is an argument I do not believe the conclusion of. I would be obliged for a diagnosis.

(1) Say that an event E is *decisive* for a later macroscopic outcome O if and only if, had E not occurred, everything else in the causal history being as it actually was, O would not have occurred.

(2) The processes that connect small events to large historical outcomes — physical, economic, institutional, all of it coupled — are chaotic in the strict sense: they possess a positive Lyapunov exponent λ, so that two states differing initially by ε separate as ε·e^{λt}. The time required for a perturbation of magnitude ε to reach macroscopic scale is therefore only on the order of (1/λ)·ln(1/ε). Even a perturbation absurdly far below the threshold of anything a historian could record will, within any historically interesting interval, alter the coarse-grained later state.

(3) Since O is itself a coarse-grained feature of the later state, it follows that for any event E in the causal history of O there exist minute changes in the circumstances at E whose subsequent amplification would have averted O.

(4) But the non-occurrence of E is simply one such minute change in the circumstances at E: the difference between the nail having been driven and its not having been driven is a local difference of exactly the sort (2) describes. Nor does the amplification discriminate among the minute differences available at E, since the same exponent λ governs every direction that is not precisely contracting, and I take the set of precisely contracting perturbations to be negligible.

(5) Hence, by the test in (1), every event in the causal history of O is decisive for O. Not merely the nail but every other nail in the campaign, every sneeze in the camp, every eddy of dust: for want of any one of them, the kingdom.

(6) Decisiveness as defined in (1) is all-or-nothing, so if every event is decisive for O, causal importance admits of no degrees, and there is no fact of the matter as to whether the kingdom fell for want of the nail or for want of the alliance system, or which of those mattered more than the weather that morning. The rhyme, if it is true at all, is true of everything, and therefore says nothing.

I cannot accept (6). It is inconsistent with the everyday practice of my own former discipline, where one says without embarrassment that the catalyst caused the ignition and that the truck passing outside the building did not, and it is inconsistent with my strong sense that some historical explanations really are better than others.

My difficulty is that I do not know where to intervene. I would most like to keep (2) and (3), since I am reasonably confident of the physics. I would also like to keep something in the neighbourhood of (1), because I cannot see what else the attribution of causal importance among historians could consist in, if not some counterfactual test. That appears to leave (4) and the step from (5) to (6), and I cannot tell whether one of them is doing all the damage or whether both are.

Two attempted repairs of my own have both stalled, and I mention them only so that you can tell me whether either is on a usable line.

The first is to replace the all-or-nothing test with a graded one. Call an event's *counterfactual leverage* the fraction of admissible minimal substitutions at that event that avert O, where a substitution is admissible only if it conserves what a physically possible world must conserve: an event cannot simply be deleted, it must be replaced by something, and the something must occupy the same spatiotemporal region and carry roughly the same energy and information. The trouble is that I have no principled measure on the space of admissible substitutions, and the answer seems to depend on how that space is carved up.

The second is a property I remember from the dynamical-systems literature, to the effect that approximate solution paths of a suitably hyperbolic system are tracked, within a fixed tolerance and for a long but finite time, by exact solution paths. This seems to cut in two directions at once. On the one hand it reassures me that the counterfactual history I construct by removing the nail is not a physically incoherent object, since some genuine trajectory runs close to it. On the other hand it seems to imply that the very same coarse-grained history — the kingdom falling on schedule — is realised by an enormous family of distinct micro-histories, in which case the link between any particular small event and the outcome is far looser than (4) requires. I cannot work out which of these readings is correct, or whether the property bears on the argument at all.

Please tell me which step you would reject, why that step nonetheless looked innocent when I wrote it down, what rejecting it commits me to, and which of the remaining steps is the least costly to give up if I have misidentified the culprit. I should also be glad to know whether either of my two repairs can be made to work.
