Hi — I need help turning a half-formed idea into an actual proposal document. It's for a nine-month dissertation fellowship; the deliverable is a thesis of about 12,000 words, and the deadline is in ten days, so this needs to be something I can more or less hand in after I add citations myself.

The idea: I've been rereading Nietzsche's 1874 essay on history (the one from the Untimely Meditations, on the uses and disadvantages of history for life) and I want to take his threefold scheme — monumental, antiquarian, critical history — and use it as a diagnostic for a situation he definately did not have in mind: permanent, searchable, algorithmically resurfaced personal and collective memory. My hunch is that the whole framework quietly assumes the past is scarce and that forgetting is the default, so the interesting question is how much history a life or a culture can absorb before it gets sick. But in an environment where nothing is deleted and old material is actively pushed back in front of you — anniversary resurfacing, feeds assembled out of your own history, permanent public archives of things people said years ago — the scarcity assumption flips, and I'm no longer sure what his claim that we also need the unhistorical even amounts to. If forgetting is no longer the default but has to be manufactured, can the capacity he calls plastic power be a property of an institution, an interface, or a legal regime rather than of a person? Or is his normative vocabulary (life, health, strength, what a culture can bear) irreducibly about agents, so that applying it to a feed is a category error dressed up as topicality?

There's a second wrinkle I keep circling and can't settle. He presents the critical mode as curative — you drag the past before a tribunal and condemn it so that you can live — but he also notes that we are the products of the very past we condemn, so the standpoint needed for criticism is the one his own premises deny us. A total archive makes that worse in a specific way: a reckoning is only curative if it can be over, and an archive that never expires removes the possibility of it being over. I suspect the critical mode is the one that structurally requires oblivion, while monumental and antiquarian excess are just excesses. That might be the actual thesis, or it might be a distraction.

What I want from you is a real proposal, on the following:

- a question narrow enough to have a determinate answer in 12k words, and phrased so it could come out either way. I don't want a proposal whose conclusion is already fixed by how the question is worded.
- why the obvious ways of doing this don't settle it: in particular why it isn't just applied Nietzsche (three modes, three apps, one section each), and why it isn't just another generic suspicion that more memory is worse. If there's a third, less obvious way of framing it that I'd have to beat, name it.
- a method: what I'd actually do, month by month across nine months, including where evidence comes from given that I can't run experiments and am not doing fieldwork.
- what a good result looks like for someone who isn't a Nietzsche scholar — I want traction for people who design or regulate memory systems, not just for people who write about nineteenth-century culture — and what a merely passable result looks like.
- what would count as the project failing, and what would count as it being too big for nine months. I especially want at least one test that separates my framework from generic anti-archive suspicion: something the tripartite scheme predicts that you would not predict from thinking more memory is worse, and ideally one case where it says a memory practice is healthy even though it looks excessive.

One interpretive decision I can't make and want you to make for me: are the three modes three genres of history-writing, or three attitudes that any single item can be taken up in? It matters a lot for my main example, because the same resurfaced item can be used monumentally, antiquarianly, or critically depending on uptake. Pick one and justify it in the proposal.
