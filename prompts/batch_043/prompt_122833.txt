Write a satirical internal guidance document: a benefit–cost circular, roughly 850–950 words, issued by a federal office that has fully committed to revealed preference as both a theory of choice and a measure of well-being. Format it as numbered subsections, and include at least one footnote, one short worked numerical example, and a closing "Limitations" section.

The voice is senior-bureaucratic and entirely deadpan: confident, procedural, courteous, with no irony markers anywhere in the document itself. A reader should never catch the circular admitting that anything is wrong, and yet should be able to tell precisely what is being sent up. To help keep it deadpan, do not let the text use the words "paternalism," "Orwellian," "dystopian," or "irrational."

Two doctrines generate everything, and the circular is simply what follows from applying them without flinching:

(1) Utility is not a mental state. It is whatever index makes observed choices come out as maximizations, and it carries no meaning beyond the transformations that preserve that fit. The office therefore classifies reported satisfaction, regret, longing, and self-described happiness as "untested testimony," inadmissible against a completed transaction.

(2) Because preference is defined by choice, no agent can ever be mistaken about what she wants: whatever was not chosen is reconstrued after the fact as infeasible, unaffordable in the relevant sense, or not genuinely on offer. Give this maneuver an official name — the Latent Feasibility Doctrine, the Silent Option Rule, something of that register — and have the circular apply it to cases where the alleged constraint is transparently not a constraint.

The circular then has to do welfare economics with these materials anyway. Please cover, in your own order and with your own case material:

- The unit problem. Any increasing (or, where the office has risk to handle, any positive affine) rescaling of the index preserves every ranking and every prediction, so the number has no natural size. The office's solution: a statutory welfare numeraire fixing an exchange rate between utils and dollars by decree, with a rule for which rescalings are "conforming."
- The two purchases in the same week that cannot both be optimal on any single stable ordering — e.g., a household that buys cigarettes and also pays for a cessation program. The office's ruling should be procedural rather than substantive (re-dating budget periods, partitioning goods into separate "accounts," declaring the goods non-comparable, or treating the buyer as a sequence of temporally distinct agents) and should be stated as if it were a data-hygiene matter.
- Agents with no recorded transactions: no data, no preferences, no measurable harm, so any policy affecting them scores as costless. Give the imputation rule the office uses.
- Adaptive narrowing: a population whose objective circumstances have deteriorated but whose observed choice pattern is unchanged, and the office's amortization schedule under which a welfare loss is re-scored as zero after a fixed number of years.
- Distribution across persons. The index is ordinal and non-comparable, yet the office must weight benefits by who receives them; have it insist that its weights are administrative parameters and not interpersonal comparisons, and let the reasoning be visibly circular.
- The office's own behavior: its choices reveal its preferences, so its budget cannot be criticized.
- One case where the doctrine's verdict is genuinely uncomfortable but not crazy — for instance a household that sincerely reports wanting to save more and never does, where forcing the saving is scored as a welfare loss. Let this one land as fair rather than as a cheap shot, so the satire is recognizable as satire of something a reasonable person might defend.
- One footnote of internal dissent from a resident "mentalist" who maintains that utility is a feeling and that people are sometimes wrong about what they want; the circular should dispose of him on procedural grounds without answering him.

Use the technical vocabulary correctly — ordinal versus cardinal utility, invariance under the relevant class of transformations, rationalizability of a finite choice dataset by a stable ordering (the weak/strong/generalized axioms and the Afriat inequalities, if you want to name them), the fact that revealed preference cannot separate tastes from constraints, the gap between willingness to pay and willingness to accept, and above all the difference between an "as if" representation of behavior and a measure of welfare. The humor should come entirely from that machinery being taken at its word: a labor economist or a decision theorist should recognize that the author knows the material and should feel the send-up is fair rather than a strawman. At the same time, the document should be legible to a reader with no economics: the absurdity must be visible in the human cases even if the apparatus is not.

Invent any epigraph, motto, office name, or official you need; do not quote or paraphrase any real text, and do not attribute any view to a real person.
