I'm a retired occupational physician, not a philosopher, and I ended up on my country's statutory insurance classification panel because somebody had to be. The panel's rulings are narrow but consequential: when we classify a state as a disease, the person gets treatment coverage, mandatory workplace accommodation, and protection against pre-employment screening for it; when we classify it as a normal variant or as a social problem, they get none of those, though they may still draw general disability support. Over three years we have ruled on the following, always unanimously and always without written reasons:

1. Type 2 diabetes with established symptoms and complications — disease.
2. Asymptomatic raised blood pressure in a 45-year-old with no symptoms, roughly doubling ten-year stroke risk — disease.
3. Congenital absence of a small forearm muscle found in a substantial minority of the population, with no measurable functional consequence — not a disease.
4. Common age-related hair loss with documented distress and real social and economic cost — not a disease, though we recommended wigs be funded under a separate cosmetic benefit.
5. A child of normal intelligence and adequate schooling, with no neurological finding we could identify, who cannot learn to read — disease.
6. A prolonged grief state: many months of intense distress and inability to work after a bereavement, no laboratory marker, and — as our own statistician told us — a duration distribution continuous with ordinary grief, so that any cut-off is a convention — disease.
7. Chronic insomnia and panic symptoms in residents of a district with night-time noise above the legal limit, where the symptoms resolved completely when people were rehoused — not a disease; we called it an environmental problem and sent it to the housing authority.
8. A hereditary variant producing unusually dense, fracture-resistant bones with no offsetting cost we could find — not a disease; we called it an enhancement.
9. Lactose intolerance in adults: a minority in our country but a majority of adults worldwide, producing symptoms only when milk is drunk. We declined to classify it either way, and explicitly left open whether the reference population for "normal functioning" is our country, the species, or something narrower.

The case now before us is a congenital sleep-wake variant, present in about 6% of the national population but in roughly a third of the population of one upland district. In carriers, sleep spontaneously divides into two blocks of about three and a half hours separated by one to two hours of quiet wakefulness; total sleep is about half an hour below the population median; the melatonin rhythm is phase-delayed relative to the population mean. On every standard measure of daytime alertness, cognition and mood they score in the normal range, and most report no distress — a good many report liking the interval and using it. Three things brought it to us. A matched cohort study finds about a 1.3-fold accident rate among carriers, but the entire excess is confined to employer-rostered night shifts and vanishes in carriers who work days or set their own hours. Three of the four claimants lost shifts or job offers after a workplace wellness programme flagged the variant. And there is an intervention — timed light plus melatonin — that does consolidate the sleep into a single block; in trials it produces consolidated sleep with no gain in daytime alertness and in some carriers a small loss, and carriers generally dislike it.

Please don't give me a general theory of disease; give me something I can take into the room. Reconstruct the two or three rules that are each consistent with all nine of our earlier verdicts — one of my colleagues has started saying our record really tracks whether the cause is inside the person's skin, another that it tracks whether the state would still be there in a better-organised society, a third that it tracks whether we are willing to pay — and say what each rule predicts here. Which way would you expect us to go, and why? Which of our precedents is genuinely the closest analogue, as opposed to the one that feels closest? And since I suspect our record underdetermines this: what principle would we be committing ourselves to under each of the two live options, and what single further case — a variant of this one, or a new submission — should we ask to be brought before us next, because the rules you have identified would have to come apart on it?
