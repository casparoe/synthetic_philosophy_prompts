I sat in on a metaphysics reading group where two PhD students got stuck arguing about Black's spheres — the two exactly similar iron spheres in otherwise empty space, offered as a counterexample to the Identity of Indiscernibles. I'm not a philosopher, and when they turned to me to referee I had nothing useful to say, because both of them sounded right at different moments. Roughly:

(1) Nadia: The version of PII that's a trivial theorem quantifies over properties unrestrictedly — everything has being identical to itself and nothing else does — so nobody is defending that. The interesting version says distinct things differ qualitatively, and Black's spheres refute it if they're possible. Fine. But then the distinctness of the two spheres is either a brute fact that no property reflects, or it's grounded in haecceities. Either way the anti-PII side is committed to primitive thisness, and that commitment is doing all the work here, not the spheres.

(2) Tomas: I don't need primitive thisness. "There are two spheres" is a structural fact about the world, not a property that either sphere has, and numerical difference isn't the kind of thing that has to show up as a differing property. Also, the point generalizes: if the permutation swapping the two spheres preserves all the qualitative structure of the setup, then it preserves any qualitative property whatsoever, so no qualitative property can distinguish them. That's why the case works, and it has nothing to do with space being empty.

(3) Nadia: Right — and here's a version that makes that vivid. Take a world crammed with junk, wildly asymmetric, but arranged so that the whole world has exactly one nontrivial symmetry: a reflection swapping two locations, each containing an iron sphere. The spheres are indiscernible even though the universe is richly structured, so the case doesn't depend on a featureless void, and you can't dodge it by adding a third object either, since a third object placed symmetrically discerns nothing.

(4) Tomas: Agreed, and that helps me: indiscernibility just is being related by a symmetry of the world. So PII collapses into the claim that actual space has no nontrivial symmetries, which is a contingent, empirical matter rather than a metaphysical necessity. Anyone who wants PII to be necessary now owes an argument that such a symmetric world is impossible.

(5) Nadia: But your own setup already contains indiscernible distinct things before the spheres arrive: the two locations themselves, if locations are entities at all. So if you're a substantivalist you have counterexamples to PII from geometry alone, and if you aren't, then what makes it true that there are two spheres rather than one sphere counted twice? Both answers look like they need a primitive thisness after all.
