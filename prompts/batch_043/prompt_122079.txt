I'm a second-year PhD student in an experimental particle physics group, and I've been auditing a foundations reading group where we keep circling around the many-worlds interpretation. I'm not confused about the reading; I'm confused about my own day job. Here is the practice I mean. My experiment sorts events into two channels; the theory assigns Born-rule weights to them; we accumulate a very large number of trials, plot the relative frequencies, and compare the shape of the resulting distribution with the prediction, treating any single event as carrying essentially no evidential weight in either direction. When the observed frequency sits far from the prediction — at the five-sigma level that the collaboration treats as its threshold for announcing a discovery — we treat the deviation as evidence that something is wrong, and we adjudicate it by writing down a short menu of candidate explanations: an ordinary statistical fluctuation, a mis-modelled efficiency or unsimulated background, an unlucky choice of selection cuts, new physics. Independent replication by a second collaboration is treated as the strongest single guard against the fluke hypotheses, and blinding the analysis until the procedure is fixed is treated as a guard against fooling ourselves. What never appears on that menu, and what I have never once heard anyone in my group offer even as a joke, is this: nothing is wrong at all, the statistics simply came out deviant on the branch of the universal wavefunction that we happen to occupy.

That omission is what I want understood. On the many-worlds picture as it gets presented to us — every outcome with nonzero amplitude is realized, nothing is selected out, the branches are all real in the same sense — a deviant branch is not merely possible; for any run long enough that a five-sigma deviation is even representable, some branch exhibits one. And if branches or observer-moments were counted rather than weighted by squared amplitude, the deviant ones would not be the rare ones at all: where the Born rule says 1 percent, naive counting says something closer to even, and most branches would then look wildly anomalous by Born-rule lights. So the practice seems to presuppose something that the spare, no-collapse part of the interpretation does not hand you: a rule saying that you should expect to find yourself where the Born measure is thickest, or at least that you should act as if you will. One friend in the reading group says this shows the interpretation is smuggling in an extra posit about degrees of reality or self-locating credence, and is therefore more expensive than its "nothing added to the formalism" advertisement suggests; my supervisor says no, the practice needs no such posit, because an agent who knows in advance that she will branch can be shown to bet as if the Born rule holds, and the laboratory's conventions are disciplined betting policies rather than claims about where in the branching structure we are located. I can follow both arguments but cannot tell what the practice actually commits us to, or whether the two stories diverge anywhere I could notice from inside a lab.

So I'd like the implicit theory reconstructed. What view of probability, of self-location, or of evidence must someone hold for this practice — the frequency-over-many-trials confirmation, the five-sigma threshold, the standard menu of explanations, the weight given to independent replication — to be the rational thing to do, and which rival views does the practice quietly rule out by never listing the deviant-branch hypothesis? Please distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably: in particular, does it require a genuine self-locating credence distributed by the Born measure, or only a decision-theoretic or betting norm, and is there any reading of the practice that an equal-weight or branch-counting version of the interpretation could keep? Is the practice underdetermined in the sense that a collapse theory or a hidden-variable theory would license exactly the same laboratory behaviour, and if so does that undercut my friend's argument, or only relocate it? Then tell me what would have to change in the lab if the presupposed view were replaced: would "we occupy a low-measure branch" become an admissible item on the anomaly menu, and if not why not; would replication by a second collaboration still do the work we think it does; would the practice need the total number of branches to be finite, or the decomposition into branches to be well defined, in order to be coherent at all? I'd also like to know which single feature of the situation is doing the philosophical work, and where your reconstruction is softest. Please don't settle which interpretation of quantum mechanics is true, and please don't route the answer through quantum suicide or immortality setups — we already have one of those people in the group. No citations or reading lists; I want the argument, not the literature.
