Hi — I'm a journalist, I write a weekly column on science and ideas for a Sunday paper, and I'm about 3,000 words into a piece on something narrower than it sounds: why we can read letterforms we have literally never seen before. Not just new fonts, but genuinely new shapes — blackletter if you didn't grow up with it, a six-year-old's handwriting, the alphabet that some generative design tool spits out in your brand's "voice." I got into an argument with a type-designer friend about it (she says readabilty lives in the whole word and I said no, you can tell an 'a' is an 'a' standing there on its own), and she pointed me at that old Letter Spirit business — the project about what makes every 'a' an 'a'. Anyway, I've been turning over two answers and I want you to compare them, because I keep convincing myself they're the same claim wearing different clothes and then unconvincing myself. Call the first one shape-descent: a token is an 'a' because you can reach it from some canonical 'a' by a chain of small stylistic deformations, so identity is inherited along a path through shape space and thins out gradually the further along that path you go. Call the second one contrast-role: a token is an 'a' in virtue of the job it holds inside a particular alphabet — it's the thing that isn't the 'o', isn't the 'd', isn't the 'g' — so the boundaries in shape space are fixed by where the other letters of that font happen to sit, and they shift when the rest of the font shifts.

The case I can't get out of my head: my friend has a variable font with a single axis that morphs the lowercase 'a' continuously into the lowercase 'o' — circle plus stem, stem shrinks, stem vanishes — and somewhere around two thirds along it stops reading as an 'a' and starts reading as an 'o', and it does so pretty abruptly even though nothing changed except the length of one stroke. Worse: take that ambiguous in-between shape and drop it into c_t (I'm using _ for the blanked-out letter) and it snaps back to being an 'a'; drop it into c_ld and it's an 'o'. And then there's single-story 'a' versus double-story 'a' — same letter, shapes sharing almost no local features, and nobody hesitates for a millisecond. A cognitive scientist I called muttered something about categorical perception and I genuinely can't tell whether that's an explanation or just a new label for the thing I'm asking about.

So: put shape-descent and contrast-role side by side on this. Which one has the resources to explain the abrupt flip, the way the rest of the word settles the question, and the fact that two shapes with nothing in common can both be 'a' while two nearly identical shapes belong to different letters? Where do they actually predict different outcomes rather than just describing the same outcomes in different words? Roughly in this order: (1) the cleanest statement of what each account says letter-identity consists in, (2) how each handles the abrupt a-to-o flip partway along the morph, (3) how each handles the c_t versus c_ld thing, (4) how each handles single-story versus double-story 'a', (5) where, if anywhere, they come apart in prediction rather than vocabulary — and which one you'd put money on.
