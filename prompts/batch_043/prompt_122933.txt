Hi all — hoping someone can tell me whether the thing I keep noticing has a name, or tell me it isn't a thing and why.

Background: I'm retired, used to be a crystallographer, and since I stopped work I've been sitting in on a philosophy of physics reading group at the university. They're very patient with me. They've spent weeks arguing about whether the mathematical object that quantum theory attaches to a system — the thing you do the calculations on to get the chances of each outcome — is something real out in the world, or just a summary of what we happen to know about the system. I've picked up that there are two Greek-ish labels for the two answers (ontic? epistemic?) but those sound like names for the verdicts rather than for the puzzle, and they don't seem to capture what's actually bothering me.

It's this. Whatever that object is, it changes in two completely different ways depending on whether anyone is checking. Leave the system alone and it changes smoothly and predictably — there's an equation, you put in how it is now and you get out exactly how it will be later, no odds involved. But the moment somebody measures the system, all that goes out the window: it jumps, discontinuously, to a new value, in a way nobody can predict but only put odds on, and where it jumps to depends on what the measurement turned up. Fine, textbook stuff. What I don't hear anyone say out loud is that each of those two behaviours, taken on its own, would seem to settle the argument in opposite directions. A smooth law-governed change that carries on while nobody is watching is what you'd expect from a real thing — a field, a wave, whatever. A sudden jump whose destination depends on what the experimenter just found out is what you'd expect from a notebook: it changes because the person holding it learned something, not because the world did. Same object, same theory, both behaviours. And the two camps look to me like they're each staring at one regime and generalising from it.

Except — someone in the group pointed out, and I think he's right, that this may prove nothing at all. Spread an ordinary probability over the possible states of a gas in classical physics and it gets carried along deterministically by the equations of motion, and then it jumps when new data comes in. Having both a law of motion and an update rule doesn't mark the quantum object out as special. So my observation may be a dud. Which is fine, but then I want to know what actually does the work in this argument: if the two-kinds-of-change thing isn't load-bearing, where is the real disagreement living?

One more thing I can't place, in case it's the same thing or a different one: the object isn't made of probabilities to begin with. You square it to get them, and its parts can cancel each other out, which probabilities never do. Does that belong to what I noticed above, or is it a separate argument that's been bundled in with it?

So: is there a name for the two-speed behaviour, and for the fact that the two speeds seem to point in opposite directions on the real-or-not question? Is there a name for the move of insisting that both kinds of change are really physics, or that both are really bookkeeping? And is my classical-probability worry a known objection with a known reply? Please don't just hand me a label — I want to know what the thing I've spotted is, what it isn't, where its edges are, and whether it matters here or is a red herring.
