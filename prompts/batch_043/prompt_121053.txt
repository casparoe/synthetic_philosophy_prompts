I TA for a third-year philosophy of mind seminar, and last week we did the badness of pain — the cases where someone reports a sharp burning sensation, localizes it fine, rates the intensity, maybe even winces, but says it just doesn't bother them: no unpleasantness, they'd happily leave the stimulator on. The discussion went in circles and I dont think I can reconstruct why, except that everyone seemed to be using "bothers me" for two different things and no one could say what. So I want to make a one-page handout for next week: a decision tree that walks a student from that one case to a named position on what the badness of pain consists in. Starting commitment: we take the case at face value — the patient isn't lying, confabulating, or misusing words — and branch from there. (We'd already covered the sensory-discriminative versus affective-motivational distinction, so use it, but don't let it be the whole tree.)

I've made up three extra variants that I want used, because I think they're where the views actually come apart, and I'd rather sort people by what they say about these than by any textbook summary. (a) The twins: two asymbolic patients with the same first-person reports, except one reliably presses a button that shuts the stimulator off at some small cost and the other never does and would have to be paid to bother. (b) The mirror case: a patient who calls the sensation agonizing and says they want it to stop more than anything, but shows no withdrawal, no flinching, no autonomic response, and will sit for an hour rather than reach for the off switch. (c) The informed case: a patient with no unpleasantness at all who says "I know this is damaging my tissue, and I very much mind that — please stop." Aim for about seven branch questions and six to nine leaves, each node phrased as something a student with no vocabulary for the debate could answer — no node that's just "is pain intrinsically bad?", and don't make "is the badness in the sensation or in your attitude?" a single node unless you split it, since that's the one we choked on. Build the tree out of the logical space first: which combinations of answers are even available — and then drop the real positions into the slots; where a combination is empty, give me one line on whether that's because it's incoherent, because nobody would want it, or because nobody's got round to it.

Two more requests. Names: give a leaf its settled name if it has one (I've heard evaluativism, imperativism, and something about motivational or desire-based views, but I don't trust my memory of which is which), otherwise label it "unnamed" and describe it in one sentence — the questions matter to me far more than the labels. And flag explicitly any pair of leaves your tree can't separate: my hunch is that the "the sensation itself is bad" family and the "pain represents damage as bad" family both just shrug off plain asymbolia, and that a desire-based view and a command-based view both explain the twins, but tell me if I've got that backwards and say what further case would pry them apart. Also, since I'm going to hand this to students as if it were neutral: mark the two or three branch decisions you're least confident actually do the work I'm asking of them, and tell me what would make you redraw the tree — e.g. if asymbolics turned out to differ in first-person report in a way I've assumed they don't, or if unpleasantness with no sensory character at all turned out to be a real phenomenon rather than something I invented.
