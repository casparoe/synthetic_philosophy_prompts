hey — I'm applying for a nine-month dissertation fellowship (human geography / env humanities, but half the committee are Earth scientists) and I need a two-page proposal out of this mess of an idea.

Where I'm at: everyone treats "when did the Anthropocene start" as a dating puzzle — pick the golden spike, mid-century and the plutonium from bomb testing, or 1610 and the CO2 dip after the Columbian exchange, or the Neolithic and early farming. I think the stalemate is that there are three different jobs a start date is meant to do and they pull apart: (1) future-detectability — a marker some stratigrapher a hundred thousand years from now could read off with no other information; (2) causal attribution — the point from which you can start holding identifiable humans, institutions, or economies responsible; (3) commitment — the point after which the Earth system was already going somewhere new, before anyone on the ground could have noticed. My half-formed claim: no single date does all three well, and each camp in the debate is quietly optimising for one of them while arguing about stratigraphy. That would make "when did it start" not a question with a hidden right answer but three questions wearing one coat — and it reframes "who decides" too: authority should track which job you want done, so the stratigraphic commission has a real but narrow claim (job 1 basically) and close to none over 2 and 3. That matters more now that the formal epoch proposal was voted down in 2024 while the word itself is used more than ever.

The other thing I keep coming back to: the Holocene's own lower boundary was defined off a climate signal in a Greenland ice core, not rock, so the complaint that the Anthropocene has no proper rock record seems to get applied inconsistently to the very unit it would replace.
