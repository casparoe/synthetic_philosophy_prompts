The question (20 marks, meant to take about 35 minutes) is this:

X is a finite set of social states, L is the set of lotteries over X, and there are n individuals plus a planner, all with preferences over L.
(a) State Harsanyi's aggregation theorem for this setting, naming the assumption on individual preferences, the assumption on the planner's preferences, and the Pareto-indifference condition; state the conclusion. [6]
(b) Sketch the argument, saying what each assumption is used for. Then: (i) what does the conclusion become if the Pareto-indifference condition is dropped? (ii) what if the achievable expected-utility-difference vectors do not span all of R^n — say, because the available lotteries only ever move everyone's utility together? [7]
(c) Now suppose individuals' preferences over lotteries violate independence (Allais-style), while the planner's preferences still satisfy the VNM axioms. Does the aggregation argument still deliver a weighted-sum representation? Say exactly where it goes wrong, if it does. [3]
(d) A classmate says: the theorem shows that the planner's utility is a weighted average of individual utilities, where the weights are the rates at which the planner is willing to trade one person's utility against another's, so the theorem hands us interpersonal comparisons of welfare for free; and once you add anonymity across persons all the weights must be equal, so you get average utilitarianism — which is the same conclusion Harsanyi reached with the equiprobability / veil-of-ignorance model. Is that right? [4]

Here is the student's answer, more or less verbatim (I've cleaned up only the handwriting):

(a) Each individual i has preferences over L satisfying the VNM axioms, so i is represented by an affine utility u_i, unique up to positive affine transformations (u_i goes to αu_i + β with α > 0). The planner likewise has VNM preferences, represented by an affine W. Pareto indifference: for any lotteries p and q, if every individual is indifferent (u_i(p) = u_i(q) for all i), then the planner is indifferent (W(p) = W(q)). Harsanyi's theorem: given these assumptions there are real constants a_1, ..., a_n ≥ 0 and a constant b with W(p) = Σ a_i u_i(p) + b for all p. So social welfare is a weighted sum of individual expected utilities. The non-negativity is what makes the result utilitarian in flavour, and it follows straight from Pareto indifference: if some a_i were negative, then a change from q to p that raises i's expected utility while leaving everyone else's exactly as it was would reduce W, and since the others are unaffected, that is just the kind of move Pareto indifference rules out. If we strengthen Pareto indifference to the full strong Pareto condition, the weights become strictly positive.

(b) Work with differences of lotteries and extend u_i and W linearly; each u_i is then a linear functional and W is a linear functional. Pareto indifference says W vanishes on the intersection of the kernels of the u_i. By the standard linear-algebra fact — a linear functional that vanishes on the intersection of the kernels of finitely many linear functionals lies in their span — W lies in the span of the u_i, which gives W = Σ a_i u_i, up to a constant term depending on how you handle the constraint that lottery differences sum to zero; the constant does not matter for the ordering. Each assumption does visible work: VNM for individuals gives us linear u_i at all, VNM for the planner gives a linear W, and Pareto indifference is what ties W to the individuals. (i) Drop Pareto indifference and nothing links W to the individual utilities: W could be any affine functional on L, for instance one that ranks lotteries by the position of the worst-off person, and the utilitarian conclusion just disappears. (ii) If the achievable utility-difference vectors span only a proper subspace of R^n, the weighted-sum representation still exists, but the weight vector is no longer unique — you can add to it anything in the annihilator of the achievable subspace — while the qualitative conclusion is unaffected.

(c) The argument breaks down, but not on the planner's side: W is still affine here. If individuals violate independence they have no expected-utility representation, so there are no affine u_i to aggregate. Individual indifference sets are no longer hyperplanes in the simplex, so the set on which Pareto indifference makes W vanish is not a linear subspace and the span argument cannot be stated. The condition can even become nearly vacuous: someone with generic non-expected-utility preferences may be indifferent between no two distinct lotteries at all, in which case Pareto indifference constrains W not at all.

(d) Broadly right. Because VNM pins each u_i down up to a positive affine transformation, and the theorem pins the weights down up to multiplication by a common positive scalar, the ratios a_i / a_j are determined by the data: they are the planner's marginal rates of substitution between persons, i.e. how much of j's utility the planner will sacrifice for a unit of i's. So the theorem does give us cardinal interpersonal comparisons — not merely that everyone's utility counts, but how much. Add anonymity, so the planner may not care who is who, and the weights cannot differ across persons; hence they are all equal and social welfare is the arithmetic mean of individual utilities (normalize each u_i to run from 0 at that person's worst outcome to 1 at the best, as Harsanyi does, and equal weights are exactly the average). That is the average-utilitarian conclusion, and it is the same one Harsanyi gets from the equiprobability model, where the observer behind the veil treats herself as equally likely to turn out to be anyone — so the theorem plus anonymity really just restates the veil-of-ignorance argument. The one real caveat is the assumption that the planner's W is affine, which some people reject because expected-welfare ranking cannot distinguish giving a benefit to one person for sure from giving everyone an equal chance at it.
