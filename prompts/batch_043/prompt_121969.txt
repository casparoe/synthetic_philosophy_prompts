A physicist friend of mine — genuinely sharp, not a crank, but convinced that Gödel already 'settled it' — puts it like this: any computer is a formal system, so there's some arithmetic truth it can never derive, and we can see that truth, so we are not computers. Last week he used it to wave off the whole question of whether a large language model could be said to understand anything, and I notice I don't have a crisp way of saying what exactly is right or wrong with the move. I end up muttering something about soundness and the second incompleteness theorem and it doesn't land.

What I actually want is a precisificaton of the slogan — the menu of distinct claims it could be making, from the ones that just restate the incompleteness theorem to the ones that would carry a real anti-mechanist or anti-chatbot conclusion. Spare me the reading list; the reading group already has the usual Lucas and Penrose material on the syllabus, I want precision rather than sources. A few places where I think the live issues are, so you know what I'm after: (i) the 'I' in 'I can see that the sentence is true' seems to need to be a fixed, sound, explicitly-describable system, and I might instead be a moving target — something that revises itself, or only converges on the right answer in the limit, the way a trial-and-error learner does, so that there's no single recursively enumerable output set to diagonalise against; (ii) even if I'm not sound, my outputs are still enumerable, so something can be diagonalised out of them, but then what you get isn't guaranteed true and the argument seems to die; (iii) my friend sometimes slides into a conditional version — for any algorithm A, if I were A then I could see A's Gödel sentence holds, so I'm not A, so I'm not any A — and I can't tell whether that dodges the problem of knowing which machine you are or just relocates it; (iv) whatever any of this establishes is about an idealised mathematical capacity, and it's not obvious to me that it touches whatever we care about when we ask whether a chatbot understands anything.

So: give me exactly five numbered readings of the slogan, ordered roughly from most deflationary to most ambitious, each one or two sentences — state the claim precisely enough that I could put it on a problem set, give your verdict on it (platitude / false / substantive and open), and say whether it would actually do the work my friend wants at that dinner table.
