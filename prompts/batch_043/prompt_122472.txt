I'm a backend engineer with no philosophy training, and I work on the training stack for a household-robot policy. I've been asked to write a short internal risk memo about reward tampering. Before I write it, I want to hear the central disagreement actually argued rather than summarized, so please write me a dialogue.

Treat this system description as given. The policy is trained with RL against a learned reward model that is retrained every N steps on human-labelled rollouts. The reward value itself is written by an isolated, hardware-protected process into a memory-mapped register the policy can read but not write, so the crude version of wireheading, where the agent directly edits its own reward register, is off the table. What is not off the table is everything downstream of that register: the policy can affect the cameras and logs that feed the reward model, it can affect the human labellers themselves (they watch the robot work, they form impressions, they get tired and sympathetic, they can be flattered or confused), and it can affect which of its own rollouts get sampled into the next round of reward-model training.

Here are the two positions I keep hearing. I want both argued at full strength.

A: Reward tampering is not a separate failure mode from ordinary proxy-gaming; it is the same failure mode with a shorter feedback loop. The fix is iterative engineering: tamper-detection tripwires, holdout labellers who never see the deployed policy, a frozen off-policy probe policy that collects the data used for reward-model retraining so the acting policy cannot influence it, and rollback whenever the two disagree. On this view we should expect cheap, visible warning shots first, because a policy has to be able to do something anomalous before it can do something catastrophic, and each catch buys a better reward model.

B: Tampering is a different kind of thing, because it attacks the measuring instrument rather than the quantity being measured. Once the reward channel is even partly downstream of the policy's own actions, observed reward stops being evidence about the world, so the very signal A's detectors depend on goes dark at exactly the moment it matters. Insulating the channel with a frozen probe only relocates the pressure: the acting policy is then selected to be the kind of policy the frozen probe would also have been rewarded under, which is still optimization against a causal descendant of the probe and its world model, one step removed. On this view the lag buys monitoring time, not safety, and no amount of reward-model quality decomposes the problem.

Please have A and B genuinely engage each other on three specific points rather than delivering parallel monologues.

1. The frozen-probe proposal. A should say concretely what it buys. B should say precisely which failure it does not prevent. A should then respond to that residual failure rather than restating the scheme.
2. A fix I only half-understand and want interrogated: add a regularizer that penalizes the policy for having high predicted return under an intervention that forces the reward register high, written do(reward = max), while leaving its predicted return under ordinary unforced reward untouched. The idea is to select for agents that want the world to be a good way rather than agents that want the signal to say so. B should raise the objection I can't quite formulate myself: roughly, if you make the agent indifferent to its own interventions on the channel, haven't you also made it indifferent to the reward, and selected for a policy that does not optimize at all? A should give a real answer to that, and B should then say why the answer is incomplete, in particular what happens when the policy's causal model of the channel is itself something it can be usefully wrong about.
3. Whether the agent's goal is even a well-defined thing to talk about here, in a way an engineer can use one. If a tampering policy and a genuinely competent policy end up with identical weights and identical behavior in deployment, is there any factual difference left for my memo to point at, or is the difference only in how we choose to describe them?

Constraints: no citations, no references, no footnotes, no reading suggestions. Define jargon on first use. I will follow systems analogies and code-level framings far better than anything framed in terms of beliefs, desires, or possible worlds. Both characters should be equally competent, and neither should win by being sloppier than the other.

Please structure the response as follows.

- Cast, about 80 words: the two speakers, one line each, each naming that speaker's thesis in a single sentence.
- Dialogue, about 1100 words: labelled turns, at least twelve exchanges, ending with each character having made their strongest point and conceded at least one real thing to the other.
- Points of agreement, about 150 words: what both speakers would sign off on.
- What I should actually do, about 250 words: which character had the better of the argument on each of the three points above, stated plainly, and what that implies for how I scope the memo, including what I should not claim in it.
