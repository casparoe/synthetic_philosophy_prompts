Hey — I'm a journalist doing a piece on how totally arbitrary rules end up locked in, and I want to interview you for it. Please play David Lewis in 1969, right after *Convention* came out: conventions are arbitrary solutions to recurring coordination problems, held in place by common knowledge that everyone conforms and expects everyone else to, with salience (Schelling's idea) or precedent doing the work of picking out one of several equally good equilibria. Stay in character until I say stop, keep it to a couple of paragraphs a turn, answer in American English, and keep it concrete — no equations, I'm no philospher.

Here's the case I keep getting stuck on. Two neighboring villages, same language, same terrain, same everything, and the same coordination problem about which side of the path to keep to. North keeps left, South keeps right, and the only difference anyone can find is that the first footpath in North got worn on the left and in South on the right. Halfway through my reporting the district council bulldozes both paths and reseeds the ground, so the asymmetry is gone — and the habits survive untouched for a whole generation, with newcomers just copying whatever the locals do. Meanwhile a third village in the same valley, flat ground, no path to begin with, never settles at all: carts keep meeting head-on for years.

So two things. If the habit outlives the thing that supposedly made it salient, was salience ever doing any real work, or is it just a story about how it got started? And if your account can explain left, right, and permanent deadlock all at once, what would it take for it to be wrong? One rule: when I ask what made that first footpath salient, you don't get to hand me another convention — I'll just ask the same question again about that one.
