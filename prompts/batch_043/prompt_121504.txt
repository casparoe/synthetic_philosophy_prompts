I'm in the second year of a joint philosophy and psychology degree, and I'm trying to settle on an essay topic for a module on the metaphysics of consciousness before I book my tutorial slot, so I'd be very grateful for a careful second opinion. We spent last week's seminar on Dennett's attack on what he calls Cartesian materialism, which I've written up as the view that there is somewhere in the brain where the results of distributed processing arrive and are thereby presented to an inner observer, and that being presented there is what it is for something to be conscious. Our handout adds that this is essentially Descartes's own position minus the immaterial soul: he needed a place where mind meets machine, and he settled on the pineal gland. As I understand the argument against it, the theatre picture is simply incoherent: if consciousness consists in a show being put on for an audience, then the audience's appreciation of that show must itself be a further piece of consciousness, needing its own theatre, and so on through a regress of inner observers each of which has to be conscious of the one before. Drop the audience, let the parallel distributed processes be the whole story, and you have the multiple drafts model.

My problem is that I cannot see how the replacement avoids being a theatre under a new name, and my seminar tutor thinks it cannot. If no draft is privileged, then it seems either that all of them are conscious, which is absurd, or that none are; so Dennett must after all be singling out some drafts as the conscious ones, which I take to be why he talks about certain contents achieving fame in the brain and winning the competition for control of behaviour and memory. But that looks like a stream of consciousness with the seating rearranged: one special sequence of winning drafts, present someplace, being the ones that count. My tutor's line is that this is the honest reading and that where it leads is eliminativism: if you really give up the theatre, you give up the idea that anyone is conscious of anything, and Dennett's reluctance to grant a determinate moment at which a stimulus becomes conscious is just that conclusion in a timid form. I find this hard to accept, but I can't locate the mistake, so I've been trying to construct a middle position instead. Call it the settling window: a draft's content counts as conscious at a time only if it would in fact remain available to the ordinary range of later probes, such as verbal report, delayed matching and later recollection, for at least a short interval afterwards, so that what makes a content conscious is its staying power in the economy of the brain rather than its arrival at any place. That seems to yield determinate answers about which drafts are conscious without needing an audience. But I now worry that it quietly reinstates the very thing Dennett was ruling out: at the close of the window the content either is or is not conscious, and there is some last moment at which it becomes so, which is a finish line again.

So I'd like to know whether the settling window is a legitimate way of keeping consciousness real without a theatre, or whether it collapses back into the picture Dennett rejects. And is my tutor right that rejecting it as well leaves eliminativism as the only coherent version of the view, or is there a form of realism about consciousness that survives giving up both the inner audience and any determinate time at which a draft becomes the conscious one? I'd also be glad to be told if the way I've set the problem up is itself confused. Please answer in British English.
