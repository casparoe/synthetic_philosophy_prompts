I am an undergraduate in an upper-level seminar on Confucian role ethics, and I am trying to get clear about one short exchange in the Analects that we are reading in the original. The passage appears in the "Zi Lu" book (Book 13) of the received text. In the original it reads:

葉公語孔子曰：「吾黨有直躬者，其父攘羊，而子證之。」孔子曰：「吾黨之直者異於是，父爲子隱，子爲父隱，直在其中矣。」

A rough working paraphrase would be: the Duke of She tells Confucius that in his community there is an upright person, and when the father made off with a sheep the son bore witness against him; Confucius answers that in his own community the upright are different, because a father conceals for a son and a son conceals for a father, and uprightness is in that.

I am not looking for background on the text or a survey of interpretations; I need help with a specific interpretive crux, and I want to test a distinction I introduced in my reading response. Following my notes, call the first reading "role-constitutive": on this view, the content of 直 in a father-son relationship is partly built out of the role-bound response of concealment, so the son who testifies does not merely express 直 badly but fails to have the virtue at all in that relational setting. Call the second reading "role-expressive": on this view, 直 has a single non-relational core (roughly, a forthrightness or absence of deception), and the father-son relationship changes only how that core is properly expressed; thus the testifying son may still have the core disposition, but he manifests it in a way that ignores the relational grammar that should shape its expression.

My question is how to understand the closing words 直在其中矣 in a way that decides between these two readings. Does the "therein" locate uprightness inside the act of mutual concealment itself, so that concealment is partly constitutive of 直 and the Duke of She's "upright person" is implicitly denied the virtue? Or does it locate uprightness inside the larger role-governed relationship, so that the concealment is the proper expression of a virtue the testifying son could in principle share, but which he expresses without the required role-sensitivity? If the text's own logic does not force a choice, what would be the most important consequence for a role-ethics reading if one opted for the role-constitutive reading rather than the role-expressive one? I would especially like to know whether my distinction collapses: for example, if 直 is already a thick, role-inflected virtue-term, then a role-expressive reading may make the Duke of She's example too easily dismissible, whereas if 直 has a thinner core of "straightness," a role-constitutive reading may be hard to state without circularity. Please walk me through how the grammar and the logic of the exchange bear on this, rather than through a list of scholars or secondary sources.
