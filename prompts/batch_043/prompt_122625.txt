I'm a philosophy PhD student — metaphysics mostly, not phil maths, which is why I'm hazy here — and over the break I read one of those popular books on infinity (a present, I won't name it). Its chapter on Hilbert's Hotel says more or less the following, and I want to know how much of it to keep:

"What makes a collection infinite, on Cantor's definition, is that it can be put into one-to-one correspondence with a proper part of itself — the evens with the naturals, say — and that is why the old principle that the whole is bigger than the part has to go. Hilbert's Hotel is the illustration: rooms numbered 1, 2, 3 and so on, every room taken, and the manager can still fit in a new guest by moving everyone up one room, or fit in a coachload of infinitely many by doubling everyone's room number. But that isn't a curiosity, it's a reductio: a hotel that is full and yet has rooms going spare is a contradiction, so an actually infinite collection can't exist — which is why mathematicians before Cantor only ever allowed potential infinities, and why finitists say there are no infinite sets. Cantor's diagonal argument then shows that the infinities we can talk about come in different sizes: the reals can't be paired off with the naturals, so there are more of them. And since you can always add one more room to any hotel, there's no biggest infinity either."

Then there's a bit I've not seen anywhere else. The book imagines an annexe, the "Rational Guest House", rooms labelled by every positive rational, also full, and says the doubling trick fails there — doubling sends each rational to another rational and frees nothing — and that this shows Cantor's pairing criterion can't really be a criterion of size, because the naturals and the rationals come out the same size even though the guest house obviously has more rooms crammed in between any two of them.

Two things I'm inclined to believe going in. First, I'm pretty sure Hilbert was on Cantor's side — didn't he say something about not letting anyone drive us out of Cantor's paradise, or am I thinking of someone else? So using "his" hotel as a reductio of the actual infinite already smells wrong to me, but I can't say why beyond that. Second, a friend in the maths department says "finitist" gets used for about three different positions and the book is almost certainly sliding between them.

So: go through it and tell me what's right, what's a harmless simplification, and what someone who actually works on this would object to. Specifically — is "full and yet able to take more" a contradiction or just a surprising consequence, and whatever it shows, does it transfer to the argument the book makes at the end, that an infinite past is impossible because you'd have had to get through infinitely many days to reach today? Does the no-biggest-infinity claim really follow from adding one room? Is the rational annexe as clever as it thinks it is — could the manager not accommodate the new guest some other way, and if so does that undermine the moral the book draws? And what would an actual finitist, and an actual Cantorian, each want to take back from the whole paragraph? I'd definately rather have the distinctions than the verdict.
