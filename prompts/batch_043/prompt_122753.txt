I've been reading LessWrong and the adjacent decision-theory forums for something like fifteen years, and I've never seen anyone press on the following feature of Newcomb's problem: the case is always posed with a *single* reliability number, and a reliability number is a statistic over a population. Populations can be partitioned. Below is a draft of a post I want to put up; I'd like comments on it before I do. Everything you need is in the draft — I'm not asking for sources or a reading list, just for the argument.

---

**Newcomb's problem has a hidden parameter: the partition**

Standard framing. An opaque box contains $1,000,000 iff a highly reliable predictor predicted that you would take only it; a transparent box contains $1,000; you may take one or both. Nearly every presentation I've seen supplies one number for the predictor's reliability — 90%, 99%, "never known to be wrong" — and then treats that number as a property of the predictor *simpliciter*. But a figure like that is aggregated over some population, and once you let the predictor's accuracy vary across sub-populations, and let the agent know the conditional statistics, several things I had been treating as fixed come loose.

**Case 1 — the stratified predictor.** Everything is as usual, except that a truthful table is posted in the room. Among agents who choose within twenty seconds ("fast"), the predictor has been right 99% of the time. Among agents who stay longer than twenty minutes ("slow"), it has been right 12% of the time. Aggregate accuracy is still high, because the fast population is enormous. You have now been in the room for twenty-five minutes.

Two things follow. Arithmetic first: in the slow cell, one-boxing is worth 0.12 × $1,000,000 ≈ $120,000, and two-boxing is worth $1,000 + 0.88 × $1,000,000 ≈ $881,000. Two-boxing wins by a wide margin, and the usual break-even accuracy — which I make out at a shade over 50% — is not close. Second, and more interesting: the standard way of defusing *I'll just outsmart the predictor* has always been the stipulation that the predictor is reliable about *you*, full stop. In this cell it is not. Conditional on being slow, you are very likely to be mispredicted, and you did not have to do anything clever to get there; you merely took a long time. Whatever made the original case feel like a trap has drained out of it. So either the stratified case is not really a Newcomb case — in which case I want to know which ingredient is missing — or the intuitive force of the original was never carried by the causal structure alone.

**Case 2 — the pill.** Before you enter, you may buy a pill for $10,000 that reliably turns you into a fast, impulsive decider. Stipulate that it changes only the speed and style of your deliberation, not your values and not your disposition to one-box or two-box. Fast agents face the 99% predictor. So: pill plus one-box is worth 0.99 × $1,000,000 − $10,000 ≈ $980,000, against $881,000 for the best available option in the slow cell. Buying the pill looks like buying $1,000,000 for roughly a hundred thousand dollars, i.e. like choosing to become *predictable* — the exact mirror image of the usual lesson that you cannot choose to be unpredictable. My worry is that this is too easy, and that the reason it is too easy is that I have not said whether the prediction about you was generated by a simulation of pre-pill you or of post-pill you. If the predictor simulated pre-pill you, then pill-takers are a new sub-population about whom no accuracy claim has been made, and the accuracy stipulation itself seems to function as a consistency constraint on which self-modifications can so much as be on offer. I think that is a real point and I am not sure I can make it precise.

**Case 3 — the announcement.** Same boxes, same predictor, same physical situation; the organizer either shows you the stratified table or shows you only the aggregate figure. The boxes do not care what you have read. Yet the evidential recommendation flips with the announcement, and it flips toward a difference of roughly three quarters of a million dollars. Is that a reductio of the evidential approach, or merely a reminder that it is a theory about what to do given an information state, so that *the thing to do* was never well defined independently of the agent's evidence?

**Case 4 — the demographic predictor.** The predictor simulates nobody. It runs a cheap model: predict *one-box* for everyone with trait T, *two-box* for everyone without. 99% of T-agents one-box; you have T. But you have excellent evidence about your own decision procedure, and it says you are an outlier within that class: you have already settled on two-boxing, and nothing in the predictor's model tracks whatever makes you different. Here the correlation is a sampling correlation over an observable cell, not a logical or simulational link to your algorithm. It seems to me plainly right to take both boxes and collect $1,001,000 — and yet this is the very move that the standard stipulation is engineered to rule out.

**The thesis I want to defend.** The dialectical force of the original problem is carried by an *admissibility* stipulation: that there is no finer partition — no admissible cell — in which the correlation between your act and the prediction breaks down. That stipulation is almost never made explicit and never argued for; it is smuggled in by the phrase *a predictor who is reliable about you*. My four cases are the original case with the stipulation locally withdrawn, one ingredient at a time. If that is right, the live question is not *one-box or two-box?* but *which partitions are admissible, and why?* — and the decision theories on offer differ less in their answers to the first question than in whether they have anything to say about the second. CDT is invariant across all four cases, which I used to count as a point in its favour and now count as a cost, since invariance is only a virtue if at least one of these cases retains the pull of the original. EDT varies with the announcement in Case 3. FDT has its best hour in Case 4, where algorithmic conditioning delivers the right answer and the headline statistic is irrelevant; it is Case 2 where I do not know what it says.

What I would like from you, in roughly 900 words, most important point first, and without summarizing the draft back to me:

1. **Accuracy of the mechanics.** My break-even figure of ~50% assumes the predictor's errors are symmetric. Give me the general condition when sensitivity and specificity come apart, and tell me whether a predictor can be *worse* than chance in aggregate and still make one-boxing the better bet. I have a suspicion that it can; if it can, that is a sharper version of my opening point than the partition framing, and I should lead with it instead.
2. **Do the cases need mechanisms?** I have stipulated error rates without saying what produces them. I suspect the mechanism matters a great deal — a predictor that inverts its reading of slow agents, one whose simulations degrade with deliberation time, and one that uses a demographic proxy are three different causal structures — and if it does, my four cases are not four variants of one phenomenon and I should reorganize around the causal structures rather than around the statistics. Say which, and say whether a mechanism-free accuracy stipulation is even legitimate here.
3. **Is the admissibility thesis doing any work,** or is it just a portentous redescription of the fact that the algorithmic approaches condition on the causal graph and the evidential ones condition on whatever you happen to know? My fear is the latter, and that the post has no thesis, only four anecdotes.
4. **Originality, in your own terms.** Does each case collapse into a move that is already stock in this discourse? I do not want citations or a reading list; I want to know, described from scratch, whether what I have written is a genuinely new configuration of the problem or an old one wearing a hat. If Case 2 just is the smoking-lesion structure with a self-modification bolted on, tell me the shortest route to seeing that, and I will cut it.
5. **Editing.** Which case would you cut, which would you lead with, and is there a compact formalization — a small table of conditional probabilities — that makes the whole thing sharper than my prose manages? I would like to land under 1,000 words including it.
6. **The hostile comment.** What is the strongest thing a committed two-boxer says in the comments that I have not anticipated and answered?
