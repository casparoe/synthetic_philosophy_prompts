I'm a senior at my school and I run the ethics club. In two weeks I have to lead a forty-minute session on Augustine's account of lying for about a dozen students. Almost none of them will have heard of him, they'll arrive assuming that the ethics of lying is obviously a matter of consequences — a lie is wrong when it hurts someone and fine when it helps — and at least two of them will say within the first five minutes that the whole question is just religion dressed up as philosophy. I've read enough to know that Augustine isn't doing consequentialism: he defines lying as saying something false with the will to deceive, holds that the wrong lies in the speaker's will rather than in the damage done, and still concludes that no lie may be told even to save a life. Don't explain the view to me. Tell me how to build the explanation.

What I need is the architecture of the session:

First, the order. What has to be in place before "will to deceive" can do any work in the argument, and what breaks if I open with the verdict — "never lie, not even to save someone" — instead of with the definition? My guess is that the definition has to land before the absolutism can look like anything other than stubbornness, but I want the dependency chain spelled out: which idea cannot be understood until which other idea is already on the board.

Second, the carrying example. My instinct is that the murderer at the door is the wrong choice: it's the case they've all met already, it turns the session into a referendum on whether Augustine would let a person die, and it makes the interesting structure — the gap between what is said, what is believed, and what is intended — invisible. If not that, what case should carry the whole forty minutes, and what does it have to contain and conspicuously lack in order for the definition and the verdict to both look forced by it?

Third, the silence move. On his account you may refuse to answer or conceal the truth where you may not lie, and there's a related trick of saying something strictly true that you know will be taken falsely. In my experience that either looks like a loophole or collapses into lying. Where in the sequence does it belong, and does presenting it early do damage?

Fourth, the misconceptions, in the order they'll actually form, with the point in the sequence where each has to be headed off rather than merely corrected at the end. The ones I can anticipate: that all lies are equally serious for him — someone told me he sorts lies into kinds by gravity, which surprised me, since I thought he forbade the lot; that the falsity of the sentence is what makes a statement a lie, so a true sentence told in order to mislead comes out clean; that his reason is really "lying erodes trust," i.e. that he's a consequentialist or just a Kantian in late-antique dress; and that a hearer who has in some sense agreed to be deceived takes the utterance out of the category entirely. Tell me which of these my classmates will genuinely arrive at and which I'm inventing.

Fifth, two stress cases I want to place somewhere, and I don't want to use either as a refutation if it isn't one. A friend who texts in advance, "if my parents ask where I am tonight, lie for me": here the person deceived has authorized the deception beforehand, so what exactly is the will to deceive supposed to be a will against? And an AI voice clone of a real person saying something she never said, posted publicly: no one speaks, but someone arranges for an audience to be deceived. Does an account built on the speaker's will have resources here, or does the case only show what the account was built for? Where in the hour do these go — as illustrations, as tests, or not at all?

Finally, one question I can put to the group at the end that would tell me they've taken the concept rather than just the verdict, and an honest statement of what I should say out loud I'm not going to settle in forty minutes.
