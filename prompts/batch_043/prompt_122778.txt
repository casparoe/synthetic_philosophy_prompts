I am writing the technical appendix for a grant proposal and need help with one objection I expect from the review panel.

The proposal is a welfare measurement pipeline. A large grocery-scanner panel gives us, for each household, several hundred observed purchases made at different posted prices. For each household we test the observed choices for consistency with the generalized axiom of revealed preference, discard or lightly smooth the households that fail, recover a well-behaved utility function from the ones that pass, and then collapse that function into a single wellbeing score per household. To make scores comparable across households we use two devices. The first is anchoring: every recovered function is rescaled so that it takes the value 0 on a fixed bare-subsistence bundle and 100 on a fixed comfort bundle, both specified in the same commodity space. The second is monetization: each score is converted into dollars of equivalent variation at one common set of baseline prices, so the final numbers are denominated in a unit that the panel and the agency funding us already understand. The dollar scores are then averaged within a region and entered into a cost-benefit scorecard that ranks transport and housing subsidies against each other.

The panel is two applied microeconomists, one behavioral economist, and one philosopher of economics. The philosopher is the one I am worried about. My understanding of the history is that the point of the revealed-preference tradition beginning with Samuelson's 1938 note in *Economica* was to purge choice theory of unobservable mental residue by defining utility in terms of observed choice. But if that is right, then the utility my pipeline recovers is just a relabeling of the choices I fed into it, and it cannot do any independent work in justifying a subsidy ranking. So my question is: what is the strongest reply to the charge that revealed-preference utility is empty or circular, and how much of that charge should I concede in the appendix rather than fight?

Two requests about form and one about substance. Please write in American English, and write so that the reply could go almost directly into a four-page appendix: no citations, no footnotes, no suggested reading, and no appeal to anyone's authority. Substantively, I would also like to know whether the monetization step is doing any work that the anchoring step is not already doing, or whether the two are the same move in different clothing. And if the objection I have described is not the one the philosopher will actually make, I would much rather be told that than be handed a defense of a position nobody is attacking.
