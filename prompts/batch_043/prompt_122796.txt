Bit of an odd one. I'm not a polisci person, I've just been reading about the small armed-neutral countries in Europe, and I keep noticing a package of features that seem to travel together. I can't find a word for the package as opposed to the bits.

The setup: an army that by ordinary standards is bad. It can't deploy anywhere, its equipment is short-ranged, most of it is reservists who go back to day jobs, and in at least one case the soldiers used to keep the rifle at home. But the point of it doesn't seem to be fighting in the normal sense. The point is that anyone who attacks ends up paying far more than the place is worth, and that everyone else can see the army could never be used against them. So the weakness is doing two jobs at once: it makes the threat to an invader work, and it makes the promise to everyone else work. And what bugs me is that the force looks like a waste of money by every usual metric for judging armies, and I think that's because of that, not in spite of it.

What I really want a word for is an extra feature I think I'm seeing. It doesn't seem to be enough that the country currently can't attack. It seems to matter that getting the ability back would take years and would be a domestic political fight: new conscription law, new procurement, bases that were closed and built over. A country that merely chooses not to project power, and could start next year, doesn't seem to get the second benefit. I've been writing "reversal cost" in my notes, which I doubt is the term.

So: is there an established concept for the posture as a whole? "Deterrence by denial" doesn't feel right, since denial is about being able to beat the attack, and here part of the point is that you can't do much beyond your own border. And is the reversal-cost thing a genuinely separate variable, or already folded into whatever the first concept is? Don't just drop a label on me — I want the nearby concepts, how each differs from what I described, and where the edges are. Does it matter whether the incapacity is technical (no transport aircraft) versus legal (can't deploy without some painful political process) versus just customary? Take two cases: one state is militarily feeble today but could buy the kit in a year; another has the kit in storage but has dismantled the legal and institutional basis for using it. My guess is only the second gets the reassurance, even though right now the first is the weaker one. Is that right, and if so what's doing the work?
