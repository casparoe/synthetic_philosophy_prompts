My friend and I spent a good part of last night arguing about aesthetic testimony, and I would like a verdict on the arguments rather than on the two of us. I have reconstructed the exchange below as six moves, stated at what I take to be each side's strongest, and I have deliberately not marked which lines were mine — I can now state his side better than he did, and I would rather you judged the reasoning.

The case that started it: I have never actually heard a certain record — a forty-seven-minute live album, call it *Nightjar* — but I have read one long, meticulous review of it by a critic whose judgments have matched my own on something like two hundred albums we have both heard. In conversation I said that *Nightjar* is a masterpiece. My friend said I was not in a position to say that.

1. You don't know that it is a masterpiece. You know that she thinks it is, and you have excellent evidence that her thinking so is a reliable guide. But a verdict about an artwork's value is answerable to what it is like to experience that particular work, and you have never had that experience. If someone challenged you, everything you could offer in defense would terminate in her say-so.

2. Two replies. First, I can do more than cite her: the review gave reasons — about how the rhythm section withholds resolution, about the sequencing of the two long improvisations — and I can rehearse them and to some degree assess them myself. Second, deference delivers knowledge everywhere else. I know when the battle was fought though I was not there; I know the soup is salty though I have not tasted it. If "it depends on experience" were disqualifying, I could never learn from anyone that a dish is good, that a trail is brutal, or that a joke is funny.

3. The saltiness case is not the right comparison: saltiness is a standing property that shows up in experience, and of course you can have testimonial knowledge of it. My claim is not really about classification. It is about the difference between a merely correct verdict and a genuine one — one that is *yours*, that you can use to direct your own attention, to disagree with someone who has heard the record, to decide what to listen to next, to teach someone else what to listen for. A borrowed verdict cannot do any of that work. Think of reading a proof versus working through it: you may come away knowing that the theorem is true, but not with understanding — and, tellingly, with a readiness to drop the belief at the first contrary whisper, which is not at all how you hold the records you have actually lived with.

4. The proof analogy cuts against you: working through a proof is a matter of understanding, not of being acquainted with an object, so you have quietly changed the subject. And now consider my Oracle. Imagine something that has never heard a note and never will, but which predicts with 99 percent accuracy the verdict that a panel of a thousand careful listeners would converge on after ten hearings of a given work — together with the reasons they would give for it. If I defer to the Oracle, do I know? If yes, then your thesis that the recipient must be acquainted is false: my acquaintance was never doing any work. If no, then your objection is really about the *source* — it must exercise judgment, not merely track verdicts reliably. But that is a different thesis from the one you argued for. You argued that aesthetic judgment is essentially experiential. You did not argue that whoever transmits an aesthetic judgment to me must themselves have had the experiences.

5. Fair enough — I will split the claim into a recipient-side requirement and a source-side requirement, and I concede that the Oracle refutes the crude version of the first: reliable, reason-giving, aggregated testimony can certainly put you in a position to classify a work correctly. But two things survive. First, a weaker recipient-side point: until you have heard it you should hold the belief with a distinctive kind of hedging — act on it, recommend it, assert it cautiously — but not rank it alongside the records you have lived with, because you cannot tell when this case is the one that differs. Second, the Oracle's output is not testimony at all; it is evidence, a barometer reading. A human witness can be wrong for reasons and can answer for herself; the Oracle only tracks. So the claim that survives is this: in the aesthetic case what testimony transmits is not a verdict as such but someone's exercise of judgment, and you can only take that up by exercising your own — which is to say, by listening.

6. If the Oracle's output is mere evidence, then so is the critic's essay in the ordinary case, where the only grip I have on her competence is that two-hundred-album track record — which is a statistic. The line between "source" and "evidence" is then psychological, not epistemic. And the "distinctive hedging" is ad hoc. Either my evidence supports the verdict strongly enough to act on, recommend, and assert, in which case refusing to call it knowledge is a stipulation; or it does not, and then you owe me an account of what the missing ingredient is — and by your own admission it is not acquaintance with the work, since the Oracle shows that acquaintance is dispensable.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: rate each of the six from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification apiece, and say at each point whether the move answers the objection it was meant to answer or slides past it. Then give an overall verdict on three questions — (a) does the person who has only read the review know that the album is a masterpiece; (b) does the Oracle case move the argument, and if so, in which direction and for which of the two sides; (c) what, if anything, are both parties missing — a distinction neither drew, a distinction one of them drew but overworked, or a structurally similar debate elsewhere that would sharpen what is really at issue. Please do not ask me which moves were mine; please write in American English; and please keep the whole answer to roughly eight hundred words.
