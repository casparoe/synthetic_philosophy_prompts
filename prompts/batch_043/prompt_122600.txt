So I teach adults to weld at a community shop, evenings, and I've been going around in circles on something for a couple of years. My brother-in-law finally told me the thing has a name — knowing how vs. knowing that — and then he said something I can't shake: he says knowing how is really just knowing a bunch of facts, and I said that's obviously wrong, and then I couldn't say why. I have zero academic background in anything, I've just got about forty students a year and I've noticed they split in ways I can't fit under one rule. So here are my verdicts, going on gut, and I want to know if there's a principle behind them.

My uncle drives a stick shift, forty years, never stalls, and he could not tell you what a clutch does if you paid him. Knows how, doesn't know that. Fine, obvious case.

A kid who's watched every welding video there is, can tell you the amperage for 1/8 rod, can recite the whole sequence, has never struck an arc. Knows that, does not know how. Also obvious.

Guy who works at the plant and can run the setup perfectly by following the laminated checklist, but if anything is off — wrong fit-up, a gap, a breeze — he stops dead and waits for a supervisor. I want to say he knows how, but it feels like a thinner version than my uncle's.

My old climbing instructor tied a figure-eight one-handed in the dark and told students to put the tail "through the little loop," and there are three little loops. Still obviously knows how. His description is just wrong.

A concrete guy I know has a totally bogus story about why he adds the water the way he does — it's about the weather, he says, and it isn't — but he's right every time. Knows how, his explanation is nonsense.

One of my better students can lay a beautiful bead with his jig and his auto-darkening hood, and take either one away and he's hopeless. I still say he knows how, but half the shop says the jig knows how, not him.

The rusty case: a woman who rode bikes at twelve, didn't touch one for thirty years, wobbles for thirty seconds and then it's back. I want to say she knew how the whole time, even during the wobbling. Same with a piano piece my hands can still find that I couldn't tell you one note of.

The one that makes me think my brother-in-law is just wrong: a tennis player who hits a fine backhand in a rally and completely falls apart the second he thinks about his grip, and a runner whose stride goes to hell when someone asks how her foot lands. If knowing how were facts in the head, adding the facts shouldn't wreck it.

And the one I keep tripping on: a pitching coach with a destroyed shoulder. Can't throw. Can watch a kid for two minutes and fix them, and they get better. I want to say he knows how to pitch — more than the video kid does, even though neither one of them can do it.

My tentative rule is something like: you know how to do X if (a) you can land it when conditions shift a bit, not just in the exact setup you learned it in, and (b) you could get a beginner closer to being able to do it, by showing rather than by handing over a list. I can see right away this is in trouble — the checklist guy flunks (a) and I still want to count him, and my gut says my jig student (case six) is a weaker case than the checklist guy even though on paper they're the same shape, and don't tell me they're the same case, I know they look identical, but one feels like knowing and one feels like the equipment knows.

So: test my rule against these, tell me exactly where it breaks, and repair it into something precise — I want a principle where I can point at each clause and say which case earned it. Then use it on two I haven't settled. First, a student who can weld fine while I'm standing next to him talking him through it, but falls apart if I walk away — I'm not touching anything, he's doing it, but only with me in the room. Second, the very first time I ran a bead that came out right: did I know how before I did it, or did I aquire it in the doing?

Take an actual position, please — don't lay out three candidate principles and leave me to pick; tell me which one you think is right and why, tell me which of my verdicts you'd overturn if you have to, and be honest about how confident you are and what would change your mind. Its fine to tell me I'm being inconsistent, just tell me which way to resolve it. And write it in plain American English, real sentences, no jargon I'd have to look up — if there's a technical term you need, explain it in the same breath.
