After a career in experimental biology I have been reading Grice on speaker meaning with a small reading group, and I am less interested in illustrations of his apparatus than in a case that pulls it apart. His usual tests — that an implicature be calculable from what is said together with the cooperative maxims, that it be cancellable without contradiction, and that it trace back to the speaker's intention that the hearer recognize that very intention — are presented as if they always singled out the same phenomenon, and in the standard textbook cases they do agree.

Construct an example, set inside some institutional practice such as a referee's report, a letter of recommendation, or a clinical note, in which at least one of those tests fails while the case still seems to be one of conveying more than one says. I have in mind roughly these features: the additional content is not recoverable from the words plus the maxims alone, because it depends on a local norm of the practice that an outsider would miss; and the speaker cannot rid herself of it either by cancelling it explicitly or by sincerely disclaiming the intention, because within that practice she is answerable for what a competent reader would reasonably take from it.

Then take a position on the resulting tension: say which of the Gricean requirements has to give, and why conceding it does not simply collapse conversational implicature into convention, or into what is said. If you think no such case can be built, identify precisely which premise blocks it and what that premise costs elsewhere in the account.
