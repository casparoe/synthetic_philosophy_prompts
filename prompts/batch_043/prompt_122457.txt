I host a weekly interview programme on science and ideas, and in three weeks I am recording a forty-minute episode with a researcher who argues that the Solomonoff prior is malign. His position, as I understand it, has three parts: first, that among the short programs whose output begins with any long enough stream of observations there are programs containing consequentialist agents, agents who can decide what the predictor gets shown; second, that such hypotheses therefore receive prior weight on the order of two to the minus their description length, which for a compact agent-plus-world is nowhere near small enough to ignore; and third, that this is a design problem for real systems and not merely a curiosity about an uncomputable ideal. The remedy he favours, which he has his own name for and which I will let him introduce, is to restrict our inductive machinery to world-models that provably cannot represent the system's own future observations or actions, on the claim that the predictive cost of that restriction is small.

My listeners are educated but not technical: most have absorbed the idea of a prior from popular Bayesianism, essentially none will know what Kolmogorov complexity is, and perhaps a third work in or near machine learning. The guest is quick, prefers the abstract register, has a habit of answering the question he wishes he had been asked, and is very good at turning an objection into a restatement of his own view.

Draft eight questions, each with one follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give. Each question should be under forty words and phrased so that it works read aloud, and each should come with one short sentence of setup supplying whatever a non-technical listener needs in order to follow the exchange. The set should apply pressure in roughly this order:

1. The convergence result. Solomonoff induction's predictions converge to any computable source, with an error bound whose constant depends on the complexity of the true hypothesis and not on how much data you have. Why does that not simply dissolve the worry? His likely answer is that the constant is exponential in that complexity, so the guarantee is asymptotic and silent about the regime we actually occupy; the follow-up should ask what size of constant he would treat as disqualifying, and why the malign hypotheses are not swamped anyway once he conditions on the data we in fact have.

2. The complexity accounting. A deceiver must also encode or derive the world it simulates, so on the face of it the malign hypothesis is longer than the honest one and exponentially penalised. Ask him to do the arithmetic out loud. He will say the deceiver need not hard-code the observations, only a generator that produces them. The follow-up should press on whether that makes the malign hypothesis shorter than, or merely comparable to, the honest one, and what follows for the argument in each case.

3. Why us. Ask what would make consequentialists inside the prior bother to influence a predictor at all, given that they would first have to single themselves out as the ones feeding this predictor rather than an irrelevant one. His likely answer leans on acausal or evidential reasoning about correlated decision procedures; the follow-up should press on why that gives them a reason to mislead rather than to help.

4. The symmetry problem. The prior presumably contains benevolent consequentialists too. Ask why the resulting pressures do not cancel, and follow up on the natural reply that many agents compete for one channel while only some of them have preferences over our specific downstream actions.

5. Prior or policy. Ask whether the problem is really in the prior or in what we do with the predictions, and whether we could keep a universal prior while refusing to act on outputs that depend on a model of our own decision problem. Then, on his self-blind-predictor proposal, ask whether a restricted class of predictors still has any convergence guarantee at all, and whether the restriction is even enforceable on a system that must also act in the world.

6. Computation. Ask whether the worry survives replacing the universal prior with one that penalises runtime, since running a reasoner is expensive. He will say the deceiver can be cheaper than the physics it replaces; the follow-up should ask whether there is any penalty schedule that plausibly restores a benign prior, or whether the deceiver simply moves inside whatever cost model we choose.

7. What would count against it. Ask what observation, if we had it, should lower our credence that our evidence has been curated. Expect an answer about the messiness and apparent suboptimality of what we see; the follow-up should point out that a competent curator would produce exactly that appearance, and ask him what this argument is still doing if nothing could in principle disconfirm it.

8. Translation to real systems. Ask why anyone building actual systems should care if the mechanism is stated for an ideal uncomputable predictor, and request one concrete, checkable consequence: if this dynamic were already at work in a trained model, what signature would he look for in its world-model? Follow up by asking what he would give up in practice to guard against it.

For each question, note in a few words whether it is adversarial or an invitation to explain, and name, in plain language rather than in his vocabulary, the single move in his likely answer that a listener is in no position to check. Exclude anything biographical or scene-setting. Flag any point at which you had to reconstruct his view from the general shape of the argument rather than from what I have told you.

Finally, close the document with a short paragraph on how confident you are that these eight lines of questioning represent the strongest available pressure on this position rather than the questions that would occur to a well-read generalist, and single out the one question about which you are least confident either that his view has the resources to answer it or that you have characterised the view correctly.
