Writing the theory chapter of my dissertation (it's on credential inflation) and I want someone to kick the tyres on my position before my commitee does. Here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) Core commitment: a signal is informative at equilibrium because cost is differential — signalling at a given level costs a low-quality type more than it's worth to them, while for a high-quality type it isn't. I take the strategic/optimality models (Grafen-style, where signal level is chosen and honesty falls out of the shape of the cost and benefit curves) as the actual formal backbone, not the verbal version.

(2) But I also think Zahavi was basically right and the deflationary reading is too quick: in the cases I care about the signal really does burn something — time, calories, risk, money — and that burden is what's holding it up. I want to say the waste is load-bearing, not a side effect.

(3) I apply all this to humans: degrees, religious ritual, conspicuous consumption, public moral outrage. On degrees specifically I hold the strong version — much of the return to a degree is sorting, not skill, so the sheepskin is largely positional waste that reorders people rather than making them productive.

(4) Methodologically I dont want to be telling just-so stories, so I require a costly-signalling explanation to earn its keep: it has to say who pays more and by how much, and it has to predict that if you intervene to make the signal cheap you destroy the information it was carrying (and that people will resist the intervention, half-sensing this).

(5) Related: for the ritual-type cases I think that when you succeed in making one signal cheap, the signalling doesn't collapse — it relocates to some other dimension of cost. I call this cost migration and I think it's general.

(6) Then I turn around and argue for policy: drop degree requirements, hire on work samples and short structured assessments, on the grounds that we can throw away most of the waste without employers losing what they were actually getting out of the credential, because the cheap direct measures pick up the same underlying traits.

(7) I'm not embarrassed by signals that cost almost nothing to produce — a uniform, a wedding ring, a badge, a blue checkmark, a reputation score. Honest cheap signals seem common and unproblematic to me.

(8) I also file "honest because of a physical or structural constraint" under costly signalling: a deep voice is constrained by testosterone, four years of turning up is constrained by conscientiousness. I treat constraint as just costliness by another name.

(9) And there's this thing I call the deference rebate: audiences often pay good signallers back in prestige and deference, sometimes by more than the signal cost to produce, so a display can leave the signaller net better off and still be a handicap. I use it to explain why all this costly displaying doesnt wreck people's lives.

My housemate (an economist) says the problem is (3) plus (6); someone in the dept says (2) and (1) can't both be true. I think they're both aiming at the wrong joint but I can't locate the right one.

So: audit the package. Which pairs genuinely conflict, worst first, and what each would cost me to fix; which pairs only look like they conflict and why they don't; whether there's some commitment I'm relying on without having stated it; and what the single revision would tidy up the most. Please don't just tell me everything is in tension with everything — I need to know which one actually has to move.
