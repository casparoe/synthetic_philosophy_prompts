I work in a job that mostly consists of producing and reviewing written argument, and for about two years I have used language-model assistants daily for exactly that: pressure-testing a line of reasoning, working out a step of a derivation I half remember, drafting something I then have to defend in a meeting. Two episodes have made me think there is a single phenomenon here that I do not have a word for. In the first, I produced a clean short argument that survived review, and a colleague asked me a week later which premise the conclusion actually rested on; I found I could not say, and, more unsettlingly, I could not tell whether I had once known and forgotten or had never known and merely experienced the argument as obvious while it passed through me. In the second, a friend who supervises medical residents described the same shape from the other side: her trainees now arrive with differentials that are better than they used to be and with noticeably more confidence, but when the tool is taken away they cannot say what would change their minds about their own top diagnosis, and they are not worried by that, because nothing in their track record has ever penalized them for it.

What interests me is that this does not seem to be one thing but three, stacked. There is the ordinary first-order loss of a skill from disuse. Then there is something second-order: my confidence in whether I understand something has come loose from whether I do, because the artifact I produce looks the same in both cases, so the feeling of fluency is no longer evidence of anything. And there is a third layer that I think is the distinctive one: the only check available to me, when I suspect I have not understood, is to ask the same system again — so the instrument that produces the uncalibrated sense of understanding is also the instrument I would use to detect it, and there is no independent vantage point left. On top of that, by the only metrics my workplace records, my output has improved, so nothing in the feedback loop is going to flag any of this.

I have looked around a bit and everything I find is near but not on it. Deskilling and automation-related accounts of skill loss concern a capacity that declines and a performance that correspondingly gets worse, which is not my case; the Google-effect literature concerns offloading memory for facts and retrieving them from elsewhere, which is not what I am describing either, since I am not failing to recall where something is stored. The illusion of explanatory depth is about overestimating how well one understands mechanisms, but it is usually presented as a standing feature of cognition rather than something induced and worsened by a dependence, and it is silent on the third layer. Automation bias and complacency concern over-trusting what the machine tells you, which is different from over-trusting your own understanding of what the machine told you. Transactive memory and extended-mind views treat reliance on external resources as normally benign and even constitutive of cognition, and part of me thinks my case is just the latest instance of that and nothing to worry about.

So my question is whether there is an established concept — in epistemology, cognitive science, education, human factors, anywhere — for this configuration in particular, especially the third layer, where the source of the apparent understanding is also the only available check on it. If there is, I would like to know the nearest concepts and exactly how each fits or fails to fit the three layers above, since I suspect no single existing term covers all of them. If there is not, please coin a term, define it precisely, and specify its boundary cases: what would count as an instance and what would not, and how it differs from relying on a calculator, a notebook, a spell-checker, or a trusted colleague. I would also like to know how one would even test for it, given that the usual measures of competence are outputs and the outputs are fine, and given that self-report is precisely what the phenomenon degrades. And I would like a straight answer on the last point: whether this is a genuine epistemic loss or just a shift in what it is efficient to internalize, and what sort of evidence would settle that question rather than merely restate someone's prior attitude toward the technology. A name on its own will not be of much use to me.
