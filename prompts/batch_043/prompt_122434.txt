Hey — I'm in a very informal reading group this semester on conservative thought (wine is definately involved) and we got stuck on something, so I want to borrow a head steadier than ours.

We were talking about Chesterton's fence: the reformer wants to tear down a fence across a road, and Chesterton's guy says in effect that if you can't tell him why it was put there, you don't get to remove it. Someone in the group said this is just Burkean conservatism in a slogan; I pushed back, and then couldn't quite say why. Here's the distinction I want to try out on you. Call a rule *reason-transparent* if the work it does could in principle be written down as an explicit justification — the cost-benefit case for a regulation, the stated purpose of a procedure. Call it *reason-opaque* if what it mostly does is stabilise expectations, fix who owes whom an explanation, or carry know-how nobody ever articulated, so that demanding a justification tends to destroy the thing rather than test it. My hunch is that Chesterton's fence is really only about the first kind, and that Burke is mostly worried about the second: on his view the fence need not have reasons anyone can *state*, because the fact that it has survived is the evidence, and forcing each generation to re-argue everything from scratch is close to the rationalist arrogance he blamed on the French revolutionaries. (Same spirit as his remark that a state with no means of change has no means of preserving itself — reform yes, but reform that grows out of what is already there.) So the slogan and the Burkean principle may actually pull in opposite directions, and I dont trust myself to work out where.

Two cases Burke obviously never saw. (1) A "sunset and rebuild" proposal for regulation: nothing survives by default, every rule dies automatically after a decade unless an agency re-justifies it on the record, so the default flips from *kept until repealed* to *gone unless defended*. On its face that looks like Chesterton's fence applied systematically — nothing gets to stay merely because it has always been there — but under my distinction it may be the opposite of Burkean, since it makes the inarticulable stuff articulate or die. (2) The move to test-optional college admissions in the US: the version of the story I half-remember is that standardized tests were pushed in the first place partly as a way around the old prep-school-and-legacy pipeline, i.e. a fence built to keep something else out, in which case dropping them may quietly restore the very thing they were built to fence off — nested fences, where the innovation is also a restoration. So: a real Burke, briefed on all of this (assume he's been given the facts rather than dropped in confused) — which way does he go on each, and where, if anywhere, do fence-reasoning and prescription genuinely come apart?
