Me and two friends run a tiny podcast on war and ethics — no academic background between us, we just argue about this stuff. Next week's guest is a philosopher (I won't name her) who defends the moral equality of combatants: the view that soldiers on both sides of a war have the same permission to fight and are equally liable to be killed, regardless of whether their side's cause is just. Her version grounds it in what she calls the "risk bargain" — you get a licence to kill the other side's soldiers in exchange for accepting the same risk yourself, and that symmetry holds even when one side is fighting an unjust war. Whenever you press her on an awkward case she fobs it off with "that's a question for the law of armed conflict, not for the morality of combat," so I'd like the questions ordered so that move gets progressively harder to fall back on.

Draft me 8 to 10 questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the reply she's most likely to give. I want at least one on the revisionist objection — that you can't acquire a permission to kill merely by being ordered to, and that the unjust side's soldiers are morally required to lay down their arms rather than fight; at least one on whether the risk bargain survives fully remote warfare, where one state fights entirely with drones and stand-off missiles and can't meaningfully be hit back, and whether her view then slides into saying riskless killing is punishment rather than combat; and one on whether the symmetry should really be indexed to how voluntary the fighting is, i.e. whether a volunteer in an unjust war has the same standing as a conscript dragged into it. Finish with a question about what kind of evidence could actually count against the view. Skip anything biographical — assume she's heard every standard objection a hundred times.
