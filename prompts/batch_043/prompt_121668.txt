Hi — I've got no philosophy background, I've just been chewing on this on my own. Everyone says sunk costs are sunk and should never count, but when I go case by case I don't actually believe that, and I can't find the line.

Walking out of a boring movie I paid $18 for: obviously I should walk out, the money's gone either way. Forcing down a gross hotel breakfast because it was included: silly. Our team pouring more money into a product that's clearly failing, because we've already spent two years on it: bad, and I'd say so in the meeting. A country staying in a war because of the soldiers already dead: not just irrational, kind of disgusting.

But: staying married twelve years when I wouldn't start it today — fine, and "we've built a life together" feels like a real reason, not a fallacy. Keeping up a twenty-year friendship when a new aquaintance is slightly more fun: not even a question. Finishing the degree I've stopped believing in, one year left: I'd finish, and "I've come this far" feels legitimate there in a way it doesn't at the movies, even though the shape of the two cases looks the same. And paying for an annual gym membership I hate, specifically because it makes me go: fine, which is wierd, because there the money is genuinely doing work.

My tentative principle: the past counts only when the past spending or effort built something that still exists and is the thing I'd actually be giving up — a relationship's history, a skill, a shared life — and doesn't count when all it bought was a shot at something I no longer want. Does that hold up? It seems to make the war case turn on whether the dead soldiers' sacrifice "built" anything, and I don't like that. Also: I keep paying storage on my dad's old tools that I'll never use. Loyalty or fallacy? Can you state a principle that gets my verdicts, say plainly which ones it can't accommodate, tell me what it says about the tools, and give me one case your principle decides in a way I'd probably hate?
