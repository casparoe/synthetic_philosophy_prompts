I'm on a small committee producing a commemorative pamphlet for my town's 250th anniversary of incorporation — a village of about three thousand in northern New England. The centerpiece is a facsimile of the original charter, and two of us argued for running, on the facing page, a parody of it: the founding document of a municipality that has solved the problem of foundations by having no outside at all. I've been reading Hofstadter on Strange Loops, and the distinction he leans on is between a tangled hierarchy — where climbing faithfully from level to level lands you back where you started — and the level that stays inviolate, the one the loop cannot swallow. In Escher's lithograph *Drawing Hands*, each hand is drawn by the other, and the thing that breaks the regress is outside the picture: the sheet, and the hand of the person who drew it. The drawing cannot depict the hand doing the depicting. It occurred to me that a town charter written as though it were pure tangled hierarchy, with no inviolate level left anywhere in it, would make a very good vehicle for the joke.

What I'd like is that document: about 800 words, numbered clauses, written deadpan in the register of a municipal charter or a set of corporate bylaws, for a fictional New England town of roughly our size (name it something plausible). The structure itself has to carry the humor: the clauses should genuinely form the loop rather than merely announce that they do. So let one section create the office of Town Clerk while a later section — which takes effect only upon being countersigned by the Clerk — enacts the earlier one; let the town seal depict two hands drawing each other; let the amendment provision amend its own amendment procedure; let the date of enactment be fixed by a section that only exists once the charter is in force; let every dispute about the charter's validity be adjudicated by a court the charter itself establishes and staffs. No narrator steps in, nobody winks, and the word paradox never appears. It should end by failing to close in exactly one place, where something genuinely outside the document presses in — a superior authority that can dissolve the town, a printer's mark on the page, a note in the margin in an unauthorized hand, an empty oval where the seal was supposed to be — and the piece should never say what that intrusion means.

Two constraints on tone and audience. First, the satire has to exaggerate a real feature rather than invent one: real charters really do bootstrap themselves, the problem of self-amendment is real, and the claim that a loop of this kind still leaves something unabsorbed is the actual Hofstadterian point — so someone who knows the material should find the joke fair and feel that the author understands what's being sent up, while a neighbor who has never heard of Hofstadter just reads a funny, faintly sinister piece of municipal paperwork. Second, don't fabricate anything attributed to a real person: no epigraphs or invented remarks from Hofstadter or Escher, no fake citations or footnotes. Call the town what you like. Finally, append a short note of 150 to 200 words, written in your own voice rather than the document's: say how confident you are that you've represented the tangled-hierarchy/inviolate-level distinction correctly, single out the one claim about it you're least sure of, and flag where the piece knowingly exaggerates or leans on a contested reading.
