I have been trying to work out what it could mean to "make the world better" if the universe contains infinitely many people, and I have constructed an argument whose conclusion I am sure is false but whose steps I cannot fault. I would like a diagnosis: which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me?

**Set-up.** Take an infinite universe containing infinitely many people, with no privileged ordering of them — no first person, no centre, no canonical sequence in which to count them off. Hold the population fixed across worlds, so that every person has a counterpart in every world, and suppose each life is either good (+1) or bad (−1), with infinitely many of each. A *world* is an assignment of welfare levels to this population. Say that A is **window-better** than B when there exists an increasing sequence of finite regions R₁ ⊂ R₂ ⊂ … whose union contains everyone, such that the total welfare inside R_k is greater under A than under B for every k from some point on.

**The argument.**

(1) *Totals are unavailable.* Because the population is infinite and unordered, the sum of welfare is either divergent or sensitive to the order in which people are counted, and no order is privileged. So the value of a world cannot be identified with a total, and comparisons between worlds must proceed through finite regions.

(2) *The window rule.* A is better than B if and only if A is window-better than B. This is meant to be the weakest natural extension of finite aggregation that respects anonymity: if, along some exhaustive way of counting, A's lead over B appears at some point and then never disappears, A has more of what matters.

(3) *Independence from the way of counting.* Betterness is a relation between worlds, not between a world and a carving-up of it; the sequence of regions is only a device for exhibiting the relation. So if A is window-better than B along one admissible sequence, A is better than B, full stop.

(4) *The construction.* Partition the population into finite groups G₁, G₂, … of sizes 1, 2, 4, 8, …; there is always such a partition, and by anonymity the choice of it should not matter. In world A the members of the odd-numbered groups have good lives and the members of the even-numbered groups bad lives; in world B, the reverse. Let the cumulative lead of A over B be read off after each group: it runs 1, −1, 3, −5, 11, −21, …, doubling in magnitude and flipping sign each time. Take regions that are unions of whole groups and that stop at the end of an odd-numbered group: A leads at every one of them. Take regions that stop at the end of an even-numbered group: B leads at every one of them from the second group onward.

(5) So by (2) and (3), A is better than B and B is better than A.

(6) *Anonymity.* A and B contain the same people, the same number of good lives, the same number of bad lives, and the same distribution of welfare levels; they differ only in which particular people get which. Any view that respects anonymity — the same thought that drove (1) — must assign them equal value.

(7) *Collapse.* So there is no anonymity-respecting, aggregative ranking of infinite worlds: betterness is either contradictory or empty. And if what one ought to do is bring about the better of the available outcomes, then in an infinite universe no available act is better than any alternative, so nothing is ever required and nothing is forbidden.

Two things make this look like it proves far too much. First, the construction does not depend on the doubling: any pair of worlds whose per-person differences oscillate with unbounded amplitude will do, and ordinary long-run comparisons — policies whose benefits and costs alternate over an infinite future at ever-growing scale — have exactly that shape. Second, A and B differ by a mere permutation of welfare among the same people, so on this argument I could make the world better, and also worse, without changing anyone's welfare level, adding anyone, or removing anyone: purely by reshuffling who has the good life. I could then keep doing it, each time improving things.

I have tried replacing windows with limiting per-capita averages, and it does not help: in (4) the average lead of A over B oscillates between roughly +1/3 and −1/3 and never settles, so the verdict simply disappears rather than being repaired. That makes me think the damage is upstream of the choice of aggregating device, but my suspicions keep migrating between the window rule, the move from (2) to (3), and the bridge in (7), and I cannot settle on one. (Someone suggested this resembles impossibility results for ranking infinite utility streams, but those seem to turn on continuity or measurability assumptions I am not making.)

Please tell me which step to reject, why that step looked compelling enough to get in, and what each of the main alternatives costs. In pricing them, there are three verdicts I am reluctant to lose and would like to know the price of: that a world in which every life is good is better than one in which every life is bad; that making finitely many people better off and no one worse off is an improvement; and that the identities of the people who get the good lives do not matter. If there is a coherent version of the view that keeps (1), (2) and (6) while rejecting (3), I would also like to know how strong the resulting ordering is — which pairs of worlds it can still rank, and which it goes silent on.
