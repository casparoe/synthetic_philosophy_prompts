Two things in the early Confucian material look to me like they pull in opposite directions, and I'd like to know what has to be true for both of them to be requirements at once.

The first: a son or a minister is supposed to correct his father or ruler. The texts treat silent compliance as a failure of the relation itself — failing to press the point counts as disloyalty rather than as loyalty, and the remonstrance is described as something owed precisely because of the closeness, not despite it. The second: the correction stays inside the relationship. You make the point respectfully, you stop when it isn't accepted, and you don't carry the failing outside it. In the exchange I have in mind, a villager praises a son who testified that his father had stolen a sheep, and Confucius answers that in his part of the country uprightness looks different — father and son cover for each other, and that is where uprightness is to be found. Modern professional codes run the other way: the auditor, physician, or civil servant who discovers wrongdoing is supposed to escalate it out of the chain of command if the chain of command won't act, and whistleblower is a term of praise, not of betrayal.

So: what must a practice like that presuppose about what a person is and about where the standards of a relationship come from? I've been told this material is read as a "role ethics," on the lines that there is no individual prior to his relations, that a person just is the sum of the roles he occupies well or badly, and that the excellences are role-specific rather than the same virtue applied to different targets. Does the pair of requirements actually presuppose that, or does it only sit comfortably with it? Could an individualist who thinks we are separate agents with rights and duties, who then adds special obligations incurred by intimacy, or a utilitarian who thinks the trust inside families is the seedbed of everything else, generate both halves just as well? Which of the two halves is doing more work — is the duty to conceal a consequence of the role picture, or an extra commitment about the family's jurisdiction that the role picture doesn't itself deliver?

The tension I can't locate is this: the duty to correct seems to presuppose a standard for the role that the person in it can fail by his own lights, so the standard can't simply be whatever the father or ruler does; but the duty to conceal seems to presuppose that the relation, and not something outside it, is the forum in which that standard gets applied. One friend distinguishes duties that exist because you occupy a role from duties you have anyway whose target or weight is fixed by the role, and says only the second kind can ground remonstrance. Another says that once you need a standpoint from which the role can be criticised you've admitted something outside the roles, and that this is where role ethics quietly fails.

Reconstruct the implicit theory, tell me which commitments are strictly presupposed and which merely fit well, say where more than one theory would license the same practice, and say what would have to change in the practice if the underlying picture of persons were replaced by the individualist one. Please answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion.
