Quick version of what i think the view is: God's knowledge gets split into three stages. (1) natural knowledge: all the necessary truths and every possible world. (2) middle knowledge: for any possible free creature and any possible circumstance, what that creature *would* freely do — and this is supposed to be settled before God decides what to create. (3) free knowledge: what will actually happen, once God has picked a world to actualize. The selling point of stage 2 is that it's meant to let God do fine-grained providence (and prophecy of specific free human acts) without ever overriding anyone's will. I think this was some 16th century Jesuit's move — Molina? — and the name "middle knowledge" is because it sits between the other two.

My three framings / worries:

1. God as context engineer, not puppeteer. The picture that clicked for me is dependency injection. A Molinist God doesn't reach in and force the return value; he picks the arguments. If he knows that creature A in circumstance C freely returns "betray", then to get a betrayal he doesn't override A, he just instantiates C. Which, from an engineering point of view, feels less like respecting freedom and more like being extremely good at setting up the environment so the thing you want falls out anyway. My question is whether that's a real problem for the view or whether it just *is* the view and I've rediscovered that everyone already argues about this. It also raises a downstream thing i find funny: on this picture God's sovereign control over history looks a lot like prompt engineering — same model, same weights, different context, different output. Is there anything in Molinism that rules out that being the whole story of providence, or is that just accepted?

2. The function signature problem (this is the one i like most). Middle knowledge is knowledge of counterfactuals of creaturely freedom: A-in-C would do X. But C has a granularity. How much of the world is inside the argument? If C is fine-grained enough to include the creature's entire causal history, brain state, every consideration that occurs to them in the moment, then the output looks determined by the input and the "freedom" part is decorative. If C is coarse — say, "Peter is in the courtyard and someone asks him a question" — then the input doesn't seem to pin down any particular output, and it's unclear there's a fact of the matter about what he'd do without adding something that does the pinning. So the Molinist needs C to sit in a sweet spot, and i have no idea what principle picks the sweet spot. In code terms: the counterfactual is a pure function whose environment/closure has to be specified, and either you close over everything (determinism) or you leave free variables (no determinate value). My attempt at a third option, which i suspect fails: God knows a *weighted disposition* rather than an outcome — the creature-in-C has, say, a 70% tendency to betray. That preserves freedom in a way i find intuitive, but then it can't do the job middle knowledge was invented for, because it won't underwrite definite prophecies or fine-grained control of specific historical outcomes.

3. The seed argument. Here's where i might be onto something, or might just be dressing up the standard "no truthmaker" complaint in jargon. The only working model i have for *definite* knowledge of a single outcome from an undetermined process is: something else fixes the outcome. Concretely, if you want to know what a genuinely indeterministic process outputs, you run it — but running it requires a seed, and seed + code determines the result. The creature inside can't predict it, experiences it as an open choice, but it's settled. So: either middle knowledge is definite, in which case it smuggles in a seed-like thing (a truthmaker, a hidden determinate something) and collapses into compatibilism with extra steps; or it isn't definite, and providence loses its grip on specific free acts. My follow-up thought, which i haven't seen anywhere: if God only gets *distributions*, he still has a move available — run the circumstance many times across many worlds, like sampling, and only actualize the branch that came out the way he wanted. Call it monte carlo providence. It keeps every actualized creature free, but it means the real world is the one that got selected for its outcomes, which sounds like it has the same queasy "filtered after the fact" feel as the manipulation worry, just one level up.
