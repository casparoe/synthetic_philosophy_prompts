So I'm a second-year physics student doing this elective with a name like "Science and its Histories" — I picked it mostly because it fit my timetable — and last week's seminar was on whether the "Scientific Revolution" was actually a thing. Afterwards, waiting for a delayed train, my flatmate put on an episode of a history podcast he's into, called something smug like "The Revolution That Never Happened," and the host disposed of the whole question in about two minutes. I'm reconstructing it from memory, more or less as he said it:

"It's pretty much settled now that there was no Scientific Revolution — it's a label nineteenth-century writers invented so they could tell a flattering story about secular reason breaking out of religious darkness. Once you actually read the manuscripts you find the Middle Ages had already done the work: Buridan's impetus theory is basically Newton's first law, Oresme had already argued that the earth moves and that the Bible's sunrise language should be read figuratively, and the Oxford crowd were writing down quantitative laws for falling bodies in the 1300s. So nothing fundamental changed in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. The 'Renaissance' is the same trick: nobody in 1500 thought they were living in a rebirth — that's a nineteenth-century French word retrojected backwards, and 'the Middle Ages' is just the leftover, a name for a gap rather than a period. Really the only thing that changed between 1400 and 1700 is that printing made it easier to find out what other people already knew. All these labels are retrospective PR, and we should just drop them."

I came out of it half-convinced, partly because I genuinely didn't know fourteenth-century people had done anything quantitative with motion and partly because I like the debunking vibe. But my seminar tutor said something I scribbled down and then couldn't work out: "continuity isn't the same as no change, and a period being a convenience isn't the same as it being a fraud" — and then the train came. So: is the podcast fair? Could you go through it roughly claim by claim and tell me what a historian of early modern science would just accept, what they'd wince at (true-ish but being made to carry more weight than it can bear), and what's straightforwardly wrong — and why?
