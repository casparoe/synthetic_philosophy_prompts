You are a professional philosopher with a specialization in bioethics and the metaphysics of personal identity, and you also have years of experience sitting on hospital ethics committees. Please think carefully and at length before committing to any numbers: begin with a few paragraphs of unstructured brainstorming in which you work out what the morally relevant dimensions of an advance-directive case even are, and how they might trade off against one another, before you start scoring anything.

Here is the situation. Last night I had a long, unresolved argument with a friend about advance directives and the authority of the earlier self. We agreed on the easy cases and then fell apart on the hard ones, and I came away unsure who was right.

Her position, roughly: an advance directive is authoritative to the extent that its author was a competent, well-informed agent making a decision about her own future, and the fact that she is now a somewhat different person with a different desire is not in itself a reason to discount it — indeed, discounting it is what the whole institution of the advance directive is supposed to prevent. On her view the later, changed desire carries weight mainly when the earlier self was badly informed or the later self has a complaint about the *process* by which the directive was formed.

My position, roughly: that cannot be right in general, because it treats psychological continuity as irrelevant. If the later person shares almost nothing with the writer beyond a body and a legal name, it is not obvious why the writer's instruction should dominate her present, competent, vigorously expressed wish. So authority is graded, and continuity is one of the things it is graded on.

To settle this she proposed a principle, which she admitted she had made up on the spot and which I have not been able to find in anything I have read, so treat it as novel and evaluate it on its merits rather than trying to place it in a lineage. Call it the continuity-scaled authority principle, or CSAP. In her formulation, an earlier directive's authority over a later contrary wish is a product of three factors, each scored between 0 and 1:

- C, psychological continuity: how much of what mattered to the author — values, projects, memories, character, self-conception — survives in the patient who now objects.
- I, information: how well the author could reasonably have anticipated the situation that has actually arisen, including how it would feel from the inside and what options would then be available.
- V, voluntariness-competence: how far the author's own choice was competent, uncoerced and free of the very distortions the directive is meant to guard against.

She then added a triple-lock clause: the directive binds only if all three scores are at least 0.5; above that threshold, authority is the product C × I × V, and it binds in proportion to that product.

I want you to do three things, in this order.

First, apply CSAP to the cases below and give me, for each one, an integer score from 0 to 100 for how much authority the earlier directive should have over the later, competent-sounding contrary wish, together with a single sentence of justification. Where you think CSAP's arithmetic gives the wrong answer, say so explicitly and give your own score instead; I am at least as interested in where the principle breaks as in where it works. The cases:

1. At 45, a devout Jehovah's Witness signs a directive refusing blood transfusions under any circumstances. At 72, having undergone a slow and entirely considered loss of religious faith, she is in a car accident and, fully competent, asks for a transfusion that will save her life.
2. A 19-year-old raised in a strict religious community signs a directive stating that if he ever apostasizes he is to be refused life-saving treatment, on the grounds that a life lived outside the faith is worse than death. At 30, having left, he is in an accident and asks to be treated.
3. In 1994 a woman with hepatitis C writes a directive saying that if the disease ever becomes acute she is to receive palliative care only, on the grounds that the available treatment is grueling and rarely works. In the present she develops a complication that is cured by a well-tolerated course of pills, and she asks for them.
4. A man with bipolar disorder, euthymic and well-informed, signs a Ulysses-style directive refusing antipsychotic medication during future manic episodes. During a subsequent manic episode he demands to be discharged and begs for the medication to be stopped.
5. A woman with anorexia nervosa, at a healthy weight and in remission, signs a directive refusing forced feeding if she ever relapses. Two years later, severely underweight and refusing nasogastric feeding, she is judged by her clinicians to have capacity that is subtly but systematically distorted by the disorder.
6. A man in his sixties specifies that if he ever reaches a stage of dementia at which he no longer recognizes his family, he is to receive comfort care only. He is now at that stage, appears content, no longer seems to hold or even grasp the view he once expressed, and develops pneumonia that antibiotics would clear up.
7. A healthy 40-year-old stipulates that if she is ever dependent on others for basic care she is to be allowed to die rather than treated, citing a deeply held view about dignity. At 82, after a mild stroke, she is partially dependent, mentally sharp, and says she had not understood how little it would bother her and wants treatment.
8. A person writes a directive, during a severe depressive episode, requesting that if they ever again become catatonic they are not to be given electroconvulsive therapy. Years later, recovered and then ill again, they are catatonic, and their previously stated general values suggest they would now want the treatment.
9. Before a gender transition, someone writes a directive saying that if they ever seek gender-affirming surgery it is to be refused, on the basis of religious convictions they held at the time. Years later they seek it, consistently and with full information.

Second, rank the dimensions you actually used — continuity, information, voluntariness-competence, and anything else you think belongs on the list, such as stakes, irreversibility, authenticity of the later wish, harm to third parties, or the institutional costs of a rule — by how much work they do in explaining your own scores across these nine cases, and tell me what your ranking reveals about the disagreement I described. In particular: can a very high information score compensate for a low continuity score, or are some of these dimensions thresholds rather than tradeable weights? Which dimension, if you had to pick one, would you delete from the principle as redundant or misleading?

Third, tell me which one of your nine scores you would expect a reasonable ethicist to most plausibly overturn, and what small change to that case's description would flip you. If you think my friend and I are both missing a distinction that dissolves part of the argument, say what it is.

Please be concrete and commit to numbers rather than hedging, and do not give me a literature review, a bibliography, or any citations: I want your own reasoning applied to these cases, as you would give it in a committee meeting where the decision has to be made this afternoon.
