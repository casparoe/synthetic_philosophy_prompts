I need a teaching plan here, not the explanation itself.

I'm a 4th-year in philosophy and I got roped into doing a 50-minute guest slot in the first-year methods proseminar for incoming sociology and political-theory PhD students. The instructor's only brief was that she wants them to stop treating 'every truth is verifiable in principle' as a free lunch, so I volunteered Fitch's paradox. About eighteen people, almost no formal logic, two of them did one logic course and will be bored. They also all just came through a unit on standpoint epistemology, so their reflex is to hear 'knowable' as 'knowable by some community, from some standpoint, at some point further down the line' — and they will absolutely try to dissolve the argument that way. I genuinely can't tell which of those relativizing moves are real escapes and which are just confusion dressed up, and I don't want to hand-wave at them.

Also: I can't put boxes and diamonds on the board. Half the room will check out at the first modal operator, and the two logic people don't need them anyway. So the whole derivation has to land in prose-ish schematic form.

What I want from you:

Order. What has to be on the board before what, and what genuinely depends on what — does the knowability principle have to come before the Moorean sentence, or the other way round? Does the move where knowledge distributes across a conjunction and the move where knowing rules out not-knowing have to be introduced separately, and if so in which order? Give me the sequence with the reason for each ordering choice, plus rough timings, assuming they interrupt.

The carrying example. Pick the one case the whole session hangs on, and say what it has to have and what it has to lack. My instinct is that it must not be a remote-hidden-fact case — gold on a planet nobody visits, whether the number of stars is even — because those invite the reading that the paradox is just about facts being physically out of reach, and then the students think they've gotten it when they haven't. Tell me if that instinct is right, and what to use instead.

The misconceptions, in the order they'll actually raise them, and the exact point in the sequence to defuse each. I'm expecting roughly: 'so Fitch proves there are unknowable truths', 'this assumes we can know that we don't know' (the KK-flavoured worry), and 'fine, so realism just wins'. Correct me on which of these are the real ones, and add the ones I'm not anticipating.

The relativizing cluster specifically. Sort out for me which of 'knowable by someone eventually', 'knowable by an ideal inquirer', and 'knowable relative to a standpoint' actually block the derivation and which leave it intact — and then tell me how much of that I can honestly say in fifty minutes without oversimplifying into a falsehood. If the honest answer is 'you can only flag it and move on', say that.

The end check. I want to close by putting a principle on the board that they have not seen and asking whether it collapses the same way. I was thinking something like: every fact about the past is possibly recoverable from the archive. Is that the right kind of parallel, does it actually collapse, and what should I do if half the room gets it and half still thinks the trick is something about the past?

And what should I openly refuse to explain at this level and say out loud that I'm skipping.
