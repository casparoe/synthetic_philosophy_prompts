I'm putting together the last problem for my MA seminar on speech acts, and my colleague Roz — who's guest-lecturing for me in two weeks and who has Opinions about performatives — has already gone on record about nearly every case I'd otherwise use, so I've been trying to reverse-engineer what she's actually running in order to guess how she'll take the one I've built. Her verdicts so far (assembled from a draft of hers she let me read and one very long department lunch): crossed-fingers "I promise I'll have the chapter to you by Friday" with no intention of delivering and no delivery — promise made, just a lousy one, and she's adamant that insincerity is an abuse rather than a misfire; the couple "married" by an officiant ordained through a website in a jurisdiction that doesn't recognise it, where everyone at the ceremony understood exactly what he was purporting to do and took it as a wedding — no marriage, uptake notwithstanding; "fiver says the dean's resigned by June" met with an eyeroll and a walk-off — no bet, met with "you're on" — bet; a judge passing sentence on a defendant who'd refused to remove her earmuffs and never heard a word, with the clerk, both prosecutors and the reporter hearing it and the transcript recording it — sentence passed; a resignation emailed to a dean who was away and only read it a fortnight later — a valid resignation, and when I pressed her on whether it took effect when sent or when read she said that was a question about the legal rule and not about the speech act, which I think is a dodge; and she's happy with non-spoken performatives, apparently — a chair typing "meeting adjourned" into the chat counts, she said, "provided people are looking at the chat," a proviso I genuinely can't tell whether she means as an uptake condition or as part of what it is for the move to have been made publicly at all.

The case I've built: our last faculty meeting ran late and nasty, and at 9:46 we passed a reappointment vote that half the room hated. At 9:47 the chair said "this meeting is adjourned" — except that the audio had dropped for everyone else four minutes earlier and not one of us heard her; we drifted off around 9:50 because the room booking expired. The recording caught it, and the autotranscription thing the university rolled out last year produced a summary reading "meeting adjourned, 9:47pm," which nobody opened for a fortnight, at which point someone trying to reopen the reappointment vote argued that the meeting was never adjourned and is therefore technically still in session. So: does Roz say it was adjourned at 9:47; does she say it became adjourned — retroactively, somehow — only when we finally read the summary; or does she say it was never adjourned at all, and the chair has to do it again?

What I want is a committed answer rather than a menu: give me the verdict you'd actually put money on, with rough odds, the rule you think she's running (the Austinian "right person, right procedure, correctly executed, no uptake required" line versus something that makes uptake by the relevant party a genuine condition and then owes us a story about deferred, institutional, or retroactive uptake), which of her earlier verdicts is the closest analogue and which one is doing the real work in your prediction, and — most useful for my handout — which further variant I should write in to force her hand, whether that's the corrupted-recording version where nobody ever learns of it, the version where one person's client stayed connected and heard it, or a better one of your own. And if the rules you can extract from her record don't really come apart on my case, say so plainly and tell me how to rebuild the case so they do.
