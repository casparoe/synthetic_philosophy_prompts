I am two drafts into a novel about a reserve police battalion — middle-aged, working-class, non-SS men from a German port city — posted to a small town in occupied Poland in 1942, and I have hit a structural problem that is really a philosophical one. The scene I keep returning to is the familiar one: a company of about sixty men is ordered to shoot several hundred civilians from the town in a clearing in the woods, the major tells them that anyone who cannot do it may step out, about ten men do, and the rest spend the afternoon at it with the help of a great deal of schnapps. A historian in my writing group read it and said the scene is useless, because it is equally consistent with both of the accounts that structure the whole debate about men like these: the situational account in the Browning line (authority and obedience, conformity and peer bonding, the diffusion of responsibility into a role, routinization, killing reframed as a job to be done competently for the sake of the unit) and the cultural-intentionalist account in the Goldhagen line (a widely shared eliminatory cognitive model in which Jews are already constituted, prior to any order, as a mortal threat that must be destroyed). He is right, and I want to fix it rather than argue with him.

So I need a matched pair of versions of that same clearing, on the same afternoon, with the same men, the same officer, the same order, the same victims and the same logistics — identical in every respect except one variable, and engineered so that the two accounts come apart. The variable I want manipulated is the exit: in the first version the offer to step out is made publicly, in front of the company, roughly as I have it now; in the second it is delivered individually the night before in a sealed envelope, genuinely costless, automatic reassignment to rear-area guard duty, nothing on the record, no one else informed, and the men have no reason to doubt that it is real. Across that pair I want three discriminating observables: the proportion who take the exit; who they are (does taking it track weak integration into the platoon — the isolated, the recently transferred, the socially marginal — or does it track cohort and exposure to indoctrination — the young men who came of age entirely inside the regime); and the texture of the killing among those who remain (voluntarism, humiliation, cruelties nobody ordered, volunteers for the next action, versus grim reluctant duty, alcohol, and apologetic idioms in the truck going home). Constraints: one location, one afternoon, a cast of no more than ten men I can name plus the officer; nobody states a motive, nobody gives a speech about vermin and nobody gives a speech about a Jewish friend; no hero, no last-minute reprieve, no deus ex machina; the public-offer version must stay equally consistent with both accounts, so that the discrimination comes entirely from the manipulation and not from how I have written the men; and every discriminating difference has to be something showable on the page — a hand raised or not, a folded form in a tunic pocket, who carries the ammunition boxes, who looks at the faces and who studies his boots — never something I would have to narrate from inside a head.

After the two versions (250 to 350 words each) I want the demonstration that the pair does what I have asked, and I want it derived from the mechanism of each account rather than from a slogan about it: what each account predicts for each of the three observables and why; which single feature of the setup is doing the discriminating work, and what happens to the pair if I remove it (my suspicion is that if the sealed offer is even slightly leaky — if the men think the company might guess — the whole thing collapses back into the baseline and both accounts predict the same afternoon, but you tell me); how a defender of whichever account comes out worse would attack the construction, including the move that taking the exit is not the same as refusing the project, since the men who step aside still round up, guard, load and drive; and finally an honest verdict on whether a novelist can settle anything here at all, or whether the most such a scene can do is make a reader feel the difference between two incompatible descriptions of the same raised hand. What I need it for is the novel, not a seminar: a reader who knows this debate should close the page knowing quietly which account the scene is arguing for, without a single character having said so.
