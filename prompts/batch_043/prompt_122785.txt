I should begin by placing myself. I spent a little over thirty years in physical chemistry, most of them on vibrational spectroscopy and on the chemometric business of inferring molecular structure from spectra; for the past four years, since retiring, I have been reading philosophy of mind and language seriously, if slowly. I have no access to a university library, and no wish to be directed to one: please rest nothing on who first advanced a view, or on titles, dates and schools, since I cannot check them and my memory for such things is unreliable. Argue the positions, not the bibliography.

The topic I want restated is the symbol grounding problem. As I understand it, the worry runs thus: a system may manipulate symbols entirely by their relations to other symbols — by shape, by position, by co-occurrence — with the result that its internal economy is elegant and self-consistent while nothing in it makes any symbol *about* anything outside that economy. The usual way of pressing the worry is a regress: training a system by giving it definitions in terms of further symbols looks like handing someone a dictionary written in the very language they do not yet know, and it seems that the only thing which can stop the regress is some non-symbolic contact with the world, of the sort perception and bodily action supply. Lately the worry has been sharpened by systems trained on nothing but text — no sensors, no effectors — which nonetheless talk about colours, textures and laboratory manipulations to a standard that would pass among non-specialists.

What I should like you to do is restate that problem, and the three responses to it that I keep encountering, in the vocabulary I actually think in. The three responses are: first, that grounding consists in causal contact through transduction and action, so that a genuine symbol is one whose use is tied down from outside the system; second, the sceptical reply that every supposed anchor is itself only another internal state, so nothing has been added and the regress was never escaped; third, the deflationary reply that reliable covariation with distal conditions, together with a role in successful action, is simply all that grounding ever meant, and that any further demand is a demand for something ineffable.

My vocabulary is that of measurement: instrument response and transfer functions; calibration curves and the distinction between calibration and validation on held-out or blind samples; the forward problem of predicting a spectrum from a structure and the inverse problem of recovering structure from a spectrum; degeneracy, where many structures are consistent with one spectrum; ill-posedness and the regularisation one imposes to make an inverse problem tractable; resolution and signal-to-noise; instrumental drift and recalibration; and, above all, the question of whether a fitted relation will travel to a new sample or merely interpolate within the range it was fitted on.

I have also been trying out some distinctions of my own, which I should like you to test rather than to humour. Call a symbol an *anchor* if its use is fixed by a causal channel I can characterise independently of the system's own activity, and a *relay* if its use is fixed only by its relations to other symbols; the *grounding depth* of a symbol is then the number of relay steps to the nearest anchor. Call grounding *read-grounding* when perturbing the distal property perturbs the symbol's use, and *write-grounding* when the system's use of the symbol perturbs the distal property, and say whether one without the other deserves the name. And I am drawn to a thesis I shall call calibrationism: that a symbol is grounded just in case the inverse problem from the system's use of it to the distal property is well posed, regularised by constraints not of the system's own devising, and licence-bearing — that is, one may act on the reading.

Two cases, then. In the first, a text-only system that has never been connected to an instrument predicts spectra for molecules it has not encountered, and laboratory measurement later confirms the predictions: on my definitions, is its talk of absorption bands grounded, and does your answer change if the predictions are right for the wrong reasons? In the second, a surrogate system is trained exclusively on the first system's textual output, so that its symbols sit one relay step further from any anchor whilst discriminating exactly as finely: does the difference in grounding depth mark any difference you would defend as real?

I should like, in sum: a faithful restatement; an honest residue report, saying which claims translate cleanly, which survive only after you have quietly smuggled in an assumption of my framework and called it translation, and which cannot be expressed in the vocabulary of instruments and inverse problems at all; a verdict on whether that residue indicts my vocabulary or reveals the original worry to have been less determinate than it looks, with particular attention to aboutness, to misrepresentation as opposed to mere instrumental error, and to the normative dimension of correctness; and a candid assessment of whether calibrationism is a genuine position or collapses into one of the three responses above, or into a causal-cum-reliabilist theory of reference wearing a laboratory coat.
