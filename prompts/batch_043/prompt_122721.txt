I was recently appointed as one of three trustees of the small foundation my father left, whose deed commits the income to reducing suffering wherever a comparatively modest sum will reduce a great deal of it. Our first substantive decision is whether a programme on the welfare of insects raised for food and feed — black soldier fly larvae, mealworms, crickets — falls inside that remit, and therefore whether the foundation should fund work on it. Two people whose judgement I rely on have been arguing about the underlying question in our shared email thread for nearly a year, and I would like the document that argument should have produced.

One of them, a comparative neuroscientist, holds what I will call the threshold view: moral status is not a matter of degree, it switches on all at once when a creature acquires a specific kind of capacity — roughly, a unified, broadly available, endogenously generated representation of how things stand with it, such that its valenced states are states of a single subject for whom things can be better or worse. Below that threshold there is only behaviour that looks as if it mattered to the animal; welfare ascription there is a category error, not a small ascription. The other, a welfare scientist, holds what I will call the dimensional view: moral status comes in degrees along several partly independent axes, and an insect is a very small but nonzero case, while a household thermostat is not a case at all and not because of any bright line — the thermostat scores at or near the floor on every axis, its set-point is fixed from outside, and nothing in its organisation gives it a stake in its own continued operation.

Please draft the joint statement these two could sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. I want, first, a numbered section of claims both can accept that are not merely platitudes: about what behavioural and neural markers (trade-off tests, conditioned place preference, analgesic and anaesthetic modulation, judgement-bias measures, sensitisation-like phenomena) can and cannot establish; about how far each side's preferred markers were calibrated on one lineage and what that implies for how much weight they carry far from it; about whether a homeostatic agent that will incur costs to hold a variable within bounds is a better reference case than a thermostat and why; and about what, if anything, follows for aggregation when the status of a single individual is small but the number of individuals is enormous.

Second, I want a disagreements table. For each crux, give the threshold view's own formulation, the dimensional view's own formulation, each phrased so that side would accept it as their position; the case or argument both agree would bear on it; and each side's prediction about that case, recorded in advance. The cruxes I would like covered include at least these: whether there can be a fact of the matter about how near a creature is to the threshold, and whether near-threshold cases reintroduce gradation at the boundary; whether the capacities relevant to the badness of a pain are the same as those relevant to the badness of a death, so that the two sides may be answering different questions; whether a creature whose valenced states are not accessible to a single decision centre but to several semi-independent ones contains one welfare subject, several, or a fraction of one, which would make moral status differ in number and not only in magnitude; and what to say about a system that reproduces the entire input-output profile of an insect, including its trade-offs and its generalisation after aversive events, by a wholly different mechanism, where the two sides should say in advance whether that settles anything.

Third, a short section identifying which parts of their dispute are verbal — I suspect that much of the argument has been about the word sentience, which sometimes means any phenomenal state at all, sometimes a valenced one, and sometimes whatever makes something a welfare subject — and saying why.

Finally, since I have to move money regardless, a brief closing section on what a funder should do while these cruxes remain unsettled, given that the residual disagreement is real and not merely terminological.
