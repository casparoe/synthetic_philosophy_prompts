hi — i run a monthly "big ideas" session at the natural history museum for the volunteer docents: mostly retired engineers, plus one retired lawyer who argues for sport. 40 minutes, one whiteboard, no slides. They will not read anything I hand them and I can't print anything anyway, so please don't give me a reading list or tell me to assign the paper. Next month I've been asked to do the Gould–Lewontin spandrels critique of adaptationism (the 1979 Spandrels of San Marco thing). Don't explain the critique to me — I've read around it. Plan how I should explain it to them. American English please.

What I actually need:

- The order the pieces have to go in, and why that order. My instinct is that current function vs. historical origin has to be in place before anything else has a chance of landing, but I can't see the dependency structure after that. Also: do I say the word "spandrel" in the first ten minutes, or hold it back until they've actually felt the puzzle it names?

- The carrying example. I've been toying with a made-up one: a fictional cliff lizard with a bright blue throat patch that's completely hidden when the throat is flat, and the same blue pigment also shows up in the lining of the mouth and on the underside of the tail. I want it invented on purpose — half the room will try to win by knowing the real biology. Tell me whether it carries the idea, what such an example has to have, what it has to lack (I worry this one invites "obviously a display signal" way too fast and short-circuits everything), and what to swap in if you think it's the wrong case.

- The misconceptions, in the order this specific room will generate them, and the point in the sequence where each has to be headed off. The ones I can predict: spandrel just means useless or vestigial; so Gould and Lewontin thought selection barely matters; a just-so story is a story that's false; and the engineers' version — it's well fitted to the job, therefore it was built for the job. There's also the flip side someone will raise around minute 25: if everything is a byproduct of something else, then nothing is an adaptation. Tell me which of these are really the same mistake in different clothes, and where each has to be defused so it doesn't set.

- A judgment call I want your read on. The critique looks to me like several definately seperable claims that everyone collapses: that some traits have no current use; that a trait's current use isn't why it was built; that some traits are forced by geometry or development rather than chosen from among alternatives; and that the standard adaptationist story isn't falsifiable because no alternative is ever really tested. Which one do you take to be the real target, and how should the arc of the session reflect that?

- The end check. I want something at minute 35, whiteboard, fresh made-up trait, that lets me tell the difference between someone who got the idea and someone who just acquired a new word. Design it: what I ask, what answer counts as getting it, what answer is parroting.

- What I should openly refuse to get into, and say out loud that I'm refusing. I'm thinking: anything quantitative or population-genetic; the real architectural argument about whether those bits of San Marco are even spandrels (I got corrected on that once and it went badly); and letting the lawyer turn the last ten minutes into a fight about evo psych. Tell me if I'm cutting the wrong things.
