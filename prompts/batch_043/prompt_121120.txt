I chair the incident review committee at a large hospital system. Within about two weeks of an adverse event, and on the basis of a written file plus one interview, we decide how the institution will respond to the clinicians involved: whether to occupy what Strawson calls the participant stance — blame, censure, a demand for an account, an expectation of remorse — or to fall back into the objective attitude, treating them as things to be managed, retrained, reassigned or removed. Our current protocol is a vague just-culture checklist; I want to replace it with something explicitly Strawsonian that a rotating committee of clinicians and one lay member can run in about an hour, roughly monthly, knowing that our finding goes into a personnel file and can affect clinical privileges.

Write me that procedure: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a no-blame outcome, and where it must stop and hand the decision to a person because what remains is a question of values rather than facts. It has to handle at least: the difference between an excuse (the agent did not really act, or acted in ignorance or under pressure) and an exemption (the agent is unsuitable as a candidate for reactive attitudes generally — a child, someone floridly psychotic, someone with advanced dementia); cases of partial or marginal participation, where several people each contributed a little; the gap between the committee's public finding and what its members can honestly feel, and the fact that the clinician's own self-reactive attitudes and the team's blame on the patient's behalf are part of what we are deciding; and, increasingly, events whose proximal cause was an algorithmic recommendation that nobody on the ward could have independently checked.

Then run it on three cases: a resident on a fourth consecutive long shift who misprogrammed an infusion pump and killed a patient; an attending who knowingly deviated from a guideline she disagreed with, relying on her own clinical experience, with a bad outcome; and a surgeon with early, undiagnosed cognitive decline whose colleagues had been quietly covering for him. At least one of these should come out somewhere the committee will not like. Close by saying what the procedure cannot do.
