I TA for a first-year epistemology course and we just ran a session on thought experiments — Gettier-style cases, trolley variants, the violinist, that sort of thing. One of the other instructors leans hard on Dennett's label and spells it out like this: the case is the pump, the intuition is the water, and the well is the pre-theoretic competence you already had before you walked into the room; a case doesn't give you any new evidence, it just draws up what was already down there, which is why a well-built case can be trusted and a badly-built one just pulls up mud. He then uses that to license something fairly strong about teaching: that the survey results showing order effects and framing effects are only ever about muddy wells, and that a good seminar discussion of a case is a way of tapping a shared, already-present body of knowledge rather than a way of manufacturing agreement out of nothing. I can tell the conclusion is doing a lot of work in how he runs the class, but I can't tell whether it comes out of the analogy or whether the analogy is just carrying it.

I've been toying with a rival picture and want it stress-tested the same way: instead of a pump over a well, think of a case as a leading question in an opinion poll, or as a dye tracer that lets you see the shape of a current you had no other way of observing — the point being that the set-up partly constructs the response it then appears to merely record. So: can you lay out the correspondence table for the pump version (what plays the water, the well, the pump, the handle, the mud, the aquifer), then separate the disanalogies that actually matter for his conclusion from the ones that are cosmetic? I care especially about the features a literal pump imports that a thought experiment arguably lacks — that the water is there in fixed quantity and pumping doesn't alter the aquifer, that the well exists independently of the pump, that the pump can only ever lift what is already down there, that there's a determinate depth and a determinate moment when the well runs dry — and whether his 'shared pre-existing knowledge, so trust the case' conclusion survives once those are on the table. Then do the same audit for my poll question / dye tracer version, tell me which picture better explains what actually happens in a seminar (including the bit where students' answers shift after twenty minutes of discussion, or after someone mentions what the case is supposed to show), and whether there's a third metaphor that does the real work both of them are fighting over. A table is fine, and please don't just tell me both are partly right — I want to know which one is doing the arguing and which one is decoration.
