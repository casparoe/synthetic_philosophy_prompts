During a long career in experimental physical chemistry I simply took for granted that my inferences ran on regularities, and it never occurred to me to ask whether anything had to *hold* those regularities in place. Having come to philosophy late and on my own, I have gotten stuck on a question about al-Ghazālī's occasionalism, and I would like a number rather than a survey of positions.

Assume, for the sake of the question only, that the occasionalist picture is correct: created things have no causal efficacy of their own, and what we call cause and effect — the cotton's burning on contact with the flame — is nothing more than God's creating both, in a settled sequence that he has made habitual, or ʿāda. Grant also what I take al-Ghazālī to grant: that the habit is real rather than a run of coincidences, that it is not a single episode, and that it can be interrupted, which is what a miracle would be.

The proposition I want priced is this: under that picture, induction from observed constant conjunction to the next instance carries exactly the epistemic warrant it carries under a Humean regularity view of causation — no more and no less. Put as a working scientist would put it: the fact that the order is contingent on a single agent's continued willing makes no practical difference to my inference, provided the order has held without exception so far. I'll count the question as resolved if it turns out that the occasionalist owes the inductive sceptic some further premise that he cannot supply, or alternatively owes him nothing beyond what the Humean already owes.

Give me your credence in that proposition, decomposed. Some terms I would like you to assign a probability to, and to add to or replace with better ones of your own: whether al-Ghazālī's denial targets necessary connection only, or any counterfactual dependence of effect on cause at all; whether the uniformity induction needs is a uniformity *in the sequence* or a uniformity *in the source of the sequence*, since on this picture what must be regular is the conduct of one agent, which makes my inference structurally closer to predicting what a person will do than to generalizing over a population of events; whether a single agent's conduct being held together by reasons and character makes it a firmer basis for prediction than mere frequency is, or a shakier one, because a change of mind is reason-responsive rather than a random fluctuation and so cannot be treated as noise; whether God, on this picture, has a standing reason to preserve the order — because agency, or knowledge, or the very possibility of being a creature who acts, depends on there being one — and whether a reason of that kind is more or less secure a basis for induction than a brute necessity in the natures of things would be; whether the bare possibility of a miracle is a localised exception that induction can absorb or a global defeater; and whether the whole dispute is idle because the warrant of induction never rested on causal powers in the first place. Then combine the terms and tell me which one the final number is most sensitive to.

I should also declare my own interest. My credence in the proposition sits near 0.65, largely because of a case from my own field that I suspect is doing illegitimate work: for thirty years I fitted rate parameters to data and treated the fitted values as brute and contingent, and neither I nor my colleagues ever thought the contingency of those numbers weakened a prediction in the slightest. A philosopher in the reading group I attend puts the proposition nearer 0.3. Give me your number, and then tell me where you think the disagreement between the two of us most likely lives.

Two practical requests. Lead with your credence in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble and no restatement of my question. And close by saying how confident you are in the analysis as a whole and which single term in the decomposition you are least sure of, with some indication of what would move it. I have no library access from where I am writing, so please do not direct me to the secondary literature or name the scholars who have argued about this — I could not check it, and it would not help me. Argue it from the structure of the position itself.
