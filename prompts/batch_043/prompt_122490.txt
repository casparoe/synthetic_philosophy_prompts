Last night a friend and I spent three hours going in circles about Good's theorem — the result that an expected-utility maximizer offered a costless signal before choosing can never do worse in ex ante expectation, since the decision rule do whatever you would have done had you learned nothing is always available to it. He thinks this is a substantive constraint on rational curiosity. I think the load-bearing step is the one that assumes the agent can act as if it hadn't heard, and that in the cases where knowing matters most it cannot. My example was the job candidate who is handed, free, the search committee's ranked shortlist five minutes before she walks into the room to give her talk: the information costs nothing, she cannot un-read it, and her delivery will show what she now knows, so the act talk as if I don't know I'm ranked fourth is not in her option set. Call these undelegable decisions — the informed agent must perform in person, in the knowing state, and its beliefs causally leak. My friend's reply was that I had only shown the signal has an effect on the world, which the theorem never denied, and that once you put belief states into the causal order you have changed the subject; I said that if that is the escape route, the theorem is a coherence test with no advice in it. We also had a smaller disagreement neither of us could settle: whether an agent who re-solves for the worst case over a set of priors after each signal can be hurt by free information, or whether that too collapses back into a failure of probabilistic sophistication.

Write a dialogue between two decision theorists, one defending the theorem and one attacking it, each trying to locate exactly where they disagree rather than to win. Both should be equally competent and should answer the other's actual points. The defender should state the theorem and its assumptions precisely enough that a philosopher of decision theory would recognize it — expected utility maximization, utility a function of acts and states alone, free disposal of the signal — and should press the reading on which the result is a constraint on coherence: that the candidate's real preference is to pay not to be told, that ex ante she would still accept the sealed-envelope version in which a clerk reads the ranking and does the talking, and that information is only ever harmful by doing something (changing preferences, causing outcomes), never by being merely known. The skeptic should give the undelegable-decision case its strongest form, answer the charge that it has merely smuggled in extra causes, and put the dilemma sharply: either the theorem's antecedent fails in exactly the situations that make ignorance valuable, or its consequent is a tautology about option sets. Each side should concede at least one genuine point, and the exchange should end with the disagreement intact but narrower and better located than it began.

Structure the response in two titled sections: The dialogue (around 800 words), then Where each of us was wrong (around 150 words) — a short note on which lines belong to me and which to my friend, and what each of us should take back.
