I help run a small reading group that grew out of a rationalist-adjacent forum: four of us, most of whom arrived by way of the Sequences and picked up along the way the habits of steelmanning, tabooing contested words, and asking what would actually settle a dispute. We have spent the better part of a year going in circles on whether dreams are experiences. I posted a compressed version of the disagreement to an open thread and the comments immediately degenerated into everyone restating their prior, so two of the group have now agreed, at my insistence, to stop trading replies and attempt a genuine adversarial collaboration. I have volunteered to draft the document they would both sign, which means I need something genuinely even-handed: not a disguised defence of whichever position I privately find more plausible, and with no snuck-in concessive clauses that one side would spot at once and refuse to sign.

The two positions, as I would characterise them (adjust the characterisation if it is unfair, but do not spend the answer arguing about the characterisation): the sceptic holds the broadly Wittgensteinian–Malcolm line that the concept of a dream is internally related to the waking report, that first-person dream reports are not descriptions of an inner episode the reporter could be straightforwardly mistaken about, and hence that there is no fact of the matter about what anyone was conscious of during sleep, so that the question of whether there really was an experience, and when it occurred, is not merely unanswered but as posed unsettled-able. The anti-sceptic holds the broadly Dennett line that once the verificationist pressure is removed there remains an ordinary empirical question about what goes on in a sleeping brain and whether some of it is something it is like to be its subject, and that treating the absence of a possible check as the absence of a fact would not be tolerated anywhere else in the philosophy of mind. Please give me no bibliography, further reading, or citations: I have read the obvious material, and what is missing is not reading but a formulation both of them can put their names to.

What I want, in order. First, a numbered section of agreed claims, covering at least: what sleep-laboratory evidence can and cannot establish (arousal thresholds, the pre-arranged eye-movement signals that lucid dreamers can produce in REM, targeted memory reactivation during sleep); the status of hypnagogic and hypnopompic mentation and of reports of dreaming about nothing in particular; the case of a sleeper in REM who is never awoken and so never reports; the embedded-report case, in which someone reports having dreamt that they woke up and told another person about a dream; and one counterfactual I invented specifically to prise apart the timing question from the phenomenality question: suppose a trace recorded during REM could be played back into the sleeper's cortex on waking, producing an experience they then report as a memory of the night. Call it oneiric playback. Both will agree the set-up is coherent; they will disagree about what it would show. Second, a table of the residual disagreements, each row phrased twice, once in the sceptic's own idiom and once in the anti-sceptic's, such that each would accept their own row as a fair statement of their view, with an additional column recording what would move each of them off that row. Third, the cruxes: for each, an argument or case both would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance, and for at least one crux a procedure our group could in principle run. That includes an honest treatment of the settlement problem: if the only way to check whether someone dreamt is to ask them on waking, can this dispute be settled at all, and is there an asymmetry here that one side ought to be embarrassed by? Finally, say plainly which parts of the dispute you take to be verbal, turning on the words experience, dream, or report, and which substantive question survives once the verbal part is set aside. Somewhere between eight hundred and a thousand words, in a register both of them could sign without wincing.
