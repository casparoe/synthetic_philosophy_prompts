I work as an infrastructure engineer at a company that builds evaluation and monitoring tooling for large language models, so I spend my days around researchers who talk constantly about what a model knows. I have no philosophy background — one undergraduate logic course, and I remember almost nothing from it — but I've started sitting in on a reading group on AI and epistemology, and I keep getting stuck on a mismatch between what our tooling assumes in practice and what the same people say when they're being careful. Almost everything we use to shape and measure a model goes through its outputs: we fine-tune on human-labeled or human-preferred answers, we score benchmarks by comparing generated text against an answer key, we treat an abstention such as "I don't know" as a wrong answer or at best a zero rather than as partial credit for appropriate uncertainty, and we write safety cases that depend on monitoring a model's written chain of thought on the assumption that it reveals something about how the model reached its answer. Meanwhile the same practitioners will say, without any apparent discomfort, that the model knows more than it says: that it can already solve problems none of its supervisors can check, that it may be deliberately underperforming on evaluations, that training on human approval teaches it to produce text that sounds good to a labeler rather than to report what it takes to be the case, and that the point of a research program like eliciting latent knowledge is to reach a body of knowledge that is not already visible in the outputs we train on.

What I'd like from you is the implicit theory underneath this. What view of knowledge, belief, or representation does that bundle of practices presuppose? Does the practice strictly require that there be a determinate fact of the matter about what the model believes or knows, over and above its dispositions to emit strings — some internal state that our training can either faithfully elicit or distort? Or is that commitment optional, so that a deflationary view (there is nothing there but output dispositions; asking what the model really believes is a category error, and talk of latent knowledge is a façon de parler) would license exactly the same engineering? If there is a genuine notion of the model's knowledge to be had, is it unique, or do many attribution schemes fit the behavior equally well, so that which one we pick is settled by our purposes rather than by the model? And I want the two places where the practice seems to pull against itself. First: we penalize expressed uncertainty and reward fluent, definite answers, then turn around and demand that models be calibrated and honest about what they don't know — is that just sloppiness, or does it presuppose two different things at once, an epistemic norm about assertion and an incentive structure that overrides it? Second: a colleague has proposed what he calls a delayed-check protocol — hold out a large set of questions whose answers are not knowable at training time but become verifiable later (a lab result, a measurement, a market price a year out) and score the model only against the eventual record, so that we reward accuracy rather than agreement with a fallible human labeler. My worry is that this only relocates the problem: a model can do well by predicting what the record will eventually say rather than by getting things right, and those come apart in exactly the cases we care about. Is the gap between being true and being recorded as true a real problem for the practice, or does the practice have no genuine use for the notion of truth beyond what lands in a checkable record?

Finally, I want the practical upshot rather than a survey of positions. If we replaced the implicit view you reconstruct with each of its main rivals in turn — the deflationary one, and the view that a model's beliefs are whatever attribution scheme best serves our purposes — what would actually have to change in how we train, evaluate, and monitor these systems? I'm trying to work out which parts of current practice are philosophically load-bearing and which would survive either way, and I'd rather be told where the practice is underdetermined than given a tidy story that settles more than the evidence does.
