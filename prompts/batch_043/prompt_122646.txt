My flatmate does ML, and last night after a few beers he killed my whole argument with one line: "no free lunch, mate — average over every possible world and every learner does exactly as well as random guessing, so there's no reason to think induction works here. We just got lucky that our world happens to be one of the friendly ones."

I'm sure that doesn't follow, but I can't say why, and I think part of the problem is that "no free lunch" is doing about four different jobs at once in his mouth. So: can you lay out the menu of precise claims that line could be making, from the ones that are basically bookkeeping (of course you need some bias before you can learn anything) up to the ones that would genuinely be a skeptical bombshell? For each one I want a verdict: trivial, substantive-and-probably-true, or actually false — plus whether it's something the no-free-lunch results themselves give you, or an extra philosophical premise someone bolted on afterwards.

A few things I specifically need pinned down:

1. If the theorem-ish reading is an average over *all* possible worlds or all possible targets, then any success here has to be paid for by failure somewhere else. Does that make the "we just got lucky" part incoherent — i.e. the result can't even express the thought that things might have gone the other way? Or can he restate the luck point using a non-uniform weighting over worlds, and if so where does that weighting come from and who's entitled to it?

2. He says the interesting reading isn't the average one at all, it's what I'd call *bias-egalitarianism*: no inductive bias is better than any other, except relative to a choice of worlds that is itself arbitrary, so preferring simplicity (or smoothness, or whatever) is just picking a lottery ticket with more confident body language. Is that a consequence of the results or a separate claim? And if it's separate, is it true? I go back and forth: half the time it feels like it collapses into something trivially true ("no bias wins everywhere") and half the time into something obviously false (we have a mountain of evidence that some biases work here).

3. Where does the old grue-style trick fit? Every time I think I've got hold of a reading that says something substantive, someone points out you can gerrymander a predicate that fits all the data so far and diverges tomorrow, and then I lose the thread of whether that's the same point as no-free-lunch or a different point wearing the same hat.

4. Does any of it bear on how much we should trust science's track record? I have a suspicion that the line is really only an argument against a certain *style* of justifying induction — the a priori, assumption-free style — and not against induction itself. If that's right, he's proven something nobody was denying.
