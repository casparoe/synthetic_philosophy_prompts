I'm on a three-person desk at a mid-sized specialty insurer where we price one-off risks: a touring exhibition of fragile works, a completion bond on an indie film, first-of-a-kind industrial equipment, a single music festival in a floodplain. Call it thirty of these a year, anywhere from $200k to $40m of exposure, and every number we write down gets re-derived by an outside reviewer each quarter — if they can't reconstruct it from the file, it comes back to us with a nasty note. I've got maybe half a day per file, my two colleagues' stats background is basically the underwriting exams, and our bonus is on premium volume rather than loss ratio, so you can guess which direction the numbers drift.

Here's the actual problem, and its not that we're bad at base rates. We've read all the outside-view stuff, we know you're supposed to look for a class instead of telling a story about the case. The problem is that for any one of these risks there are six classes you could honestly reach for — all touring exhibitions, touring exhibitions with four or more venue changes, exhibitions of works on paper, projects with a first-time logistics partner, projects where the counterparty is late on other jobs — and the frequencies differ by an order of magnitude. Which means the number isn't really being found, it's being picked, and it's getting picked to justify a price somebody already wants. I want to stop that.

So: write me a pre-registered procedure my desk can run on a file, in a fixed order, with what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure stops with a hard no (we do not issue a number), and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is really about our risk appetite and not about facts. A few things it has to handle, because these are the places we actually screw up:

- A membership test. I want a class to be disqualified unless you could say, before the outcome was known, whether a historical case was in it. Classes like "delays that turned out to be genuinely unavoidable" or "claims that were really accidental" are only sortable after the fact, and we've literaly used them. Tell me what to do when the only class with decent data fails this test.
- How narrow to go. Give me an actual cutoff for how far down you split a class before the sample is too thin to mean anything, and be honest that whatever number you give me is a judgment call wearing a lab coat.
- What to do when the natural class has one or zero members. There has to be something better than "guess," but I also don't want a step that launders a guess into a number.
- A ledger rule. We keep a register of the classes we've used, and I caught us counting the same shipment as in-class for one risk and out-of-class for another, six weeks apart. I need a rule for reusing a class without re-describing old cases to fit, and for adding new cases as they come in.
- An adversarial step. Someone whose interest runs the other way picks the rival class and gets a number out of it. If the two numbers diverge by more than some margin, we don't split the difference — we escalate. Suggest the margin and say what escalation actually looks like.
- It shouldn't presuppose that probabilities are frequencies. Two of us think these numbers are just calibrated credences and the reference class is an anchor you adjust off of, not the definition of the number. Make the procedure work on either view, or tell me exactly which step takes a side.

Then run it on three files. (1) A touring exhibition, ~90 fragile works, four venues, 14 months, two of the venues have never handled this kind of work, and the shipper is new to us: probability that at least one venue loses ten or more days to damage or transit failure. (2) A completion bond on an indie feature, first-time director, experienced line producer, 42-day shoot, one exterior location somewhere with a real rainy season, completion cashflow from a distributor that's already late on two other films: probability the film delivers more than 15 days late. (3) The one I think you'll tell me I can't price: a first-of-a-kind direct air capture unit with no operating history anywhere, availability guarantee over year one. If the procedure stops at no on that one, say so and tell me what I hand the chief underwriting officer instead of a number.

Also flag which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and where the whole thing will misfire — especially the ways my desk will find to shop for a class until we get the answer we wanted. No citations or reading lists, I need something three underwriters can run in an afternoon. In American English please.
