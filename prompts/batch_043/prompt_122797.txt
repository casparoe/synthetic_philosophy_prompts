I work in a machine learning theory group, and there is an argument I have now had, in nearly identical form, with my officemate, with two visitors, and with a referee: whether Solomonoff induction — the universal prior that weights each computable hypothesis by 2 to the minus its Kolmogorov complexity, together with the dominance result saying that its cumulative expected prediction error against any computable source is bounded by a machine-dependent constant — constitutes a real vindication of Occam's razor, or merely a mathematical curiosity dressed up as one. Nobody ever moves. The pro side says simplicity is thereby shown to be what optimal prediction looks like rather than an aesthetic preference, that the invariance theorem guarantees the choice of reference machine shifts things only by a constant and so cannot matter, and that minimum-description-length practice inherits the warrant. The con side says the prior is uncomputable and so cannot be a norm for any agent that has to act, that the constant that invariance tells us to ignore is unbounded and can be made arbitrarily large, hence can swamp any finite run of predictions, that for any hypothesis you like there is a universal machine that makes it the simple one, and that in any case the razor scientists actually use ranks theories stated in human vocabulary — this differential equation, that equation plus an extra term — whereas Kolmogorov complexity ranks bit strings, so the theorem, even if true, is about something else. A third voice says the point was never the infinite-limit result but the practice of two-part codes, and the theorem is only inspiration.

My own sense is that 'Occam's razor' is being used to name at least three separable theses that nobody distinguishes: razor-as-prior-weight (simplicity is the right way to distribute credence), razor-as-search-order (simplicity is the right order in which to consider hypotheses under a computational budget), and razor-as-prediction-guarantee (simple-first prediction converges as well as anything can). I also suspect a hidden empirical question is doing the work: whether the description languages human scientists actually use form a class over which simplicity orderings are near-stable — a kind of quasi-invariance that is not the mathematician's invariance theorem, since the latter is a statement about all universal machines and mine would be a contingent fact about our practices — and that neither side has any evidence about this, or even a way to gather it. I offer both as hypotheses, not as conclusions.

What I want is a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement, not a verdict on who is right and not a survey.
