I'm a high school senior who runs our school's philosophy club, and I want to close our last meeting of the year with a sestina about Thomson's lamp — the one that is switched at half a minute, three-quarters, seven-eighths, and so on, so that the flips pile up at the one-minute mark with no final flip among them.

The form should carry the idea rather than decorate it. Choose six end-words whose locked rotation feels like the accelerating flips: each stanza another halving, the same words returning in a cycle they cannot break out of, no stanza ever getting to be the last one. The three-line envoi should not resolve anything. It should feel like arriving at the limit — all six words crowded in at once, none of them given the final say, so that having every state and having no state come to the same thing.

Two points I want it to get right. The first is the contrast I keep coming back to: with a dial that turns half a rotation at each step, nobody expects a determinate final orientation, but with two states the question on or off feels compulsory, and that feeling is doing all the work. The second is symmetry — you can swap the labels on and off throughout and tell the identical story, so the story cannot be what selects an answer. I would like the poem to land, without preaching, on the thought that the description fixes every instant before the limit and is silent at the limit, which is a limit on the story rather than a contradiction in the lamp.

Keep it followable when read aloud in one pass, and please answer in American English.
