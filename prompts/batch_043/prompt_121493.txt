I've been reading about animal ethics on my own for a few months — no philosophy background, I got into it after going vegetarian last year and then fell down a rabbit hole — and there's a guy on a podcast I listen to who keeps using Kant as his punching bag. He's self-taught like me but way more confident, and this is kind of a hobbyhourse of his. His summary of Kant's position goes roughly like this: "Kant says animals are things and not persons, so they have no rights and we have literally no duties to them — they're just resources lying around for us. The only reason not to be cruel to a dog is purely instrumental: cruelty hardens you, so you'll end up crueller to people afterwards, and that downstream effect on humans is the only thing that makes it wrong. Basically Kant is Descartes with extra steps — animals are complicated machines that don't really feel anything, so there isn't anything there to be cruel to in the first place. And you can see the view is broken because of what it implies: if you were the last human on earth, torturing animals would be totally fine, since there's nobody left to be corrupted and no people to be nasty to later. Same for factory farming — on his own terms it could only be wrong because of what it does to the character of the people running it or eating the meat, never because of what it does to the pigs. Which is why every Kantian who works on animals now has had to quietly drop the whole direct-versus-indirect duty thing and staple animal ethics onto his system from the outside."

Some of that I'm pretty sure is unfair — the Descartes bit especially, because if animals don't feel anything then why does his argument keep going on about their suffering? — but the last-person-on-earth case I genuinely can't see a way out of, and I dont have anyone to ask. I also saw someone mention (I can't remember where, some blog comment) that there's a philosopher who is herself a Kantian and argues you can get real, direct duties to animals out of Kant's own framework without cheating, which makes me doubt the "everyone abandoned it" part.

So: how much of this would an actual Kant scholar or a sympathetic Kantian accept, and where is he caricaturing or sliding between two things Kant keeps apart? I'm especially interested in whether the duty is really supposed to be about future effects on other people, or whether it's meant to be owed to yourself somehow, and whether that difference is enough to block the desert-island implication or just moves the problem around. And does his big conclusion — that Kantian ethics is useless for animals and you have to be a utilitarian about them — actually follow from the parts of the summary that are accurate? If it helps, I'd also like to know what would even have to be true for a duty to animals to count as "direct" in Kant's sense, and whether the podcast guy is helping himself to the assumption that only direct duties really count.
