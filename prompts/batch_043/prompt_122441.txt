Me and two friends from a philosophy of probability reading group got into it after class on tuesday and I've been going in circles for a week, so here it is. We were on the reference class problem — the thing where any single case sits in indefinitely many classes with different frequencies, so a claim like "the probability that this particular 52-year-old smoker develops lung cancer within ten years is about 0.3" seems to have no answer until you say which class you're reading the frequency off. Our tutor said this is mainly a headache for frequentists and that the Bayesians can go home early. But when I wrote it out as an argument it started looking like it either collapses under its own weight or eats considerably more than frequentism, and I can't tell which. Also, if it does work, it seems to take single-event causal attribution in tort law down with it — the asbestos-exposure guy whose cancer "was probably caused by" the exposure — which is litigated every day and can't just be nonsense. Here's what I've got:

(1) A statement of the form "the probability that individual a has outcome A is p" isn't complete as it stands: it gets a truth value only relative to a reference class C containing a, and means something like "the frequency of A in C is p."

(2) For any individual a, any outcome A, and any number r between 0 and 1, there is a class containing a in which the frequency of A is exactly r. Sketch: start from the natural class (52-year-old smokers), frequency p. Now define a property P_S = "being one of the members of S," where S is whatever subset of that class you like; pick S so that the proportion of its members with A is r. a is in the class of P_S-ers, and the frequency of A there is r.

(3) Nothing in (1) picks out one of these classes as the right one, and they disagree arbitrarily badly, so there's no fact of the matter about single-case probabilities: for any a and A, every value in [0,1] is equally well supported, and "the probability that a has A" is indeterminate.

(4) But single-case probability judgments demonstrably earn their keep: actuarial tables, clinical risk scores, and precipitation forecasts come out calibrated — of the days forecast at 70% rain, roughly 70% get rain; of the patients scored at 30%, roughly 30% have the event — and insurers, surgeons and farmers are better off for trusting them.

(5) That calibration is only possible if, for each single case, there's a correct probability the forecasts are tracking. If every value were equally well supported, systematic calibration would be a miracle.

(6) So (3) is false. And since (3) came out of (1) plus (2), and (1) is basically just what the reference class problem says, it's (2) that has to go: some classes containing a must be inadmissible.

(7) But the only filter I can think of is epistemic — a class is admissible only if we have enough members in it for the frequency to be stable, the usual move of taking the narrowest class we have decent statistics for. And that makes the probability depend on how much data we happen to have: it shifts when the registry updates, two equally competent agents with different data sets get different answers, and we've lost the objectivity that made (5) attractive. Which is absurd.

So: which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? I'd most like to keep (1) and (4) — (1) feels like a description of the problem rather than a commitment, and (4) is just a fact about forecasting practice — but I'll drop whatever's cheapest if you'll price the alternatives, including what (5) is really assuming. My friend Priya thinks the exit is to ban classes defined by mentioning the outcome ("you can't use the class of people who get cancer or don't"), but that seems useless to me: take some biomarker or postcode that happens to be a near-perfect proxy for the outcome and define the class that way — you've gerrymandered just as hard without ever naming the outcome. Is there a version of her ban that actually does the work, or is the proxy problem fatal to any fix that operates only on the vocabulary? Someone else suggested requiring causal homogeneity instead — the right class is one that's causally uniform with respect to A — and I can't tell whether that escapes the argument or just relocates it one level up. And does any of this reach the Bayesians, or did our tutor get that right? Tell me where you'd put your confidence on each claim and flag the bit you're least sure of.
