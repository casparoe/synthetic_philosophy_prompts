I sit on the committee that writes the consultation triggers for a hospital clinical ethics service — the list of circumstances in which a treating team must call an ethics consult rather than decide on its own. A senior member keeps repeating the old proverb "circumstances alter cases" to block any written list: any trigger we could codify, she says, would be either vacuous or wrong (we had been about to write "consult whenever a competent patient insists on leaving against medical advice," and she pointed out that where the patient is leaving over a billing dispute and the clinical question is already settled, a consult adds nothing), so the money should go to training clinicians' judgment instead. I want to take her seriously, but I can't tell how strong a claim she needs.

Please lay out, as a menu, the distinct theses the proverb could be expressing — from the platitude that a reason can be outweighed by other reasons; through the claim that a feature counting in favor of acting in one case can be no reason at all, or a reason against, in another; through the denial that any true exceptionless moral principle can be finitely stated; up to the claim that moral judgment is a perceptual skill that cannot be codified even as a rough guide — and for each say whether it is plausible, whether it is merely a truism dressed up, and whether it would actually support writing nothing at all. I am also unsure whether the thesis doing the work here is a claim about what makes actions right, about how we ought to decide, or about how moral competence is acquired, and whether those can come apart: could someone grant her the first and still reasonably insist on a list of merely default, overridable triggers, given that our reviewers are junior, rotate frequently, and are expected to be consistent with one another? Which reading is she most likely invoking, and is there a stable middle option — a short list explicitly framed as defaults rather than as rules — that even a defender of the strong readings should accept?
