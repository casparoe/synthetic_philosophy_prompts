Last night, over several hours, my oldest friend and I returned for perhaps the tenth time to an argument we have never resolved: whether there would have been a Nazi Germany without Hitler. We are both amateurs — I read philosophy, he reads history — and we kept discovering that the disagreement was not where we thought it was. We agreed quickly that some kind of authoritarian, revanchist, antisemitic regime in Germany in the 1930s was close to overdetermined by the Depression, the collapse of the Weimar party system, the elites' turn to presidential government, and the shared aims of the German right. We agreed, too, that the industrialised murder of European Jewry is the outcome that carries the moral weight of the question, and the one where our intuitions diverge most. Where we stalled was the point at which the structural account seems to dissolve the individual: he said, in effect, that if the regime's radicalisation came from below and was merely ratified at the top, then the top did not have to be Hitler; and I said that a process of anticipating and outdoing the leader's wishes still presupposes a leader whose wishes were known and stated. We then noticed that we had never settled whether removing Hitler means removing him in 1889, 1923, January 1933, or 1938, and neither of us had any idea how to think about that difference.

I would like you to continue the argument for us, as something I can send him.

Write a dialogue of roughly 1,400–1,800 words between two scholars of the period, old enough to have had this argument before and equally competent at it, one defending the claim that Hitler was indispensable to what happened and one defending the claim that the structural explanation is the one that does the real work. Give them names and distinct voices. Neither is a strawman, and neither is permitted to win by talking past the other: every major move has to be answered, and each speaker should concede at least one thing that genuinely costs them.

Please have the dialogue cover the following.

First, fix the question. Separate at least four outcomes that the argument usually conflates: an authoritarian right-wing regime in Germany by the mid-1930s; that regime being led by the NSDAP under something recognisably its programme; a war of conquest in Europe; and the genocide. Say explicitly whether the indispensability claim can be true of one of these and false of another. Then confront the possibility that the original question is partly constitutive — if the regime is defined in part by personal allegiance to Hitler, then of course there is no Nazi Germany without him — and restate the question in whatever form the two speakers agree is worth arguing about.

Second, work through what I'll call the depth of the deletion. Removing Hitler as a child, as a failed putschist, as an appointee to the chancellorship, and as an established dictator after the consolidation of his rule seem to be four different counterfactuals, and the two speakers should say whether their claim's strength varies with depth and why. Include the backtracking worry that deleting his political career requires changing antecedent conditions — the war, the collapse of the old order, the Bavarian milieu that made him — which were themselves structural products, so that the counterfactual may quietly smuggle in a different 1933 rather than the same one minus a man.

Third, engage the distinction between filling a role and creating one. In the elite bargaining of 1932–33, the NSDAP could supply something no other party could: a mass plebiscitary base willing to legitimate a presidential-authoritarian restoration. If that is right, then Hitler may have been structurally indispensable at that moment — the structure's requirements were met by exactly one available person — while it remains open that an earlier deletion would have produced a different occupant of a role that had not yet been created. Both speakers should address what indispensability at a moment does and does not establish about indispensability for the outcome.

Fourth, make each side answer the other's strongest evidence. The indispensabilist must deal with the fact that implementation was carried out by the army, the civil service and the party bureaucracy; that radicalisation arose substantially from competition among agencies; that the party's vote share had already fallen from its peak before the appointment; and that comparable interwar cases suggest a structural pattern. The structuralist must deal with the fact that the movement was personalist and had no succession mechanism; that a distributed apparatus can only co-ordinate on a shared guess about what the leader wants if there is a leader with known obsessions; and that the specific decisions of 1939–41 look hard to derive from structural pressure alone.

Finally, have each speaker name the single claim they would have to abandon if the other were right, and the single piece of evidence that would most move them. No footnotes, no citations, no invented documents or quotations: argue from features of the record that any historian would grant — the electoral trajectory, the elite manoeuvring around the appointment and the Enabling Act, the 1934 purge of the SA leadership, the 1938 crisis in the army high command, the killing operations in the East. Do not resolve the dispute or append a synthesis; end with the remaining disagreement stated precisely enough that my friend and I can tell what we would have to find out in order to settle it.
