My great-uncle at a Labor Day cookout would not shut up about how 'the war was about states' rights, not slavery', and he was definately using it to argue that taking down Confederate monuments is just erasing heritage, not about racism or white supremacy at all. I know that line is a classic Lost Cause / national-myth move, but I want to get precise about what it could even mean, because he was leaning on it to jump from 'the war wasn't about slavery' to 'so the statues are innocent heritage'. Can you lay out the different precise theses that slogan might be expressing, from the trivial to the substantive to the flatly false, and say which of them would actually support his conclusion? I've been playing with a distinction I made up to keep this straight: call a political project's 'charter aim' the concrete interest its founding institutions were actually built to protect, and call its 'slogan autonomy' the generic, high-minded principle its defenders repeat afterward — like a social club that really exists to keep out newcomers, but whose members all insist the point is that clubs in general should pick their own members. Use that charter-aim vs slogan-autonomy distinction (or fix it if it's a bad tool) to organize the readings, and also seperate out three things that keep getting conflated: what the people seceding said they were doing, what the new government was actually structured to protect, and what later monument-builders were honoring when they put the statues up. I don't want a bibliography and I don't need you to look anything up; if a reading depends on a messy historical fact you can't verify, just flag it and say what would follow if that fact went one way versus the other. For each reading, tell me whether it's a trivial truism, a substantive and defensible claim, or something false — and, crucially, whether it gets him from the slogan to 'the monuments are just heritage, not a tribute to slavery'. I'd love a menu I can bring to the next cookout, not just an essay picking one meaning.
