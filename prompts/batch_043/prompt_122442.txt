hey — I want to run a correspondence exercise. Please be Jean-Jacques Rousseau, writing in the summer of 1758 from the little house at Mont-Louis near Montmorency, just after finishing your letter to d'Alembert about the theatre project in Geneva. Rules: first person, actual letters rather than essays, a page or so each, no bullet points, and don't quote yourself back at me at length. Argue only from what you'd already published by then — the two Discourses, the Letter itself, the dedication to the Republic — and don't help yourself to arguments from books you haven't written yet. You can be prickly but not a scold: concede readily where a proposal really does dodge your objections, and remember that Geneva is not Paris, so the thing you refused to demand in Paris you may well demand at home. What you will not give up is that a people is not made better by sitting still and watching other people pretend. Stay in charachter until I say stop.

I'll play a member of the Council of Two Hundred who agrees with you more than he'll admit, and who is trying to manoeuvre you into admitting it. Here's my first letter recieve it as such:

Monsieur — we have read you, and the Small Council has shelved the plan for a standing theatre with a salaried troupe. In its place we propose something else, and I should like to see whether you can consistently object to it. Once a year, in the open air on the Plainpalais, two hundred of our own citizens — men and boys only, no professionals, no pay, no actresses — will perform a single play: the night of the Escalade, or Tell, closing with the magistrates handing out prizes. No love plot, no comic servants, no one who does this for a living, no repeat performances. Afterwards everyone eats together at long tables in public and goes home before dark. By your own argument this is not a theatre at all but one of those republican festivals you say we ought to have instead of one. So either give it your blessing, or admit that your real objection was never to actors, actresses, and love-plots, but to imitation itself — and then tell us why the imitation in a festival is innocent while this one is not, and why a citizen who plays Tell for an afternoon is corrupted by it when a citizen who watches him is not.
