My supervisor has been walking me through cases, ruling on whether something is a "real pattern" and which of the three stances (physical, design, intentional) actually earns its keep on it. Her verdicts so far: the centre of gravity of a falling body and a planet's orbit — real pattern, but physical stance only, nothing else gets a grip. A thermostat and a hand-coded chess program — real pattern, design stance is the cheap apt one, intentional stance is just a façon de parler. A wasp's nesting behaviour and genes "trying" to get themselves copied — real pattern, and here the intentional stance genuinely pays for itself. The shape of a soap bubble and Zipf's law in word frequencies — real pattern, but no stance has any purchase. A face in the clouds, and a sonnet a monkey happened to type — not a real pattern. A decision tree overfit to noise — not a real pattern. A glider in Conway's Life — real pattern, and you can talk about it in design-stance terms, though she muttered that Life was designed by somebody, so it might not prove much.

The case we have to rule on next: a cellular automaton rule picked by hashing today's date, so nobody chose it for anything. You can say where it'll be in a thousand steps with a few lines of arithmetic instead of simulating every cell, so it's a massive compressor — but nothing ever selected for it, it was never designed, no variants ever competed, and it dosen't turn up in any neigbouring rule.

Does she call it a real pattern, and does the design or intentional stance get any grip on it (is it "supposed to" copy itself, does it "want" to)? I care less about the verdict than about which rule she's been running: cheapest-prediction/compression, some history requirement where a pattern has to be the product of design or selection, or a multiple-realizability test where a real pattern has to survive being implemented in another medium. Tell me what each rule predicts here and which one you think best fits her track record.
