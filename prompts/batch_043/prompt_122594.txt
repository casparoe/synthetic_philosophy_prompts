Over dinner last night I had a long and entirely unresolved argument with a friend about whether Goodman's so-called new riddle of induction is a genuinely distinct problem from Hume's older problem of induction, or only the same problem wearing a more eye-catching costume. I would be grateful for a careful comparison of the two, and for a verdict on which of us came closer to the truth.

Let me state both problems as we used them, so that nothing hangs on either of us having summarised Hume or Goodman correctly.

The old problem: our evidence consists of observed regularities. Nothing in that evidence, together with any purely logical or conceptual truth, entails anything about unobserved cases. So any inference from the observed to the unobserved requires a substantive premise to the effect that the unobserved resembles the observed, and that premise cannot itself be justified either by demonstration or by experience without circularity.

The new problem: every emerald examined so far has been green. Fix a future date, and say that a thing is 'grue' just in case it is examined before that date and green, or not examined before that date and blue. (For symmetry one can also introduce 'bleen': examined before the date and blue, or not examined before it and green.) Both 'all emeralds are green' and 'all emeralds are grue' fit every observation made so far perfectly; yet after the date they predict different colours. So the question of which generalisations are confirmed by their instances is not settled merely by settling whether nature is uniform.

What we actually said to each other, in order:

1. Her: the new riddle is the old riddle with the lights turned up. Hume's challenge is that observed instances never entail anything about unobserved ones, so induction needs a substantive uniformity premise that cannot be non-circularly justified. Grue simply exhibits how very many candidate uniformities are consistent with any finite body of evidence. Once you see that, the grue puzzle is Hume's puzzle plus an unusually vivid illustration of the underdetermination. One problem, two presentations.

2. Me: no. Suppose I hand you the uniformity premise gratis: stipulate that there is some true, exceptionless generalisation covering all emeralds, and that the unobserved really does resemble the observed in the respects in which it has so far. Even with all of that granted, the grue puzzle is untouched, because 'all emeralds are green' and 'all emeralds are grue' are each perfectly uniform and perfectly exceptionless regularities — they differ only in the vocabulary in which the uniformity is stated. The old problem asks whether to trust induction at all; the new problem asks which of the many regularities that fit the data is the one to project, and no answer to the first question settles the second. The new riddle concerns the form and vocabulary of projected hypotheses, not the justification of projection.

3. Her: that is either empty or circular. 'The generalisation our evidence supports' already presupposes a choice of predicate, so the gift you claim to be granting is not really granted. And in any case you have relocated the bump rather than removed it: we have excellent, ordinary, inductive reasons for preferring green, since green has figured in an enormous number of successful past projections and grue in none. That reason is exactly the sort of reason Hume was asking us to justify. So we are back inside the old problem, and the new riddle is a special case of it in which the uniformity premise has to be formulated in one vocabulary rather than another.

4. Me: the relocation is the whole point. If the only support for projecting green is the past success of green-projections, that support is unusable as a solution, because in order to count the past successes you must already have decided which predicate they were successes of; stated in grue-and-bleen terms the very same observations are equally orderly. More importantly, the new riddle does not need time at all. Define 'grane': a thing is grane just in case it is in Europe and green, or outside Europe and blue. Every emerald examined so far was examined in Europe and was green. The emeralds in Australia are not future emeralds, and nothing in this case asks whether the future will resemble the past; the puzzle is entirely about which regularities extend to an unexamined region of the instance space. If the two problems were one and the same, this variant ought to be impossible to construct.

5. Her: it is trivially possible, because Hume's problem was never essentially about time. The old problem generalises without residue to unobserved instances of any kind: the Australian emeralds are simply unobserved, and the substantive premise required is that the unobserved resembles the observed in the respects that matter. Your grane is grue with a date replaced by a coordinate, and the moral it teaches is Hume's moral — that which respects matter is not something the evidence alone can settle. If anything, grane supports my side.

6. Me: granted, on time. But here is the residual difference. A Humean sceptic can at least be answered after a fashion, whether by some pragmatic vindication of induction or by going Bayesian and letting conditionalisation do the work. Grue survives all of that. The Bayesian must assign priors to hypotheses, and hypotheses come stated in a vocabulary; the choice of prior as between 'all green' and 'all grue' is settled neither by the evidence nor by the probability calculus, and if you say the prior should favour the simpler or better-entrenched predicate you have only renamed the problem. Different problems, then, and the new one is the deeper.

7. Her: in that case it is not a problem about induction at all. Which prior one ought to have over hypotheses stated in which vocabulary is the general problem of priors; it arises in every domain of inference and has nothing specifically to do with projecting from observed to unobserved. You have not shown that the new riddle is distinct from Hume's; you have shown that it is not a riddle of induction.

8. Me: and what of the thought that the asymmetry between green and grue is metaphysical rather than epistemic — that green marks a natural property while grue is an artefact of how we carve things up, so that the right response is not 'green is better confirmed' but 'grue is not a possible respect of resemblance in the first place'? She said that thought concedes my case; I said it concedes hers. We left it there, hungry and irritated.

Specifically, I would like:

(i) a clean statement of what each problem is asking, and a judgement about whether each is best understood as a problem of justification, a problem about confirmation, or a problem in metaphysics;
(ii) a comparison: the two or three respects in which the problems genuinely come apart, and the two or three in which they collapse into one another;
(iii) a move-by-move adjudication of the eight exchanges above — for each, say whether the move succeeds, partly succeeds, or fails, with a sentence or two of justification. I would much rather be told plainly that one of my own moves is bad than be told that we were both partly right;
(iv) an answer to the question I raised at move 8, if it has one;
(v) what, if anything, both of us were missing.

There is no need for citations, references or reading suggestions — I have the relevant texts to hand — and I would rather you argued from the cases as I have set them out than from any doctrine about what is said elsewhere in the literature.
