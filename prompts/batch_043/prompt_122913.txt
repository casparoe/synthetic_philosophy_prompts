I sit on the collections committee of a small municipal museum, and we have been going in circles on a policy question that keeps getting settled by an analogy rather than by an argument. In 1971 the museum bought, in good faith, a seventeenth-century panel painting catalogued as by a named master. Three years ago technical examination and a connoisseur's report moved it to "imitator of, probably twentieth century." A majority of us want to keep it on the wall under a new label — "in the manner of," plus a short account of the reattribution. Our chair will not have it. A forgery, he says, is like a lie. A statement shown to be a lie does not become true by being footnoted, and a picture shown to be a forgery does not become genuine by being reattributed; the defect is in the thing and not in anyone's beliefs, so keeping the picture up is keeping the lie up, and the honest response is withdrawal.

I would like an audit of the analogy rather than a verdict on our case.

Give me the correspondence table first: for the lie, the speaker, the utterance, the propositional content, the audience, the intention to deceive, the moment of utterance, the later discovery, the retraction; and for the painting, the painter, the work, the catalogue entry or label, the visitor, whatever plays the role of intent, and whatever plays the role of the moment of utterance. Then tell me which of the mismatches actually matter for the conclusion he wants — permanent contamination, hence withdrawal — and which are noise.

Three cases I would like the table tested against:

One. Suppose the panel was made openly in the 1620s as a studio product, sold and catalogued as such for two and a half centuries, and was "upgraded" only in 1890, when a dealer dropped the qualifying phrase from a label. The object never changed; something a third party did in 1890 seems to have converted an honest object into a lie. If the analogy holds, what happened in 1890, to what, and why is the object rather than the label the site of the wrong?

Two. An imitator sells to a collector who knows exactly what he is buying, does not care, and hangs it in his study with an accurate label. Nobody is deceived and nothing is asserted to a dupe. Is there a lie here at all? If not, is the object nonetheless defective in the way the chair's conclusion requires?

Three. Genuine works are demoted and later promoted again when opinion turns. If attribution can move in both directions while the object stays numerically the same, can the analogy accommodate the upward case, or does it only work downhill?

Then the two directions of distortion. What does the analogy import into the target that objects do not have — a speaker with a datable intention, a content that can be re-asserted truthfully, an act whose wrongness is complete at the time and is not repaired by correction, the possibility of confessing — and what does it leave out on the other side, given that who made this is a causal claim about the world maintained by an ongoing institutional practice rather than a single speech act? Please keep separate the three questions the chair slides between: whether the picture is aesthetically inferior, whether it is morally tainted, and what an institution should do with it.

If the analogy cannot carry his conclusion, I want a candidate evaluated rather than just a dismissal. I have tried "perjury" — the wrong is institutional and is not undone by later truth-telling — and "plagiarism" — the wrong is a misdirection of credit and survives detection — and I cannot tell whether either is an improvement or merely relocates the difficulty.

Finally, tell me what would change your mind about which way we should go, and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail. My suspicion is that it will rest on an empirical claim about what visitors actually take away from a relabeled picture, so say explicitly if you are relying on one and what happens to your recommendation if it is false.
