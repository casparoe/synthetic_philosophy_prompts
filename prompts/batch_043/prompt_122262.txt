Two colleagues of mine — one a theorist who works on the thermodynamics of computation, the other a condensed-matter physicist who does not — got into an argument after a department colloquium on Maxwell's demon, and they asked me to referee it. I couldn't. I'd like you to adjudicate the exchange move by move, and I'd rather you showed the accounting than announced a verdict.

First, the apparatus they both accepted. Take a Szilard engine: a chamber holding a single molecule, in contact with a heat bath at temperature T. A partition is inserted at the midpoint; the demon measures which half the molecule is in and records the answer as one bit; the molecule then pushes a piston isothermally back out to the full volume, doing kT ln 2 of work and drawing kT ln 2 of heat from the bath. The partition is removed and reinserted for the next cycle. To run again, the demon's one-bit record has to be back in its standard state, and Landauer's principle — that resetting a bit by dumping it into the bath costs at least kT ln 2 of work — gives the standard exorcism: the work the demon wins is exactly paid back when its memory is cleared.

The disagreement is about whether that exorcism can be dodged. Call my two colleagues Nils and Hana.

Nils opens with a variant he calls the endless-ledger demon. Give the demon not a one-bit register but an indefinitely long tape of cells. Each cell is a particle in a symmetric double well: state 0 is the left well, state 1 is the right well, the barrier between them is high compared with kT so the cell holds its bit indefinitely, and the two wells have exactly the same energy. Every cell on a fresh tape is in state 0. Now modify the cycle: after the measurement and after the work has been extracted, the demon clears its record not by erasing it but by swapping it with the next fresh cell on the tape — a permutation of the joint state space, hence in principle reversible and in principle dissipationless. The record is returned to 0, the cell is left holding a bit correlated with where the molecule was, the tape advances, and nothing is ever erased. Nils's conclusion: Landauer's principle is not a necessary condition on the demon at all, only one sufficient way of failing to beat it. If the demon can be built as described, kT ln 2 of work per cycle is extracted from a single bath forever with no compensating cost, and the usual story about why demons fail must be wrong or incomplete.

Hana replies that the tape is not free. A fresh tape of known zeros is a low-entropy resource, thermodynamically on a par with a tank of fuel or a wound spring: a cell in a definite known state has k ln 2 less entropy than a cell carrying an unknown bit, and the demon consumes one such cell per cycle. She grants that the demon's own operation dissipates nothing, but insists the cost has merely been relocated — from the demon's eraser to the tape factory, since preparing a cell in state 0 from a cell in an unknown state just is erasure and costs at least kT ln 2. On this reading the endless-ledger demon is not a perpetual motion machine but an engine running on blank tape, and the second law is untouched.

Nils's reply is that the fresh and the used cell are physically on a par, and that this is the crux. A used cell is also a particle confined to one well of a symmetric double well; it has the same energy, the same accessible volume, the same temperature, and — the two wells being exactly degenerate — the same partition function, and therefore the same thermodynamic entropy, as a fresh cell. Thermodynamic entropy is a state function; it cannot depend on what anyone happens to know. So if Hana wants to book k ln 2 of entropy against the used cell and none against the fresh one, she has made entropy agent-relative, which would have the absurd consequence that you could run a heat engine off a tape merely by forgetting what is written on it. Therefore, he says, the tape is not a fuel, the swap really is free, and the paradox stands.

Hana then concedes the point about the individual cells and relocates the difference into the correlations. What distinguishes a fresh cell from a used one is not its own state but its relations: a fresh cell is uncorrelated with everything else in the world, whereas a used cell is correlated with the molecule's earlier position and hence with everything that position has since interacted with. Entropy properly attaches to joint states, and the joint entropy of the cell together with the rest of the world is k ln 2 lower when that correlation is present than the sum of the separate entropies would suggest. Correlations, she insists, are a perfectly physical, non-epistemic feature of a joint state — no one has to know about them for them to be there — so consuming uncorrelated degrees of freedom is consuming a real, finite resource, and that is exactly what the demon does once per cycle.

Nils's closing move is that this changes the subject. Correlations lower entropy only relative to the marginals, and Kelvin's formulation of the second law does not trade in marginals: it says that no device operating in a cycle can convert heat drawn from a single bath entirely into work with no other effect. His demon is cyclic — the chamber, the piston, the demon, and the register all return to their initial states each cycle — and the only things that change are tape cells whose own thermodynamic states, on Hana's own admission, are unchanged. All that has changed is which other things a given cell happens to be correlated with. Correlations are not a sink that can absorb kT ln 2 of entropy per cycle, and even if they were, the total entropy of the universe has not gone down, merely been rearranged — so nothing balances the kT ln 2 of heat that disappeared from the bath. Either Kelvin's law fails here, he concludes, or the reason demons fail must be something other than the tape paying for them.

Hana's last word was a construction rather than an argument. Take a cell known to be in state 0, remove the barrier, and let the particle expand isothermally into both wells against a load: you extract kT ln 2 of work from the bath and leave the cell carrying a random bit — precisely the time-reverse of reversible erasure. So a fresh cell has kT ln 2 of available work that a used cell lacks, and the fact that you need to know which well to couple your load to is no more an embarrassment here than it is for any other fuel.

Nils answered: your construction describes two cells in identical physical situations — a particle in one well of a symmetric double well — and says that one of them can do work and the other cannot. Show him the physical difference between them without using any form of the word know.

Please work through the exchange and grade each move, from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with the reasoning that earns the grade. Then say who is more right overall, and what both of them are missing. In particular I would like you to settle: whether a tape of fresh cells is a thermodynamic resource and, if so, what exactly the resource consists in; whether the endless-ledger demon as described actually threatens any standard formulation of the second law, and if not, where precisely the cost lands; and whether either party is entitled to the background idealization they both help themselves to, that measurement and reversible logic can be carried out with arbitrarily little dissipation. Where the two sides are using the same words to mean different things, say so. Assume ordinary thermodynamics; no citations or further reading are needed.
