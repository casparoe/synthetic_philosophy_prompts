Hey. I teach a third-year seminar on the ethics of reproduction (18 students, mostly philosophy majors with a few bioethics minors) and I'm writing the final right now. Full disclosure on why I care: my partner and I are in the middle of IVF and the clinic handed us a brochure about tacking on polygenic scoring, and I havent been able to stop thinking about it since, so I want this exam to go at the real crux instead of the stuff everyone half-remembers from week two.

What I want is ONE essay question — 60 minutes, about 1000 words, 30 marks, closed book — on procreative beneficence, that a student cannot pass by writing out the principle neatly and then listing three stock objections (the disability-rights critique, procreative autonomy, and some version of the eugenics worry). Please build the question around the crux below, and build the model answer and the marking scheme around it too.

The crux, as I see it — reframe it if you like, but keep it load-bearing:

The view says roughly this: of the possible children you could have, you should bring about the one expected to have the best life, or the best chance of the best life. The phrase I keep getting stuck on is of the possible children you could have. That comparator set is not handed to you by nature; it is partly something you make. You can wait six months and try again, you can do another stimulation cycle, you can use donor gametes, you can have children with a different partner, you can start earlier or later. So the option space is elastic and partly endogenous to your own earlier choices. That seems to give two horns:

1. If the set is fixed by whatever reproductive attempt you happen to be in the middle of, the principle looks arbitrarily indexed to a decision — when to try, with whom, whose gametes — that is itself morally loaded, and near enough empty as a guide.
2. If the set ranges over everything you could in principle bring about, the principle becomes an indefinitely expandable maximising instruction that either gives no determinate advice or tells you to keep enlarging your options forever.

Second layer I want in there: what if the traits being selected on are positional or competitive — height is the easy example, but something like expected educational attainment works too, where the payoff depends on what everyone else's children are like. If everyone follows the principle, the ranking reshuffles and nobody's child ends up better off. That looks like a collective-action or ratchet structure. So: does applying the principle to positional traits undercut the very consideration — the child's expected well-being — that was supposed to justify it in the first place? And is that an objection to the principle, or only to a naive reading of best life?

Third thing, if you can fit it: there's a move students always reach for, which is that we don't think parents are obliged to move to a better school district or change jobs or spend down their savings to improve an existing child's prospects, so why should they be obliged to pick the best embryo. I want the question to make them earn that analogy — what exactly would it have to show, and where might it break down (selection versus improvement, whether the child's identity is at stake, cost, the natural-lottery baseline). I dont want it just deployed.

Deliverables:

1. The question as the students will see it. I'd like a concrete vignette — some clinic scenario with, say, four embryos plus a realistic option to wait or do another cycle — and an explicit instruction to deal with the comparator-set problem and the positional-goods problem. Not a discuss-the-literature question.
2. A model answer, 700 to 900 words, that actually performs the moves rather than naming them.
3. A marking scheme: split the 30 marks into named components with marks each, say precisely which moves separate an A from a B and a B from a C, and give a one-line note for each component on how students standardly get it wrong.

Constraints: no citations, no further reading, nothing that depends on anyone knowing a specific paper or a year — mentioning Savulescu by name is fine but nothing should hinge on a title or date, since the exam is closed book and half of them will misremember anyway. Don't build the vignette around disability; I'm sick of that example and it lets them run a memorised script. And please don't let it turn into a discuss both sides and split the difference question — I want there to be answers that are just wrong.
