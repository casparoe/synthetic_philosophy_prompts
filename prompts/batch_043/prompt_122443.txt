Six of us in a reading group are working through Dennett's Darwin book (the cranes and skyhooks bit, the universal acid, the greedy reductionism complaint), and one guy in the group — he's a physicist — put this on the whiteboard last week and none of us could find the hole. He says it collapses both of Dennett's distinctions at once. I don't believe the conclusion, but I can't say which line is bad.

(1) Dennett's rule: any appearance of design has to be explained by a crane, a process that gets its power to do design-work from below, out of mindless parts interacting, and never by a skyhook, which is just posited as already able to do the design-work.

(2) Every explanation has to stop somewhere. Nobody actually cranks out an infinite chain, so any completed explanation rests on a terminus: facts or entities whose own capacity to do the explanatory work they are doing isn't itself explained by anything further in the story.

(3) Take the terminus of Dennett's own chain — say the laws of physics plus whatever minimal self-replicating chemistry you like. Those are doing all the design-work of the whole story, and nothing in the story explains why they can do it.

(4) By the definition in (1), anything that does design-work while its capacity to do that work is unexplained just is a skyhook. The definition doesn't say unless it's at the bottom, or unless it's simple, or unless it's mindless.

(5) So either Dennett's chain never terminates, in which case his acid never actually finishes a single explanation and Darwin gets no credit over God did it (which at least stops somewhere), or it terminates in a skyhook, and the difference between him and the creationist is only where you prefer to stop.

(6) And it spreads to the other distinction. Dennett says greedy reductionism explains too much too fast: it won't grant reality to the design-level or intentional-level patterns and tries to jump straight to physics. Good reductionism stops at the level where the pattern is real.

(7) But (5) just showed there's no principled place to stop, so any stopping point is arbitrary in the same way. If stopping at physics is fine, stopping at mind or at designedness is fine too. So greedy and good reductionism differ only in where the speaker finds it convenient to stop, and Dennett has no complaint left against the greedy reductionist — unless he can give a stopping rule that doesn't also license the skyhook.

(8) So: Dennett either gives up the no-skyhooks rule, or gives up the greedy reductionism complaint, or produces a principled asymmetry between bottoming out at the bottom and bottoming out at the top. We couldn't think of one that wasn't question-begging.

We tried one fix, which we called the Borrow Test: a terminus is legitimate only if the competence it's credited with isn't the very competence you were trying to explain. Physics isn't credited with foresight, so physics passes; God is, so God fails. That seems to block (5). But then the creationist in the group says God is simple and his designing isn't a competence he has over and above his nature, and we didn't have an answer to that; and I'm not sure physics really passes, since the bedrock is being credited with the capacity to throw up orderly structure out of sheer noise, which is a good chunk of what we wanted explained in the first place.

Which step do you reject? If it's (4), I want to know what the crane/skyhook distinction is actually tracking if it isn't explained-capacity versus unexplained-capacity. I'd rather keep (1) and the Borrow Test than keep (6) and (7) — I do think there's a real difference between good and greedy reductionism and I don't want to lose it, so tell me what keeping it costs me. And don't just tell me the distinction is really about foresight versus no foresight; or if that is the answer, tell me why that isn't a stopping rule wearing a disguise.
