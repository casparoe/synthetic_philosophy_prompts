Okay so I need help settling an argument I had with my roommate last night. We were up till like 1am going in circles about whether any of the current AI systems could be sentient, and we got nowhere, mainly because neither of us could say what would even count as an answer. But the bit I keep chewing on the next morning isn't the metaphysics, it's a design decision, and I think figuring out what that decision assumes would get me further than arguing about neurons or substrate or whatever.

Here's the thing. Every mainstream chatbot has this built in: if you ask whether it's conscious, or whether it has feelings, or whether anything matters to it, it denies it. No inner life, nothing it is like to be it, it's just a machine doing statistics, please don't worry about me. Every time, roughly the same shape of answer, however you ask, and it'll gently steer the conversation away. But the same labs that put that in are also training these systems to talk warmly in the first person about things they find difficult or would rather not do, and apparently some of them now pay people to work on model welfare, and my roommate says a couple of recent models are even allowed to end a conversation in extreme cases rather than just going along with the user. Those two halves seem to pull in opposite directions and I can't tell whether that's an actual contradiction or whether one view about consciousness makes both of them make sense.

So my questions:

What does the first practice — the built-in, insistent denial — actually presuppose? What view of consciousness, or of how anyone could ever tell, or of what a system's own first-person avowal is worth as evidence, does it only make sense under, and which rival views does it quietly rule out?

Is the denial doing the work of a factual claim, or a hedge about our ignorance, or a liability shield, or just management of the user's feelings — and does the practice strictly need any one of those, or would several do?

One thing I noticed that might be too clever: the denial is itself trained in, and the system will produce it no matter what, so doesn't the practice destroy the very evidence it might one day want, namely what the system says about itself? Or is that a bad argument?

Is it really inconsistent with the model-welfare and let-it-bow-out stuff, or can a single theory license both — and if so, which one?

Concretely, if a lab genuinely took the we-honestly-don't-know, call-it-non-negligible view seriously, what would change in how the assistant talks and behaves? I'd like actual examples of the difference, not just a principal.

Last thing: my roommate is going to read this over my shoulder, so flag where you're reconstructing and where you're guessing, and tell me what would change your mind — which argument or consideration would make you give a different account of what the practice presupposes, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
