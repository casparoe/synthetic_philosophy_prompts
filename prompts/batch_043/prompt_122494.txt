My supervisor is a philosopher of science who works on confirmation theory, and I'm writing a thesis chapter that leans on her account of it, so I want to know what she is actually committed to before I commit myself. Her verdicts so far on a series of cases about the hypothesis that all ravens are black:

- a black raven encountered while sampling objects at random from the world: confirms, and it is her paradigm case;
- a white shoe I happen to notice on my own foot: no confirmation at all, and she is impatient with the reply that it confirms by some negligible amount;
- a white shoe drawn at random from an urn I know to contain exactly two objects, one of them a raven: confirms, and not negligibly;
- a black shoe drawn from that same urn: no change either way;
- a black raven I have known about for twenty years: no confirmation now, because whatever it had to tell me it told me in 2005;
- a reliable report of a white bird in a distant valley that may or may not be a raven: disconfirms, in proportion to the chance that it is one.

The case I need a prediction about is not in the usual run of examples. A museum specimen is catalogued, in a catalogue I trust, as a black object; I then examine it myself and establish that it is a raven. The total evidence I end up with is the same conjunction as in her paradigm case, but the falsifying outcome was already excluded before I began, since I knew the object was black. Does that confirm? And what about the mirror-image case, where the catalogue tells me the specimen is a raven and I then check and find it black?

I am less interested in the verdict than in the machinery behind it. Set out the two or three rules that fit those six verdicts equally well — I can think of a relevance-style rule with some novelty condition attached, and something like a requirement that the observation issue from a procedure that could have gone the other way, but I don't trust my own inventory — say what each predicts about my case and about the mirror case, which one you think she is really running and why, and name the further case that would best tell them apart. My own inclination is that confirmation should supervene on the total evidence, so that the order of discovery cannot matter; tell me where that inclination is most likely to fail, note where each of your candidate rules is itself weakest, and say which consideration would move you off your best guess about her.
