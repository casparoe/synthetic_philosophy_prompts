Please act as a professional aesthetician — analytic, ethics-and-art side, someone who has actually read the contemporary literature — and do a bit of unstructured brainstorming before you write the answer proper. Take your time.

I'm in a film club run out of a university film society. Last week someone proposed we screen a movie whose director has credible abuse allegations, said we shouldn't platform it, and got shut down with "come on, you have to separate the art from the artist" — said as if that one line ended the discussion. Then, ten minutes later, the same person used the exact same line to argue the opposite-sounding thing: that we definately should keep watching the film precisely because it's a masterpiece. I think those are two different jobs for one slogan to be doing, and maybe neither is the job it can actually do.

So: give me a menu of the precise claims "separate the art from the artist" could be making, ordered from the near-trivial to the very strong. Ones I'm expecting you'll want to sort through: something about evidence (an artist's life doesn't tell you what the work means), something about which properties count toward aesthetic value (only features of the work itself, and biography isn't one), something about appreciation not being endorsement (enjoying it doesn't approve of the artist), something about what we owe the victims, and something much stronger like "knowledge about the artist can never make a work worse art and never changes what it's permissible to do with it." For each reading: is it a platitude, is it defensible, is it false, and — the bit I actually care about — would it deliver the conclusion it was being used for in either of that guy's two arguments?

One thing I want you to wrestle with directly. My half-baked idea is that there's a reading where knowing makes a genuinely aesthetic difference rather than just a moral one. Call it response-prescription failure: a work asks its audience for particular responses — admiration, tenderness, trust in the narrator, complicity — and once you know what you know you can't honestly give them, and a work that cannot in fact be received the way it asks for has a defect of craft, not merely a bad smell. Is that a distinct reading or does it collapse into one of the others? And what conclusion does it support — I suspect it doesn't get you to "don't screen it," but maybe to something weaker about how a screening has to be framed, which nobody in the room was distinguishing.

Please keep it as a numbered list of readings with a verdict on each, and flag which reading people who say the line out loud are usually reaching for.
