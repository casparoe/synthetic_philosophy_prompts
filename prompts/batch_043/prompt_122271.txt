In my biology class we are told repeatedly that DNA "is a code": the genome is described as the organism's instructions, a gene is said to "tell" the cell to build a particular protein, and particular sequences are said to "specify" traits. When I asked whether that is literally true or just a vivid way of talking, my teacher said it is literal, and gave two reasons: first, that the mapping from three-base codons to amino acids is chemically arbitrary — nothing about a codon's shape or chemistry fits it to its amino acid, so the cell must consult a table rather than simply react — and second, that the machinery which reads DNA is itself built from instructions stored in DNA, which he says gives the system the structure of a language rather than of a chemical reaction. He then used this to settle a further question in the same breath: that DNA is what causes an organism's form, while things like nutrients or incubation temperature are mere background conditions.

Could you set out, as a list I could take back to class, the different precise claims "DNA is a code" might be making, from the trivial to the strong, and say for each whether it is defensible and whether either of his two arguments actually establishes it? Two things especially confuse me. If a sequence means something, what does it mean — a protein, a trait, or a way of building a body — and does the phrase "coding for" pick out one of those rather than the others? And what would entitle us to call DNA a code while denying it of a tree's rings, which "say" something about past rainfall, or of the temperature that decides a turtle's sex: if that asymmetry is the work the slogan is doing in his argument, what exactly is the supposed difference, and does it hold up?
