My PhD student — well, he only submitted last month, so I suppose I should stop calling him that — is marrying an effective altruist on Saturday, and I've stupidly agreed to give the groom's toast. They're both the real deal: over half their income gone, careers rearrangned around it, the works. His father is a hard-nosed act-utilitarian who, I've been warned, will be listening specifically for whether I claim the marriage is justified by the good it does. The rest of the room is mostly her Catholic family — her grandmother is devout and will hear anything about "grounds" as a slight against the sacrament — plus half our department, who'll pick up anything Williams-adjacent instantly. Jake's one request: no list of reasons why she's the right person. He says if he'd needed reasons he'd have married a spreadsheet, and I think he's instinctivly groping toward the one-thought-too-many point, except displaced onto me: a toast that itemises her virtues would make it sound as though he acted *on* them.

So write me a four-minute toast that honours the marriage as a ground project neither of them owes an impersonal justification for, that never once grounds it in her qualities, that gives his father nothing he can take as a utilitarian audit of their life together, and that her grandmother won't hear as a denial that grace is doing any work. One thing I don't want: don't let "no impersonal justification required" quietly become "beyond moral criticism" — integrity isn't immunity, and if the toast implies it is, it'll be dishonest in exactly the way Williams wasn't. It should land as warmth for both halves of the room, not as a lecture with jokes bolted on.

Give me the toast first (around 500 words, no stage directions), then a section called "What I had to leave out" (around 150 words) on where the integrity objection and the one-thought-too-many constraint actually bit — including anything you wanted to say and cut.
