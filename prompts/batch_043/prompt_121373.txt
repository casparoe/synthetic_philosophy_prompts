I chair the quarterly continuation review of a public innovation fund. Three times a year, over two days, my committee of five — two engineers, a finance officer, a programme manager and me — decides whether each of roughly a dozen ongoing multi-year projects receives its next tranche. We have about forty minutes per project. The project teams appear in person and lobby hard, and they control most of the numbers we see: cost-to-complete, risk registers, the probability the remaining work delivers. Our recommendations go to a board that almost always accepts them, and the sums at stake in a single decision range from a few hundred thousand to tens of millions.

I need a written procedure we can actually run in that setting, and I need it to be more discriminating than the textbook instruction to disregard sunk costs. That instruction is right often enough that I teach it to new committee members, but in something like a third of our cases the money already spent seems to carry real information, or to have created a situation in which stopping is genuinely the worse option — and in another third it merely feels that way. Telling those apart, under time pressure, with the project champion in the room, is the job.

So give me the translation, not the theory: the questions in the order I should ask them, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure returns a definite no and we stop discussing, and the points at which it has to hand the decision back to a human being because the remaining question is one about what we care about rather than what is the case. It has to be runnable by people with no philosophy and no time; if a step cannot realistically be completed inside a forty-minute slot, say so and say what to do instead.

I have two hunches I want you to adopt, correct or discard rather than humour. First: I have been separating reasons for continuing into ones that work at the level of this single project and ones that work only at the level of a standing policy — the partner who will never co-fund with us again if we abandon a demonstrator plant, the signal sent to future applicants about how we behave when costs overrun. My test for the second kind is whether we would want a rule with that content and whether we could publish it in our guidelines without inviting manipulation. I suspect this is also the place where bad arguments hide, since almost any project-specific reason can be redescribed as a policy. Second: I suspect some of our projects have sharply increasing returns to completion — the final tranche is where nearly all the value sits, so that the earlier spend is what makes the remaining spend worth making — and that this thought is doing more work in my reasoning than I can currently defend or bound.

The procedure must survive three hard cases: the champion whose career is tied to continuation and whose cost-to-complete estimates have already been wrong twice; the project where the only honest reason to continue is that our agency publicly promised a community or partner we would finish; and the project we would continue mainly because cancelling would read as an admission that the earlier decision was wrong.

Then apply the finished procedure to three sketches of mine. (1) A six-year ecological monitoring network, five years in, 78 per cent of budget spent, whose dataset answers the question it was built for only at year eight, while a cheaper satellite method has since emerged that answers most of that question now. (2) A demonstrator plant at 40 per cent of budget, costs at 2.5 times the original estimate, core technical risk unresolved, with a co-funder who threatens to withdraw from all future programmes if we stop. (3) A project I personally championed six years ago, 90 per cent of budget spent, delivering everything specified in its original charter, whose remaining tranche buys an output no current user wants. At least one of these must come out with an answer I will not want to hear; tell me which, and why you think I will resist it.

Close by telling me what the procedure cannot do — including which of its steps is most likely to be quietly corrupted by the fact that the champions supply the numbers, that we meet only quarterly, and that we never observe what happened to the projects we killed — and what single finding about our portfolio would most quickly convince you that the procedure should be withdrawn.

No literature and no citations; I need something my finance officer can run on a Tuesday. Nine hundred words maximum, the numbered procedure first and the applications after it.
