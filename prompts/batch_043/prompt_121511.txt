I write a column about forecasting, AI and the places where statistics keeps bumping into philosophy, and I've got a half-finished piece about wether anything is settled before it happens. Before I file it I'd like someone to check that the views I'm about to publish in it actually fit together, because I suspect two of them are fighting and I can't see which. Here's the set, roughly in the order I'd defend them.

One: no gaps. Any definate claim about the future — Aristotle's sea battle tomorrow, the election next week — is already either true or false. No third status, no "not yet".

Two: no free-floating truths. If a claim is true, something about the world makes it true, and I don't think the future is the sort of thing that can do that job: there isn't an already-there tomorrow for our claims to hook onto. Which is why the move a philosopher friend muttered at me over dinner — something about one privileged branch of the future being "the" future, a thin red line — sounds like word magic to me, though maybe I just didn't get it.

Three: objective chance is real. Some outcomes aren't settled by anything, and that's not just ignorance on our part — decay events, and the macro stuff that rides on them.

Four: fatalism is bunk. When I deliberate it really is up to me what happens; the deliberation isn't theatre.

Five: an infallible predictor knowing in advance what I'll do wouldn't take any of that away. God, a demon, a perfect model — knowledge isn't a leash.

Six, from my own reporting. Election eve, the forecasting market has X at 97. I want to say it was still an open question whether X wins, and I mean "open" as more than "we didn't happen to know"; then on Friday I write that it was true all along that X would win, and I don't feel I've taken anything back. And in a bank-run piece last year I claimed that before anyone printed the rumour there was no fact of the matter about whether the run happens — yet afterwards the prediction is just plain correct.

So: which of these genuinely collide, which only look like they do, and for each real conflict what has to give and what would giving it cost me? Order them worst first, tell me which one is load-bearing and which is decoration, and say whether that thin-red-line business is cheating or the actual fix. My hunch is that five is the wobbly one, but that's probably only because it's the one I've thought about least. Write it like an email rather than a paper — no headings, no bullets, no bold, no tables, just continuous prose — and don't point me at books or papers, I'm on the road and can't get to a library. A few hundred words would be perfect.
