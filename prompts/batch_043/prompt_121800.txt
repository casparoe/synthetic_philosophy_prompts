I'm a final-year undergraduate splitting my time between evolutionary biology and a philosophy of biology seminar; we have just spent two weeks on the adaptationist programme and on Gould and Lewontin's spandrels critique of it, and my sense is that the argument has stalled somewhere neither side notices. I want to apply for a twelve-week independent-study fellowship to write about 6,000 words on this, and before I draft the two-page proposal I need its skeleton. The panel also asks for a five-point project summary, so please give me exactly that and I will expand it into prose myself.

My half-formed idea is this. Both camps appear to agree that there is a single question about a trait — adaptation or byproduct — and then to disagree about how often the first verdict is correct. I think that question only looks well-posed while traits are individuated loosely, and I want to split it along two axes that the debate slides over. The first I have been calling arrival versus retention: for a trait to be explained adaptationistically, the variant had to be available to the lineage in the first place, in the sense that its developmental and mutational machinery could produce it, and it then had to be retained because of what it did. On this way of putting it, constraints are claims about arrival rather than about retention, and the Panglossian vice is not that adaptationists forget drift or history but that they test only the retention half of an explanation while treating the space of alternatives as though every variant were equally likely to turn up. The second axis is etiological versus dispositional: whether a trait was selected for the effect it now has is a different matter from whether the lineage would still possess something functionally equivalent to it under the perturbations it actually faces, which I have been calling functional entrenchment. A trait can be entrenched without having ever been selected for, and selected for without being entrenched, and my suspicion is that a good deal of the heat in this dispute comes from one side reading the key claim the first way and the other side reading it the second way.

What I would like the project to produce is something a working biologist could use: an account of what has to be specified before an optimality model counts as having tested an adaptationist hypothesis rather than merely illustrating one. Two requirements, as I currently see it. First, the model has to state the counterfactual variant set it assumes, an accessibility audit, rather than comparing the actual trait against an unconstrained optimum. Second, it has to say which reading of adaptation it is testing. Then a small number of worked cases where the two readings come apart, chosen partly because my biology background lets me read the primary literature rather than only the commentary on it.

Two things I would rather you did than me. Please give me no references at all, and no survey of who has said what: I will add the citations myself, and I would rather you did not name papers I would then have to chase up. What I need is a question with a determinate answer, an argument that the standard positions do not settle it, and a method that is genuinely the one my distinctions make available. In particular, say why my approach beats the three obvious alternatives I can see: attempting some kind of head-count of how often adaptationist explanations in the literature turn out to be wrong; doing conceptual analysis of function and stopping there; or defending the historicist alternative wholesale. If one of those is the better project, tell me which and why.
