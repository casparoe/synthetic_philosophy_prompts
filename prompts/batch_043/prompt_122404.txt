I'm a software engineer with no philosophy background, and two colleagues on my team — both engineers, neither of them any more trained in this than I am — got into an argument at lunch that I can't stop turning over. They've asked me to referee and I have no idea how. At work this is an argument I have every quarter about whether two product metrics can be collapsed into a single score, and I've never had a principled answer, only a deadline, so I suspect that's why it stuck with me.

As far as I can tell the subject was whether pleasure and pain are really quantities of the same kind of thing, and what follows if they aren't. Nadia was arguing that severe suffering is not merely very bad but is the sort of thing that cannot be balanced off by adding up enough mild goods. I think she said the position has a name — something like lexical threshold negative utilitarianism, which I may be mangling. Rob was arguing against her. Their exchange went roughly like this.

1. Nadia: Once a pain is intense enough, no amount of sub-threshold pleasure outweighs it. This is not the claim that the exchange rate between pleasure and pain is very large; it is the claim that above the threshold there is no exchange rate at all. So the practical content is: reduce severe suffering first, and stop counting pleasant afternoons against a migraine.

2. Rob: A lexical priority is just an infinite weight with better PR, and infinite weights produce crazy verdicts. If her view is right, then averting one brief episode of above-threshold pain takes priority over preserving an arbitrarily large quantity of mild pleasure — you would have to shut down whatever gives a million people modest enjoyment in order to spare one person one bad night. Separately, the whole view lives or dies on where the threshold sits, and there is no non-arbitrary place to put it. A large but finite weight gets her everything she actually wants without the discontinuity.

3. Nadia: His reductio only bites if you have already assumed that pleasures and pains are quantities on one additive scale, which is the very thing in dispute; he is helping himself to commensurability and then presenting the result as a discovery. As for arbitrariness, every threshold is arbitrary in his sense — there is no bright line between very intense and merely intense — but vagueness has never refuted a threshold. And the finite-weight alternative just relocates the arbitrariness into the choice of number, while pretending to be more principled than it is.

4. Rob: A new objection, and the one I found hardest to answer. An additive view is insensitive to the order of operations: add up the momentary values first and then compare lives, or compare moment by moment and then add up, and you get the same ordering, because addition is associative. Hers is not. Suppose the threshold applies at the grain of momentary experience: then a life containing one five-second spike above the threshold is worse than any life containing no such spike, no matter what else is in either life. Suppose instead you first sum each life's sub-threshold pains and test that total against the threshold: now a life of long, merely moderate pain can cross the threshold in aggregate and dominate. Same two lives, same threshold, opposite verdicts. So her view returns no determinate answer until someone specifies the grain, and nothing about suffering itself settles it — how long one pain lasts looks at least as conventional as the exchange rate she accused him of assuming.

5. Nadia: She conceded that the view needs a stipulation about grain, but said the objection isn't a knockout, because grouping-sensitivity is not special to lexical views. Flat-rate addition is grouping-invariant only because it is flat, and flat is exactly what his own migraine example shows we don't want: the moment you add diminishing marginal value, or any priority weighting applied at the level of persons rather than moments, the answer depends on whether the curvature is applied before or after aggregation. So the grain question is real and must be answered, but it is a general problem for any structured way of adding up well-being, not a reductio of hers.

6. Rob: Even granting that there is no common unit, lexical priority does not follow. From the claim that pleasure and pain are two dimensions rather than two directions on one line, what follows is that some pairs of outcomes may be incomparable — neither better nor worse nor equal — and a view on which many comparisons simply have no answer has none of the fanatical implications he objected to in move 2. It does not tell you to trade a million mild pleasures for one spike; it tells you that the value comparison does not settle the choice and that you need some other decision rule.

7. Nadia: Incomparability is fine for an individual dithering over a weekend, but a donation, a policy, or a roadmap has to choose, and any rule for choosing under incomparability will either weight the two dimensions — an exchange rate in disguise — or prioritize one of them, which is her view back again. Rob's answer, and where they left it, was that this is a point about how to make decisions, not about what is better than what, and that you cannot read a need for a decision procedure back into the value facts.

I'd like you to adjudicate this rather than survey the field. Go through the moves one at a time and tell me which are clearly right, which are partly right and where exactly they overreach, and which rest on a mistake — and if either of them is leaning on a technical term in a way that can't bear the weight being put on it, say what the term actually commits them to. Then tell me who is more right overall, and what, if anything, both of them are missing: whether the disagreement as they framed it has an option they didn't consider, or a distinction that would dissolve part of it. Please don't give me reading recommendations or name-dropped references — I want the arguments, as actually made, taken apart.
