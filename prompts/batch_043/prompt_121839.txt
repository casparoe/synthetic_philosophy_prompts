I teach an upper-level seminar called Rationality, Games, and Computation — 16 students, mostly philosophy majors plus four or five CS minors, one 90-minute slot, room with movable chairs and a whiteboard but no projector (and the department will spring for pastries, so small stakes are fine but real money isn't). Next week I want them to feel, before they're told anything, why letting players submit computer programs that can read each other's source code changes what can happen in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma. I don't want a lecture up front and they shouldn't hear the label for it until the debrief. So: design me three rounds. Round one, a plain sealed-envelope one-shot PD so they feel the usual pull. Round two, the open-source round: each student writes a program in a tiny language you specify — I'm imagining something like five or six legal primitives: choose C or D outright; condition on whether the opponent's source is character-for-character identical to mine; condition on whether the opponent's source contains some string; SIMULATE(opponent, me) with a step limit; and maybe an ASK THE JUDGE primitive where I truthfully answer yes/no questions about what some program would output against some other program — and I act as the machine, interpreting their sources in front of the room. I want the timeout case to matter, so decide what happens when two programs chase each other's simulation forever, and pick whichever assignment (mutual defection? something worse for the asker?) makes the halting constraint land hardest. Round three is the twist I've been toying with: I pass each program through an obfuscator that preserves input–output behavior but scrambles the text — renaming variables, reordering clauses, inserting dead code, translating into an equivalent phrasing in the other allowed dialect — before the opponent sees it, and I'm also debating whether to announce, only after submissions are in, that every program will additionally be run against an unmodified copy of itself for a third of the points. Tell me whether that self-play rule is a good idea or whether it muddies the lesson, and what it would teach if it works.

What I need from you beyond the rules is the arc: exact wording of the program language so 16 people can actually write in it without a CS background, payoff numbers in points, a timeline with minutes, and a debrief that maps specific moments onto specific ideas — why changing the strategy space means we may no longer be playing the game the dominance argument was about; why any arbitrary clique (cooperate iff the other program mentions the word banana) sustains cooperation, which turns the result into an equilibrium-selection problem rather than a solution; the gap between syntactic identity and behavioral identity that the obfuscator is supposed to expose; the sense in which simulation-based rules are the robust ones and why robustness there costs you the halting guarantee. A colleague in CS keeps telling me this whole apparatus is just correlated equilibrium with extra steps, or just another way of describing commitment and delegation, and I'd like the debrief to stage that argument in the room rather than me settling it from the front. Finally, be blunt about what will predictably go wrong — the CS students running away with it, pairs colluding out loud or just agreeing to submit identical code, someone writing something uncomputable, someone writing defect-always and winning round two and concluding the exercise is rigged, ambiguity in my language spec that they will exploit — how to handle each, and an honest note on which part of the real idea the activity simply cannot reproduce and I'll have to state myself. I've ran something vaguely like this once before and it definately fell apart in the last ten minutes, so err on the side of over-specifying the transitions.
