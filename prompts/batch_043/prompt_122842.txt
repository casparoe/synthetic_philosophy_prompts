I spent thirty-odd years in experimental biophysics — mostly NMR of membrane proteins — and since retiring I have been sitting in on a philosophy of mathematics reading group at the local university, which is how I came to be unsettled by something I had always treated as plumbing. In graduate functional analysis I used Zorn's lemma a handful of times, to get a Hamel basis or to extend a functional, and never once asked whether it was true; it was simply part of the toolkit that came with the axioms. I have since gathered that over ZF it is equivalent to the axiom of choice, that the existence of a basis for every vector space is likewise equivalent to full choice while the Hahn-Banach theorem follows from something strictly weaker, that Banach-Tarski cannot be proved without some choice principle, and — this is the part that actually bothers me — that essentially all of the mathematics physicists use can apparently be recovered in ZF with only countable or dependent choice. If any of those five claims is wrong, or more contested than I have been told, please say so before you begin; I would rather have the correction than an elegant answer built on a mistake.

What I want is not an opinion but a number with its derivation. Take the proposition to be this: Zorn's lemma is true, where true means true of the intended universe of sets — the cumulative hierarchy, however exactly one is supposed to think of it — and not merely that it is an axiom of the system most people work in. Give me your credence in that proposition to two decimal places, decomposed into the sub-questions it turns on, with a number and one line of justification for each, and then show me how the terms combine, since I am as interested in the arithmetic as in the answer. The terms I would expect to see are at least these: whether there is a fact of the matter about set-theoretic claims at all (realism about a single universe versus a multiverse, pluralist or formalist reading — which may make my question ill-posed rather than hard, in which case say what number it would be sensible to ask for instead and give me that one); conditional on there being a fact of the matter, how much of the case for Zorn's lemma is intrinsic — the thought that a universe generated by iterating the power-set operation through all the ordinals leaves no room for a chain with no maximal element — and how much is extrinsic, resting on the fruitfulness, elegance and coherence of ZFC; and then the worry I have started calling the selective-indispensability problem, namely that the applications which earn set theory its scientific keep appear to be supplied by the choice-free fragment, so that an indispensability argument of the sort that would move a working natural scientist may reach ZF and countable choice without ever reaching Zorn's lemma. Price that last worry in two steps: how confident are you that the applicable fragment really is choice-free, and how much damage would it do to the extrinsic case if some piece of mathematics our best physics needs turned out after all to require full choice?

Two further requests. First, tell me which single term in your decomposition your final number is most sensitive to and what would move it most — I take the candidates to be a demonstration that the iterative conception settles maximality by itself, a physically indispensable use of full choice, or a new axiom incompatible with choice that achieves something like the standing large cardinals now have. Second, my own credence before reading you is about 0.65, and a friend in the group, a retired analyst, puts his at 0.95; tell me which sub-question the two of us are most likely disagreeing about, because I suspect we are not disagreeing about sets at all. Please answer in British English and in under 700 words; and please do not open by remarking that it depends on what I mean by true — price that question, then go on.
