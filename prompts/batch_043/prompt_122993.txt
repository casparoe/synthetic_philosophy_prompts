I work in reinforcement learning, in a group that sits between control theory and computational psychiatry. I am comfortable with Markov decision processes and Bellman recursions, with discount factors and the difference between the return an agent evaluates and the return an outside observer scores, with Boltzmann/softmax policies and temperature parameters, with model-based evaluation versus model-free habit, with hierarchical policies and options, with KL-regularized or information-limited control as a model of bounded rationality, and with inverse reinforcement learning and its identifiability problems. I have never taken a philosophy course.

What I want is a translation, and then an honest report on what the translation loses.

The thing to be translated is the philosophical literature on weakness of will, or akrasia, and its relation to hyperbolic and quasi-hyperbolic discounting. As I understand the dialectic, it has four moves. First, the classical puzzle: can an agent, at the very moment of acting and without ignorance or external compulsion, do one thing while judging another to be best, and if so how is that possible, given that judgment is normally what moves you to act? Second, the standard modern formulation, associated with Davidson: yes, this happens, and it happens synchronically — the agent's all-things-considered judgment at the time of action is that A is better, and the agent intentionally does B instead — so the puzzle is a real one about the causal and rational role of judgment, not merely about changing one's mind. Third, the diachronic deflation: there is no such moment, because what looks like acting against your own better judgment is really just the shape of your discount function interacting with the passage of time; every temporal slice of the agent is perfectly consistent with itself, and the appearance of conflict comes from the fact that the plan that is optimal at one time is not the plan that will be optimal later. Out of this comes the intertemporal-bargaining picture, in which an agent cannot alter any single future choice in isolation but only whole bundles of similar future choices, so that self-control consists in adopting personal rules or bright lines rather than in winning isolated battles. Fourth, the backlash against that deflation: if each temporal slice is a rational maximizer, then no one ever acts against their own better judgment and the phenomenon has been dissolved rather than explained; and relatedly, nothing in the multiple-selves model picks out which slice's preferences are the normative ones, so calling present-bias a bias is an extra judgment imported from outside the model rather than a result delivered by it.

Translate those four moves into my vocabulary, one at a time, as faithfully as you can, flagging each place where you have to bolt on machinery that the standard framework does not already contain.

Do the translation against a concrete environment rather than in the abstract, because abstraction will let you cheat. Call it the serial recertification task. There are N consecutive days; on each the agent chooses to work or to skip. Working yields an immediate negative reward of magnitude c and advances a progress counter. Skipping yields nothing and resets the counter to zero. The agent needs N consecutive work-days inside a hard horizon of 2N days, and if it reaches N it receives a large terminal reward R at the end; the horizon does not extend when the counter resets, so a sufficiently late skip makes R unreachable. At the initial time, and only then, the agent may pay a cost k to install an irrevocable action mask that removes the skip action from every future day.

Now let two hypotheses compete over what kind of agent is in this environment. H1 is the standard quasi-hyperbolic agent with present-bias parameter beta and long-run factor delta, in both a sophisticated variant (it knows its own beta and plans accordingly) and a naive variant (it plans as if its future selves will have beta equal to one, and is repeatedly surprised). H2 is a rival that a colleague in my group keeps proposing: the agent discounts exponentially, with no present-bias at all, but its policy is Boltzmann over Q-values with a temperature that rises as the terminal reward looms closer in time, or as cumulative effort accumulates, so that behavior looks present-biased because action selection degrades near temptation while valuation stays time-consistent.

For H1-sophisticated, H1-naive, and H2, work out the qualitative predictions on this environment: whether the mask is purchased at the initial time, whether day one is worked, whether the final feasible day is worked, and in particular what happens after an unplanned skip at day j — does the agent resume and grind out a fresh run, or abandon the terminal reward, and does the answer depend on j in a way that differs across the three agents? Then tell me where H1 and H2 make different observable predictions and where they come apart not at all. Separately: on this environment, does anticipating your own inconsistency leave the sophisticated agent worse off in expected return than the naive agent would be, or better, or does it depend on the parameters — and can you show which, rather than assert it?

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually care about.

Give me a mapping table for the following notions: judgment; all-things-considered judgment; the better judgment one acts against; intention or plan; the effort of self-control, or willpower; temptation; regret; the claim that the agent could have done otherwise; blameworthiness; and the agent itself, considered as the same thing over time. For each entry say whether it has a clean counterpart in the framework I named, whether it translates only after you add nonstandard machinery — a separate model-based evaluator alongside a model-free actor, options or hierarchical policies as intentions, a KL penalty from a default policy as the cost of control, action masking as commitment — or whether it has no expression in that vocabulary at all. Be specific about the third category. In particular: the claim that the agent knew better and did it anyway — does that claim carry any behavioral consequence whatsoever in an environment like the one above, or is it invisible from the outside? And what in my vocabulary could play the role of a reason, as opposed to a reward, given that optimality is always optimality with respect to some reward?

One further wrinkle I want you to take seriously. In my field a discount factor does double duty: sometimes it is a genuine time preference, and sometimes it is a modeling device standing in for a constant hazard of termination, a trick for keeping infinite sums finite, or a way to trade bias for variance. I have a recollection that if you take exponential discounters and aggregate them over an uncertain hazard rate, the resulting aggregate discount curve has a hyperbolic shape, which would mean that what looks like present-bias could be a correct response to uncertainty about whether the future will arrive at all, rather than any distortion of valuation. Check the mathematics of that rather than taking my memory of it; if it is wrong, say so plainly and say what is true instead. If it is right, then the translation has a serious problem to report: the framework has no way to mark the difference between an agent that undervalues its future and an agent that correctly discounts a future it may never get, and I want to know whether that is a defect of the vocabulary, a sign that the philosophers' notion of a bias was doing less work than it appeared to, or both, and in exactly what proportion.

Finally, suppose you are restricted to observing trajectories from the serial recertification task — state-action sequences, no self-reports, no interventions, no neural or physiological measurements. Can you in principle distinguish H1 from H2, and can you in principle distinguish a failure of valuation from a failure of control? I expect the answer to turn on degeneracies between reward scale, discount factor, and temperature, and I want the argument made properly. Then say what follows for the original philosophical dispute: if the two hypotheses are not identifiable from behavior, is the disagreement between the deflationist and the Davidsonian about something that could ever show up in an agent's behavior, and does that make it empty, or merely make it a disagreement about which model to score the agent against?

Commit to a verdict on that last question, and on whether the residue you found is mostly a limitation of my vocabulary or mostly a sign that the philosophical claims were never as sharp as they looked. Do not settle for the observation that it is, in some sense, both.
