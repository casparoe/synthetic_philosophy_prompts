I'm writing my dissertation on comparative memory cultures, and I've been asked to give the ten-minute keynote next month at the dedication of a memorial to three Black men lynched in my hometown — a town of about four thousand in the Mississippi Delta — on the lawn where it happened. The ceremony is co-hosted by a delegation from our German sister city, whose mayor asked to speak about how his town came to terms with its own National Socialist past; the expectation in the room, I suspect, is a talk about what we can learn from Germany.

Two constraints shape what I can say. First, the memorial committee voted narrowly — and bitterly — to name the three men but not the white families locally understood to have taken part. The victims' descendants have asked that no speaker present that omission as reconciliation, and some of them think the memorial should not go up on those terms at all. Second, I have no standing to confess anything: I'm thirty-one, I wasn't there, and I won't thank the German delegation for showing us how it's done.

I want a speech built on two things I actually believe. One is Jaspers' fourfold distinction among criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt, and specifically the gap between guilt one can accept as a citizen of a polity and guilt no one can assign to another person. The other is from my own work: I argue that German memory culture is organized around confession — the perpetrator generation's admission, with a shape, a climax, and an ending — whereas what the South needs is what I call custodial memory, the maintenance of a record that no one in the room made and no one can close.

The audience is the victims' descendants, people whose grandparents were in the crowd, the mayor, the German delegation, and a block of high-school students taught the both-sides version. Make it land for the guests and the memory-studies people without losing everyone else.

After the speech, add a short note on where it takes liberties with the comparative literature or leans on a contested reading of it — Jaspers included — and flag the single claim in it you are least confident about. Begin with the speech itself: no preface and no commentary.
