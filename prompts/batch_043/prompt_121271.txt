For a speculative-fiction workshop I help run alongside my philosophy department, I'm putting together a session on the rule-following paradox — the "Kripkenstein" reading of Wittgenstein, where the worry is not that we might be wrong about what we meant but that there may be no fact of the matter at all — and instead of assigning the usual essay I want a story that does the philosophical work dramatically. So I'd like you to write the story; I'll supply the setting, but develop or alter it freely wherever the fiction wants to go.

The setting: the generation ship *Palinode*, four centuries into a voyage, is governed by a body of inherited rule-governed procedures its crew calls the Reckonings — hull-splice intervals, ration tallies, the Long Count of days, the computation of landing windows. Nothing in the Reckonings is stated once and for all; each generation of Keepers trains and certifies the next, and every act of certification is itself just another instance of going on in the same way, never a formulation that stands outside the practice and settles it. The ship's Charter of Sameness declares that the Reckonings have been preserved unchanged since the founding. Then a junior auditor, re-deriving the old checksum tables, discovers that every surviving record of the last two centuries — and every disposition the crew currently has — fits the Reckoning-sign equally well whether it means ordinary addition or a deviant function that agrees with addition on all values small enough ever to have arisen on board and diverges from them everywhere beyond, beyond the point where any real computation has ever reached. The founding archive is intact in the vault, but it is written in a notation whose conventions are themselves among the things that were passed down. And the stakes are not academic: if the hull-splice Reckoning has drifted, the ship comes apart in the ninth century, and the crew's own practice — what they do, what they correct each other for, what they count as getting it wrong — is all anyone has to go on.

What I want is a short story, roughly 900 to 1,500 words, in which the characters run through the obvious escapes and find each of them unsatisfying, without the story ever turning into a lecture. Someone should try to settle the matter by consulting a record or a founding formulation, and it should become clear why that fails: any sentence or table or diagram is itself something that has to be taken one way rather than another. Someone else should propose that meaning is just what the crew is disposed to do, or the simplest rule fitting the data, and the story should let the crew feel why that is inadequate — their dispositions run out, since no one has ever gone on past a certain point, and what they are disposed to do is not the same as what counts as a mistake, and the deviant rule is not noticeably less simple than the right one. A third character should try to locate the missing fact in some inner episode, a private act of grasping or seeing that the rule requires this, and that move should be shown to do no work for them either. Then the crew has to decide what to do next, and that decision is the ending: it should be a liveable one, one in which they can still go on with the Reckonings and still hold each other to them, and a careful reader should afterwards be able to say which kind of response to the paradox the ending embodies — one that reconstrues the Charter's claim about sameness as a claim about what the practice licenses us to say about each other, or one that insists there is a real fact here and tries to place it somewhere.

Two constraints, please. Keep the philosophy inside action, dialogue and image rather than in authorial exposition, and don't let any character cite or paraphrase Wittgenstein, Kripke, or the secondary literature — nobody on the *Palinode* has heard of them. And don't resolve the tension by importing a deus ex machina that makes the fact of the matter discoverable after all; whatever settles things for the crew has to be the kind of thing that could settle it for us, here, about our own words.
