Hey — I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I'm putting in for a nine-month writing fellowship that wants a two-page project proposal. Here's my half-formed idea, which I need turned into something a committee will take seriously.

Everyone reaching for big ideas about AI right now keeps grabbing Dennett's ladder of minds — Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, Gregorian creatures — and the move I keep hearing is that a language model is obviously a Gregorian creature, since what gets installed in it is our words, our memes, our cognitive tools. I think that's too fast, and maybe backwards.

What bugs me: each grade up that ladder looks like it's defined by a timescale of selection (species, individual lifetime, deliberation inside a lifetime, culture) plus the creature itself being the thing that does the selecting at that timescale. A deployed frontier model doesn't learn during anything we'd call a lifetime. The learning happens across a huge population of surrogate episodes — gradient steps, rollouts, accepted and rejected samples — that no individual ever lives, and then the result is frozen. So is it really a Gregorian creature, or is it a grade the taxonomy has no name for: something whose ontogeny is farmed out to a population of lives that were never lived, or something with no ontogeny at all? I've been toying with "episodic" or "Baldwinian" creature as a label, but I want the concept right before I bother naming it.

Second, seperate thread I want in there: the Popperian grade is supposed to be about letting your hypotheses die in your stead. Is chain-of-thought plus process-level feedback actually that, or just the Skinnerian shaping of verbal behavior that merely looks like hypothesis-testing from outside? And does it matter which thing is doing the dying — the policy, the reward model, the training run as a whole?

What I need from you: the question stated tightly enough that someone could argue against it; why Dennett's own framing and the standard way people are applying it leave it open; what I'd actually do month by month for nine months as a journalist rather than an academic (I can read the technical material and interview people, I'm not running experiments); what the deliverable is — I'm imaginging one long reported piece plus one shorter polemical thing; and an honest note on whether this is too big for nine months. Most importantly, what would count as the project failing: if the honest conclusion is that "Gregorian" was always loose enough to cover anything trained on human text, I'd rather find that out in month two than in month nine. No citations, I'll do my own fact-checking and sourcing.
