Here's the question as it went out:

Q3. A pragmatic randomised trial compares an intensive regimen (A) with the standard regimen (B), 1000 patients in each arm. One-year mortality: A 190/1000 = 19%, B 140/1000 = 14%. The trialists also report the data split by whether the patient completed at least four cycles — something decided after randomisation and strongly influenced by toxicity:

Completers: A 30/600 = 5%, B 90/900 = 10%
Stopped early: A 160/400 = 40%, B 50/100 = 50%

(a) Show that the direction of the comparison reverses when you condition on completion. (b) Explain in a sentence or two how that is arithmetically possible. (c) A hospital guideline committee has to recommend one of the two regimens. Which comparison should they rely on, and why? (d) Could this kind of reversal happen even if both arms had the same proportion of completers? Yes or no, with a one-line reason. Work with risks/proportions throughout, not odds ratios.

And here's one student's answer, aplart from me stripping the name:

(a) Overall A looks worse (19% vs 14%), but inside each stratum A looks better: 5% vs 10% among completers and 40% vs 50% among those who stopped early. So the comparison flips depending on whether you split by completion status.

(b) The overall rate in each arm is a weighted average of its two stratum-specific rates, weighted by how many patients that arm has in each stratum. A has 600 completers and 400 who stopped, B has 900 and 100, so A's average gets pulled much harder towards the high-mortality stratum. Weighted averages can reverse the ordering of the numbers going into them when the weights differ, so there's no contradiction here — it's Simpson's paradox.

(c) The committee should rely on the disaggregated comparison, which shows A is genuinely the better regimen. Completion status is far and away the strongest predictor of death here, and you should always break a comparison down by a variable that predicts the outcome that strongly; pooling across it mixes very different kinds of patient, so the 19% vs 14% figure is an artifact of case mix rather than a real effect of the regimen. And because assignment was randomised, we dont have to worry that the subgroup comparisons are confounded either.

(d) Yes. The reversal comes from the stratum-specific rates, not from how patients are distributed across the strata: even if both arms had, say, 75% completers, the within-stratum differences could still produce the flip, since what's doing the work is the rates rather than the weights.
