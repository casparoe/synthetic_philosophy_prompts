hey — I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so sorry in advance if I mangle the vocabulary. My next book is set in a drought year in an irrigated valley, and the whole plot turns on one woman, the watermaiden, who controls a sluice gate. Downstream is an orchard that will die without water. She has a legal right to the water and uses it for her own trees. In the version I drafted first she simply doesn't open the gate, and the orchard dies. My beta readers split hard on whether that's the same moral act as actively diverting the flow — my editor says the first is obviously less bad, my sister says the distinction is fake and it's really about how much it would have cost her, and my philosopher friend (who did a PhD a decade ago and has since forgotten most of it) sent me off to read about killing versus letting die and doing versus allowing. I did read around a bit — the bathtub cousins, the trolley stuff, the doctrine of double effect, the bit where everyone argues about whether withholding treatment is different from withdrawing it — and what struck me is that this has been going in circles for fifty years with the same handful of examples, and nobody moves. So I want a diagnosis from you, not a verdict on who's right, and definitely not a reading list or citations: I've seen the survey material and I need your own take. Is this a verbal dispute wearing a substantive costume, or two different questions under one heading — one about whether an act is *permissible* at all versus one about how bad the resulting state of affairs is — or is it bedrock intuition on both sides, or a hidden empirical question in disguise, about whether omissions can be causes, or about what people are actually responding to in these cases?

Here are the things I'd want the diagnosis to actually engage, since I built the novel's scenes to pull the usual explanations apart. Case two: she opens the gate for an hour each morning to water her own trees and shuts it again, and because of how the canal works this leaves the orchard slightly *worse* off than if she'd never opened it at all — my readers treat that as clearly worse than case one, even though she's still not "killing" anything and the orchard dies either way. Case three: she gets herself deliberately locked in her shed on the morning she knows she'll be asked, so that she can't open the gate; everyone I've shown it to finds that at least as bad as diverting the water, which makes me think the doing/allowing line is doing no work at all and what's carrying the judgment is intention or the cost of prevention. Case four: she takes away the sandbags that were holding back a separate flash flood that would have finished the orchard anyway — the "double prevention" thing, or whatever it's called — and my readers can't even agree which side of the line that's on. Case five is the one that really broke me: I rewrote the backstory so that the orchard had a prior customary claim on the water, and suddenly half my readers flip and call the exact same omission "starving" it rather than "allowing" it to die, which suggests the whole distinction rests on a baseline — on what would have happened anyway — that the two sides are setting differently without saying so. My own hunch, for what it's worth, is that the debate is stalled because every canonical example is engineered so that the doing/allowing line, the intention/foresight line, and the baseline all line up on the same side, so they can't discriminate between the explanations; my editor's hunch is that the distinction is just a proxy for how much the agent is being asked to sacrifice. Tell me which of us is closer, or why neither is.

I'd like the answer in American English, in plain prose without footnotes or bibliography (I have no access to a library and I can't check anything you'd cite anyway). What I want is: what kind of disagreement this is, what each side is really committed to, whether there's a distinction or a formal result or a piece of evidence that would count as progress given your diagnosis, and an honest answer about whether it should be expected to end or whether it's the sort of thing that just bottoms out. And since I'm the one with the deadline: given your diagnosis, which of these disagreements is the one actually worth dramatizing in my scene, and is there a version of the sluice gate case that would make a reader *feel* the real fault line instead of the fake one?
