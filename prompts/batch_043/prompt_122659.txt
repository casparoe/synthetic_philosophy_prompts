A chapter in a popular-level book on mathematical reasoning — my reading group is working through it this term — introduces something the author calls "logical induction" and summarises the result roughly as follows:

"The trouble is that a Bayesian agent is logically omniscient. Give it a prior over models of Peano arithmetic and it already assigns probability 1 to every theorem, so no proof can ever surprise it and it can never learn anything from reasoning. Logical induction fixes this. Treat each arithmetical sentence as a share worth £1 if the sentence is true and nothing otherwise, and let a 'market' post prices for all of them; those prices just are the agent's degrees of belief. The correctness condition is the familiar no-Dutch-book one: the prices are right when no trading strategy can make money off them. It turns out that a computable algorithm can meet this condition, and what it delivers is remarkable. It converges on the truth: every true arithmetical sentence eventually gets probability 1 and every false one eventually gets probability 0. It learns any pattern a computer could in principle detect — that late digits of pi are 7 about a tenth of the time, for instance — usually long before it has the resources to check the individual cases. Because it also has accurate beliefs about its own beliefs, it sidesteps the paradoxes of self-reference, including the Gödelian and Löbian ones. And its prices satisfy the probability calculus at every stage, so they can be fed straight into expected-utility calculations. The upshot is that logical omniscience is, for practical purposes, solved: a bounded agent can have a computable probability distribution over all of mathematics, and the only question left is how fast you can run the algorithm. That closes with an obvious application: an AI system whose beliefs come from such a market could be trusted to check itself, since it could refuse to act unless its own probability that the action is safe exceeds 0.99, and that figure is guaranteed to be accurate."

I've also read the abstract of the paper this is based on and a couple of blog posts about it, so I suspect the chapter is broadly right but oversold in places. The bits I personally balked at are "no trading strategy can make money", the claim that every true sentence converges to probability 1, and the closing application to self-certifying AI systems. But I can't tell which of those worries are real, and I'm sure I've missed things I didn't balk at.

Please audit the passage more or less sentence by sentence. For each significant claim I'd like to know whether someone who knows the result well would accept it as stated, would regard it as a fair simplification for a general audience, or would say it is wrong — and, where it is wrong, what the defender's actual position is. In particular:

- Does the correctness condition really have the shape the chapter gives it, or do the qualifiers carry the weight (what the trader is allowed to compute, how much it may risk, whether it must profit without bound, over what timeframe)?
- Is the convergence claim right as stated, or does it only hold for a subclass of sentences? What should one say about a sentence like the twin prime conjecture if it happens to be independent of the background theory — and about how long convergence takes?
- The chapter says the prices obey the probability calculus at every stage; elsewhere I have seen the outputs described as "prices, not probabilities". Is that merely rhetoric, or does it mark a real difference about what may be assumed at any finite stage?
- Does the closing application follow from the parts of the summary that are accurate, or does it lean on something the chapter got wrong?

Finally: does the chapter's headline conclusion — that logical omniscience is solved for practical purposes — survive once the inaccuracies are corrected? I would rather have the unsentimental version than the reassuring one, since I am the one summarising this for the group. Please write your answer in British English.
