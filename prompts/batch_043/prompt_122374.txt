I got into an argument at a barbeque a few weeks back and it's been rattling around in my head ever since, so I want to actually work it out instead of just having vibes about it.

The thing I keep coming back to is that famous essay about whether artifacts have politics, the Winner one I think from around 1980, and specifically the example everybody quotes: the low overpasses on the Long Island parkways built while Robert Moses was running things, where the story is that they were made too low for buses, so that people without cars, poorer people and mostly Black New Yorkers the way the story usually gets told, couldn't reach the beaches. My friend's response, wich I think is just plain common sense and wich I couldn't beat at the time, was: the concrete didn't decide anything. A man decided. The bridge is just where his decision ended up. Call that politics if you like, but it's his politics, not the bridge's, and saying the overpass itself is political is just a cute way of talking.

I want a real analysis of that episode run through the essay's actual machinery, not a summary of the essay. So:

1. The mapping. As I understand it the essay distinguishes two ways a thing can be political, one where the politics is baked in and the technology basically can't exist without a certain kind of social arrangement propping it up, and one where it's more contingent, a decision that happened to get frozen into an object. Which one are the bridges supposed to be? Spell out what the theory says the options actually were, who or what counts as the actor, and what would count as evidence that the politics is in the thing rather than the man. And be honest if the famous example is really the weaker of the two cases, with the stronger and weirder one doing all the philosophical work. Related: the essay's other famous example is a mechanical tomato harvester that supposedly changed who could stay in the tomato business in California. Do the two examples fail in the same way under my friend's objection, or does one survive it better than the other?

2. Where the lens breaks or goes quiet. The bridge is also just a bridge, it carries cars and sheds water. The exclusion, if there is one, works by effect (buses don't fit) rather than by anyone's intent, and it hit buses and trucks and everyone riding in them regardless of why the clearance was chosen. The story we tell about the object also seems to be doing a lot of work: if nobody had ever written the story down, would the overpass still be political, or is the artifact really functioning as a record of a decision rather than as a cause of anything? I also don't want the answer to hang on the history, because I've seen people argue the clearances were ordinary for the period and the whole tale is oversold. Tell me what happens to the analysis if that's true.

3. My own idea, which I want you to either sharpen or kill. What seems distinctive about artifacts isn't that they exclude people, since laws exclude people too, but that they make the exclusion expensive to undo. A sign saying no buses comes down in an afternoon. A bridge you have to jack up or demolish. So maybe the politics of a thing is really the cost of disagreeing with it, and the harder it is to route around, the more political it is. Does that save the claim from my friend, or does it quietly gut it by turning it into something boring like some decisions are durable? Does it handle the case where nobody intended the exclusion at all, stairs where a ramp could have been? Does it give you a scale you could actually apply, and if so does the bridge come out high for the right reasons?

4. Then run it the other direction. What does this episode do to the theory: is it a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case the theory never really anticipated? And give me the strongest version of my friend's position before you answer it, and say where that strongest version finally runs out, if it does.

No reading lists, no citations, no suggestions that I go look up so-and-so. Plain language, and where the theory genuinely has nothing to say about some part of this, say so instead of forcing it to talk.
