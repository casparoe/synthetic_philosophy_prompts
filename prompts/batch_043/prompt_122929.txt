Hey — I'm in a seminar this term on the political philosophy of AI (cross-listed between philosophy and the public policy school) and we each have to hand in a short "values profile" — basically, what should the value-formation target for a general-purpose AI system be, and what makes it legitimate — that the rest of the class then gets to attack. I've been drafting mine and I have this sinking feeling the pieces don't fit together, but I can't locate the crack. Here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) I don't buy strong longtermism. The move where you say the future could contain astronomically many lives and so tiny shifts in probability dominate everything strikes me as a reductio rather than an argument, and I'm not willing to bite the fanaticism bullet.
(2) But I do think the target should give real weight to people who don't exist yet, and — if they can matter at all — to digital or artificial systems we might build later. I also like the "moral progress" framing: the system should be corrigible toward the moral truth rather than frozen at whatever humans happen to want right now. Locking in 2020s-vintage human values forever seems obviously wrong to me.
(3) I want the justification to be procedural rather than substantive: what makes a target legitimate is that it's what you'd get from a fair aggregation over everyone affected, not that some ethicist can argue it's the true morality. Concretely I'm broadly welfarist about what gets summed. And here's the bit I keep going back and forth on: I don't think "human values" are just sitting there waiting to be measured. Whatever elicitation procedure you run partly constructs the thing it measures, so there's no pre-procedural fact of the matter to appeal to.
(4) I'm attracted to liberal neutrality — a system deployed in a pluralist society shouldn't be justified by appeal to any thick conception of the good. I know the "neutrality of effect is impossible" point, so I mean neutrality of justification or aim.
(5) I also think legitimacy needs actual uptake. I really don't like the idea of a handful of researchers in one lab setting the target and calling it humanity's values. So: real consultation, and real deference to actual democratic settlements. And since these models get deployed everywhere, where local norms conflict with the norms of the mostly Anglophone, mostly WEIRD place the model was trained, I think local norms should win, with only a very thin global floor.
(6) One case verdict I'm confident about and can't let go of: I think it's fine — good, even — for a deployed assistant to refuse to help a user do something that's legal and genuinely popular where they live, because the lab judges it morally bad. The case I keep using is helping someone plan a big expansion of a factory farm. I can't bring myself to call that illegitimate paternalism, though it feels like it should clash with something else I've said.

Can you audit this? I want to know which pairs are actually in tension and which only look like it — someone in my study group said (1) and (3) can't both be true, and I sort of see why but I think they're wrong. Rank whatever real tensions you find by severity rather than listing them as equally bad, tell me what would have to give for each, and say which single revision would clean up the most. Also: if one of these commitments is doing more work than I realise, say so, and if the natural fix for one tension makes another one worse, I want to hear that too. I'm not precious about any of them, I just don't want to walk into the Q&A with something that collapses the first time anyone pokes it.
