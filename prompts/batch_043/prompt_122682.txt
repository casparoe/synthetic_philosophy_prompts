Imagine a near-future setup (made up, but not crazy): it's the 2030s, most people wear a lifelogger — call it Mnemos — that keeps basically everything: audio, video, where you were, everything you typed. On top of that there are "reconstruction engines": AI systems that rebuild supposedly deleted material from traces *other* people still hold — a screenshot on someone's phone, your face in a stranger's lifelog, cached fragments. So the legal right to have your past erased (in this made-up scenario, the Erasure Act) has become nearly meaningless in practice: you can delete your own copy, but the past is now a property of everyone else's archive instead of yours.

Now put Nietzsche in front of this. His 1874 essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life argues that a person or a culture needs the power to forget — a plastic power, the capacity to feel unhistorically — and that a surfeit of history is a kind of sickness. But the forgetfulness he's describing is a capacity of a living organism: something in the skull. What I can't work out is whether his argument actually needs that. Two distinctions I've been using:

- endogenous vs exogenous forgetting — the trace decaying in you, versus nobody being able to get it back from anywhere.
- presence vs availability — the archive is mostly dormant; almost nobody re-reads it. Does the damage depend on the past being *present* to you, or only on it being *retrievable*? I've started calling the strong version "retrieval-proof forgetting."

So: what would he say about the Erasure Act — for it, against it, or "you're asking the wrong question"? And does the case for forgetting generalize to exogenous memory at all, or does it secretly depend on forgetting being something *you* do?

Complication: in the Genealogy's second essay he spends real energy praising the brutal mnemonic techniques that burn memory *into* people so they can become creatures who can promise. So maybe he doesn't hate memory as such, only inherited, second-hand, unmetabolized memory. Does that change the answer?

And what would he say to Margalit (and Rieff, in his own register) about a duty to remember atrocity — would he call it ressentiment / therapeutic culture dressed up as ethics, or the monumental use of history doing genuine work? Does it matter whether the remembering is done by the perpetrators' descendants or the victims'?

Give me a number 0–1 for how confident you are in the main call, and tell me which single discovery would move it most. No citations needed, just argue it.
