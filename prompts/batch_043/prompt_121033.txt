I'm a PhD student in programming languages, and my group has roped me into a reading group with some philosophers on the justification of deduction. We spent the whole last session on Lewis Carroll's 1895 Mind dialogue where the Tortoise keeps granting Achilles every hypothetical he asks for while still refusing to assert the conclusion, and Achilles ends up writing down an endless list of further conditionals. I came out of it convinced the whole thing is trivial and the philosophers are overcomplicating it: my version of the point is just that a rule of inference is not a premise, so of course you can't discharge an application by adding another assumption. My friend in the group said that's exactly the diagnosis Carroll himself gestures at, but that the interesting question starts there — what it is to *follow* a rule, and whether any justification of deduction can avoid helping itself to the thing it is justifying. I didn't have the vocabulary to say why I thought that question was either empty or already answered.

So I want the translation done properly in the vocabulary I actually have. I spend my days writing an elaborator for a small Martin-Löf-style dependent type theory: judgmental structure versus propositional structure, formation/introduction/elimination rules, definitional equality and normalization, metavariables and unification, tactic scripts that build derivation terms, internal versus external soundness statements, inductive families used to encode syntax, quoting and reflection primitives, the usual awkwardness about a total self-interpreter for a strongly normalizing language. I'm fluent enough that you can talk to me in those terms without explaining them; I have never had a philosophy course.

Here's the specific thing I want you to translate, and I want you to translate the strongest version of it rather than the strawman. Take Carroll's regress and the rule-versus-premise diagnosis, and restate them in my vocabulary: is the moral just that implication elimination belongs to the judgmental structure while a conditional belongs to the object language, so that "accepting" a rule is not a state that can be expressed as the inhabitant of a type? Say what the Tortoise's refusal corresponds to in an elaborator — a stuck term, an unsolved metavariable, a non-terminating loop, a missing license — and be honest about which of those it really is. Then take the case I actually care about, the one I don't think Carroll's text reaches. Suppose I add to my language a genuinely internalized notion of derivation: an inductive family of quoted derivations, quote and unquote, a term-level function that mirrors implication elimination on codes, and a self-describing type checker that takes a code and returns a derivation of the decoded proposition. Call that rule reification. Once the language can talk about its own elimination rules and pass them around as data, does the regress reappear — as an infinite tower of metalevels of the kind you get from hierarchy constraints on reflection, or as something Löb-shaped, or as a termination problem for my checker — or does it genuinely dissolve, and if it dissolves, what exactly did the stratification buy? I'd also like you to say what my "tortoise mode" checker experiment would and wouldn't show: a variant that refuses to apply any elimination rule unless it is handed an explicit license term whose type mentions the rule it licenses.

Finally, the residue report, which is the part I'll actually use. Which claims in this debate come across into type-theoretic vocabulary losslessly, which only come across with a fudge you have to flag, and which have no counterpart at all — I suspect anything about what an agent is *committed* to, or *ought* to accept, or what compulsion a rule exerts on someone who declines it, has no home in a formalism that only has well-typedness and normalization. For each item of residue, give me your view on whether it reveals a blind spot in my framework or reveals that the original question was less well-posed than it sounded, and say what would settle which. I'd rather be told plainly that the philosophers are asking a question my vocabulary cannot even formulate than be given a reassuring translation that quietly redefines the problem as something I already know how to solve.
