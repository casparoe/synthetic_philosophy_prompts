I'm a second-year PhD student in a reading group on the foundations of computation, and our convener — a senior philosopher of physics who has been thinking about the Church–Turing thesis for decades — has been ruling, week by week, on every purported counterexample we put in front of her. Her record so far: an oracle machine equipped with a halting oracle is dismissed on the ground that an oracle is a stipulation rather than a mechanism; accelerating machines of the supertask variety that perform infinitely many steps in finite time are dismissed because no positive lower bound on step duration survives; Shannon's general-purpose analog computer and other continuous-time analog devices are dismissed because they outrun Turing machines only under idealizations of exact real-valued parameters and unbounded measurement precision, and collapse back once realistic noise is put in; recurrent networks with high-precision real weights are dismissed on the same grounds; Malament–Hogarth spacetimes are dismissed as at best nomologically doubtful and in any case as requiring the observer to survive a supertask in order to get the answer out; the Deutsch-style treatment of closed timelike curves is dismissed because even taken at face value it stays inside the computable; the Lucas–Penrose argument from Gödel's theorem is dismissed as question-begging and as beside the point for anything we could build; quantum computers are set aside as a threat only to a separate feasibility claim about efficient computability rather than to the thesis itself; and the classical example of a wave equation whose unique solution at a fixed later time is non-computable although its initial data are computable is dismissed because those initial data are computable only in a weak sense and are not physically preparable. What I cannot work out is which thesis she is actually running, because every one of those candidates fails both of the tests I can imagine her applying — it is not physically buildable, and it is not a surveyable, step-by-step procedure whose output we can vouch for — so her verdicts are overdetermined, and two remarks she has made in seminar pull in different directions: one to the effect that the thesis is a contingent claim about what the physical world permits and that she would give it up if a working hypercomputer were wheeled into her lab, another to the effect that the thesis analyzes our pre-theoretic notion of an effective procedure and that a physical process which is not rule-following all the way down is not a computation at all, however it behaves.

The case scheduled for next week is a proposal from a condensed-matter group down the hall, and it seems to me to pass both of her tests at once. There is a computable recipe that, given the index n of a Turing machine, prints a description of a two-dimensional lattice of order-n sites with nearest-neighbor interactions drawn from a fixed finite alphabet of local terms, every coupling strength a dyadic rational specified to order-n bits, so there are no real parameters and no infinite precision anywhere. The lattice has a spectral gap bounded below by a positive constant independent of n; in its ground state a designated site points up if machine n halts and down if it does not; and the group has an engineered dissipative process that provably drives the lattice to its ground state from an arbitrary initial configuration in time polynomial in n. So in principle you build the lattice, wait, measure one spin, and read off the n-th bit of the halting problem — no supertask, no unbounded precision, no exotic spacetime, bounded energy, and one uniform effective recipe for the hardware at every input size. The wrinkle is that the Hamiltonian is not frustration-free, so no local measurement certifies that the system really reached its ground state rather than a long-lived metastable configuration; what you have instead is a reliability that can be pushed arbitrarily close to one by building independent copies and taking a majority, with no way of ever telling, for any given run, whether it succeeded.

Predict how she will rule on it. Lay out the candidate theses that fit her whole record equally well — I have in mind something like a bold physical reading, a conceptual reading on which the thesis is the analysis of an informal notion, and some kind of certification requirement, but I would rather you found the ones that actually fit than that you ratified my list — and say what each of them predicts here, since this is the first case on which I expect them to come apart. Give me your best guess with the reasons for preferring it, tell me which of her precedents is the closest analog and what exactly she would be committing herself to by ruling either way, and then name the variant of the case that would actually separate the readings from each other. I have floated two candidate discriminators of my own, a frustration-free redesign whose ground state can be certified by local measurements and a version in which the relaxation step is replaced by an explicit and fully surveyable numerical procedure, but I cannot tell whether either of them really cuts between the readings, and I would rather not bring a case to seminar that she waves away in a sentence.
