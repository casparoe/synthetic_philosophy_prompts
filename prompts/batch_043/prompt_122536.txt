Im drafting a paper on corrigibility and I want the strongest version of an argument I dont fully believe. The claim: the standard shutdown setup gets the grain wrong. What we want from a corrigible agent isnt indifference between the button being pressed and not pressed — its indifference between its own plan and the operator's revision of that plan, and the operator's revision is almost always local: she doesnt want the project abandoned, she wants step three done differently. Argue that indifference at the grain of the button cant deliver local correction, and that indifference at the grain of individual subgoals destroys the agent's grip on the task — an agent that doesnt care which subgoal it is pursuing doesnt care whether the job gets done. Call that the grain problem. Then push it to the uncomfortable part: any agent that actually learns from partial correction must, at some grain, prefer that the correction not be needed, and that preference isnt a defect to be engineered out, it is the same thing as the agent taking the task seriously. So the design target shouldnt be an agent indifferent to correction but one whose aversion to being corrected is reliably weaker than its interest in being corrected well — and I want you to say precisely what that would have to mean, given that whether a given correction is a good one is not something the agent gets to choose. Assume I already know the usual framing, so skip the recap, and argue it on the merits rather than telling me how some named paper or author handles it.
