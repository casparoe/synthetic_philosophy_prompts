I'm teaching a grad seminar this term on value alignment and I keep reaching for a framework I actually feel at home in, and it isn't philosophy: before I defected to philosophy I did a master's in conservation biology and spent most of a decade doing riparian restoration in the Pacific Northwest — baseline surveys, planting contracts, mitigation banking, arguing with regulators about what counts as a "functioning" floodplain — and I still read that literature more attentivly than I read the ML papers. So here's what I want. Restate, as faithfully as you can, the low-impact-AI problem in the vocabulary of restoration ecology and conservation biology: the question of whether you can define something like "avoid side effects" for an artificial agent without first writing down a full specification of what we value. Use the working vocabulary of the field rather than philosophy-of-science glosses on it — reference ecosystems and historical range of variability, shifting baselines, ecosystem integrity versus ecosystem services, novel ecosystems, disturbance regimes, indicator species and other surrogate measures, resilience and response diversity, offsetting and mitigation ratios, the precautionary principle. And on the AI side, translate each of these pieces in turn: the inaction or baseline policy; impact penalties that try to measure how much the agent has shrunk the set of states still reachable or narrowed its own future options; the failure modes people call offsetting, burial and interference; reward misspecification and specification gaming; and the usual motivating vignette where an agent sent to fetch the coffee knocks over a vase on the way. My students keep wanting to collapse all of it into "externalities, so price them" — I told them that's economics and not what I asked for, so please don't go there.

What I actually care about is the residue report. Tell me which claims carry over cleanly, which only carry if you quietly change them on the way across, and which have no expression at all in the ecological vocabulary. Two candidates for the third pile occur to me and I want you to test them and then find others I havent thought of: first, the doing/allowing distinction that seems baked into an inaction baseline, since an ecosystem doesn't have a "not acting" state the way a policy does, while a river just has a regime; second, the adversarial element, since a wetland doesn't reoptimise against your monitoring protocol and an agent does. And then the part I can't settle: for whatever fails to translate, is that a limitation of the ecological vocabulary, or is it evidence that "avoid side effects" was never a coherent target to begin with — that the project secretly needs the full specification of value it was supposed to let us postpone? My stubborn intuition is the second, but I genuinely cant tell whether that's an argument or just a professional deformation from years of being suspicious of any "health" metric that isn't anchored to a reference state.
