I teach a second-year option in formal epistemology: eighteen students, one ninety-minute slot, a whiteboard and a projector, no preparatory reading. Last week they met the Dutch book argument and came away with the impression that probabilism is a theorem about not losing money. My slot has to replace that with the accuracy-first vindication — that credences have a distinctly epistemic utility, that this utility is closeness to the truth, and that an incoherent credence function is thereby accuracy-dominated: some coherent rival is closer to the truth at every single world, with no expectation taken and no money changing hands. I want a plan for teaching that, not the explanation itself.

Four things I especially need decided.

What has to be in place before the question "why should your credences satisfy the probability axioms?" even sounds like a question with a non-pragmatic answer. My own diagnosis is that the blockage is the thought that accuracy is all-or-nothing, so that grading a whole credence function for accuracy looks like a category mistake. Tell me if that diagnosis is wrong and what actually has to come first.

The one example that carries the session. It has to be small enough that the class can compute every relevant number in full, more than once, at the board, and the credence function that gets dominated has to be one a real person would adopt rather than a straw man.

Where the measure of accuracy enters, and how the class is made to see that it is not arbitrary. I do not want to name the quadratic score and let authority do the work. I want them to try the obvious alternative — add up the absolute distances from the truth values — and watch it make certainty optimal whatever you believe. Then I need to know what to say to the student who asks whether the domination result survives a switch to the logarithmic score.

There is a complication I have to handle rather than dodge. The department grades its forecasting exercises with what a colleague calls the conviction score: you assign probabilities summing to one across a list of mutually exclusive outcomes, and your mark is the probability you gave the outcome that occurred minus the largest probability you gave any outcome that did not occur. It rewards calling it right decisively. I have half-convinced myself that with two outcomes it makes extremism optimal whatever you actually believe — back your favourite to the hilt — but I cannot tell whether that is an artefact of there being only two outcomes or the real defect, and I am not going to assert either in front of them. Settle it; decide whether the conviction score is the best available foil for the real thing or a distraction to be discarded for the day; and, if you keep it, place it to the minute.

Misconceptions I expect, in no particular order: that the accuracy-first argument is the Dutch book argument with the money deleted; that if truth is the only epistemic good then accuracy must favour extreme credence; that the quadratic measure is a bare stipulation; and that the domination result vindicates more than coherence, when on its own it says nothing about responding to evidence. Where in the sequence each must be headed off, and which has to be pre-empted before it can even form.

I know I will have to refuse three things openly: the lemma behind the domination result (I will assert it, not prove it), the question of which among the strictly proper scores is the correct one, and anything involving chance and credence. Tell me what else I should be refusing, and where in the sequence to say out loud that I am refusing it. The last ten minutes must leave them seeing that vindicating coherence is not the same as vindicating conditionalization; tell me what that closing move depends on.

Commit to one plan: one example, one measure, one order, one place for each misconception, one minute count. Where you think my framing is mistaken, give me your judgement and the correction rather than a menu of alternatives with their costs and benefits. Assume no library and no internet on my side, and do not give me a reading list.

Give the plan as a single markdown table, one row per step, with exactly these columns: `step` (a number and a five-to-eight-word label), `minutes` (cumulative), `move` (the thing said or put on the board, in one sentence), `needs` (which earlier step this rests on, and what breaks if I skip it), `misconception_closed` (the exact wrong thought this step forecloses, or an em dash), `check` (the one question or board vote that shows me whether it has landed).

After the table, a single JSON object with exactly these keys: `carrying_example`, `example_rejected` (what you considered and dropped, and why), `scoring_rule`, `conviction_score_verdict` (one sentence: proper or not, and the shortest computation that settles it), `refusals` (array), `why_not_just_dutch_book` (one sentence for the student who asks), `coherence_vs_updating` (one sentence for the last ten minutes), `biggest_risk` (what is most likely to go wrong when I run this). One definite value per key, no options offered, and your reasons inside the values where they fit.

Write in British English.
