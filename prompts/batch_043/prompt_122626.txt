I sit on the Research Priorities Committee of a large agricultural research institute. Every spring we allocate a restricted endowment to a single two-year postdoctoral fellowship, and this year we are down to two finalists. One proposal would make controlled-atmosphere stunning for broiler chickens cheaper and more reliable in mid-sized processing plants. The other would develop and validate welfare indicators for farmed black soldier fly larvae: substrate temperature thresholds, larval stocking density, and the choice of killing method at harvest. The argument for it is that insect protein production is scaling up quickly, that welfare standards written while the industry is still young are the ones it will inherit, and that the field currently has almost no agreed outcome measures. Both finalists are strong, so this is not a decision we can make on file quality.

What is dividing us is a number. The insect proposal's summary argues that farmed insect numbers already exceed farmed vertebrate numbers by three or four orders of magnitude and are growing far faster, that the standing number of insects alive at any moment is on the order of 10^18, and that even if the probability that larval insects have any valenced experience at all is as low as one or two percent, the expected scale swamps anything achievable in poultry. Two members want to adopt exactly that arithmetic as our ranking rule: individuals affected per dollar, multiplied by a probability of sentience, multiplied by the intensity and duration of the suffering averted. Two others think that formula is a machine for manufacturing conclusions from inputs nobody can defend, and that we should stay where the sentience premise is not itself in dispute.

Two further complications came up at our last two meetings and I want them addressed rather than noted. First, a colleague argues that the relevant population is not the farmed insects at all: production facilities leak or release enormous numbers of animals, and any welfare standard we help author will also change how many insects get farmed in the first place, plausibly upward, since welfare certification makes a fast-growing industry politically more comfortable. On that picture we cannot even sign our effect on total insect-years, let alone its size. Second, our terms of reference tell us to fund work whose findings could reasonably change purchasing or husbandry practice within five years, and no one on the committee expects the sentience question to be settled either way in that window, even though the indicator work would yield usable results regardless.

Draft the decision memo I will circulate to the committee before our April 17 meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame: one of the two fellowships, or a structured alternative such as funding one while commissioning a small piece of scoping work on the other, or deferring with a specified trigger. It should give reasons at a level of detail the unsuccessful applicant could actually respond to, and it should engage the strongest version of whichever case you reject: in particular, whether multiplying by a probability we cannot estimate is distinguishable from multiplying by an invented number, and whether an intervention whose net effect we cannot sign can still be worth funding on expected-value grounds. Be explicit about what you are assuming on the sentience question rather than trying to resolve it. Aim for roughly 1,200 words, in a form I can put straight into the meeting packet.
