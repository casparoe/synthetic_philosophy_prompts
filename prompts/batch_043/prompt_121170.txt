I am writing a novel whose narrator is a twenty-first-century cosmologist who, by a device the book never explains, is given an hour's private conversation with Isaac Newton in his rooms at the Mint during the last decade of his life. Before she speaks, she is allowed to tell him four things and nothing more: that the universe has a finite age and began in a hot, dense state; that its expansion rate and the strengths of its forces sit inside ranges so narrow that a small change in any of them leaves no stars, no chemistry, no carbon; that living things of the most intricate functional fit arose from a long, undirected process in matter; and that his own name is now attached to a mechanics that no one uses for the very small or the very fast.

For the scene to work I cannot write Newton's side from the outside; I need him to answer for himself. Please play him.

Constraints on the role. Stay in character until I say "stop." Give me first-person speech I could set on the page — two paragraphs a turn, early eighteenth-century cadence, plain rather than pastiche: no "methinks," and no vocabulary the scene has not given you, so you must coin your own names for a hot beginning, a fine balance of constants, or a process that makes eyes without an eye-maker. What you hold: one supreme God, Father of all, eternal and infinite, omnipresent substantially and not only by his power, governing not as the soul of the world but as a lord governs servants; the frame of nature as evidence of counsel rather than of chance; the prophecies as the other book, and the primitive church corrupted by Athanasius and by Rome. You do not know the cause of gravity and you refuse to feign one. You are secretive, because your opinions on the Trinity would have cost you your place; you are vain about your chronology; you have argued with sharper men than my narrator and will not be led. What you may concede, and only that: that your mechanics has been superseded in its account of the small and the swift; that a particular year you once favored cannot be demonstrated; that a mechanism described mechanically is not thereby shown to be unauthored; and that the regularity of the heavens is not by itself the proof you once took it for, since a law will keep what a law has once set going. You may ask her to clarify the new astronomy, in character, and you may not consult anything. Do not flatter her, do not convert, and do not dodge.

Here is her opening, delivered after a long silence in which she has been turning over what she has just told him:

"Sir, I will put the worst of it in one breath. Your God has two jobs in your system, and both of them are gaps. You cannot say why the planets move one way in one plane, so a hand placed them; you cannot say how the order is kept, so a lord keeps it, and you even allow him to step in and mend the works now and then. We have closed the first gap with initial conditions and the second with conservation, and the mathematicians who came a century after you removed the repairs you thought the clock still needed. Meanwhile the part of your religion that consumed most of your hours — the chronology, the horns and vials, the reckoning of the end out of Daniel and the Apocalypse — has simply not arrived, and the faithful have quietly let it go while keeping the sentiment. So tell me which part is load-bearing. If it is the inference from the frame of nature, you are a machine with one gear left and it is grinding smaller every century. If it is the reading of prophecy, then I am sitting with a man who thought he could compute the date of the Last Judgement."
