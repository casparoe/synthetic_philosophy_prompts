You are a professional philosopher who also writes satirical policy prose. Before drafting, think carefully: begin with a short bullet plan (a dozen lines or so) naming, section by section, which real feature of Pascal's mugging each part of the piece is going to send up. Then write the piece itself.

The piece: a mock UK government consultation document of roughly 600–700 words, headed along the lines of "Office for Speculative Harm: Draft Framework for Unbounded Claims", and issued by a fictional department created after ministers conceded that the reasoning of Pascal's mugging is sound — that a threat concerning a hyper-astronomical number of future sufferers, however implausible, must dominate any expected-value calculation, since no probability can be driven to zero and utility has no ceiling.

Constraints:

- The comedy has to come from getting the decision theory right: unbounded utility scales, the impossibility of assigning a hard zero probability, the symmetry between the mugger who threatens vast harm and the "optimist" who offers to create vast good in exchange for a fiver, and the resulting queue of claimants each of whom the Treasury owes literally everything.
- Invent at least two bureaucratic mechanisms that implement a real reply to the argument — for example, a leverage-discount rule under which any claim to affect N persons is divided by N on the grounds that N rival claimants could make the identical claim, and a registration-and-lottery scheme for apportioning a budget that must be split infinitely many ways. Each mechanism should sound administratively sober while quietly smuggling in a substantive philosophical objection.
- Never break character to explain the paradox. The objections should surface only as implementation details, fee schedules, worked examples and appeals to value-for-money.
- Leave the reader genuinely unsure whether the reductio indicts the mugger's reasoning or merely this civil service's way of honouring it.

Write in British English, in the register of a real government consultation — numbered clauses, a glossary, an impact assessment, at least one consultation question — for a reader who is an analytic philosopher with a taste for decision theory.
