Im a science-and-ideas journo doing a short piece on how a big scientific society rewrote its inclusive-language style guide, so I've been sitting in on their language committee (they let me observe, on background). I keep coming back to Hofstadter's 'Person Paper' — the move where you swap 'person' in wherever 'man' shows up, notice which results sound ridiculous, and then ask why the original didn't — but I definately can't tell which version of that test this committee is actually running.

Their rulings so far:

- chairman → chair, with chairperson explicitly voted down as clunky
- mankind → humankind
- man-made → synthetic
- manpower → workforce
- man-hours → person-hours (a person-compound was fine here)
- the verb to man a booth or a telescope → staff or operate
- manuscript, manual (as in manual override), manufacture → no change, their stated reason being that the man in those is from the Latin for hand and nobody hears a male in them
- human, humanity → no change
- manhole → no change, but on a 3–2 vote, the dissenters saying that if man-made goes this has to go
- a motion to just mechanically swap person in everywhere, satire-style, voted down with almost no discussion

Four items are on next week's agenda and I want to call them before they do: unmanned aerial vehicle (does it matter that unmanned also has that older, other sense?); man-in-the-middle attack in cryptography; mannequin, both in a methods section and in the shop-window sense; and hey guys as a way of addressing a mixed room at their own conference.

What do you think they do with each, what two or three rules fit the record above about equally well, and which further case — not one of my four — would best tell those rules apart? Also flag where your guess is thinnest: which single extra ruling, if I can get a committee member to give me a hypothetical answer, would most change your prediction, and where is your reading of the track record most likely to be wrong (the manhole dissent is what worries me).
