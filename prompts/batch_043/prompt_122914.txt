I'm a third-year undergraduate in a 300-level philosophy of biology seminar. Our TA has split the revision session before finals between us and asked each of us to draft one practice exam question on a block of the course, together with a model answer and a marking scheme. I've been given the block on genetic information: whether DNA is literally a code, whether "information" and "coding" in molecular biology are doing real work or are just vivid metaphors, and what would settle the question.

Please act as a professional philosopher of biology who has spent years setting and marking papers at this level. Before writing anything, spend a short paragraph brainstorming two or three candidate ways into the topic and say explicitly which one you are choosing and why it discriminates better than the others — I would rather see your reasoning about what makes a question hard than have you skip straight to the answer.

The problem I keep running into is that every question I can think of is answerable by writing "some people say the code talk is literal, others say it is heuristic, here is one argument each way." The whole cohort can already do that in their sleep, and it will not separate anyone. So please build the question around a crux that has to be worked out in the exam room, using the hypothetical below (invented for the exam, so nobody is at an advantage for knowing some empirical detail):

Suppose we discovered a lineage of single-celled organisms — call it lineage V — whose entire codon table has been rewritten: every triplet is assigned a different amino acid or stop signal than in its close relatives, and its transfer RNAs and the enzymes that charge them have been rewritten along with it. Yet lineage V's proteins come out with almost the same amino acid sequences and the same functions as those of its relatives, and the organisms are otherwise ordinary. So the codon-to-amino-acid mapping has drifted wholesale while the products have stayed put.

Now the two things I want students to have to confront:

First, what this drift does to the arbitrariness claim. A table that can be rewritten while the output stays fixed looks like the strongest possible evidence that the mapping is not dictated by the chemistry of the nucleotides and the amino acids. But it also looks like evidence that the mapping is causally downstream of everything that matters, a bookkeeping device we project onto the system from outside, since no difference in the organism's proteins or phenotype tracks which table it uses. Students should have to say which of those readings is right, or explain why both are compatible, rather than note that both exist.

Second, the regress about the reader. The charging enzymes and the translation machinery in lineage V are themselves products of the very genome they translate, so there is no decoder standing outside the system that could have stipulated the mapping the way a convention needs a stipulator. I want students to say whether this should trouble someone who calls DNA a code, or whether it is simply irrelevant because codes do not need interpreters, only mechanisms that reliably map inputs to outputs. And whichever way they go, I want them to say what work the word "code" is then doing that a purely causal description — this nucleotide sequence reliably produces that amino acid sequence — could not do.

Constraints on the deliverable:

- Level and format: a 45-minute in-class essay question for upper-level undergraduates, maximum 900 words, worth 20 marks. The stem should be self-contained: state any assumption about how translation works inside the question so that no one can gain or lose marks for knowing a fact from outside the course.
- The model answer must be committed. Take a definite position on the hypothetical and on the two issues above, and argue for it. I do not want a balanced survey that sets out the options and leaves me to choose; if you think the code talk survives but only in a deflationary form, say that and defend it, and likewise if you think it collapses into causal description or survives robustly. Give reasons a marker could reconstruct.
- The marking scheme should be built around five must-hit points, with the marks each is worth (totalling 20) and what, concretely, an answer must do to earn them. For each point add one line on how students in my cohort typically go wrong there — for instance pat answers that repeat the label without the argument, or moves that sound like objections but are really just restatements of the question.
- Nothing in the model answer or the scheme may depend on naming a particular author, paper, or historical source. Any point made in those terms must also be statable in a student's own words, and a candidate who phrases it in their own words should get full credit.
- Finish with a short note on where your model answer is most contestable — the one place you would expect a strong student to push back — so I can flag it in the session and be ready for the argument.
