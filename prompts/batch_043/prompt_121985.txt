I am a backend engineer with no formal training in philosophy. For the past few months I have been writing, in my spare time, a long-form piece for a small audience of programmer friends, in which I try to reconstruct Leibniz's claim that this is the best of all possible worlds as a *well-posed optimization problem* — and then try to repair it when it turns out not to be one. I would be grateful for critical comments on the notes below before I invest more time in developing them.

Two framing requests. First: I would rather be told that the central mapping is a category error than be told that it is promising and needs polish. Second: I cannot check references, so please do not rest any point on citations. If something depends on a scholarly dispute, just flag it as disputed rather than telling me who is on which side.

---

**Notes toward "God's build script"**

**1. The target, as I understand it.** Leibniz holds that there are infinitely many possible worlds — candidate total histories sitting, so to speak, in the divine understanding — and that God actualizes the best one. Not everything possible can coexist: some collections of possibles are *incompossible*, so a world is a maximal collection of mutually compossible things rather than an arbitrary grab bag. God's choice is, on my reading, morally necessary but not logically necessary: the reasons incline without necessitating, so there is a genuine selecting rather than a deduction. The measure being maximized is not human happiness but something like total *perfection*, which I take to be a blend of richness — the greatest variety of phenomena — and *order*, roughly, law-governedness and structure. Evil comes in three grades: metaphysical (sheer finitude, i.e. being a creature at all), physical (suffering), and moral (wrongdoing). And the characteristic move of the theodicy is not that each individual evil has a local justification, but that removing a given evil might require selecting a different, globally worse world: you cannot patch one event and hold the rest of the world fixed, because the patch propagates.

**2. My re-implementation.** I want to make this precise enough to argue with, so I have been treating a possible world as *the output of a program*: the laws together with the initial conditions are the program, and the total history is what it prints. Compossibility then looks like satisfiability — take individual concepts as constraints, and a world is a maximal satisfying assignment. Perfection becomes a value functional V over worlds, and creation is argmax over the feasible set. Writing it that way exposed three problems that I had not seen stated in the popular summaries.

*Well-posedness.* Maximizing a functional over an infinite feasible set need not have a solution: the supremum may not be attained. The informal version of this is the familiar protest that for any world God could have added one more flourishing creature, or made everyone one degree happier. If the feasible set is not compact in the relevant sense, the best of all possible worlds may simply not exist, and the doctrine collapses before we get to anything about evil.

*Aggregation.* Even given a well-behaved feasible set, total perfection over infinitely many creatures behaves like a divergent series. Depending on whether you sum by taking a limit of averages over finite sub-populations, or by comparing densities, or by using a lexicographic or non-Archimedean ordering that is sensitive to finite differences, you get different winners. When the answer to a metaphysical question depends on which summation convention you adopt, that is a bad smell.

*The single-miracle regression test.* This is the one that worries me most, and it is very natural to an engineer's habits. If a world is the global maximum, then no *small edit* of it can itself be feasible and better — otherwise the original was not the maximum. So: consider a world that is exactly like ours up to some time, except that one particular incident of terrible suffering does not occur, and which then continues in the ordinary law-governed way with all the downstream consequences we would expect from that one difference. It looks compossible, it looks better, and it looks short — one line of diff. If such patches are admissible, the theodicy fails a unit test that any of my colleagues would run in the first five minutes.

**3. My proposed repair.** Add a penalty for the length of the specification. Let the objective be perfection minus some positive multiple of the description length of the world's laws and initial conditions — a simplicity term, in the spirit of minimum-description-length or Solomonoff-style priors. Three things seem to follow.

(i) The penalty acts as a regularizer: it can turn an unattained supremum into an attained maximum, because the worlds that push perfection up without bound also push description length up.

(ii) It blocks the regression test. A world containing a one-off exception to its own laws is *less compressible* than a law-uniform world; even if the patch gains local value, it pays in bits. God does not miracle-patch because miracle-patching decompresses the world.

(iii) It retrodicts something. If creation is simplicity-weighted, we should expect a world that is lawful, compressible, and simple at its base while being riotously various at its surface — which is roughly what we observe.

And it yields a theodicy with a new shape: some suffering is not a price paid for a greater good elsewhere in the chain of causes, but a compression artifact. Getting maximum variety out of a minimum specification is lossy; the artifacts are not placed where we would have placed them, and they are not *for* anything. I think this is a genuinely different shape of answer from the one Leibniz is usually summarized as giving, and half the reason I want to keep writing is to see whether it can be stated without embarrassment.

**4. What I dislike about my own repair.** The objective now has a tuning knob, and the choice of weight looks arbitrary: at one extreme you get back the ill-posed problem, at the other you get a crystalline world with no phenomena at all. The penalty also sounds *epistemic* — it is about how concisely the world can be *described* — whereas Leibniz's order is meant to be a feature of the world, not of our encoding of it; I do not know whether that gap is fatal or merely cosmetic. I also half-remember something about Leibniz prizing hypotheses that are simple in their assumptions and rich in their consequences, which sounds close to what I am doing, but I do not trust the memory and I do not know whether it is a remark about scientific method rather than about creation. Finally, my repair may change the dialectic in a way that hurts me: it replaces *we cannot be expected to see the reason* with *the reason is a known engineering trade-off*, which is testable, and I am not certain the test comes out in my favour.

**What I would like from you**

1. **Fidelity.** Where have I misread Leibniz? In particular: is perfection really a variety-plus-order composite, is compossibility merely consistency or something stronger and stranger, is the maximal-consistent-set mapping more distorting than illuminating, and am I leaning more weight on moral-necessity-than-logical-necessity than it can bear?

2. **The failure mode I care about most.** Is treating creation as an optimization over *specifications* — worlds as programs, not worlds as objects — a category error from the start? If so, tell me plainly, and tell me which of my three problems survives even after I drop the programming metaphor.

3. **The regression test.** Is the single-miracle test a fair statement of the intuitive problem, or does my stipulation that the patched world continues law-governed downstream already concede too much or too little? I am especially unsure whether holding everything downstream fixed is even coherent.

4. **Whether the simplicity term is a cheat.** Does it really make the maximum exist, and does it really block miracle patches? My own worry is that a *general* law preventing a class of suffering could be shorter to specify than the messy arrangement we actually have — in which case the compression theodicy predicts a lessevil world than the one we are in, and refutes itself. Is that worry correct, and can the move survive it?

5. **The knob.** Is there a non-arbitrary way to fix the trade-off between perfection and simplicity, or does every candidate weighting collapse into one extreme or the other?

6. **Triage.** If you had to name the single weakest sentence in these notes — the one a professional philosopher would stop on — which is it, and why? And is there a residual version of the project (creation as *regularized* optimization over world-specifications, evil as an artifact of the regularizer) that is worth developing honestly, or is the whole program a case of an engineer seeing his own tools everywhere?
