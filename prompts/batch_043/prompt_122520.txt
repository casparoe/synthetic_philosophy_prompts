I write about science and ideas, and I'm doing a piece on how hospitals decide who gets the last ICU bed, which is how I got dragged into this. A philosopher friend is very into that old Taurek paper — you know, should the numbers count — and he laid out an argument I haven't been able to poke a hole in, even though I'm sure the conclusion is wrong.

(1) Something can only be a bad outcome if it's bad for someone. Five deaths aren't worse for anyone than one death: there's no sixth subject who bears the extra four, and each of the people involved either lives or dies.
(2) So in a choice between saving one stranger and saving five, the fact that five would die rather than one gives you no reason at all. All that's left is that each of the six is a separate person with a separate claim on you.
(3) If those six claims are equal and none outweighs another, the only way to respect them all equally is to give each person the same chance of coming out alive.
(4) And the only way to do that without counting people is to toss a coin between the two rescue options: save the one, or save the five.
(5) Nothing in (1)–(4) turns on the number six. So you'd have to toss a coin between saving one person and saving a million. And since (3) is about equal respect rather than about what's merely permitted, a triage protocol that reliably sends the bed to the larger group isn't just optional, it's unjust.
(6) Which is nuts. Nobody wants a health service that flips coins, and nobody thinks it's fine to shrug and save the one.

I'd much rather keep (1) then (5), but I can't say where the thing breaks. Which step do you give up, why did it look sound, and what does giving it up cost you — does it force you to accept some impersonal value sitting out there, or can the argument be fixed some other way?
