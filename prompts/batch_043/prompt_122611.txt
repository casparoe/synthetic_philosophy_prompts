I'm a PhD student finishing the second chapter of a dissertation on the situationist critique of virtue ethics, and I have a gap I keep failing to fill. The negative half of the chapter is in decent shape: I argue there that the standard virtue-ethical replies — the retreat to local or situation-specific traits, the claim that the experimental situations aren't the ones in which the virtue is at issue, the point about small-but-real effect sizes — each concede more than their users notice. What my committee wants, and what I can't construct myself, is a worked case that runs in the direction nobody ever writes: not the familiar one where the situationist denies a robust trait and the virtue ethicist insists on one, but the reverse, where the situationist's own evidential standards commit her to attributing a robust, stable, predictive disposition and the virtue ethicist is the one who has to withhold the virtue.

So here is what I need from you: a single realistic scenario — a hospital ward, a lab, an office, a school; one paragraph; no emergencies, no authority figures issuing orders, no experimenter deception, no strangers in need of change, and nothing that counts as a strong situation of the sort that lets a virtue ethicist say the trait was merely swamped — in which an agent behaves, across a run of ordinary situations and over a long stretch of time, in a way that an ordinary observer and a friendly interpreter would both describe with an ordinary-language virtue term (conscientiousness, honesty, fairness, loyalty: something both sides want to claim), and behaves that way with enough cross-situational consistency and predictive validity that the situationist critique's own criteria for a robust trait are satisfied, with no single situational variable left doing the explanatory work. The catch, which is the point of the exercise: the correct virtue-ethical verdict must be that the agent does not have the virtue, because the disposition generating the behavior is a lookalike — stable, reliably productive of right action within the situations anyone actually samples, but generated by a mechanism that the virtue ethicist is committed, by her own standard conditions on virtue (acting for the right reasons, for the sake of the fine, with the right affect, under the guidance of practical wisdom — pick your preferred formulation and say which you are using), to counting as non-virtuous. I want that verdict derived from those conditions rather than announced by stipulating that the agent lacks practical wisdom, and I want the lookalike to be a near miss rather than a plain vice: it should not simply be cowardice or sycophancy producing behavior that superficially resembles courage or friendliness, since then the denial is too cheap to be interesting.

The constraint that makes this hard, and the one I keep violating: the lookalike and the genuine virtue must be indistinguishable to the situationist's instruments — identical in coded behavior, identical in anything the agent would say about her own motives — and must differ only in their counterfactual profile across a class of situations that you specify in advance and can characterize independently of what the agent does in them, since otherwise the construction is circular and you have simply picked out the situations in which she happens to go wrong. My own hunch is that the feature doing the work is an ambiguity in robustness: a frequency-weighted reading, on which a trait is robust if it predicts behavior across the situations people actually encounter, weighted by how often they encounter them, versus a normatively loaded reading, on which a trait is robust only if it holds across the range of situations that matter for whether the state deserves the name of the virtue — ones where acting rightly requires breaking a rule the agent has internalized, or acting with no audience, or resisting her peers' consensus, or overriding her own settled policy. If you think a different feature is carrying the case, say so and argue for it rather than indulging my hunch.

Then, after the scenario, give me four things. First, what the situationist says about the case and why, judged by the critique's own standards for attributing or denying a trait. Second, what the virtue ethicist says and why, with the mechanism shown working from the theory down rather than asserted. Third, the single structural feature the case turns on, together with a demonstration that removing it collapses the case: my expectation is that making the mechanism behaviorally visible inside the sampled situations turns this back into an ordinary situationist study in which both sides deny a trait, while making the disposition robust under normative variation as well turns it into the virtue proper — but tell me if the collapse actually goes some other way. Fourth, the two or three strongest lines of counter-attack available to a situationist. The ones I have in mind are that the critique was only ever about the empirical existence of robust traits and that which traits count as virtues is a separate question; that my discriminating situations are contrived and robustness is frequency-weighted by definition; and that the virtue ethicist has no non-question-begging epistemic access to the mechanism, so the lookalike distinction does no empirical work. Tell me which of these should worry me most in a dissertation chapter and what the one-paragraph reply is. Keep the whole response under about nine hundred words, keep the psychology to mechanisms a working social psychologist would already recognize — no clinical disorders, no invented neuroscience — and do not dress the case up with invented study citations: I want a case, not a fabricated empirical result. Above all, since the point is that the two verdicts should fall out of the two theories rather than be written into the scenario, flag any place where you find yourself stipulating a verdict instead of deriving one.
