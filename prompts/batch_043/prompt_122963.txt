I'm running a grad seminar on divine providence and foreknowledge this term, and I've got two students who've been going at each other since week two. One is a full-bore Molinist: middle knowledge is a genuine third logical moment, counterfactuals of creaturely freedom have determinate truth values before any decree, and that's what makes providence, prophecy and petitionary prayer work. The other holds something like simple foreknowledge plus a grounding objection: there is nothing to make a counterfactual of freedom true except God's decree or the creature's own future choice, so middle knowledge is either empty or quietly collapses into one of the other two moments. Under mild duress they've agreed to write an adversarial collaboration instead of another two 30-page papers, and I volunteered to help them draft the thing. So: write the document.

I want a numbered list of claims both of them can sign — including about the machinery itself, not just platitudes. E.g. that the three moments are at least coherent as a taxonomy; that whatever God knows, the knowing isn't discursive or learned; that if counterfactuals of freedom are true they are accidentally necessary and not up to the creature; that any view here has to give the same account of what it is to know a contingent truth about a future free act. You'll find better ones than these, and please don't pad it with a bibliography or a reading list — the department has seen enough of those.

Then a table of the disagreements, one claim per row, each phrased so that the side affirming it would actually sign that wording. No position summaries. I specifically want rows for:

1. Semantics. On anything like a closest-world (Lewis/Stalnaker-ish) semantics for counterfactuals, it looks like the conditionals Molinism needs can't be robustly true for a libertarian agent, since in the nearest circumstances-worlds some go one way and some the other. Does Molinism need a special primitive here, and is that a cost or just the right semantics for a kind of conditional ordinary semantics never aimed at?
2. Grounding and the tu quoque. Does the grounding objection overgeneralise — whatever makes future-tensed contingent propositions true for the simple-foreknowledge view, why can't the Molinist use the same story for counterfactuals of freedom? Both of them agree this question carries real weight, so put down what each predicts the right answer is.
3. Something I've been pushing in seminar and want in the table: separate *weak* middle knowledge (God knows every counterfactual of freedom but the pattern across feasible worlds is just brute and unpatterned) from *strong* middle knowledge (the counterfactual facts hang together character-wise, so knowing some constrains the rest). Nobody I've read bothers with this. My worry is the feasibility cliff: for all Molinism says, the set of feasible worlds might be wretchedly sparse — nearly every creature transworldly depraved in every world where it's significantly free — in which case middle knowledge gives God knowledge of a menu of bad options and buys you almost none of the providence it's advertised as delivering. Is the strong/weak distinction something the Molinist should be committed to, and does the sparseness worry land?
4. Which parts of this are actually verbal. I suspect at least three: 'freedom' (leeway vs. sourcehood), 'sovereignty' (control over which world is actual vs. control over what happens within it), and what 'could have done otherwise' is even supposed to track. Say which rows are terminological, and for each one state the substantive question hiding underneath. Relatedly: the Molinist keeps calling the other one a crypto-determinist and she flatly denies it — tell me whether that's a real disagreement or a failure of formulation, and if it's the latter, write the row properly.

For each real crux, give one concrete case or argument both would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in a form that could turn out to be wrong. The case I made up for (1) and (2) was a clerk, call her Aliya, who is never in fact offered the bribe: ask whether it's determinately true now that she'd freely refuse in circumstances C, and then ask competent speakers who have no theology at all. Both my students agreed in class that how ordinary counterfactual talk behaves is evidence here, and then immediately disagreed about what it shows. Use that case if it's any good, replace it if it isn't.
