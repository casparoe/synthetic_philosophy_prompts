hey — I run the eval/oversight team at a mid-size lab, and we're building a training signal for tasks where our labelers definately can't tell a good answer from a merely plausible one: a 600-line diff that supposedly fixes a bug, a four-page synthesis of some empirical literature, a memo saying a product feature is fine under a rule none of our annotators know. Roughly forty of these task types have landed on me and I decide, per type, what oversight setup we run: plain human labeling, breaking the answer into chunks we can each check, or the two-arguer debate setup where a judge weaker than either arguer picks a winner. Right now we choose by vibes, it's inconsistent, I've got about three days per task type, and whatever I pick has to survive a quarterly sign-off from a safety reviewer who wasn't in the room.

So: give me an ordered decision procedure I can actually run on a task type, grounded in whatever the scalable-oversight-via-debate idea really implies about when adversarial argument helps instead of just relocating the problem onto the judge. Don't give me a reading list or a summary of papers. And don't stop at "use debate when the truth is hard to verify" — I need the distinctions that survive being turned into questions I can answer in an afternoon, in a form someone who dosen't know the theory can follow.

The part I keep getting stuck on, and where I want the procedure to do real work: I think there are three different reasons a task can be hard to check, and they don't all want the same setup. One, the answer is long but every piece of it is checkable in a few minutes — that smells like a decomposition problem, not a debate problem. Two, the answer is hard to check but wrong answers leave fingerprints: to hold a false claim together you have to keep adding specifics, and every specific is another chance to get caught. Three, the answer is hard to check and a competent deceiver can produce a self-consistent story with no seams at all. I want the procedure to separate these with tests I can actually run in a pilot, to say what to do when I can't get a number and the test comes back inconclusive, and to flag the places where it's honestly a judgment call wearing a procedure costume.

I also need it to catch the failure where debate looks fine but isn't: judges reward fluency and confidence, both sides get better at arguing than at being right, and the accuracy gain comes from the arguer being smarter than the judge rather than from the truth being demonstrable. What do I measure in the pilot to spot that, and what's the abort condition — when does the procedure say this task type gets no training signal from us at all, and we either change the task or ship it unlabeled?

Then run it on three cases, as a table at the end:

A. Decide whether a model-written patch to a repo we didn't write really fixes the reported issue and doesn't break something else. We have the repo, we can run the test suite, nobody here knows the codebase, and the issue reports are vague.
B. Grade whether a four-page answer summarizing an empirical question represents the strongest opposing result fairly, when the judge reads at the level of an interested non-specialist.
C. Vet a model-drafted memo claiming a proposed product feature is fine under a consumer-finance regulation, where our annotators know the product but not the regulation.

C is the one I'll like least: tell me straight if the procedure routes it to an outside expert and caps what we can learn from it, and don't soften it into something I'd rather hear.
