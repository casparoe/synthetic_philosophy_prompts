Me and a friend have been going back and forth on this for like two months and I need someone to point at the broken step, because the way I built it, the argument seems to eat itself and then eat everything else too. I have no philosophy background, this is just me thinking in the evenings, so sorry if I'm reinventing something with a name.

The topic is whether you can get somewhere on aesthetic questions by being told. Here's the chain:

(1) Believing something on the basis of testimony just means: the fact that someone said it is part of your reason for believing it. Part, not necessarily the whole thing.

(2) Testimony is a general-purpose pipeline. If someone knows that P, then barring weird defeaters you can come to know that P by being told. There's no restriction on subject matter built into testimony as such.

(3) Aesthetic judgments can be known. People know that a song is shattering, that a film is clumsy, that a building is garish.

(4) But the only thing that can put you in a position to make an aesthetic judgment about X is experiencing X yourself. Someone online called it the acquaintance principle. A hundred reviews still leave you unable to judge until you've actually seen it.

(5) Being told is not experiencing. So testimony can never be your reason for an aesthetic judgment.

(6) So no aesthetic judgment can be known on the basis of testimony.

(7) (2), (3) and (6) can't all be true. (1) is basically a definition and (3) is obvious, so drop (2): testimony does have a subject-matter restriction, and what it tracks is experience.

(8) And the restriction in (4) isn't really about art at all. It's about anything whose correctness depends on what the experience is like: how hot the chili is, whether the hotel is noisy at 3am, whether the job is soul-destroying, whether the joke is actually funny, how bad someone's back pain is, whether the ward is grim.

(9) So nobody can ever come to know, from being told, that the chili is mouth-numbing, or the job is crushing, or the ward is grim.

(10) That's nuts. I've rebooked flights because a friend described a hotel's noise. People pick careers off that kind of report. Doctors act on pain reports. If (9) were right most of what we think we know about anything experiential just evaporates.

(11) So (4) has to go, and testimony can be a reason for aesthetic judgments after all — which means the person who has only read the reviews can know it's a masterpiece in exactly the same way the person who saw it does.

(12) But I really dont want to say that either. There's something the person who actually heard it has and I don't, and it's not just extra information.

Two cases I made up that I think break the easy moves, and I'd like you to deal with them rather than around them.

A. Discordant unanimity. Ten thousand people whose tastes have basically nothing in common — people who disagree about nearly every film, song and building — all independently say some new piece is shattering. The usual move I've seen is that aesthetic testimony only ever tells you about your own likely reaction, not about the work, and that's why it feels different from being told the train is delayed. But in this case there's no correlation of taste for that explanation to lean on, and the testimony still seems to land on me. So what is it telling me, if not something about the work?

B. The sealed novel. A novel is sealed for a hundred years. Twelve critics read it under agreement, come out and all say it's shattering; nobody else will ever be able to read it in their lifetime, including me. Is there anything I now know? If experience is a necessary condition on the judgment, it looks like the verdict is permanently unavailable to me and yet I'm not prepared to say I learned nothing at all.

My own attempt at a fix was that aesthetic testimony never hands you the verdict, it only hands you a reason to go look — a pointer rather than a proposition — but case B makes that look wrong, since there's nothing to go and look at.
