A guy in my reading group has been pushing this argument for weeks and I think its broken but I can't put my finger on where. He says it's basically Hume's case against miracles, tidied up, and that once you tidy it up you see it proves way more than Hume let on.

His version, as close as I can get it:

(1) A miracle, in the sense that matters, is not just a surprising or rare event -- it's an event contrary to a law of nature, something that can't have happened unless a generalization we've confirmed countless times has an exception.
(2) We have no reason at all to believe any law of nature except uniform experience: we've seen the regularity hold again and again and we've never seen it break.
(3) So the evidence that the law holds on this occasion is as strong as inductive evidence gets. He uses Hume's term for this: a "proof" -- not logical certainty, but a body of experience so large that no finite pile of contrary reports can be stacked against it.
(4) The evidence for a miracle is testimonial, and testimonial evidence is only ever as good as our track record for witnesses of that kind in circumstances of that kind, which is always finite and imperfect.
(5) Both bodies of evidence are, in the end, just records of experience, so they're commensurable and the bigger one wins. A finite imperfect record can never outweigh a proof.
(6) So no quantity of testimony -- however many witnesses, however independent, however sober and disinterested -- can ever make it reasonable to believe a miracle occurred. The rational response is always to conclude the witnesses were mistaken, or lying, or that the report got garbled on its way to us.
(7) And none of this depends on the content of the miracle, or on lawhood specifically. The same reasoning goes through for any well-confirmed regularity: if we've confirmed that X doesn't happen, testimony that X happened loses by default.

(7) is the step he thinks is interesting, because it means the argument isn't really about religion. And (7) is also where I lose him, because it seems to swallow things I'm not willing to give up:

- Take a network of, say, forty instrument chains on four continents, sealed, self-calibrating, triple-redundant, results cryptographically signed and archived with no human in the loop, all reporting that a body in a vacuum chamber accelerated upward for three seconds. He has to say the right response is "the instruments are wrong." But that means no amount of instrumentation could ever disconfirm a law, which I take to be obviously false. He bites the bullet and says we'd have been right to go on believing the law -- which is insane, but I can't say why.
- Take a modern court case: thirty independent witnesses, none of whom know each other, testify that a man certified dead by two doctors sat up in the morgue and walked out. No law of nature is violated there -- people survive overdoses, doctors misdiagnose -- but "people certified dead don't get up and leave" is about as well confirmed a regularity as you could want. Juries convict on less. His argument seems to say they shouldn't.
- And then the case he actually cares about: a resurrection-type report attested in several apparently independent sources from two thousand years ago. Here I want his conclusion. I really don't want to end up saying that's credible.

He has one move I half-understand. He calls it the closure of the test set: a law is only ever confirmed for conditions inside some tested class, and a report from outside that class isn't competing with the law at all, it's just silent about it. That sounds like it dissolves the whole argument -- and he agrees it would, but says it doesn't matter, because we've got no positive reason to think this case is different from the tested ones, and "we have no reason to think it's different" is all the argument ever needed.

So: which step do I reject? And be honest about the bill -- I'd rather keep (1) through (5) and I'd rather keep (6) for the religious case than give up that the instrument network could ever be right, so tell me what I'm paying if I insist on that combination, and tell me why the step I'm rejecting looked fine in the first place. Also, if it's (7) that goes, I want to know what work "law of nature" vs. "well-confirmed regularity" has to do afterwards, because I have a wierd feeling that the version of the argument that stops at laws is too narrow to be interesting and the version that doesn't eats everything.
