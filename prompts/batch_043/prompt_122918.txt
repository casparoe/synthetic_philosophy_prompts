hey — I'm a second year PhD student working on blame and the reactive attitudes, and I keep hitting the same proverb in seminar: people in glass houses shouldn't throw stones. Last week I was arguing that hypocritical blame can still be *fitting* — the target really did the wrong thing, the blame really does track that wrong thing, and nothing about the blamer's own record seems to change either of those facts — and a classmate leaned back, said "people in glass houses", and treated it as settled. I don't think it is, but I also can't get a grip on what precise claim it's supposed to be expressing, because every time I push on it, it slides into a different one.

Could you lay out the menu of precise theses that one-liner could be standing for, with verdicts? The axes I keep getting tangled on:

- what counts as a stone: blaming, or censuring out loud, or punishing, or demanding an apology, or just form a private judgement that someone acted badly? Does the proverb even cover the last one?
- what counts as living in a glass house: the very same fault, any fault at all, a relevantly similar fault, or specifically a fault you *haven't owned up to*?
- what the "shouldn't" is: prudential (you'll get found out and look worse), moral, conversational (inapt, out of order), or something stronger about the blamer lacking the standing to do it at all?
- what the *consequence* is supposed to be. This is the bit I care most about. Is the claim that the blame is unfitting? That it's unfair to the target? That it's merely rude? That it lacks some kind of authority? That it gives the target a legitimate complaint even though the blame is otherwise in order? Or that it's defeated / overridden by a separate norm even though it's perfectly fitting?

And two cases I can't get the proverb to give a stable answer about, which is why I suspect the readings come apart:

- the confessed hypocrite: someone who says, out loud, "I did the same thing, I was wrong to, and so are you." No deception, no pretense of innocence — does the proverb still bite, and if not, does that show the real target was always the hypocrisy rather than the blame?
- the ex-hypocrite and the role-holder: a tobacco exec who smoked for thirty years, quit five years ago, and now blames a colleague for smoking around his kids; and a judge with a tax problem of her own who sentences someone for tax fraud. Does the house stay glassy forever, or does reforming / confessing / being punished fix it? Does blaming *in an official capacity* make a difference even when the person has the same fault?

I'd love the readings as a list or table with which is a platitude, which is substantive and defensible, and which is just false — and, most usefully for me, which reading my classmate would have to be committed to for the proverb to do the work he wanted (shutting down my "fitting anyway" claim), and whether that reading survives the two cases above.

Also, one thing that bugs me: every critical-thinking course teaches that "you too" is an ad hominem fallacy, and yet this proverb sounds like plain common sense. Is there a reading of the proverb on which both are true, or does one of them have to go?
