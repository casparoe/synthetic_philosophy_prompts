Here's my opening move. Imagine a study — call it the two-protocol study. Subjects see an array of colored letters flashed briefly and masked; we only care about one location, position 3. Half the subjects run under protocol A: "list everything you saw, and don't guess." Under A, they essentially never mention position 3, and when debriefed with the display in front of them they say they saw nothing there. The other half run under protocol B: "don't tell us what you saw — just rate your confidence, 0 to 100, that there was something at position 3, then describe whatever it was like in your own words." Under B, mean confidence at position 3 is around 70 while confidence at empty positions sits near 5, and the free-form part is full of sentences like "there was something there but I couldn't put a name to it." They also name the color right more often than chance. Same stimulus, same population, same everything except which question the experimenter asked.

Here's why I think this is a problem for you and not just for psychophysics. Your method says: take the subjects' seriously but don't believe them, and out of the whole corpus of reports build the heterophenomenological world they inhabit. But in this case the reports are protocol-relative. Protocol A builds you a world with nothing at position 3, protocol B builds you one with something dim and nameless there, and these aren't two perspectives on one world, they're two inconsistent ones. You can't say the world is whatever the total text depicts, because what's in the text depends on my choice of questionnaire. You can't choose A over B on the grounds that A is the more neutral question, because that judgment is exactly the theory-laden, first-person-flavored call you're not supposed to be making — and if you choose B over A you're doing it because you already think the dim experience was probably there. Meanwhile the realist about consciousness has one sentence: there's a fact of the matter about whether those subjects had a faint visual experience at position 3, protocols are just better or worse instruments for detecting it, and the difficulty is measurement, not ontology. You can't even state that hypothesis in your own vocabulary without giving up the method.
