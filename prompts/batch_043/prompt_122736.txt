I am a novelist, and for the last two years much of my working time has gone into a sequence of short prose pieces built on a single borrowed premise: a character is told, by something they cannot argue with, that the life they are living will recur exactly as it is, in the same order, down to the smallest detail, and that there is no exit from the sequence. The premise is Nietzsche's, from the well-known passage in *The Gay Science* in which a demon visits you in your loneliest hour; the variations are mine. I have been reading these pieces aloud and discussing them with audiences, and I have noticed something I cannot name. I am after the carving, not the label.

The observation is this. The quantity that the idea officially turns on — that the recurrence goes on without end — seems to do very little of the emotional work. Staged as a single repetition, the scene leaves listeners indifferent; staged as a hundred repetitions, attentive; staged as unending, visibly shaken. But when I ask what in that disturbance is tracking the difference between an enormous number and no end at all, nobody can say. And when I staged a version in which the life recurs a vast but finite number of times and the character remembers every previous cycle, audiences found it far more dreadful than the unending version in which nothing carries over. The dread seemed to be answering to two other features instead: that there is no way out, and that the repetitions do or do not accumulate.

Those two then pull apart. In one piece a woman relives the death of her child; by the several-hundredth cycle she knows precisely what she will feel and when she will feel it, she moves through the day with terrible competence, and what audiences report is not grief but a kind of mastery, or tedium — the scene became unbearable for the wrong reason. In another, where nothing carries over, a man drinks the same cup of coffee at the same hour, and the scene simply reads as ordinary life. Under either specification the setup declines to produce the verdict it was built to produce.

So the thing I want identified: a thought experiment whose stated engine is not what gives it its force, whose effective engine can be adjusted independently of the stated one, and whose force under each independent setting comes out differently, and not in the direction the doctrine requires. A philosopher friend said it is just an intuition pump; that is true and useless, since every philosophical anecdote inclines us somehow and it says nothing about this particular failure. Nor do I want to be told simply that recurrence is not a cosmological claim but an ethical one — my worry survives that distinction, because it concerns what does the work in a reader's response to either version. What I want to know is whether this has a name of its own; if it does not, what it should be called and how it should be defined; how it differs from an ordinary defective thought experiment, one that is merely invalid, or assumes what it is testing, or rests on an incoherent supposition; and where its edges lie. Specifically: is a thought experiment whose stated engine and effective engine can come apart always defective, or can the gap be productive? My suspicion is that in my stories the gap is doing the aesthetic work, and I would like to know whether philosophy has a way of talking about that.
