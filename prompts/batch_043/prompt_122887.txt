Hey — I'm a backend engineer, ~12 years of distributed systems, zero formal philosophy beyond a half-finished Coursera course on epistemology that I bailed on in week three. Over a few weekends I built a little generator for Bongard problems (you know the format: six little boxed figures on the left that share some rule, six on the right that violate it, you have to say what the rule is), and I've gotten kind of obsessed with them in a way my friends at puzzle night have stopped indulging.

The thing is, I want to apply for this small independent-research grant a friend forwarded me — six months part-time, about $15k, for "curiosity-driven work at the boundary of cognitive science and AI" — and the application is a two-page proposal. My half-formed idea is this: the interesting thing about Bongard problems isn't that neural nets are bad at them, it's that the format bakes in an underdetermination problem that nobody has isolated cleanly. With only twelve figures, infinitely many rules fit the data, and yet humans mostly converge on the same one within a minute. Something is doing the work, and everyone gestures at "simplicity" — but I think 'simplicity' is at least two different things wearing one word: how short the rule is when you write it down in a shared vocabulary, and how easy the relevant feature is to *see*. I've got two constructs I want to build the project around. Call the first "grue-pairs": generate a problem with intended rule R, then generate a second rule R' that agrees with R on all twelve displayed figures but disagrees on figures drawn from the same generator but held out; show people the twelve, then ask them to sort twenty new figures. Since both rules fit the evidence equally, whatever they do is prior rather than evidence, which seems like the cleanest handle anyone has gotten on the old grue-style worry (Goodman's, I think — I haven't actually read it) about which predicates are projectible. Call the second "salience decoys": build problems where a vivid but irrelevant regularity runs across *both* sets — every panel has exactly five dots, say — and vary whether the intended rule is short-to-describe-but-hard-to-see, or long-to-describe-but-pops-out-immediately. If those two levers dissociate, description-length simplicity and perceptual salience are different things and the literature is conflating them; if they never come apart, my question was probably malformed.

It should have: the question stated precisely enough that it could come out either way; why the obvious existing answers (it's all just Bayesian prior; it's all language-relativity; it's all perceptual chunking) don't settle it; the method month by month for six months part-time, including what I'd actually pilot and roughly how many subjects I'd need for the effect to show up at all; why this design rather than the obvious alternatives like just benchmarking a bunch of vision models or just running an introspection study where people report what rule they had in mind; what a successful deliverable looks like for someone deciding whether to fund it; and an honest statement of what would count as the project failing — I'd rather hand them a proposal that says what would falsify me than one that sounds safe.
