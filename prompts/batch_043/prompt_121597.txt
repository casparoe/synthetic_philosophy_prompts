I work in performance engineering, so I don't trust a quantity until I can say which variance it is a ratio of. On that basis a friend in quantitative genetics finally got me to accept the textbook line on heritability — and then used it to argue me into a corner. Please take the numbers below as stipulations rather than findings; I don't want the question to turn on whether the empirical claims hold.

(1) Narrow-sense heritability for trait T in population P is additive genetic variance divided by phenotypic variance: a ratio measured in P, under the environments P actually occupies. It is not a property of T alone, not a claim about what causes any individual's value, and not a claim about the mean.

(2) So the same trait can be 0.8 heritable in one population and 0.2 in another, both correctly, and neither number predicts what the mean does once a new environment is introduced.

(3) Everyone in P now receives intervention I — a nutrient, a tutoring programme — a cause that previously had no variance in P. Mean T rises by a full standard deviation.

(4) Re-measured afterwards, total variance has grown too, because people respond to I differently depending on genotype, and h² comes out at 0.85. The intervention moved the trait, increased the absolute genetic component, and left the ratio higher than it started.

(5) A number that can rise when you successfully move a trait by changing the environment is not a measure of how movable that trait is.

(6) So h² carries no information about the effectiveness of environmental interventions. Not merely the crude slogan ("0.8 heritable, so policy can't touch it"), but the weaker and more respectable claim that a high h² is at least some evidence that any given intervention will have small effects.

(7) But whatever h² tracks is also what makes the breeder's equation work: select on P and the mean moves by roughly h² times the selection differential. If (6) is right, h² shouldn't predict that either — and it does.

(8) So either h² does constrain intervention effects, and the "it says nothing about malleability" line I was taught is overstated; or it constrains only some interventions, and I can't say which — which sounds like no distinction at all.

I want to keep (1) and (2), since the variance-decomposition reading is the only one I have ever found credible and it is what kills the "it's genetic, so nothing can be done" move I actually care about killing. I want (3) and (4) to survive too, since they are the concrete part. But (7) is true and (8) is unacceptable. Which step do I give up, and what does giving it up cost me? Concretely: does "highly heritable" end up telling us anything about malleability or not — and if it licenses predictions about selection but not about supplements, what is the difference between those two kinds of intervention that makes h² informative about one and silent about the other?
