Hi — I'm the lay member on our university's animal research ethics committee (the others are two vets, a neurophysiologist, and a moral philosopher who mostly just sighs), and we're in the middle of rewriting the tiering system we use to decide how much scrutiny a protocol gets. We've already made a pile of calls and I'm trying to work out what on earth our implicit rule is before the next meeting, where we've got two new proposals to rule on. Our track record so far: mice and zebrafish stay in the lowest tier for brief procedures; minipigs get exactly the same protection as full-size pigs even though the whole animal, brain included, is about a third the size; chimpanzees are off the table entirely; after a sentience review a while back we folded octopuses and edible crabs into the protected tier, which surprised me given one of them has a nervous system you could balance on a pinhead; a proposal involving a colony of roughly fifty thousand ants went through with no extra protection for the colony as such, even though the colony does things no individual ant can do; a study using older dogs with quite advanced cognitive decline had to be run under the full dog rules, and when someone suggested the tier could come down because these particular animals' capacities were clearly reduced, the committee said no quite firmly; and a protocol using lab-grown human cortical organoids — a few million neurons, no body, no sensory input, no behaviour — was recieved as a ordinary tissue-governance matter, i.e. no welfare protection at all. When I asked one of the vets whether any of this had anything to do with brain size, she said something like 'size is a proxy, not the thing that matters' and left it there, which I found deeply unhelpful.

The two new proposals both involve lines of pigs engineered for research use. In the first, the brain is roughly three times normal volume at the same neuron packing density, so around three times as many forebrain neurons; the applicants claim — and I'm happy for us to just stipulate this rather than argue about it — that these animals are noticeably better at reversal learning and at tolerating delayed reward than ordinary pigs. In the second, the brain is also about three times normal volume, but the extra volume is mostly cerebellum and neuropil, with larger and more sparsely packed neurons, so the forebrain neuron count is basically the same as an ordinary pig's and the behavioural data looks allready indistinguishable from controls. Same species, same size of animal, quite different neurology.

So: what should we decide about each of these if we want to be consistent with ourselves, and which of our earlier decisions is the closest analog in each case? Please lay out the two or three rules our track record is equally compatible with — I can come up with candidates like 'protection tracks forebrain neuron count', 'protection tracks absolute brain mass', 'protection tracks encephalization, i.e. brain size relative to what you'd predict for a body of that size', 'protection tracks behavioural sophistication assessed case by case', 'protection is fixed by taxon and every member of a protected taxon gets the same tier whatever its current capacities', and 'protection requires a single integrated organism with a capacity for experience' — then say what each predicts for each new proposal, where they come apart, which one you'd bet the committee actually converges on and why, and what principle we'd be tacitly adopting if we went the other way. If you think our precedents genuinely don't settle it, give me one concrete case — a proposal you'd have us put in front of the committee next — that would do the most work in telling the surviving rules apart. Please write in British English, and do flag anywhere you think my description of the cases has smuggled in an assumption that's doing the philosophical work for me.
