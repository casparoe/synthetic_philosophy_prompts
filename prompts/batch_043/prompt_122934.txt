I'm a PhD student in political theory and I've been asked to run one session of our first-year proseminar on social choice — about 14 people, mixed phil/econ/polisci, 90 minutes, and roughly half of them have already seen Arrow's theorem, so they'll file whatever I do under 'another impossibility result' unless I actively prevent that. The topic is Sen's liberal paradox (the Paretian liberal one). I want to do it as a game, not a lecture, and I'd rather they hit the wall before anyone says the word 'impossibility' out loud.

The skeleton I have: everyone gets a card with a preference ordering over three social alternatives plus a 'protected pair' they're decisive over; one person plays the rule-enforcer and has to produce a single group ranking that respects unanimity/Pareto and everyone's decisive pair. With the right profile they cycle and can't produce one.

Two things I haven't seen done and don't trust myself to build:

1. The usual Prude/Lewd framing makes everyone think the moral is 'don't be a prude' or 'mind your own business', which isn't the moral. Can you rebuild the case so the meddlesome preference is one people actually sympathise with — wanting an overworked colleague to take the day off, or not to send out a half-finished draft — and with stakes that are real but tiny (who presents next week, what we do with the last twenty minutes)? I want them to feel that both conditions looked beyond reproach when considered separately.

2. A second round where at least one person's protected pair concerns nobody's business but two *other* people's — say, which of two other participants has to present. My suspicion is that the cycle still goes through, which would definately show them that the 'private sphere' story is a gloss rather than something doing formal work in the condition, but I don't know how to make that land instead of looking like I cheated when I dealt the cards.

I also want a third round where people may hand their protected pair back if they want — rights as waivable options rather than constraints on the outcome. Does the cycle survive, and does it depend on who waives first? And if the group escapes by refusing to rank some pairs, or by choosing without ranking anything, I want the debrief ready to say what they just gave up.

What I need: the actual preference cards and the profile (please check it really cycles), the rules as I'd read them out, a timeline with rough minutes, and a debrief mapping specific moments onto the conditions one by one. Then what will predictably go wrong — my bets are someone trying to fix it by changing their preferences rather than the rule, and the econ students bolting for mechanism design — plus an honest note on which part of the result the game can't reproduce and I'll just have to state. I dont want to assert as received wisdom which condition is supposed to give; find a way to stage that as something they argue about instead. No reading list needed, I've got that covered.
