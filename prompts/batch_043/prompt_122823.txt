I've spent the past two years on my town's public commemoration panel, and the subject has recently stopped being academic for me. The library where I have worked for thirty years was built with money from the mill owner whose bronze stands on the lawn in front of it; my mother's family came to this town to work in that mill, and my great-grandfather lost two fingers to a carding machine there at fourteen. The panel deadlocked twice, and the compromise we reached is that the statue stays and a second plaque is set beside the original dedication of 1901. I have been asked to read something at the ceremony in June — a hundred and fifty people or so, roughly half of them descended from mill families, and two of them his great-grandchildren — and I do not want a poem that lets anyone leave feeling that the vote settled the matter.

What I would like is a sestina. The reason is the rotation: six end-words returning in a different order every stanza seems to me the right shape for a case in which nothing new has been learned about the man — every fact about him was already on the record — and yet the sentence those facts add up to has changed, because the words have changed neighbours. Choose the six yourself, and tell me in a line or two of prose why those six; I'd like at least two of them to be words that already appear on the 1901 plaque, and none of them to be his name.

Three things I want the poem to get right rather than gesture at.

First, the second plaque is not a retraction. An inscription cut in 1901 cannot be unsaid by a second inscription in the present; what will exist afterwards is not one corrected verdict but two verdicts standing a few feet apart, and the interesting question is what the space between them now does — whether juxtaposition is a form of judgement at all, or just two utterances each waiting for the other to be removed.

Second, the asymmetry between a name and a statue. A street name gets used: you navigate by it, arrange to meet under it, and in using it you need not endorse anything, so the honouring can wear off a name in a way it cannot wear off a statue. A statue has no job except honouring, which is why it can only be reframed, never worn smooth into innocent use. I'd like that distinction carried somewhere in the middle of the poem, and I'd like one stanza spoken by someone using the name without thinking of him at all.

Third, that the honour was always addressed forward, to us, rather than backward to him: the plaque says at least as much about who we intend to be as about what he did.

Constraints: strict rotation, and all six words back in the three-line envoi. His name may appear exactly once in the whole poem, inside the last line, and the last line must not end on it. The envoi should not resolve anything — no reconciliation, no verdict. No theorist, movement, or doctrine named anywhere in the verse; someone who works on the ethics of commemoration should recognise the distinctions as correct, and someone who just lives here should hear a poem.
