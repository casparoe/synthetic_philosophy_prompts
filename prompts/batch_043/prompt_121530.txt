I want a specification before I look at candidate solutions: a list of desiderata that any adequate account of how to handle tiny-probability, astronomically-large-payoff gambles must meet. The thing being specified is a decision rule, not a metaphysics of value: it takes as input a set of options, probability estimates, and a valuation of outcomes, and it returns both an ordering of the options and — where money or resources are at stake — an amount.

**Purpose, which fixes what counts as cheating.** The rule is for a real allocator: a foundation deciding each year whether to move budget from a well-evidenced intervention with modest, measurable expected value to a long-shot intervention whose case rests on a small probability of an enormous outcome — say a probability somewhere between 10⁻⁶ and 10⁻², itself uncertain within that range, over an outcome affecting 10¹⁰ or more future lives. So *the probabilities are unknowable, so do nothing* is not an acceptable output, and neither is *be moderate*: the rule has to return a ranking and, ideally, a budget share. I will also test it on a deliberately artificial pair of cases. First case: a stranger asks you for five dollars and says that if you refuse they will, by entirely mundane means, cause 10ᴺ units of suffering, for an N they name. Second case: a second stranger makes the exactly parallel promise — pay them the five dollars instead and they will prevent 10ᴺ units of suffering — and the two demands cannot both be met.

**Requirements on the list itself.**

- Give six to eight desiderata. For each: state it in one sentence; give the case or consideration that motivates it in at most three sentences; and mark it non-negotiable or negotiable, with a single clause saying why it gets that marking.
- Derive, do not stipulate. No desideratum may simply be *must not be fanatical* or *must track expected value*. Each must be backed by a case in which a rule lacking the desideratum produces an output that is unacceptable or incoherent for a reason you state.
- At least two desiderata must be about failure modes of the rule rather than of the gambles: how it behaves when an adversary can cheaply manufacture arbitrarily many mugger-shaped opportunities; and how it behaves under re-graining or re-description of the outcome space and the hypothesis space — including the case where the probability of a grand claim is not given but inferred from a prior over an unbounded space of possible claims, so that multiplying and comparing is ill-defined until the rule says which prior it uses and why that prior is not itself a threshold in disguise.
- At least one desideratum must concern the shape of the answer rather than its direction: the rule should degrade gracefully instead of flipping under small perturbations of the inputs, and it should say how much rather than only whether.
- At least one desideratum must cover the interpersonal version of the problem — a billion people each facing an independent one-in-a-million risk, where you can avert one person's risk for a price — and it should not let the rule collapse that case into the single-person case without saying why the numbers are or are not the same. Say also whether the rule must treat tiny chances of enormous harms symmetrically with tiny chances of enormous goods, and rule on it with a reason.

**Then, briefly.**

1. Identify the smallest subset of your non-negotiable desiderata that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and display the tension in three or four sentences. My own guess is that the conflict sits between refusing the mugger, respecting the aggregation of very many independent small chances into one large one, and banning arbitrary cut-offs — but argue it in your own terms, and tell me which member of the subset you would drop and what you would lose by dropping it.
2. Say which single desideratum does the most work in excluding candidate rules; name one style of non-fanatical rule it knocks out and one style that survives it.
3. Anticipate the renegotiation: pick the desideratum that a defender of the knocked-out rule is most likely to reject as a prejudice of the expected-value camp, and give their objection at its strongest in two sentences.

Optional, and only where you are confident: a two-sentence scorecard for one candidate rule you actually know. Do not name views you are unsure about, and do not cite papers or authors — I want the specification, not a literature.

Maximum 800 words. A table for the desiderata is welcome, but the motivating cases must be sentences, not cell-fragments.
