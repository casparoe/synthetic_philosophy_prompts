I write backend code for a living, no philosophy or political science training, but Ive been going to my city's charter reform meetings for about two years now and somehow I'm the one giving the 20-minute talk at next month's session on sortition. I need Q&A prep, not a defense of the idea in the abstract.

My thesis, roughly: sortition is usually sold as a way to get better or more representative decisions, and I think that's the wrong pitch, and it's why these proposals keep dying. I think the real argument is about what I've started calling the nomination chokepoint: in any elected body, most of the actual power sits upstream of the vote, in whoever decides what gets a floor vote, in what order, and drafted which way, and every one of those people is protecting a career. A randomly selected body has no career to protect, so it's the one institutional form that can't be leaned on with a threat about the future — you'd have to buy a fresh majority every six months, out of people who are gone in six months. (The engineer version I keep using in these meetings: elections are a lossy compression of the preferance space, one bit per seat every few years, and most of the pathology is in the compression ratio, not in voters being dumb.)

Concretely what I'm proposing to the commission: replace the council's agenda-setting committee — the thing that decides what gets a floor vote and when — with a panel of 24 residents picked by stratified lot, six-month terms, staggered so half turn over every three months, paid a real stipend, staffed by two people. It can't pass anything, can't amend, can't kill a bill permanently; it only decides what comes to the floor and in what order. That's the whole thing.

The room (about 40 people, mostly standing): a retired state judge who chairs the commission; a political science professor from the local university who works on participation and deliberation and is, I'm told, sympathetic but thinks the evidence base is thin; two union reps; a real-estate lawyer; a woman who ran a participatory budgeting process here that the council then mostly ignored and who is still annoyed about it; a couple of people from a citizens' assembly nonprofit; and a lot of regular residents who mostly want to know why any of this matters for housing and potholes.

What I want: the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this room, ordered by likely times damaging, each one phrased the way that person would really say it (the judge is not going to say "legitimacy deficit," he's going to say something about accountability and about voters not having chosen these people). For each: a two-minute reply in plain English I can say out loud, one thing I should concede inside it, and a note on where the reply is soft. Tell me which objection I should concede fully up front before anyone raises it, and which single reply has to land or the proposal is dead in this room. And include the question I'm hoping nobody asks — my guess is something about who designs and triggers the lottery itself, i.e. whether I've just moved the chokepoint one level up, but tell me if you think the really bad one is a different one.

Please commit: don't hand me a balanced menu. If you think the no-career-to-protect argument is wrong, or that my panel would just end up deferring to its two staffers, say that plainly, tell me what I should be arguing instead, and give me your confidence. I'd rather hear it now than in front of the judge.
