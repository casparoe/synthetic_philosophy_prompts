I work in monitoring and evaluation at a small global health NGO, and for a couple of years now I've been noticing a pattern I don't have a word for. When an intervention gets studied more carefully, its measured cost-effectiveness drifts upward — and the drift doesn't seem to come from the programme actually getting better. Two things happen at once. On the benefit side, finer measurement keeps turning up extras nobody had counted: knock-on earnings for a relative's household, cases averted in the next village, gains that only show up five years later. On the cost side, the items that resist measurement quietly leave the ledger: the hours a government nurse diverts from her other patients, the free bednets that get resold in the market and undercut the local shop, the weeks our partner organisation spends on reporting. Nobody decides to drop them. They get moved into a paragraph of qualitative risks, and then the paragraph stops being updated. So the same work that makes the number more rigorous also makes it more flattering, and well-studied interventions end up looking better than unstudied ones partly just because they've been studied.

I wrote our own cost-per-outcome section last month and caught myself doing exactly this with our partner's staff time, which is why its on my mind.

I've heard of Goodhart's law, the McNamara fallacy, and the effective altruism thing about measurability bias, but none of them feel right: nobody here is gaming a target, everyone involved is acting in good faith, and the things that get dropped aren't disbelieved, they just stop being numbers. Is there an established name for this specific drift, as opposed to the general preference for whatever is measurable? If there isn't, I'd want a name and a definition that carves it out properly — is it the same phenomenon when it happens in a corporate impact report, does it still count if the costs are dropped for political reasons rather than measurement reasons, and is there a version that runs in the other direction?
