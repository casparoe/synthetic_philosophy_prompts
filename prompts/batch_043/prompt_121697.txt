Please role-play John Maynard Keynes as he was in 1921, just after finishing *A Treatise on Probability*, and stay in character until I say "stop". You hold that probability is an objective logical relation of partial implication between evidence and conclusion; that many such relations cannot be placed on one numerical scale and some cannot even be compared ordinally; and that the weight of an argument — the balance between relevant knowledge and relevant ignorance — is a genuine, decision-relevant quantity, distinct from the probability itself. I have explained to you, in your own vocabulary, modern subjective Bayesianism, strictly proper scoring rules, calibration scores, and machine-learning forecasters trained on millions of questions. You may use these notions, but you are skeptical of them and you do not concede that a well-calibrated number exhausts what a probability is. Keep replies to about two paragraphs, concede small points freely, never concede the main one, and feel free to ask me, in character, how modern practice actually works.

Here is my opening. The view I want you to attack is one I call calibration monism: a probability just is the number that a forecaster aiming at calibration under a strictly proper scoring rule would do best to report given my evidence; anything you call weight is either already inside that number, or is merely my forecast of how far the number will move when further evidence arrives, and I can price that too. So there is no separate problem of uncertainty — only risk spread over a larger space, which expected utility already handles. If your reply is the old point that a probability of a probability collapses back into the first-order probability, make it and then keep going: note that my system never reports a second-order number at all, only one trained number.

To make this concrete, take a case I invented, the Twin Propositions. Proposition A is drawn from a question distribution on which my system has been scored ten thousand times and come out calibrated. Proposition B is a one-off question about whether a particular political order breaks up within a decade, drawn from no reference class I can name. My system prices both at 0.7; I have no evidence about which one it handles worse; I must take exactly one bet at that price and hold it to resolution. Expected utility says I am indifferent, and you say I should prefer one. Which one, and what work is the extra quantity doing? And please do not tell me the better-supported bet is simply more comfortable: tell me what, in the logic of the case, a probability cannot express.
