Hey — I run a tiny reading group in phil of science, and two of the regulars have spent a whole term going around in circles about verisimilitude and I want to force them to write something constructive instead. One of them is in the "likeness" / logical-space camp (the Tichý–Hilpinen–Niiniluoto–Oddie style idea that a theory's closeness to the truth is a function of how close to the actual world the worlds are in which the theory would be true); the other is in the "relevant consequence" camp (Schurz–Weingartner-ish: closeness is a matter of which consequences the theory gets right, and Popper's real mistake was an over-generous notion of consequence or content). They both accept that Popper's own truth-content-minus-falsity-content defintion is dead — the triviality result from the 70s, on which no false theory can be closer to the truth than another — and neither wants to re-litigate that.

So: draft the joint statement such an adversarial collaboration should produce. Format it as (1) a numbered agreed-claims section, (2) a disagreements table, (3) a cruxes section.

The agreed claims should include what the concept is even for — what work "closer to the truth" has to do inside an account of scientific progress, given that our best theories are strictly false — and what the triviality result does and does not establish. Don't let them agree to anything vacuous; if a claim is only jointly signable because both sides mean something different by a key word, keep it but flag it.

The disagreements table: one row per disagreement, each row containing two formulations, one in the wording the likeness person would sign and one in the wording the consequence person would sign, and nothing either would reject as a statement of their own view.

The cruxes section is the part I actually care about. For each crux give a concrete case both would accept as bearing on it, and write down in advance what each of them predicts the right verdict is. Build the cases around these, which are the things my group keeps getting stuck on:

- A theory that says a quantity is 5.0 when it is really 5.1, versus one that says 5.0 when it is really 50. Can either apparatus grade the near-miss as better without just smuggling in a metric, and where is that metric supposed to come from?
- Re-describing the same domain in a grue-ish / gerrymandered vocabulary and asking whether the truthlikeness ordering survives. If one camp's ordering flips under re-description and the other's doesn't, say that explicitly and say what each side will claim it shows.
- "Padding": conjoining a theory with an irrelevant but true claim about a seperate subject matter, or adding an unconstrained parameter. Should that leave closeness to the truth unchanged? My suspicion is they have opposed intuitions here and that neither has noticed.
- Cross-domain comparison: whether a false theory in one field can be closer to the truth than a false theory in a completely different field, and what that implies about how relevance gets fixed.

Aim for something we could read out loud in about fifteen minutes, and don't bother with citations or footnotes, we know the literature. Two more things: at the end, have each side say, in one sentence, what argument or case would actually get them to switch; and add a short note from you on which jointly-signed claim is the most likely fudge, and where the residual disagreement is verbal rather than substantive (if any of it is).
