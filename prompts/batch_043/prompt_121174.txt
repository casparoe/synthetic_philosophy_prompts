I'm comfortable in all of that; I'm not comfortable in philosophy. I keep running into people online saying Occam's razor is either obviously right or completely ungrounded, and then someone gestures at Solomonoff induction as if that settles it, and I can't tell what claim is actually on the table.

So here's the ask. Take the philosophical position that simplicity is truth-conducive — the claim that among hypotheses that fit the data, the simpler one is more likely to be the *true* one and not merely more convenient to work with — together with the Solomonoff-flavored defense of it, where the universal prior hands each hypothesis a weight of roughly two-to-the-minus-its-description-length and there's a convergence result saying the posterior eventually locks onto the truth, with total error bounded by a constant times the description length of the true source (I don't remember the constant and don't want it). Restate that whole package in my vocabulary as faithfully as you can. And please don't smuggle my framework's assumptions in and then call it a translation: if the only way to say "simpler hypotheses are more likely true" in my terms is "shorter programs get more prior weight, and here's why betting that way is a good idea," then say that out loud, because that's already a different claim than the one I started with.

Then the part I actually care about, the residue report, and I have three suspicions I want you to either confirm or wreck. The first is the additive constant: the invariance theorem says description length only depends on the reference machine up to a constant, so in the limit the razor is language-independent, but my datasets are finite and small and the constant — basically the size of the interpreter you get for free — can easily be bigger than the entire description length I'm comparing. Related, my team has a name for an annoyance we hit constantly: we call it the library discount, the bits you don't have to spend because the function you needed is already sitting in the shared codebase. So is the philosophers' simplicity measured before or after the library discount? If after, it looks relative to whatever a community already happens to believe; if before, it looks like it depends on the choice of universal machine, and I can't tell which one they want. Second: the Solomonoff setup seems to assume the world is generated by something computable and that what we're doing is predicting an infinite sequence of discrete observations, whereas my team's actual problem is choosing between explanations that posit unobservable stuff — latent factors, user intent — where "fits the data" and "is true" come apart in a way the sequence-prediction framing doesn't seem to have room for. Third, and this is the one I'm most suspicious of: "the posterior converges to the truth" is a long-run statement and none of us will ever be in the long run, so I want to know whether the razor has any content at finite n or whether it just evaporates into "you can't be fooled forever."
