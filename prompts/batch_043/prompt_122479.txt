I'm putting together the closing lecture for my graduate seminar on population ethics and the far future, and I've found that I don't actually know where I stand on a question I've been lecturing around for years: whether the simulation argument, taken seriously as a live epistemic possibility rather than a conversation piece, should dampen longtermist fanaticism. Let me make that precise. By fanaticism I mean the package consisting of (i) expected-value calculations in which the sheer number of possible future lives does nearly all the work, (ii) the conclusion that interventions aimed at securing or improving that future dominate near-term interventions of comparable cost, and (iii) a willingness to act on very small probabilities of very large payoffs. By dampened I mean: a competent, impartial evaluator who accepted the simulation possibility at a non-negligible credence, and who reasoned correctly about it, would either lower their expected-value estimates for far-future-directed interventions by at least an order of magnitude relative to a physical-reality-only baseline, or give up (ii), or withhold on (iii). For resolution: suppose that by mid-century we had a settled picture of whether ancestor-simulations of the relevant kind are feasible and whether they are in fact run in large numbers, together with whatever professional consensus then exists on the treatment of measure, duplication and truncated futures; I'll count the claim as true if, under that settlement, the median specialist in this area would say the dampening conclusion had been right all along.

Give me your credence in that claim, and I want the decomposition rather than the number. Here is the tree I've been working with, though please add terms you think belong and drop any you think are idle, saying why. First, the probability that we are in a simulation of the relevant kind at all, and how much of that probability is really about base rates over civilizations versus about the specific feasibility of the computing. Second, what I'll call horizon truncation: conditional on our being simulated, the probability that the simulated future extends far enough — in simulated centuries or millennia rather than in simulated years — for longtermist stakes to be astronomically large at all; the worry is that most runs would be commissioned to answer a question about a particular epoch and wound up when that question was answered. Third, what I'll call external continuation: the probability that a shutdown or discard of our run is not the end of the lineage, because the population is checkpointed, migrated, or carried forward into a successor run, which would make our extinction inside the simulation less bad than extinction simpliciter. Fourth, rerun multiplicity: whether the same run being executed many times multiplies the stakes linearly, is decision-theoretically inert, or does something in between, and whether the relevant measure is per-copy or per-type. Fifth, the simulators' purposes: how to split the probability mass between runs that are forensic or historical in aim and runs whose point is the welfare of the simulated population, and whether indifference on the simulators' part matters morally or merely causally. Sixth, selection for interest: conditional on being simulated, how likely we are to be in a run chosen because the period is critical or dramatic, and whether termination is then correlated with the end of that critical episode. Give me a number and one line of justification for each node, say how they combine (and whether multiplying them is even the right operation when some of them are about measure rather than about outcome), and give me the total.

Then three further things. Tell me which single node the final figure is most sensitive to, and what argument or evidence — empirical, conceptual, or formal — would move it most; I'd rather know where the leverage is than be given a tidier number. My own credence is around 0.6 and a colleague in our decision-theory reading group puts it near 0.15, on the grounds that the simulation possibility is evidentially symmetric and mostly inflates variance rather than depressing the mean; given those two figures, tell me which of the nodes above is where our disagreement most likely lives. And please mark, for each number you give, how much you'd move under pressure — I want figures you would defend aloud in a seminar, with a note wherever you'd shift by more than ten points if pressed. Finally, if you think the request as posed is confused, say so plainly rather than producing a number I could put on a slide: if the simulation argument really changes the shape of the distribution rather than its mean, if the counterfactual median specialist is not a well-defined reference, if the whole exercise is swamped by cluelessness about higher-order terms, or if I have bundled together claims in the definition of fanaticism that come apart, tell me that and tell me what the honest version of the question would be.
