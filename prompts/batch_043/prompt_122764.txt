Two colleagues of mine — a postdoc in quantum foundations and a visiting philosopher of physics — got into an argument after a departmental seminar, and afterwards they asked me to referee it. I couldn't, partly because I don't know the literature well enough to tell a good move from a confident one. Below is the exchange as I remember it, with the technical claims reconstructed as carefully as I can; if I have garbled the setup, please say so before adjudicating.

The framework they were arguing inside: a system has a complete physical state, its ontic state, written λ. Preparing a system in a pure quantum state ψ does not determine λ outright but samples it from a probability distribution p(λ|ψ), and λ together with the measurement setting fixes the probabilities of measurement outcomes. Call such a model ψ-ontic if the distributions associated with two distinct pure states do not overlap, so that a complete specification of the system always tells you which pure state was prepared; call it ψ-epistemic if there are ontic states compatible with more than one pure state, so that the same underlying physical situation can answer to different wavefunctions. A no-go theorem published in 2012 (Marek referred to it by the authors' initials, but the name matters less than the content) shows that, if systems prepared independently have independent ontic states — an assumption called preparation independence — then no model reproducing the predictions of quantum theory can be ψ-epistemic. One gloss on the result is that denying preparation independence would involve action at a distance.

The exchange, as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) Marek: So the ψ-epistemic view is dead. It survives only by denying preparation independence, which means the ontic states of two systems prepared in separate labs, with no causal contact, are correlated in a way the theory cannot explain — the same rescue that keeps local hidden variables alive after Bell by letting the hidden variables know in advance what the experimenters will choose. Keeping a favoured ontology by postulating unexplained correlations isn't a research programme; it's a loophole with a metaphysics attached.

(2) Dana: That analogy doesn't hold. In the Bell case the correlation is between the hidden variables and the experimenters' later setting choices — a correlation between what you freely decide in the lab and the pre-existing state of the particle, which really is conspiratorial. Preparation independence is a different kind of claim: it says the ontic state of a composite is nothing over and above the ontic states of its parts. Any theory in which the whole has degrees of freedom the parts don't — holism, relational ontologies, theories with global constraints — violates it, and such theories are held for independent reasons, not invented to dodge a theorem. Nor is the cost action at a distance: Bell's own lesson is that correlations between separated systems need no signalling between them.

(3) Marek: Grant that the cost is payable; the benefit has evaporated. The selling point of ψ-epistemism was explanatory: overlap explains why nonorthogonal states cannot be perfectly distinguished; it makes collapse look like Bayesian updating rather than a physical process; and it takes the sting out of the wavefunction living in an enormous configuration space. But if preparation independence holds there is no overlap at all, and if it fails there is overlap only among systems that were never independently prepared — so for any pair of systems you could actually prepare side by side and compare, λ determines ψ. The view is unfalsified and hollow: epistemic in name only.

(4) Dana: That misdescribes the ambition. Overlap was one mechanism for one corollary, not the core claim. The core claim is that ψ codifies a constraint on what can be known or predicted about a system rather than cataloguing its intrinsic properties — that ψ functions as a compressed summary of the ontic state's dispositions. Nothing in the no-go result rules that out: two distributions over a large ontic state space can be nearly disjoint while each is still what you get by coarse-graining, and a coarse-grained variable can still be the thing you update when you learn an outcome. Nor does the result touch the collapse-as-updating payoff. And the approach has real content: there is a toy model, built on a principle that maximal knowledge must equal maximal ignorance, in which the analogue of the quantum state is incomplete information about an underlying state, and which reproduces a striking range of putatively quantum phenomena — noncommutativity, interference, no-cloning and no-broadcasting, teleportation, a form of entanglement — with the state playing no role as a physical field.

(5) Marek: Two replies. First, the summary defence is verbal. Every theory needs a map from its state to the statistics; the question is what structure that map has. Here it has the structure of a field on a high-dimensional space, with interference and entanglement structure that no coarse-graining of a classical-like state has ever reproduced. Calling it a compressed representation relocates the mystery without dissolving it. Second, the toy model is evidence against you. It reproduces what it reproduces precisely because it is local and noncontextual; it cannot reproduce Bell-inequality violations or Kochen–Specker contextuality. Knowledge-based reconstruction succeeds exactly on the fragment of quantum theory that is classically tractable and fails at the point where the theory stops looking like a theory of ignorance. That is not a theorem, but it shifts the burden of proof.

(6) Dana: Your first reply begs the question: describing the map as a field on configuration space is the ψ-ontic reading smuggled in as a neutral description of the mathematics. Your second is a wager about a research programme, not an argument. The toy model was never offered as a candidate fundamental theory; it was an existence proof that a large cluster of phenomena usually called irreducibly quantum follows from an epistemic constraint alone. An existence proof need not be complete to do its work.

(7) Marek: Then let me make the dispute empirical. Imagine an ontic readout device: for a single system it returns a complete specification of that system's ontic state — the whole physical truth about the system, whatever the true ontology turns out to be. Prepare many systems in two nonorthogonal states ψ and φ and read each one out. Either (i) the readouts reliably discriminate the preparations, the distributions do not overlap, and λ fixes ψ — the ψ-ontic outcome; or (ii) the readout distributions overlap and individual readouts occur under both preparations — the ψ-epistemic outcome. That is a clean operational criterion, and any position that refuses to say which outcome it predicts, or that declares such a device impossible in principle while still claiming to be a realist theory about λ, has left the field.

(8) Dana: Your device cannot settle it, for two reasons. In the overlap case, I — or any ψ-ontic theorist — can say: quite right, ψ is not an intrinsic property of the system taken alone, it is a dispositional or relational property of the system together with its environment or with the whole experimental arrangement. Your device reads only the system, so it cannot distinguish ψ is not real from ψ is not intrinsic. In the non-overlap case, I can say: the readouts discriminate the preparations, yes, but that shows only that ψ is recoverable from λ, and recoverability is compatible with ψ being a law-like constraint on how λ evolves rather than a thing among things. So your criterion decides whether ψ is intrinsic and whether it is recoverable, not whether it is real. And notice that building the device at all requires an antecedent notion of what counts as a complete physical specification — which is the very point in dispute.

I'd like a point-by-point adjudication:

1. First check my setup: are the definitions of ψ-ontic and ψ-epistemic, and my statement of the no-go result and its preparation-independence assumption, correct? If any premise above is wrong, say which and how it changes the verdict.
2. Go through moves (1) through (8) in order. For each, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right — a rating plus a sentence or two of justification — and say explicitly when a move is valid in itself but fails to land against the target it was aimed at.
3. Is the ontic readout device in (7) genuinely decisive, or does it merely restate the definitions in operational dress? If the latter, could any operational criterion settle whether the wavefunction is real, and what would such a criterion have to look like — or why would no such criterion be possible?
4. What are both of them missing? Say whether the disagreement trades on a conflation — for instance between being real and being intrinsic, between being real and being recoverable, between being a thing and being a law — and whether there is a third position neither of them considers.
5. Give an overall verdict: who is more right, about what, and by how much; and say whether the question "is the wavefunction real?" is well posed as they are arguing it, or what question it should be replaced with.

Please reason from the structure of the arguments rather than from who holds which view, and flag any place where you think the honest answer is that the field has not settled the matter.
