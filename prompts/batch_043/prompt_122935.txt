so I'm about 60k words into a novel about a real person and I've worked myself into a knot I cant think my way out of, and I want you to argue the side I don't want to be true.

Setup: she's my great-great-aunt, died 1891, unmarried, obscure — wrote some temperance tracts, kept a diary for four years in her twenties and then stopped. The entire record is basically eleven letters, that diary, a court entry about a debt, and one photograph. I've used her real name. The invented stuff: a night where she gets drunk in somebody else's house, and a scene where she burns a bundle of letters. No evidence for either, and the drunk night cuts straight against everything she published and was known for.

My own view, which I've been defending to my family for a year, is that truth is the wrong norm here. What I owe her isn't truth, it's *aptness*: the invented thing has to be the sort of thing the record leaves room for and the pressures in the letters make sense of, apt the way a metaphor is apt — answerable to the shape of the life, not to the ledger of what happened. And if a reader comes away believing she got drunk, well, almost nobody will, and if they do nothing follows from it.

But I keep hitting a case I have no answer to, which I've started calling *eviction*: because there's basically nothing else, my novel is going to *be* the record. There won't be a competing version. The archive gives you four sentences and a photo, and googling her name gets you my book. So I'm not adding one more story to a pile of stories about her, I'm replacing the pile. And it isn't only beliefs that change — what you can *do* with her changes: she stops being a person in an archive and becomes a character, available for a netflix thing, a walking tour, whatever comes next. That availability is my invention.

So please argue that I owe her something stricter than aptness. Ground rules:

- Don't rest it on the law (I don't want an argument that collapses if the law is different from what you think), and don't rest it on the feelings of living relatives — my cousin is upset and I don't think that's the interesting wrong.
- Grant me nearly everything: grant that the dead can't be harmed in any literal sense, that there's nobody left to consent or object, and that aptness really is the right norm for invented *dialogue* and probably for compressed or composite minor characters too. Take the strongest version of my view and then say where it breaks.
- Cash it out where I live, which is at the desk. What concretely would I have to change or give up? Is it the drunk night in particular — does it matter that it contradicts her published positions rather than just the silence of the archive — or is the problem the whole project of writing her interior life? Would renaming her fix it or is that just laundering it? Is a note up front enough, or does an afterword that sets the thin real record next to the fat invented one actually answer the eviction worry, or does it just make me feel better?

If you think aptness and eviction are the wrong frame entirely, say so and hand me better ones — but you still have to argue the strict side. No quotes at me and no reading lists; I want the argument, not the literature.
