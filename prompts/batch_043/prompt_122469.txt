Every couple of weeks my roommate and I get stuck on the same argument about illusions and the timing of conscious experience, and she is very firm about the two labels she uses. By Orwellian she means that you really did have the earlier conscious experience and then, after the later stimulus arrives, your memory of that experience is quietly rewritten afterwards, the way a bureaucracy edits the past once the fact is in; by Stalinesque she means that the edit is made before the experience ever reaches consciousness, so that all consciousness ever sees is the finished, pre-approved story, like a verdict decided in advance and staged for the record. I know the labels come from a book by some philosopher, but she has never made me read it, so please work from her rough gloss and from the pattern of her verdicts. Her track record is: standard color phi, where a blue dot at the top of a screen is followed quickly by a red dot at the bottom and viewers report a single dot moving and switching from blue to red partway along, saying the switch happened before the second dot was shown, she calls Stalinesque; the cutaneous rabbit, where taps at the wrist followed by taps near the elbow make people report taps hopping up the forearm with extra taps in between even though they say the first taps were felt on the wrist as they happened, she calls Orwellian; chronostasis, where after you move your eyes onto a clock the second hand seems frozen at the position it had when your eyes landed, as though you had been seeing it there longer, she calls Orwellian; and the flash lag effect, where a flash physically aligned with a moving ring is seen lagging behind it, she also calls Orwellian.

Now I want to know what she will say about a case I found in a preprint last night, which nobody has asked her about yet. The authors call it the vibrotactile frequency phi: a 30 hertz vibration is applied to the left shoulder for six hundred milliseconds, long enough that every subject later says they clearly felt a low buzz on the left shoulder first, then after a very short gap a 180 hertz vibration is applied to the right shoulder for a hundred milliseconds; even with that long first exposure, subjects report a single vibrating object travelling from the left shoulder to the right, say its frequency shifts from low to high halfway across, and insist the shift happened before the second vibration reached them. If I ask her about it, will she call it Orwellian or Stalinesque? I have two guesses about the rule she has been running, but I do not trust either, so please tell me which one actually fits her earlier verdicts, whether a third rule fits them just as well, and what each candidate predicts here. My first guess is that she calls a case Stalinesque whenever the illusion makes people report a change in a qualitative feature such as color, pitch, or vibration frequency, and Orwellian when it only adds, moves, or stretches events without changing what they feel like. My second guess is that she calls it Stalinesque only when the later stimulus is needed to settle what the earlier experience was like, and Orwellian whenever the earlier stimulus already produced a real time conscious experience and the later stimulus only edits how that experience is remembered. Say what each of these rules predicts about the vibrotactile frequency phi, and give your best guess about her verdict with the reasons for preferring one rule over the other.

Finally, if those two rules really do come apart, name one follow up variant of the illusion that would best force her to reveal which rule she is actually using, and tell me what she would say about that variant under each rule. I am much less interested in which label is philosophically correct, or in any deep theory of consciousness, than in the pattern behind her verdicts and in the case that would expose it.
