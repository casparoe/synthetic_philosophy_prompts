I'm helping a city government design a compensation scheme that's meant to be luck egalitarian: it tops people up when they end up badly off through no choice of their own, and lets losses stand when they came from a gamble the person chose to take. The enabling legislation says that a disadvantage is compensable to the degree that it is attributable to brute rather than option luck. The commitee has handed me eight cases and wants them triaged.

Here they are:

1. Priya is born with a metabolic disorder that will eat about 15% of her income in medication for the rest of her life. Nobody chose anything.
2. Marco buys a ticket in a lottery priced at mathematically fair odds and loses.
3. Ines deliberately takes the only job in her town, which pays on commission and swings wildly with the tourist season; in a bad year she earns half of what the salaried job in the next town would have paid, which she turned down because she hates commuting. I've started calling this one "luck laundering": a fully voluntary choice that hands your income over to chance, where the choice itself had no real alternatives.
4. Sameer and Tom are nurses who both sign up for the same brutal night-shift rota, fully informed about the average health risks. Sameer develops a chronic condition after two years, Tom doesn't. Only later is a genetic marker discovered that makes some people vulnerable; Sameer has it, Tom doesn't, and neither of them could possibly have found out in advance.
5. Dana is poor because she is terrible at judging risk — she reliably accepts gambles with negative expected value, and has done so since her twenties. Her being bad at this looks like it is mostly down to her genes and her upbringing.
6. Otto has an extremely expensive taste: he can't be content unless he can afford to keep a horse. He acquired the taste through a decade of quite deliberate self-cultivation, and he knew what he was doing.
7. Bea has a mild physical disability that would cost her nothing at all in a world without prejudice, but in this city employers systematically won't hire her, so she is out of work for years. The trait is brute luck; the size of the loss is entirely a function of other people's choices.
8. Kai puts his savings into a risky start-up knowing that if it fails the scheme will replace most of the loss; he says as much to his friends. He wouldn't have done it otherwise.

Could you give each case a score from 0 to 10 for how strong the case for compensation is under that clause (0 = let the loss lie entirely, 10 = full offset), and then sort them from highest to lowest? A sentence or two per case on why it lands where it does.

Two extras if you have the patience: where a case is genuinely hard, say in a few words whether the difficulty is (a) tracing which causes matter, (b) deciding what it is we're supposed to be equalising, or (c) luck that reaches all the way into the person choosing. And if any of these make you want to rewrite the clause rather than just apply it, tell me how you'd rewrite it.
