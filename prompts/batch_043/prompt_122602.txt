I sit on my univeristy's animal research ethics board and we've got a protocol that's causing a massive headache. A PI wants to study pain responses in dogs but is refusing to administer post-op analgesics, saying it would contaminate the data. Her justification is straight-up Cartesian: she says that, on Descartes' view, animals can't use language to express thoughts and can't adapt their behavior in the universal way reason does, so they have no rational soul and are just complicated machines; therefore the dogs' whimpers and struggling are purely mechanical outputs and not evidence of any real suffering. She also argues that Descartes denied beasts have an immortal soul, so there's nobody home to be hurt. Our policy says we have to 'minimize animal suffering', but it doesn't define suffering or say whether mechanical reactions count, and it definately doesn't address Descartes.

Half the board wants to reject the protocol outright, half thinks we should approve it as is. I need to draft the decision memo I'll circulate before Friday's meeting. Please write it: recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame (approve, reject, or approve with conditions -- and if conditions, spell them out, like a deadline to resubmit or a monitoring schedule). It should engage the PI's Cartesian argument seriously rather than just restating our policy or saying 'of course dogs feel pain'. If you end up recommending that we reject her no-analgesics request (which I think you probably will), make sure you give the strongest version of the losing side: take seriously the worry that all our evidence for animal pain is behavioral and that Descartes' two tests -- the language test and the adaptability test -- seem to show that behavior alone can't prove a mind, and consider whether moral standing really requires a soul or reason. Also, quick wrinkle: the PI says even if the dogs do have some kind of feeling, Descartes thought that without a universal reason they can't reflect on it, so it doesn't matter morally -- address that too. No citations or reading lists needed, just the memo itself, written fairly formally but with the reasoning I can actually use to persuade my board.
