I am a PhD student in philosophy finishing a paper on lexical threshold negative utilitarianism (LTNU) and, relatedly, on whether pleasure and pain are commensurable on a single cardinal scale. I am also running a graduate reading group on axiology next term, and I want the first session to open with a classification instrument rather than with a survey of views: twelve students, one handout, fifteen minutes to fill in. I need you to design it.

Please approach this as a professional philosopher with a research specialism in axiology and population ethics, and think carefully rather than quickly. Before you write the instrument itself, spend a few paragraphs brainstorming the raw logical space: list the branching questions you considered, note explicitly which pairs of them are not independent (so a tree cannot branch on both, or can only branch on one before the other), and note which candidate questions you discarded as pseudo-questions or as smuggling in a label. Only then give me the finished instrument.

The instrument should be a decision tree (with a short scoring key that lets someone tally their own path) whose nodes are concrete cases and concrete claims, never self-descriptions. No node may ask anything of the form "do you think suffering is lexically prior to happiness?"; every node must be a case or a pairwise verdict on a case, phrased so that a student who has never heard the label can answer from intuition or prior reflection. Roughly eight branch nodes and ten to twelve leaves. Use descriptive leaf labels; attach a traditional name only where you are confident one exists, and mark a leaf "unnamed" where you are not. I am working offline, so no citations, no bibliographies, no reading suggestions, and no claims about who holds what that you could not make with confidence.

Some constraints on content, because a generic tree would be useless to me:

The following leaves must appear: full (extreme) negative utilitarianism; lexical threshold NU; a scalar or continuous suffering-focused view on which intensities above the threshold still matter; a two-sided view with lexical structure on the good side as well; an anti-aggregation or "the numbers don't count" variant; and a position on which pleasure and pain are not on one scale at all.

The following case families must do the branching work, in whatever order you think best: (i) a "ladder of nails" family, in which pain intensity rises while the number of sufferers falls, designed to show whether the threshold is a cliff or a shelf; (ii) a "borrowed agony" family, in which one person's single agony is pitted against the same total quantity divided into a very large number of imperceptible micro-pains spread across very many people, designed to separate thresholds on total quantity from thresholds on individual experience-token intensity; (iii) a "reversed ladder" family on the good side, to test whether anyone's asymmetry is principled or merely temperamental.

One case must be a chaining case: a series of pains each only imperceptibly worse than the last, with a small pleasure sweetener available at each step, so that transitivity plus the sweeteners appears to entail that agony is compensable by enough scattered pleasure. Please build a node around this, or, if you think it cannot be made into a clean branch, place it as a flagged "tree-breaker" and say what it breaks.

I also want my own current view placed rather than indulged. Tentatively, I hold that the lexical threshold is not a point but a band, that within the band compensation is indeterminate rather than merely unknown, and that the band is hysteretic: approached from below, added pleasure still carries some justificatory weight, but once total suffering is above the band, added pleasure carries none at all. If this view is genuinely a distinct leaf, give it one and say which node isolates it; if it collapses into a leaf you already have, say which and show me why; if you think it is confused, say so and say which of its commitments is the load-bearing mistake.

For the empty regions: enumerate the answer-combinations your tree permits that no leaf occupies, and for each give a one-line diagnosis of why it is empty — incoherent, merely unattractive, collapses into a neighbouring leaf under scrutiny, or simply unexplored. Flag explicitly any two leaves your tree cannot separate, and say what further case would separate them if you had room for a ninth node.

Finally, be committed rather than even-handed. Give me the one node ordering you would actually use in the handout, with reasons; commit to which leaf is in your judgement the most defensible and which is the least stable under small changes in case description; and, as a self-critical note, mark the single node where your own answer is least settled and the placements in your taxonomy that rest on stipulations about the cases rather than on the logic of the positions.
