I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about whether the Exodus happened, and the argument kept collapsing into the same two moves: he would point to some silence in the archaeological record and treat it as decisive, and I would point out that we have almost no trace of plenty of small populations that certainly existed in the same region and era, so that the silence proves nothing. Neither of us had any account of what would actually settle the question, or even of what a settled answer would look like. So I want to stop arguing cases and ask about the standard instead.

The purpose I have in mind is fixed, and it is not an academic one. A national education ministry has to decide what a secondary-school textbook may assert about the Trojan War, about King Arthur, and about the Exodus, and for each of them it needs to be able to say whether the textbook may present the tradition as history, as a story with a historical core, or as a story only. So I want the specification: what an account of the historicity of a tradition has to deliver to be good enough to underwrite that kind of public verdict.

Give me the requirements list, each with the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark for each whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of one camp. Some of the distinctions and cases I think it has to handle, so that you can see the level of granularity I want rather than so that you can just repeat them back:

- It has to keep apart at least four questions that the word historicity normally runs together: whether the central event occurred; whether the surviving narrative reliably reports it; whether there is an unbroken chain of transmission from the event to the text we have; and whether the named individual at the centre of the story existed at all. A tradition can be in the clear on one and hopeless on another, so I want to know whether the theory is allowed to decompose, and what it costs if it does.
- It has to handle fusion: a real destruction of a city in the Late Bronze Age, remembered locally, later fused with an epic composed centuries afterwards in a different language and for a different audience, with both layers now indistinguishable in the text. Can a tradition be archival evidence for one period and pure invention for another?
- It has to handle the true-in-outline, false-in-the-famous-part case, and I would like it tested on a case where we already know the answer: imagine historians in the year 2400, working from fragments, applying the theory to twentieth-century traditions one of whose most-repeated details is certainly wrong. A theory that returns the verdict we know to be correct only because we can tune it after the fact is not what I am asking for. Say how it should be calibrated against cases with known outcomes and how loose it is allowed to be.
- It has to handle evidence the tradition itself produced: a story says a city was founded in a certain year, later inhabitants build a commemorative layer on the strength of that story, and archaeologists four centuries later date the layer and announce that the story is confirmed. The theory has to be able to discount feedback of this kind.
- It has to handle name-drift, where a real person's name ends up attached to the deeds of someone else, or a real place-name attracts invented events to itself.
- It has to say where its prior comes from. Traditions of a founding migration, traditions of a city destroyed by fire, traditions of a conquest commanded by a god, and traditions of a single combat between champions do not, I suspect, have anything like the same base rate of being substantially true, and until the theory says which reference class a given tradition belongs to and why, an appeal to base rates is just a number with a mood attached. This is the requirement I care most about, because I think it does more of the work than anyone admits.
- It has to avoid a literacy bias: a preliterate society cannot be expected to produce contemporary written attestation, so a theory that in effect requires it will sort traditions by the accidents of who kept archives rather than by anything about the traditions.
- It has to say how much weight an absence carries, and when. A neighbouring literate empire's victory inscription naming a people of the relevant name, in the relevant land, in roughly the relevant period, but saying nothing whatsoever about the tradition's central episodes, is evidence of something; whether it is evidence against the tradition, or only evidence about the empire's interests, is exactly the kind of thing the standard should settle in advance.
- It has to have a stopping rule. It must be able to return not answerable without that being a defect, and it must say in advance what finding would change the verdict, so that the ministry is not just being told to wait for a dig that may never happen.
- It has to be robust to the tradition's present usefulness: whether a story is load-bearing for a modern nation or a religious community should not move the verdict, even though the funding for excavations and the survival of manuscripts have both historically followed exactly that usefulness.

Three further things I want, and they are the part I expect to be interesting rather than confirmatory:

First, tell me whether any subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, because if the list cannot be met all at once then the list itself is the finding, and the ministry needs to know that it is choosing which requirement to betray. In particular I want to know whether decomposing the question into four is compatible with issuing the single binary verdict a textbook needs, and whether the reference-class requirement can be satisfied at all if there is no privileged way to place a tradition in a class.

Second, tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding approaches people actually hold, and reconsider whether that requirement is a genuine desideratum or a prejudice of one school dressed up as one.

Third, and only briefly, score against the list: the criteria-of-authenticity style of argument used in ancient-text scholarship, the conservative stance that a tradition is reliable until it is disproven, the minimalist stance that a tradition is a late construction unless independently attested, an explicitly base-rate or Bayesian treatment, and an anthropological treatment in terms of social memory and oral transmission. A few lines each is plenty. I want the criteria, not the scoreboard.

If the right conclusion is that when does a legend become history is not one question but several, say which questions it splits into and reissue the desiderata question by question.
