So I'm a novelist — third book, badly overdue — and there's one line that keeps getting thrown around in my writing group: a self is just a center of narrative gravity. It got used on me twice in the same month, in ways that I'm pretty sure contradict each other. First time, to tell me to stop worrying that my narrator at nineteen and my narrator at forty-seven don't feel like the same person: there's supposedly no deep fact of the matter, so stop trying to make them cohere. Second time, in a completely different tone, to tell me my protagonist has no arc because there's nothing to her but the story I've been telling about her — which was meant as an insult. When I pointed out that those are kind of opposite moves, everybody shrugged.

The book itself: first person, three strands. Chapters from her diary at nineteen; chapters from now, aged forty-seven; and a set of second-person chapters where the older one talks to the younger one, sometimes tenderly and sometimes not. There's also a plot hinge I can't write around — she made a promise at nineteen, and the last chapter has to decide whether keeping it now is loyalty or just self-destruction, and I honestly can't draft it until I know whether "is it still her?" is a real question or a pseudo-question I'm supposed to dissolve. One wrinkle that's been eating at me: a friend says fine, but a center of gravity isn't nothing — the center of mass of a chair does genuine work in the equations, it's just not an extra bit of stuff. And a center of *narrative* gravity, if I'm hearing it right, is different in a way that matters: you can get several incompatible ones depending on who's telling and why (her mother's version of her, her ex-wife's version, the version in the police statement), and nothing in the physics-flavoured analogy tells you which one is the right one. So is that the crux — is the line saying "real, but abstract," or "made up, and possibly several at once, with no umpire"? Those give me very different final chapters, and I'd like to know which one the people repeating it actually mean.

Can you lay it out as a menu rather than picking one and running with it? I don't want a survey of personal identity, and I really don't want to be told to go read a stack of papers — I've tried and bounced off. I want the distinct precise theses the slogan could be expressing, from the boring to the wild, with verdicts. Sections, please, in this order: "The readings" (numbered, 600–700 words, each one stated as a claim somebody could actually believe and defend, ideally with what it would predict about a case like mine); "Trivial, defensible, or false" (about 300 words, tagging each reading on your list — say which are platitudes nobody denies, which are substantive and worth arguing about, and which are just wrong); "What my group was doing with it" (about 250 words — which reading each of the two uses needed, and whether that reading, even if true, actually gets them their conclusion that I should stop worrying / that my character is empty); and finally "So what about the last chapter" (150 words or so — what, if anything, follows for a book built like mine, including 'nothing follows, the question was never going to be settled this way' if that's the honest answer). Oh, and one thing I keep loosing sleep over: if several incompatible stories each generate their own centre of gravity, is there any non-arbitrary reason to treat the one she tells about herself as the one that settles whether she's betraying anybody? Please say if that's the point where the analogy breaks.
