Two philosophers who disagree about whether agents with conflicting final values should trade with one another have argued past each other for a year and have now agreed to write up an adversarial collaboration. Produce the document they should produce.

Background the document may assume. By moral trade I mean an arrangement of the following kind: A and B disagree about what is finally valuable; each performs the action the other regards as most valuable, or funds the cause the other regards as best, possibly with money as the medium of exchange. Each may thereby end up better off by their own lights than if each had simply acted on their own verdict, in the way parties to an ordinary trade are better off. Assume, except where a case explicitly says otherwise, that neither party is uncertain about the other's values, about the other's abilities, or about the non-moral facts. Where moral uncertainty enters, treat it as each party's having credences spread over competing moral views and a rule for acting on them.

The two positions. (A) The trade optimist holds that pervasive and persistent moral disagreement, combined with large differences in how much value each party can produce per unit of resource, makes the gains from such swaps large, largely unexploited, and worth building institutions for. (B) The trade skeptic holds that nearly all of the apparent gains are already available to a single agent who simply spreads her resources across the moral views she is uncertain between, with no agreement, escrow, or trust required, and that what remains is eroded by three things: the incentive to be paid for what one would have done anyway, the fact that for many moral views the tradeable region is only what is permissible rather than what is required or forbidden, and the loss involved in acting against one's own verdict at another's behest.

Deliverable. First, a numbered section of agreed claims: what both would actually sign about the structure of the situation, about the conditions under which a swap is beneficial by each party's own lights, about what a completed and verified swap would show, and about what neither side claims. Each claim should be substantive rather than hedged into emptiness. Second, a disagreements table whose rows are cruxes and whose columns give the optimist's own formulation of the point at issue, the skeptic's own formulation, one case or argument both accept as bearing on it, and each side's prediction about that case written down in advance.

The cruxes I want covered at minimum:

1. Decomposition of the surplus. Separate the component that comes from comparative advantage in production, where each party has skills, access, or causal position the other lacks, from the component that comes merely from diminishing marginal returns to spending on any single cause. Ask whether the second component is entirely captured by one agent diversifying internally, so that the positive case for trade must rest on the first.

2. The permissible margin. How much of what parties value lies in the region that is optional for them rather than required or forbidden, and whether this makes moral trade structurally biased toward agent-neutral or maximizing views. Say whether such a bias is itself a cost, and for whom.

3. Incentive compatibility. The would-have-done-anyway problem, the incentive to exaggerate the value one places on one's own cause or to understate what one would have done unaided, and whether repetition, reputation, or third-party verification repairs the problem or merely relocates it.

4. What is lost, if anything. Whether acting against one's own considered verdict in exchange for a reciprocal act involves a loss not already counted in the trade's expected value, and whether that loss is moral, aretaic, or merely psychological. Each side should specify what kind of loss would count against its own view.

5. Second-order disagreement. Parties who disagree not only about what is valuable but about how to act under moral uncertainty, one maximizing expected value, another minimizing worst cases, another averse to variance. Can a mutually beneficial swap still be constructed, and if so is it unique.

6. Diagnosis. Which rows are really disputes about magnitudes neither party has measured, which are verbal, and which are genuinely normative. Where a row is verbal, state the single well-posed question both should be asking instead.

Constraints. Argue from the structure of stylised cases rather than from published empirical findings or citations, since neither side can check any sources here. Invent at least two cases yourself, one of which should be the two-donor case in which the parties have identical resources, abilities, and opportunities and differ only in what they are certain of. Wherever a verdict turns on a parameter, such as the curvature of a value-production function, the size of the non-tradeable region, or the shadow of the future in repeated interaction, state the threshold at which each side's verdict would flip. Prefer formulations that a hostile reader on the other side would still accept as an accurate statement of their own position.
