I manage a four-person quantitative team at a catastrophe risk firm. We build Monte Carlo models for perils where we have little or no loss data, which means we constantly have to spread probability evenly across a range because we know nothing else to do. The thing I keep tripping over is that the answer depends on which quantity we spread evenly over. Our version of the textbook case: a hazard track clips a circular exposure zone, and the probability that the segment of track inside the zone exceeds some length is 1/2, 1/3, or 1/4 depending on whether we randomize the track's midpoint along a radius, its two crossing points on the boundary, or its midpoint over the area of the zone. All three are uniform in something, and I have been told this is called Bertrand's paradox and that the background idea is the principle of indifference. What worries me more than the paradox itself: being told to specify a physical generating story instead — straws dropped on a circle, or a storm track as a line in the plane with an orientation and an offset — seems to push the arbitrariness down one level, since the hand dropping the straws, or the distribution of the offset, is itself something we have to assume.

Please write a procedure my team can run in under an hour on any model where we are tempted to assume evenness. My analysts have one statistics course each, so it has to be a numbered list of at most eight steps with a plain-language statement of what counts as a sufficient answer at each step. It should end in a one-paragraph justification we file with our regulator, naming the quantity being spread evenly, what licenses that choice, and the units or scale in which it is flat. Mark explicitly where the procedure stops with a no and sends the analyst back for data or for a better-posed question, and where it hands the decision to a senior reviewer because the remaining question is a modeling judgment rather than a fact. I want it to include a test I have been using informally, the handover test: an analyst who has never seen the model must be able to reproduce the number from the specification alone, and if they could reasonably get a different one, the specification is rejected. Include a units-and-reparameterization check as well, since our results are reported on a log loss scale and our priors are sometimes on rates rather than their logarithms. Tell me what the procedure cannot settle.

Then run it on three cases: the hazard-track geometry above, where I expect a generating story to rescue us; our current default of a uniform prior on a component failure rate between zero and an upper bound, which I suspect the procedure will tell us to abandon; and a request we just got for the probability that an unnamed quantity, future regulatory fines for a new peril, falls in a given range. Keep the whole answer under 800 words.
