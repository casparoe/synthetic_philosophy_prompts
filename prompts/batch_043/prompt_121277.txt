THE QUESTION

Question 3. It is often said these days that historical writing is a literary artefact, and some novelists have drawn a strong conclusion from it: that a novel set in the past can be truer to that past than a history of it is — truer, perhaps, because the novelist admits the shaping she does while the historian hides it behind a flat, unadorned style.

Consider the following invented case, which we will call the Total Record. For one year, in one city, a complete and wholly reliable record survives: every event that took place there in that year, dated and located, including everything every inhabitant said and wrote down — and nothing else. No explanations, no causes, no summaries, no interpretations, no judgements, and no omissions. Just every particular, in order.

(a) Would a historian with the Total Record in front of her have achieved what Ranke meant by writing the past as it actually was? Say what you take that ideal to consist in, and what it was a reaction against.
(b) Would the record answer Carr's worry that the facts of history never reach us unmediated — that they are always selected, arranged and refracted by somebody?
(c) Would it answer Hayden White's worry about emplotment: that what turns a heap of events into a history is a narrative form — tragic, comic, romantic, satiric — which the historian brings to the material rather than finds in it?
(d) Suppose a novelist claims that her novel is true to the past in the same sense in which a good history is, and that the difference between the two genres is only a difference of convention. Using (a) to (c), say exactly what she would be committing herself to, and whether she is right.

Throughout, keep three things seperate: (i) whether the particular statements in a history are true; (ii) whether the selection of facts is adequate or distorting; (iii) whether the shape given to the whole can be assessed as better or worse at all, and if so, how.

THE STUDENT'S ANSWER

Ranke's famous phrase is best read as a demand that the historian get herself out of the account: no judging of the past, no lessons for the present, no turning what happened into an edifying example. What he was attacking was the habit of writing history as a brief for one's own side. So the ideal is largely a negative one about what the historian must not add, not a promise that the past can be copied onto the page. Ranke was himself a source critic — he worried about which witnesses to trust and how far — and that practice only makes sense on the assumption that the evidence is incomplete and has to be sifted.

That makes the Total Record a strange object as far as Ranke is concerned. In one way it satisfies his ideal completely: nothing added, nothing judged, nothing left out. In another way it makes the historian redundant, because there is nothing left for her to do. I think the right thing to say is that the record is not what Ranke was asking for, but not because it falls short of what he wanted — rather because it bypasses the problem entirely. He was telling historians how to treat imperfect evidence, and the thought experiment removes the imperfection instead of answering him. It doesn't refute him either.

Carr's worry is that the facts of history never arrive on their own. Somebody decided what got written down; some things survived and some didn't; and the historian comes to the archive already asking questions, and asking a question is already a way of selecting. The facts are real on Carr's view, but they are always somebody's facts. The Total Record seems to help here, since the accidents of survival have been removed. But it doesn't settle the matter. A complete list of everything that happened in a city over a year would be endless and unreadable, and any historian would still have to choose a handful of events to write about, and would choose them because of a question she brought with her. Selection isn't a defect produced by missing documents; it is what having a subject consists in. So the record answers the crude version of Carr's worry, the version about gaps, and not the serious one.

White goes further than Carr, but in the same direction, and the difference between them is one of degree: Carr holds that the facts are refracted by the questions we bring, White holds that they must be supplemented by a shape of our own. His claim is that however many facts you have, they arrive as a chronicle and leave as a story, and that the conversion is done with forms the historian brings to the material. Because no record is ever complete, the historian has to fill out the sequence with a beginning, a middle and an end, and that shape is made rather than found. This is what White means by calling a history a verbal fiction: not that the events didn't happen, but that the account is manufactured out of them. It follows that there is no fact of the matter about which emplotment is the correct one — the choice between them is aesthetic and political rather than evidential, which is why White treats it as in the end a moral choice.

What all this means for the novelist is that she occupies exactly the position the historian occupies. Both begin from a heap of particulars; both must impose a shape on it; neither can extract the shape from the particulars. The historian's plain style is a convention that conceals this, and the novel's frankness about invention is a convention that displays it, but conventions are all they are. So if the novelist claims to be true to the past in the same sense that a good history is, then on the argument above she is right: the two genres differ in what their readers expect of them, not in how they stand to the truth. The one thing she cannot claim is (i) — that her particular statements answer to the evidence — since she has invented them freely. But that again is a rule of her genre, and a rule is only a convention too.
