Had a three-hour argument with my friend last night about religion in politics, and now I've somehow agreed to help draft a civility code for our state legislature's rules committee (my uncle is on it and asked for a memo), so I need to know whether my own position hangs together before I inflict it on anyone. The rule on the table is a two-track thing: members can say whatever they want in floor debate, but every bill needs a one-paragraph "civic rationale" in its committee report that someone with no religion could in principle sign. Here's roughly where I landed:

1. Coercive law needs public justification — no statute should be enforceable unless there's a rationale for it that doesn't rest on contested claims about the sacred or about what makes a life go well.
2. It's illiberal to tell religious citizens to translate their reasons into secular ones, or to leave their deepest convictions at the door before they speak in politics.
3. Secular comprehensive views are on exactly the same footing: a utilitarian, a Marxist and a Thomist are all reasoning from comprehensive doctrines, and the state doesn't get to call its own favourite comprehensive view "just reason."
4. Case verdicts I'm not budging on: I'm against the proposed state constitutional amendment defining marriage as heterosexual; I'm for letting a pharmacist step back from dispensing emergency contraception when a willing pharmacist is two blocks away but against that same exemption in the county where the next option is ninety minutes off; I think a judge who rests an opinion on scripture has done something wrong even though I defend a legislator voting on exactly that basis; and I'm fine with public money following kids to faith-based charters schools that teach their religion, provided they hit the same academic benchmarks as anyone else.

My friend says 2 and 3 quietly eats 1, and that 4 is doing work my principles can't pay for. I think she's wrong about at least one of those claims but I can't tell which, and I don't want the memo to be self-undermining. Audit the set: which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look like it, and what has to break. Give me a table ranked most to least severe with these columns: commitments_involved (the numbers), real_or_apparent, severity (1–5), hidden_assumption (the extra thing I'd have to be committed to for the tension to bite at all), what_has_to_give (the smallest revision to one commitment), and cost (what I lose elsewhere in the set, and especially which verdict in 4 I'd have to swallow). Then add two short named fields after the table: cheapest_single_revision (the one edit that cleans up the most) and verdict_i_cant_afford (the case verdict in 4, if any, my principles can't actually deliver — and what dropping it would cost me).
