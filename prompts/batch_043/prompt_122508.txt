I'm on our campus working group on generative AI, and I've agreed to write a short piece for the faculty newsletter laying out where I actually stand — not the committee's line, mine. Before I put my name to it I'd like someone to check whether the package holds together, because different colleagues have picked at different bits of it and I can't tell who's right. I do epistemology, so sorry if this is more in the weeds than a newsletter deserves.

Here's the position, roughly as I wrote it down for myself:

1. These systems don't understand anything. They're not knowers and they're not even believers, so talk about what a model "knows" is loose talk, and I want to keep saying that in public.
2. But my own understanding is partly made out of external stuff — my notebook, the shared drive, the colleague who always remembers who said what and when. I'm happy to be some kind of extended-mind person about this, at least for the real cases.
3. The reason relying on a model is different in kind from (2) is that the model has no stake in being right. My colleague cares whether she's got it right. The model doesn't care about anything.
4. Students don't need to memorize facts anymore, or be able to do long division, or spell. Lookup is free and instant, and insisting on it is nostalgia dressed up as rigour.
5. Students do need to be able to write a whole essay with nothing open but a blank page. Someone who produces good philosophy only with the model in the next tab has been miseducated, even when the paper that comes out is excellent.
6. I grade process, not product. Credit for outlines, drafts and revision, and I genuinely don't care whether the final version was polished with a tool.
7. I use these things constantly myself — summaries, poking holes in arguments, literature I'd never otherwise get to — and I think my own understanding has got better, not worse.
8. There's a phenomenon I've started calling the checker's regress: you can in principle check what the model hands you, but checking it well requires exactly the understanding you offloaded, so the check hollows out as the offloading goes on. That's what I actually worry about with my students.
9. I also think a student can genuinely come to understand something by arguing back and forth with a model. I've watched it happen in office hours, and its not just the student feeling like they understand.

So: which pairs really pull against each other, and which only look like they do? I'd like the genuine tensions ranked by severity rather than all flagged as equally fatal, and for each one I want to know what would have to give and what that costs me. Two I especially want pressed: whether (3) can survive (2) and (7) together, and whether (6) quietly makes (5) unenforceable. And if I can only revise one commitment before this goes out, which revision cleans up the most?
