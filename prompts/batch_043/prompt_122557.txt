I'm auditing a graduate seminar in formal epistemology — we spent two weeks on ordinary Bayesian conditioning and have just started on updating under uncertain evidence, which I'm finding much harder to think clearly about than I expected. To prepare, I read the relevant chapter of a popular book on Bayesian reasoning that a friend recommended, and it introduces the fix for uncertain evidence like this:

Ordinary Bayesian updating handles only the case where you learn something outright: once you are certain of E, your new credence in any hypothesis H should simply be your old P(H|E). But most real evidence doesn't arrive that way. You don't learn that the cloth is green; you glance at it under bad shop lighting and end up, say, 70% confident that it is. Jeffrey conditionalization — also called probability kinematics — is the generalization that covers this. You take the partition {E, ¬E} consisting of the possibilities your experience bears on, leave the conditional probabilities P(H|E) and P(H|¬E) exactly as they were, and redistribute probability across the cells to match your new confidence: P_new(H) = P(H|E)·P_new(E) + P(H|¬E)·P_new(¬E). This is really just Bayes' theorem applied cell by cell. It is strictly more general than ordinary conditioning, which is the special case where P_new(E) = 1, and it is the standard Bayesian treatment of uncertain evidence: whenever your evidence is uncertain, this is the rule to apply. And since all the rule does is reweight the cells, it makes no difference what order you process a series of such experiences in — you land in the same place regardless.

The clause about leaving the conditional probabilities untouched is what I keep tripping over: it sounds to me like an extra assumption rather than something you get for free from Bayes' theorem, but I can't say when it ought to fail. So I tried the rule on a case from my own work. D is the proposition that a patient has a particular infection, with prior P(D) = 0.2. A rapid test is read off a phone app: sensitivity 0.9, false-positive rate 0.3. There is also a 0.1 chance that the swab was mislabeled and came from another patient, in which case the reading is pure noise — P(positive | mislabeled) = 0.5 regardless of D — and mislabeling is independent of D. Then two things happen in the course of one evening. First, the app's result is read in glare off a ward computer; the experience leaves me 0.8 confident that the app said "positive" and 0.2 confident that it said "negative", though conditional on what the app actually said it tells me nothing further about the patient. Second, a nurse remarks that the labels were smudged that morning, which moves me to 0.4 confident that the swab was mislabeled. Applying the rule first to the partition {app said positive, app said negative} with 0.8, and then to the partition {mislabeled, not mislabeled} with 0.4, I get roughly 0.29 for P(D); doing the two in the opposite order gives me roughly 0.28. I can't tell whether that gap is my arithmetic or something real.

Could you go through the book's paragraph sentence by sentence and tell me what a formal epistemologist would accept, what they would wince at, and what is simply wrong? In particular: is it fair to describe Jeffrey conditionalization as strictly more general than ordinary conditioning, or does "more general" conceal something? Is "whenever your evidence is uncertain, this is the rule to apply" defensible, or does the rule have preconditions the book is glossing over — and how exactly does the rigidity condition do the work the book seems to attribute to Bayes' theorem itself? Does the order-independence claim hold up, and if not, is my 0.29-versus-0.28 gap an instance of that failure and does it indict the rule or only my use of it? Finally, for my case specifically: is a Jeffrey update on the {positive, negative} partition even the right tool, or should I have built a single space containing the infection, the app's reading, the mislabeling and what I actually saw on the screen, and then conditioned directly on what I saw — and if so, what would have to hold for that procedure to return the same answer the rule gives?
