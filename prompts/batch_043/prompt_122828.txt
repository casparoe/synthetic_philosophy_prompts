My flatmate's finishing a thesis on whether there are any genuine moral dilemmas, and she's got suprisingly firm verdicts on the cases everyone drags out:

- two strangers drowning the same distance away, you can only reach one: not a genuine dilemma, save either one, nobody is wronged
- two equally serious promises to two people you love, impossible to keep both, and no way to make it up to whoever you let down: genuine dilemma
- breaking a small promise to your roommate in order to stop something much worse from happening, where you can explain and apologise afterwards: not a genuine dilemma
- a nurse in a blackout with one dose left and two patients, where hospital policy says treat in order of arrival: not a genuine dilemma, the policy settles it
- staying with your mother versus going off to join a resistance movement, where either way someone you love is devastated: genuine dilemma

The case I want to try on her: you're the only person who can stop a colleague from getting a job they got by stealing someone else's research, but the only way to do it is to pass on something a friend told you in confidence. Doing it is clearly the lesser evil and clearly what you ought to do, and it will wreck the friendship beyond repair.

What will she say? I can think of two rules that fit her verdicts so far — one roughly like you're in a genuine dilemma only if every option open to you is one you'd be doing something wrong in taking, another roughly like you're in one whenever whichever you pick leaves a remainder you can't clean up — and they come apart here. Which is she more likely running, what other rules fit her track record just as well, and what case would you put to her next to tell them apart?
