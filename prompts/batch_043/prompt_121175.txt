Hi — I'm a PhD student in political science working on jury decision-making (mostly mock-jury experiments plus some observational work on hung juries), and I've been going in circles on something that I think is really an epistemology question wearing a procedural-costume. Wondering if you can help.

The object is the criminal jury, and specifically the way the institution handles disagreement between the people it asks to decide. Three features, which seem to me to pull in different directions:

First, conviction has to be unanimous — or at least it does in the US now; until 2020 two states allowed 10-2 convictions and the Supreme Court struck that down in Ramos v. Louisiana, overruling Apodaca. One holdout and there's no verdict at all: hung jury, mistrial, and then the state is free to retry the whole case in front of a brand-new jury, and to keep doing that until some jury is unanimous. Only an actual acquittal is final.

Second, at the same time, the practice seems to protect the holdout. There's an old instruction, approved by the Supreme Court in 1896 in Allen v. United States and often called the dynamite charge, that tells a deadlocked jury that minority jurors should go back and ask whether their doubt is really reasonable given that it makes no impression on the others — but the same instructions also tell jurors not to give up an honest conviction just to go along, and roughly half the states prohibit the instruction altogether. So the court sometimes leans on the minority to defer to the majority and sometimes tells them not to.

Third, Scotland had — I think it was abolished quite recently, I may be behind on where the bill ended up — a third verdict, not proven, next to guilty and not guilty, with fifteen jurors deciding by simple majority rather than twelve deciding unanimously.

So: what does any of this presuppose about disagreement? My sense is that the practice is shot through with some view about what disagreement among roughly equally-informed people is evidence of, and that the two big families of view in that literature — the conciliationist ones, on which an epistemic peer's contrary verdict is itself a reason to move or at least to lose confidence, and the steadfast ones, on which your own evidence lets you discount it — would recommend quite different institutions, and I can't tell which one the jury is secretly built on.

Things I specifically want your take on:

- Are jurors even meant to be peers? They're randomly selected to be a cross-section of the community, which is a strange way to build a panel if what you wanted was twelve people with identical evidence and identical competence at weighing it. Random selection looks like it presupposes the opposite: that disagreement among diverse strangers is informative rather than noise. There's that simulation result I half-remember — an economist and a political scientist, I think — where a diverse group of mediocre problem-solvers outperformed a group of the very best individual solvers; I don't remember whether it held up and I don't need it to. The question stands without it. Does the practice presuppose that heterogeneity is epistemically better than peerhood, or is cross-sectionality only there for impartiality and legitimacy?

- The unanimity rule versus the retry rule. If persistent disagreement really is evidence that the case is weaker, then retrying until you draw a jury with no holdout looks like junk: you're sampling until the inconvenient disagreement goes away, which is exactly what you shouldn't do if disagreement is a signal. If disagreement among non-peers carries no evidential weight, then retrying is fine — but so is 10-2, and unanimity collapses into a bare threshold rule with no epistemic content. Is there a reading on which both halves are coherent together? And which half, if either, does the practice strictly require as opposed to merely fitting well with?

- The dynamite charge looks like the one place where the law explicitly instructs people to treat the fact that others disagree with you as a reason to revise — and it's also the one part that's half-banned and widely thought coercive. Coincidence? Does an institution that otherwise refuses to rank the jurors' evidential positions have any non-arbitrary way to deliver that instruction? And can anyone tell from outside whether a holdout who caves after such an instruction was rationally persuaded or just worn down — my understanding is that in most places you generally can't put jurors on the stand afterwards and ask how they reasoned, which seems to me like the practice deliberately sealing off the very thing you'd need to know.

- The part I actually care about, which is a reform I've been toying with. Keep the jury, change the record: every juror writes down a private verdict before any deliberation happens; the jury then deliberates as normal; but the official outcome is computed off the pre-deliberation distribution against some threshold, while the post-deliberation verdict is recorded and non-binding. Run enough cases, score the panels against some proxy for the truth, and you could test which aggregation rule actually does better. What does that design presuppose about disagreement that unanimity doesn't? Is it secretly committed to thinking that deliberation destroys information rather than produces it? If the pre-deliberation split came out 6-6, what should the institution do — is a hung panel an outcome, or a failure to have one?

I dont want a reading list or citations; I've read the survey material. What I'd like is: reconstruct the implicit epistemology of disagreement that the existing practice only makes sense under; separate what it strictly presupposes from what it merely sits well with; flag where its parts are inconsistent with each other; say what the practice would have to become if we swapped in the rival view of disagreement; and give me a verdict on the reform, even if the verdict is that it's underdetermined and here's the empirical finding that would settle it.
