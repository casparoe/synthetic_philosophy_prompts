I write historical fiction, and my current manuscript turns on a scene I cannot seem to get right. In it, a consortium of marine underwriters in 1748 must set a premium for a voyage to a coast none of them has charted: the entire evidence base is a handful of contradictory pilot reports, one wreck, and a rumour. My protagonist, the syndicate's actuary, refuses to name a single number. He insists — and I want him to be able to insist plausibly rather than merely stubbornly — that the honest response to evidence like this is a range rather than a point, and that there is a defensible way of acting on a range. Chasing whether that is even coherent led me into the literature on imprecise probabilities, where I have now been stuck for the better part of a year.

The dispute, as I have reconstructed it, is between those who hold that a rational agent's confidence in a proposition is, or is rationally required to be, a single real number, and those who hold that it may legitimately be indeterminate — represented by a set of admissible probability functions or by an interval — with action then governed by something like maximising the worst case over that set, or by treating as choiceworthy any option that comes out optimal under at least one admissible member. Neither side looks to me to be arguing past the other in any obvious way, and yet a friend who works in formal epistemology assures me the exchange has had roughly this shape since the early 1990s: the sharp side produces a paradox — diachronic vulnerability, decision-theoretic paralysis, or the odd result that conditioning on a partition can leave you less precise about an event no matter which cell of the partition turns out to obtain — and the imprecise side replies that the paradox is a feature of honest bookkeeping, or that it merely shows we have conflated the question of what you believe with the question of what you should do. On her account, essentially nobody converts.

What I want is a diagnosis rather than a verdict, partly because I have a hypothesis and do not trust it. My hypothesis is that the two sides are not really disagreeing about probability at all, but about the unit of rational assessment: whether rationality attaches to a single momentary decision taken against a fixed body of evidence, or to a policy or disposition evaluated across many cases, where it is the long-run record of the policy that does the work. If that is right, the fight about whether confidence can be indeterminate is the shadow of a fight about what a normative theory is answerable to. Relatedly, I have been experimenting with a rule for my actuary that I have not encountered in anything I have read: call it the least-correction rule, on which, given a set of admissible probabilities, you act on whichever member of that set would require the smallest total adjustment if the evidence were enriched to the point of sharpness — so that you act decisively without pretending to a precision you do not have. Is that a genuine third option, or merely a redescription of a familiar rule such as admissibility relative to the set or minimisation of regret? And, more importantly, given whatever diagnosis you favour, is this disagreement substantive, verbal, a difference about which cases count as data or about what a theory owes us by way of action guidance, or a sign that the founding question is ill-posed — and what, if anything, would count as progress here: a formal result, a clean distinction between doxastic states and decision rules, an empirical finding about long-run performance, or nothing at all?
