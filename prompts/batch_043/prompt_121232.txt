I'm a PhD student working in formal epistemology, and this term I'm helping to run a third-year philosophical logic seminar, for which I'm putting together a short handout on the surprise exam paradox. Before writing it I read a chapter on the paradox in a recent popular book of philosophical puzzles, to see how the dialectic is presented outside the literature, and I want to know how much of it I can safely put in front of undergraduates. The chapter's account runs roughly like this: 'The teacher announces on the last day of term that there will be exactly one exam in the coming week, on a day the class cannot predict in advance. The class reasons backwards: it cannot be on the Friday, because if they reached Thursday evening with no exam they would know it had to be on the Friday, and then it would not be a surprise; but with Friday ruled out it cannot be on the Thursday either, and so on back to the Monday. So the exam cannot be a surprise. Yet the teacher sets it on the Wednesday and the class is surprised — which shows the announcement was true after all, and that flawless reasoning led them to a false conclusion. Philosophers have now largely agreed on the diagnosis. The announcement is self-referential: it is, in effect, a claim about the class's own inability to know it, and so, like the sentence "it is raining but I do not know that it is raining", it is a truth that cannot be known by the very people it is addressed to. The class's reasoning is valid, but it requires them to assume that they know the announcement is true, and they cannot; so the argument never gets going, and Wednesday's exam is surprising precisely because the announcement is the sort of thing that cannot be known. The deeper lesson is the one Gödel taught us: the announcement is a fixed point of a "this cannot be proved" operator, and the paradox is really the second incompleteness theorem — or Löb's theorem — wearing a story.'

What makes me suspicious is a version I've been playing with that removes the knowledge-talk altogether, since it struck me that a handout example ought not to wait on a theory of knowledge. Suppose the teacher announces, about an unending sequence of class meetings d1, d2, d3, …, that there will be exactly one exam; that its date is drawn from a distribution P which she fixed in advance and has now written on the board; and that P has this property: at the start of every meeting, the class's credence that the exam is that day, conditional on its not having come earlier, is at most one half. If P is geometric, say P(d_n) = 0.4 × 0.6^(n−1), then that conditional credence is 0.4 at every meeting, so the announcement looks true, known to be true (the class can do the arithmetic), and yet the exam is at no point to be expected. Over a finite week of N meetings, by contrast, no distribution at all can satisfy the condition: on the morning of the last meeting the conditional credence has to be 1. So the same backwards induction goes through with no mention of knowledge, self-reference or proof anywhere in it. My guess is that what is really doing the work is the existence of a last day — the well-foundedness of the ordering of days — rather than anything specifically epistemic. But I cannot tell whether I have changed the subject: perhaps "surprise" is not a credal notion at all, and my version is a different puzzle that merely shares a name, in which case it would put no pressure on the chapter whatsoever.

So: how fair is the chapter? I'd like an accuracy audit I can act on — go through the summary and separate what a specialist would accept as correct, what is a defensible simplification I could pass on to third-years, and what is a genuine misrepresentation or overstatement; and where the chapter goes wrong, tell me what a defender of the view it is caricaturing would say in reply. I am inclined to distrust the Gödel passage and to take the unknowability passage seriously, but that may be exactly backwards, so please don't defer to my leanings. Use my credal variant as a test of the chapter's diagnosis rather than filing it as a separate curiosity, and tell me whether it does the work I think it does — including whether the finite/infinite contrast really isolates what I claim it isolates, and whether the fact that the announcement in my version is both true and known is a problem for the "unknowable announcement" line or merely an artefact of my having weakened the surprise condition. Give me a committed verdict on where the real crux of the paradox lies — a definite claim with reasons, not a survey of the options — and flag where your verdict itself depends on a contested reading of "surprise", or on a contestable assumption about what the class is entitled to presuppose in reasoning about the announcement. Please write in British English, and I'd much rather have the argument than a bibliography; references I can chase up myself.
