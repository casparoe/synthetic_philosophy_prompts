The question:

Question 4 (20 marks). (a) State Skolem's paradox. (b) Explain why it is not a contradiction: what exactly does the model assert, and what is true of it from outside? (c) Does the paradox show that the question how many real numbers there are has no determinate answer? Discuss.

The student's answer:

Suppose ZFC has a model, which it does if it is consistent. By the downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem it then has a model M whose domain is countable. Since M satisfies ZFC, it satisfies Cantor's theorem, so M contains an object P that M takes to be the powerset of its natural numbers, and M asserts of P that it is uncountable. But M itself is countable, so P has only countably many members, and there is a bijection from the natural numbers onto P. So P is both uncountable and countable. That is Skolem's paradox.

It is not really a contradiction, because the two claims are made from different standpoints. When M says that P is uncountable, all it means is that no member of M's own domain is a bijection from the natural numbers onto P. The bijection I described above exists from the outside but is not an element of M, so M cannot see it. The deeper reason M can go wrong here is that a model of set theory does not use the real membership relation: the symbol ∈ of the formal theory is interpreted by some binary relation the model happens to supply, and so the object that plays the role of the powerset of ω inside M need not be the genuine powerset of ω at all. It is really only a surrogate, and it may omit subsets of ω that exist in the real universe. Once we notice that the membership symbol and the quantifiers are being reinterpreted, the tension disappears.

What the paradox therefore shows is that cardinality is relative to a model. P is uncountable relative to M and countable from the external standpoint, and since no standpoint is privileged — we can never step outside every model at once — there is no model-independent fact of the matter about how many real numbers there are. The moral is that first-order axiomatisations cannot pin down the intended universe of sets, and that the remedy is to axiomatise set theory in second-order logic instead, since second-order formulations are categorical and so leave no unintended models for the argument to get a grip on.
