I'm about two-thirds of the way through a novel whose narrator is a neuropsychologist, and the plot depends on a piece of fictional hardware I'm calling an ambient metacognitive prosthesis: a device that reads a patient's early visual cortex and induces in that patient assertoric states about her own seeing, so that a woman with dense blindsight — first-order visual states intact, no spontaneous access to them — comes to have conscious visual experience of the left half of her world. I want the device to be metaphysically optional rather than magical, and the version of the higher-order thought theory I was given at a reading group seemed to make it so: add the right higher-order representation and a previously dark first-order state lights up. But someone in the group wrote out an argument on a napkin that I have not been able to escape, and I would rather find its flaw than rewrite two hundred pages.

Here is the argument as I have reconstructed it, cleaned up but I think faithful. (1) A mental state M of a subject S is conscious if and only if, and because, S has a higher-order representation that represents S as being in M. (2) Higher-order representations are themselves mental states. (3) So by (1), substituting a higher-order representation H for M, H is conscious only if S has a still-higher-order representation representing S as being in H. (4) The same substitution applies again, and again: for every n, an nth-order representation is conscious only if S has an (n+1)th-order one, so consciousness of anything requires an actually instantiated infinite hierarchy of distinct representations. (5) A finite brain, in the fraction of a second during which a single experience is conscious, can instantiate only finitely many distinct occurrent representational states. (6) So either no mental state of ours is ever conscious — which cannot be right, and which would also rob (1) of the phenomenon it was introduced to explain — or (1) is false. (7) The replies I was offered all seem to make things worse. If the regress is said to be harmless because the higher levels need only be dispositional, then (1) is false as stated, since a merely possible representation cannot be what makes an actual state conscious now. If it is said to be harmless because a higher-order representation can take itself as its own object, then a single state is both first-order and higher-order, which is self-representationalism; and once one state may do that work, I cannot see why the original perceptual state cannot do it, so the theory has become its own rival and my prosthesis is doing nothing a first-order state could not. If it is said that higher-order representations are conscious in some different, non-relational sense while first-order ones are conscious only relationally, then that second sense is doing all the explanatory work and (1) has explained nothing. (8) Worse, on the reading where a higher-order representation confers consciousness while being itself non-conscious, an unexperienced subpersonal monitoring state suffices for consciousness — and then my device works, but so would one made of silicon, and so, seemingly, would any system that merely tracks its own states, which is far more than I want.

I would rather keep (1) than any of the others, and I would rather keep the theory genuinely different from self-representational and first-order views, because the novel turns on a character who has all the first-order machinery and none of the higher-order states. So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, I want to know whether the non-consciousness of the higher-order state is a feature the theory can honestly claim or a bullet it is only pretending not to have bitten, and whether the hierarchy has to be actually infinite, merely indefinitely extendable, or something else that I am muddling. If the argument is valid and I simply have to give up a plausible premise, tell me which premise is cheapest to surrender and what I lose for the plot.
