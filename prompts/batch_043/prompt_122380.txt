Writing the third book of a trilogy here and I've been stuck on one chapter for six weeks, so apologies if this is a weird way to ask a question. My first reader, Marta, did a philosophy degree about twenty years ago and has never once let me get away with a hand-wave, and I want to know what she's going to say before I hand her the draft, because if she thinks I've cheated she'll take the whole third act apart. The series is set a few decades after "transit" became routine — you get scanned, the body is destroyed, a new one is assembled at the far end from local feedstock — and over the last two books she has given me verdicts on five setups:

- ordinary transit: "that's him, I'd take the train myself."
- a slow neuron-by-neuron replacement over ten years, no scanning, no gap, nobody notices a thing: "non-issue, obviously still him."
- the double-build glitch — replica assembled on Mars, original on Earth never destroyed, both alive, both equally continuous with the man who went in: "the one on Mars is a copy. the man on Earth is the man. and if the Earth one got hit by a bus the next day, the Mars one still wouldn't become him — it'd just be a copy with nobody left to contradict it." She got wierdly heated about that one, which makes me suspect she half believes the opposite of what she said, and I definately don't know which half.
- two bodies assembled from one scan, each with the full psychology: "neither one is him. two successors, and neither has a better claim."
- a rebuild from a scan taken forty years earlier, so the man who steps out is twenty-two and remembers nothing of his marriage, his kids, or the war: "not him. that's a stranger in a borrowed face."

Everybody keeps telling me to go read Parfit and I'm sure I should, but that isn't what I need at the moment — I need the shape of the rule she's actually running, so I can write a character who is consistent with himself.

Two cases coming up in the new manuscript. First: a "wet" failure. The Mars body is built perfectly — full psychology, remembers his wedding, his daughter's name, the fight with his brother. The Earth body isn't fully destroyed: the remnant lives another two years in a care home, can't lay down new memories, recognises nobody, maybe keeps a scrap of temperament and a fear of thunder. Which one is Teo while both are still alive, and which one is Teo after the remnant dies? The chapter turns on his daughter deciding which bedside to sit at, and I need to know whether that's a genuine dilemma for her or whether Marta's rule has already settled it, and settled it which way. Second, and this is the one I have never once seen anybody discuss: a collision in the scanner merges two people. Teo and his estranged brother Aris go in, one body comes out, and it's a real blend — all of Teo's memories and most of his temperament, plus Aris's memories and a lot of his habits; call it two-thirds Teo. Does Teo survive that? Is the person who walks out both of them, neither of them, or a third person who happens to remember two childhoods? And emotionally: is this a mercy or a horror — should Teo, knowing the machine is faulty, be more frightened of this than of dying outright, less frightened, or exactly as frightened as he is of ordinary transit? Does any of that change if the blend comes out a clean fifty-fifty, or if it comes out ninety-ten?

So what I'm after: two or three different rules that fit all five of her earlier verdicts, what each one predicts about my two cases, your best guess at which one she's running and why, and the one extra case I should spring on her over dinner to finally pin her down.
