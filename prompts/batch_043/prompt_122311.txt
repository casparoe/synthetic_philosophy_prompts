My book group — we're a handful of ex-programmers who read one philosophy paper a month, badly — got stuck on the Frankfurt-style cases and the principle of alternative possibilities, and I've been chewing on something ever since that I can't find a word for.

Here's the thing I keep noticing. In every one of these stories — the guy with the device in his head who'd be forced to vote a certain way if he showed any sign of wavering, and all its descendants — the blocking mechanism has to be *watching* the agent. It has to be sensitive to something about him, otherwise it wouldn't know when to step in. And that sensitivity seems to leak. Because it fires on a sign, the agent always retains some sliver: he could have decided otherwise before the sign ever appeared, or he could have tried and visibly failed, or he could have blushed, hesitated, or in some other way made the machine kick in. Whenever somebody tightens the story to squeeze the sliver out, they seem either to reintroduce it somewhere smaller or to quietly make the trigger deterministic — which is supposed to be the very thing the example stays neutral about. I've started calling this 'the leak,' and I genuinely can't tell whether it's a structural feature of the argument or just evidence that people keep writing the stories sloppily.

So I tried to build a version with no watching at all, and got three results I can't place. First, a *timer* version: nobody monitors you, it's just rigged so that if the thing isn't done by noon it gets done for you. Nothing about your decision is tracked, so there's no sliver to leak — but this doesn't feel like the same animal at all. It feels like a case where the outcome is fixed while the action stays wide open. Second, a *reasons* version: the device can't move your body, it can only edit what you want, so that if you begin to waver it puts a desire in you that gets you there anyway. That one reads to me as coercion or manipulation, not as the clean case. Third, from a friend: a person who installs the device on himself in advance because he doesn't trust himself — and there the loss of alternatives seems to make him *more* responsible, not less.

What I'm after is a map, not a label. Is there an established name for the sliver? For the pattern that the mechanism's sensitivity is what makes the sliver unavoidable — or is that just the standard objection to these cases restated in my own bad words? And is there a term for the difference between 'the outcome was guaranteed' and 'the action was guaranteed'? My timer case feels like it sits on one side of that line and the classic case on the other, and I don't know whether the distinction does any real work for the principle of alternative possibilities or whether I've just reinvented something ordinary. If nothing established quite fits, coin something and tell me where its edges are and what it doesn't cover — I've already got my own bad names, so a bare name won't help me.
