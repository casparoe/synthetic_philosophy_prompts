I've been trying to get my own thinking about food into some order for a couple of years now, ever since I ran into the argument — in a podcast and a couple of blog posts, none of which I can now find — that vegetarianism isn't quite the clean-hands position people take it for, because the animals living in and around crop fields are themselves affected by how we farm, and because different animal products require very different amounts of land per unit of food. My partner thinks I've tied myself into knots; my own worry is the opposite, namely that I hold six or seven confident reactions that no single rule fits, and that when I'm not looking I switch between rules. So here are the verdicts I'd actually give, case by case, as honestly as I can state them.

- Swapping beef for chicken, which I did about three years ago: I'm confident this was an improvement, and I'd defend it mainly on land use — much less land per gram of protein, so far fewer wild animals living on land dedicated to my dinner — even though I accept that this means many more individual domestic animals are killed per kilogram of meat. I appear to let the wild-animal effect outweigh the domestic deaths, and I can't say why it should.
- Chicken to lentils and grains: I want this to be a clear further win, and I mostly feel it is, but when I think about the animals killed in the harvest my confidence drains, and I'd now call it at best a modest win. I also can't explain why the harvest deaths shouldn't swamp the domestic ones here when I apparently let the same consideration run the other way in the beef-to-chicken case.
- Grass-fed or pasture-raised beef: I think this is worse than feedlot beef per kilo, not better, because the land footprint is larger and the pasture displaces whatever was living on it. This is the verdict my friends think is mad, and I notice that here I let land use override how good the animal's life actually was.
- Farmed prawns: I feel these are worse than chicken per gram, and the reason I give myself is simply the number of individual animals in a single portion. The feeling survives my reminding myself that each of them has a far smaller nervous system than a chicken, and even that they may not be conscious at all — I can't argue the unease away.
- Insects killed by pesticides and by harvesting: I count these as zero, or close enough to zero. This plainly sits badly with the previous item, and I know it does.
- Wild-caught fish: I feel worse about this than about farmed fish of the same species — the bycatch, and deaths that are slow rather than quick — and I also feel worse about eating one large, long-lived animal than the same total weight of small short-lived ones, which seems to pull against how I handled the prawns.
- Eggs from hens kept well and killed at the end of lay: I feel these are much better than broiler meat, close to not eating animal products at all. Note that here I let the quality of the life do real work, which is exactly what I refused to let it do for the grass-fed cow.
- Dairy: I currently treat as roughly like the eggs case, but I'm less sure of it, and I don't know whether I'm following an argument or a habit.

The case I genuinely can't give a verdict on, and keep turning over: purely on animal grounds, should I be glad or sorry that the rough pasture behind my house is being turned into housing? One part of me says fewer wild animals means less suffering; another says that if this is where the reasoning ends, I've taken it too far somewhere.

My tentative principle, which I don't trust, is: the badness of a food per unit is roughly (the number of individuals whose lives are affected by producing it, counting wild ones who live on the land involved, not just the ones we eat) × (the expected suffering or lost welfare per individual), with per-individual welfare discounted by something like nervous-system complexity, all scaled by how much land and how much time the production takes. Can you test that against my verdicts and repair it where it breaks — or replace it with something that does better? Specifically, I'd like: the principle stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case you haven't been given; the clause-by-clause explanation of which of my verdicts motivates each part; an honest account of which verdicts the best available principle cannot save, including whether any of them are probably just noise or rationalization on my part; what the principle says about the housing case, and about a second case I haven't decided — whether I should be supporting habitat-restoration and rewilding projects, which I've been inclined to like; and a note on which empirical questions (relative land use, animal densities per hectare, which taxa are conscious at all) my verdicts are hostage to, since I'd rather know now that several of them turn on numbers I haven't checked. And if you think the whole expected-suffering-arithmetic frame is the wrong thing to be doing with a grocery list, say that plainly too — but then tell me what I should be doing with these reactions instead, and which of them survive the change of frame.
