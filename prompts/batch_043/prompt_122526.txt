Hey — I'm on the program commitee for a tiny funder and we've got room for about four two-year postdocs, restricted to conceptual work on AI alignment: philosophy, decision theory, formal epistemology, that kind of thing. Assume these people can read ML papers and do math but can't run training runs of their own. We keep talking past each other, and I think it's because "the alignment problem" isn't one problem. Here's the decomposition I've been carrying around, which I half made up and half absorbed from arguments I can't now source:

One: the vanishing principal. The trainers, the deployers, the paying user, the fine-tuner and the person labelling eval data are usually different parties with partly opposed aims, so "aligned to what" may have no determinate answer even in principle — not merely an epistemic one we're too rushed to get.

Two: the delegation regress. Whatever target we hand the system was itself produced by a process (a lab, a legislature, an earlier model) that has its own alignment question, so the question recurses upward and maybe never grounds out anywhere.

Three: the training-process reading. Maybe alignment isn't a property of a model at all but of the dynamics that made it — a question about which attractors a loss landscape plus a data distribution actually selects — so two models with identical input-output behaviour could differ in how aligned they are.

Four: aims that only exist downstream. Some objectives aren't well-defined until the system is capable enough to have them, so there may be no fact now about what we'd want later, which makes "specify it correctly up front" the wrong shape of aspiration.

My own hunch is that two is overrated — it smells like the regress objections people raise about testimony and never actually worry about — and that three is the one where philosophers could say something real. But I'd rather be talked out of it than confirmed.

So: which of these is genuinely worth four postdoc-years, and which would you drop as either not a real question or not tractable for people without a compute budget? I care much less about your ordering than about the reasoning behind it. What makes one of these upstream of the others, in the sense that you'd be wasting time on the rest until it's settled? What's actually tractable with philosophy-department tools in two years versus what just feels tractable? What's neglected relative to its importance? What kind of result — a theorem, a worked toy example, a counterexample that kills a framing, a clear statement of where the disagreement bottoms out in something non-empirical — would move something up or down the list eighteen months from now? And if I've carved the space wrong, say what the real crux is instead and why it beats the four I've got. Continuous prose please, no headings or bullets or bold or tables, just write it like a long rambly email to someone you're trying to talk out of spending money badly.
