I had an argument with a friend last night that I have not managed to put down, and I would like something usable out of it.

I work in the analysis unit of a national consumer product safety regulator. Roughly thirty docket items a year come to us in the same shape: a defect or design choice that imposes a trivially small cost on a very large number of people. A firmware update that makes a smart speaker wake falsely a couple of times a week. A subscription flow that adds fifteen seconds of friction to cancelling. A low-level chemical exposure across an entire population. Our standing method is to estimate the per-person harm, multiply by the affected population, and put the resulting number next to the cost of a recall or a redesign. Because the populations are in the millions and the costs of remediation are in the hundreds of millions, the multiplication almost always wins, and we end up recommending interventions that strike the commission as absurd.

My friend, who teaches moral philosophy, said this is just the torture-versus-dust-specks problem wearing a lanyard, and that we are running one side of it while refusing to look at the other. She made three points I could not answer. First: if a harm so small that nobody ever notices it still enters the ledger, then the ledger is committed to saying that enough of them outweigh anything, including preventing a single catastrophic injury, and if we are not willing to say that, we need a principled place to stop rather than a shrug. Second: she thinks the philosophically interesting asymmetry is not between tiny harms and severe ones, but between a small harm that is certain and a large harm that is improbable — that probability times severity and certainty times triviality produce the same expected number while being, in her phrase, different kinds of reason, and that our framework's inability to mark the difference is a bug rather than a feature. Third: she proposed what she called a sharing rule, on which trivial harms aggregate within a single person's life but across persons only when they are fungible within a life — that is, only when ten specks landing on one person really are ten times as bad as one — and she insisted this is an empirical question about the shape of well-being that our agency has simply been assuming an answer to.

I need a decision procedure my unit can actually run, not a position paper. Concretely: one analyst with a master's in economics and no philosophy training, about one working day per docket item, producing a two-page memo with a recommendation; the memo is signed off by a five-member commission and has to survive an appeal by the firm, which means it cannot lean on any contested moral theory by name — I cannot write that we are recalling a product because of an aggregation doctrine a reasonable person might reject. So I want the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points where the procedure terminates with a no, and the points where it stops and hands the decision to the commission because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts.

It has to survive the following cases, which are the ones we actually get. Harms nobody perceives and nobody could report, where I cannot tell whether the harm is zero, small, or merely unmeasured. The sorites worry my friend pressed: any threshold I pick for what counts as too small to matter is arbitrary, and I need to know either how to choose one defensibly or how to operate without one. Harms that are trivial per person but which one person receives thousands of times, as opposed to one instance each across thousands of people. Severe harms carrying a small probability, where I have to decide whether the product of the two is the right object or whether the dilution changes the kind of case. Harms to people who will never learn they were affected. And harms that were cheaply avoidable by the person who suffered them, where I do not know whether avoidability discounts the harm or merely relocates the fault. There is also a standing practical constraint: often we cannot estimate the per-person harm at all within the docket window, and I need the procedure to say what to do with a bounded guess rather than pretending to a number it does not have.

Then run it on three live-shaped items. One: a firmware update causing roughly two false activations a week in about eight million households, which no user can be shown to have noticed, where the fix costs the firm around two hundred million and would pull the same engineering team off a security patch. Two: a children's product with a component that detaches in roughly one unit in forty thousand, where detachment carries perhaps a one-in-five-hundred chance of a choking incident requiring hospital treatment, across three million units sold, against a recall cost of about sixty million. Three: a cancellation-friction design affecting some forty million users, where I will tell you in advance that if the procedure aggregates the friction it will produce a total that dwarfs our flagship injury-prevention program, which averts on the order of a dozen severe injuries a year — and I want to know whether the procedure really is telling me to defund that program, or whether I have mis-specified something upstream.

Be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed as procedure, about where the procedure will misfire, and about the questions it cannot settle at all.
