I am convening a closed one-day workshop on whether it is ever literally true, and not merely useful, to ascribe beliefs to an artificial system. I want the centrepiece of the day to be a joint statement drafted in advance by the two camps that have spent the last several years talking past one another, and I am asking you to draft it: the document both parties could put their names to, with the residue of the disagreement made exact rather than smoothed over.

The two parties are as follows. On the one side, the stance-first or attributionist: a belief ascription to a system is correct when taking up the intentional stance towards it — treating it as tracking its situation and responding to reasons — is the best predictive and explanatory strategy available at any level of description, and there is no further fact of the matter as to whether it really believes; the patterns that support the stance are real patterns, and being a real pattern of that kind is all that a belief could consist in. On the other side, the architecture-first or structuralist: predictive payoff is at most defeasible evidence, because prediction can be had cheaply — from a regularity in the training distribution, from a look-up structure, from a reporting channel dissociable from the state it reports — and a state is a belief only if it was fixed as a belief by the right sort of history and occupies the right sort of place in the system's economy: counterfactually robust, integrated with the system's other states, and available to whatever downstream processing the system actually uses in acting.

Refer to positions by description rather than by author. I do not want a literature survey, and I do not want any claim attributed to a particular paper or researcher unless you are certain of it. Define any technical term you use.

What the document must contain.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims — eight to twelve of them — that both parties could sign without wincing. I will reject anything of the form both sides agree the question is difficult. At least three of the claims should be ones a reader would not guess from the labels of the two positions: for instance, something about the cases both sides concede (a thermostat; a pure look-up table; a compressed index of a human corpus, where both must say whether the system differs from the corpus itself and in what respect); something about counterfactual robustness, since the attributionist needs it too; and something recording that both sides allow internal structure to defeat an otherwise successful ascription, and that both allow predictive failure to defeat one. I would rather have eight claims I could not have written myself than twenty I could have.

Second, a disagreements table, one row per issue, with these columns: the issue; the attributionist's own formulation, worded as the attributionist would sign it; the structuralist's own formulation, likewise; whether the disagreement is verbal, and if so which genuine question the verbal one is disguising; the crux the row turns on; a case or argument both accept as bearing on that crux; and then, written down in advance, what each side predicts that case will show. Where a row is genuinely about the extension of the word belief rather than about what the system is like, say so and relocate the substance to a row that is not about words.

Third, the cruxes. These are the ones I want addressed, and I would like at least the first three treated at length, plus any you find that I have missed.

The coding-symmetry crux. Take a trained system, and consider two rival mappings from its internal states onto candidate contents, each of which supports exactly the same predictions across the whole range of situations at issue, but which disagree about what the system believes. Is this a genuine indeterminacy that the intentional stance cannot resolve, or is one of the two mappings simply not an intentional interpretation of the system at all? The attributionist's answer must engage with the possibility that the stance's own resources — charity, unity of the agent, the interests we impute — do the disambiguating work; the structuralist's must engage with the possibility that they are being asked to draw a distinction that makes no predictive difference anywhere, and is therefore idle.

The restricted-niche crux. Suppose a system behaves as the ascription predicts across a circumscribed range of situations, and fails outside it in a way that is predictable from the statistics of its training corpus but not from the content ascribed to it. Is the correct response to qualify the ascription — believes that p, relative to that range — or to withdraw it? And if we qualify it, is range-indexed belief belief? I expect this to be where the real work is: each side should say in advance which way it expects the practice to go, and whether it thinks the other side is quietly describing the training distribution in intentional vocabulary, or quietly smuggling in a structural requirement.

The report–state dissociability crux. Suppose an intervention on the system that leaves its task behaviour intact while changing what it says about its own states, or the reverse. Both sides must state in advance what such an intervention would show, and must say whether a system's verbal self-report has any evidential role not shared by any other piece of its behaviour, given that its training corpus consists largely of human avowals of mental states.

The adversarial crux. Consider a system trained against human opponents, which must model its interlocutor to do well. Do its second-order ascriptions — the system believes that you believe that p — stand on the same footing as first-order ones, and does competitive success count as stronger or weaker evidence of belief than cooperative success? Both sides should commit.

Fourth, a short closing section: for each party, the single finding that would lead it to abandon its own formulation, and not merely concede a case; whether there is anything at all that both agree would settle the matter rather than merely move one side; and what the collaboration should be judged to have achieved if, as I expect, neither party converts.

Format it as the numbered agreed-claims section, then the table, then the crux-by-crux discussion, then the closing section; roughly fifteen hundred to two thousand words. Address the document to the workshop rather than to me. British English throughout, and no citations or footnotes.
