I am a graduate researcher on a project that maintains a comparative register of starvation episodes, and every entry has to carry a verdict on the one question the register exists to answer: whether the episode was a famine in the technical sense, a collapse of entitlements for which some agent or arrangement bears political responsibility, or a natural disaster in the ordinary sense, something that happened to a population rather than something a political order did to it. The verdicts are not mine. They are made by the project's director, a development economist working in the entitlement tradition associated with Sen; I research the episodes, draft the entries, and then defend or attack her classifications in our weekly meetings. Her rulings on the seven episodes we have finished are these: Bengal in 1943, where the aggregate food supply per head was close to the preceding five-year average, prices were driven up by war-related expenditure and panic purchasing, exports continued, and the mortality fell on landless labourers, fishers and artisans rather than on cultivators with land — famine, not a natural disaster; Ireland in the late 1840s, where the blight was a genuine natural shock but grain and livestock went on leaving the country under guard, relief was tied to workhouses and road work, and evictions continued — famine, on the note that a natural trigger does not make the outcome natural; the Great Leap Forward, where output was systematically over-reported upwards, procurement was assessed against the inflated figures, and net exports continued — famine, and in her notes the clearest case in the set; a wartime siege of an encircled city where no food was to be had at any price and mortality fell across classes roughly in proportion to their pre-war consumption — declined for the register entirely, on the ground that no entitlement arrangement could have fed the city, and filed instead under deliberate starvation; a Sahelian drought in which the harvest failed and pastoralists' terms of trade against grain collapsed to a fraction of their previous level while a relief programme reached the towns first — famine, with the note that the natural trigger operated through a collapse of exchange entitlement; a highland province where the harvest genuinely failed and the state did nothing at all, no procurement and no relief, for eleven months, in a province that had voted against the governing party — famine, on the note that omission is as much a political act as procurement; and a small island whose staple was destroyed by a crop disease across the whole food system, with port capacity to import only a fraction of what was needed and a metropolitan power that shipped what it could — a natural disaster, not a famine, despite the deaths. The only thing she has said about episode eight so far is that it is not like the island and not like the siege, which struck me as true and unhelpful.

Episode eight is the Mara Delta, in the federal state of Vandara, 2031 to 2033. Two consecutive floods failed, because upstream dams in a neighbouring country held back the water and the regional drought deepened, and with them went the recession agriculture and the pasture on which the delta's pastoral and fishing households depend; the irrigated plot-holders along the main channel were largely unaffected. The national grain balance stayed in small surplus throughout, a northern province had a record harvest and falling prices, and the country continued to export a coarse grain that the delta does not eat; in the delta towns food was on sale the whole time at prices that roughly tripled. The mortality was concentrated among pastoral households, fishing households, and the migrant labourers who harvest the delta crop, in that order. The state extracted nothing from the delta: no procurement, no export, no eviction. Three months before the emergency declaration, however, it removed a transport-fuel subsidy under a creditor-backed fiscal programme, which raised the cost of hauling grain into the delta substantially; the public works it opened paid a wage set at about two-thirds of the pre-crisis casual rate, and were sited some forty kilometres from the worst-affected settlements, so take-up was low even where the works existed. Capacity was genuinely thin: reserves were nearly exhausted, debt service absorbed a large share of revenue, and the finance ministry's own note said that an adequate programme would have required suspending debt service, which the central bank predicted would collapse the currency and push urban food prices up by more than the delta deaths could be said to justify; donor grain arrived in month nine. On the political side, delta residents are voting citizens, the region is administered by a centrally appointed commissioner with no elected local council, an independent newspaper reported the mortality in month five, parliament debated it in month six, and the programme was expanded in month seven; roughly a third of the dead were seasonal migrants from across the border, with no papers and therefore formally ineligible for the works, although they had harvested in the delta for years.

Before I draft the entry I want the argument worked out, and I do not want citations or further reading, since I can find the literature myself. First: what do you expect her verdict to be, and what rule do you take her to be running, stated sharply enough that it reproduces all seven earlier verdicts? Second: give me at least two other rules that fit those seven verdicts at least as well as the one you prefer, and say what each of them predicts about the Mara Delta, since I suspect the earlier set is jointly consistent with a mechanism-based rule about how starvation is transmitted, a culpability-based rule about what agents chose or failed to choose, and something like an inclusion rule about whether the starving group had the political standing to compel a response, and that these three come apart here. Third: tell me which verdict the register should adopt if the object is consistency with its own precedents, which earlier episode is the closest analogue and why, what principle we would be committing ourselves to whichever way we go, and how much of the delta file the verdict actually rests on. Fourth: name the single further finding about the Mara Delta, or the single further hypothetical episode, that would best tell the leading rules apart, and say what result it would give under each.
