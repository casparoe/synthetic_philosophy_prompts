I spent most of my working life as an X-ray crystallographer, and since retiring I have been attending a small reading group of retired people in town that works slowly through books on moral philosophy, mostly for the argument. We recently spent two sessions on blame, and a disagreement I keep having with a friend who used to sit as a lay magistrate has convinced me that I have noticed something for which I have no word. The familiar case is easy enough: someone censures another for a fault she possesses herself, and we feel the censure is somehow out of order even when the judgement it expresses is perfectly true. But the case I keep running into is not that one. It is the case where the person doing the censuring has not yet done the thing, and may even sincerely hate it in herself, but fully intends to do it, or is reliably going to do it, later on. My neighbour is a lifelong smoker who has relapsed four times; she believes, with complete conviction, that smoking is a stupid, degrading habit, and she told her grandson so, at some length, in a way that struck everyone present as slightly off — partly because she smoked two cigarettes on the way home. The odd thing is that it does not seem to be her past that makes it off. A colleague of mine who gave up smoking thirty years ago can say exactly the same sentences and sounds exemplary. What seems to poison my neighbour's version is that she is going to do it again, and everyone in the room knows it, and she knows it too. I find the same structure elsewhere: a senior scientist who publicly rebukes a junior for quietly trimming an inconvenient data set while his own manuscript, not yet submitted, contains a procedure he privately intends to defend as different but which he would recognise as the same if it appeared in someone else's paper; a councillor who denounces a tax avoidance scheme in a speech in April while her accountant has already set the identical arrangement up for the following January. In none of these is the person insincere, and in none of them has the past wrong actually been committed yet.

What I want to know is whether this has a name and, more importantly, whether it is really one thing. I have tried to line it up against the neighbouring cases and it does not sit comfortably with any of them. Ordinary hypocrisy, as I understand it, seems to require that the person already has the fault, or at least that she quietly exempts herself from a rule she applies to others; my neighbour does not exempt herself, she convicts herself, and she is still going to do it. And this is clearly not the debating trick of deflecting criticism by pointing at the other side's comparable behaviour, where nothing about the speaker's own conduct is at issue at all. There is also a case that goes the other way and puzzles me: the person who never did the thing and never will, but only because the occasion never arose, and who would very likely have done it had it arisen. Nobody says of her that her censure is out of order, and yet in the historical cases my reading group likes — ordinary people under a dictatorship, people who profited from arrangements nobody around them questioned — the thought that we would have done the same seems to unsettle blame quite seriously, even though by hypothesis the person being judged did it and we did not. So: is the defect in my cases a temporal version of the ordinary one, so that past, future and counterfactual transgressors should all be treated alike, or do they come apart, and if they come apart, what does that tell us about what is actually wrong with the censure in the first place?

Please don't send me to the literature — I no longer have university access, and a list of papers or authors would be of no use to me. What I want is the carving, not the label: whether these cases are one phenomenon or several, where the boundaries fall, and what the underlying norm would have to be for the boundaries to fall where they do. In particular I would like to know whether the defect varies with how likely the future transgression is, or with whether the speaker knows it is coming; whether it survives if the speaker is self-deceived about her own future and would be shocked by the suggestion; whether it matters if what she is going to do is worse than, or less bad than, what she is condemning; and whether the same defect attaches to neighbouring speech acts — warning someone off the fault, praising someone for avoiding it, grading it in an official capacity, voting to punish it — or whether it is peculiar to blame. I would also like to know how far it travels from persons to bodies: a university that expels students for plagiarism while quietly planning not to notice it in a newly hired star, or a government that condemns a weapons programme it is itself about to start, seems to me to raise the question in a sharper form, since a university has no intentions of its own in the way my neighbour does. If there really is an established concept here, tell me what range of cases it is used to cover and whether mine fall inside or outside it; if there is not, give me a name and a definition I could use, and say what the definition is meant to exclude.
