I'm teaching an upper-level seminar on moral status and I want to close the unit on graded versus threshold views of sentience with Kafka's *Metamorphosis*, which half my students have read before and all of them think they understand. I'd like a reading that treats the novella as a genuine interlocutor in that debate and not as a decorated version of a point the readings already make.

The framework the seminar has been using treats moral status as admitting of degree along three separable dials. Call them *affective bandwidth* — the range and resolution of valenced states available to a creature at a time, which is what separates a thermostat, with its setpoint and nothing it is like to deviate from it, from anything that can be said to fare badly; *diachronic integration* — whether a creature's states at different times belong to one continuing subject with concerns that persist, so that harms can accumulate into a damaged life rather than occurring as a scatter of unowned episodes; and *reflexive uptake* — whether the creature represents and cares about its own states, so that how it is treated can register as humiliation, betrayal, or insult and not merely as damage. Alongside these I've had them test a relational proposal, which I more or less made up for the occasion and now want held to the fire: that some share of what we call a being's status is not fixed by its intrinsic psychology at all but is sustained by relations of mutual address, and can lapse when those relations lapse, without anything internal changing.

So here is what I want. Trace the arc of Gregor's treatment against those dials and tell me where the decline in how he is treated tracks an actual decline along one of them, and where it tracks only a change in how his family sees him — the office manager at the door, the furniture being cleared from his room, the apple, the violin, the lodgers, the cleaning woman, the tram ride out of the city at the end. Is Kafka writing about a being whose status falls, a being whose status is withdrawn, or both, and what in the text would let us tell the two apart? Attend to the nasty possibility that several of the capacities whose loss seems to justify the downgrade — his movement, his appetite, his responsiveness, eventually his simply lying still under the sofa — were destroyed by the treatment itself, so that the story shows status being withdrawn on grounds the withdrawal produced. Then say where on the scale the ending actually leaves him: closer to the insect, or closer to the thermostat, and whether that is a verdict the novella endorses or one it wants us to flinch from.

Two constraints on the answer. Be honest about where the reading breaks: Gregor is a human mind in a verminous body, and the story is arguably silent about real insects, so it may be useless on exactly the cases the syllabus cares about, and I'd rather hear that than a false payoff. And don't let it dissolve into a general meditation on dehumanization — I want the three dials, the relational proposal, and the question of which of them the story vindicates, complicates, or simply doesn't reach.
