I write long-form pieces on science and ideas, and the one I am finishing now is about the claim that violence has declined. I do not want a he-said-she-said piece; I want to put numbers on the page. So I am asking for your credences, decomposed, with the weak points of each one labeled as such.

Start from a stipulated quantity, which I will call the interpersonal violence rate, or IVR: for a given year, the share of all living human beings who over the course of that year are deliberately killed or physically injured by another human being. Count acts by state agents — soldiers, police, prison guards — on the same footing as acts by private individuals. Count omissions only where the omitter was under an acknowledged duty to act, so a guard who starves a prisoner counts and a distant government that does not relieve a famine does not. Count each person once regardless of how many incidents befall them, and do not weight injuries by severity. Assume we are asking about the true value of this quantity rather than what our sources can establish: put measurement error in a term of its own instead of folding it into the estimate.

Give me your credence, with a decomposition, on each of the following:

1. IVR was lower in the period 1990 to 2020 than in the period 1750 to 1800.
2. IVR was lower in 1990 to 2020 than in any earlier century for which we have evidence of any kind, going back to the beginning of settled agriculture.
3. There is a fact of the matter about whether violence has declined, over and above the choice of some particular metric like the one above — that is, one candidate metric is the correct one, rather than the question dissolving into a decision about what to count.

For each: name the sub-questions your number depends on, attach a probability to each, say how they combine, then flag the term you are least confident in and the evidence or argument that would move it most. I want the arithmetic visible, because I intend to quote it.

There are four complications I want you to fight with rather than define away, since each of them is where I suspect the real disagreement is hiding:

The denominator. Per capita normalization quietly assumes that the right baseline is a person-year. But the number of people within reach of any given person — potential assailants and potential victims both — has grown by orders of magnitude with sedentism, density, and transport. If the quantity that tracks how violent a life actually is is violence per social encounter, or per person within one's settlement, rather than per person-year, the trend may look very different even holding the underlying behavior fixed. Tell me how much of your number is conditional on person-years being the right denominator, and what your number becomes under the alternatives.

The mean and the tail. Suppose the mean falls while the upper tail thickens, so that a rising share of a century's violence is concentrated in its single largest episode. If what we ought to care about is catastrophic risk rather than expected burden, then a downward trend in the average may answer a question nobody had reason to ask. Say how much this consideration moves each of your three numbers, and whether it moves them in different directions.

Displacement rather than reduction. A great deal of the pacification of everyday life over the last few centuries was produced by policing, incarceration, border control, and the credible threat of state force. The question is whether violence was prevented or relocated into forms my metric does not register. Give me a probability that a substantial fraction of the measured decline is displacement, and say what that does to numbers 1 and 2.

The asymmetry of evidence erosion. Older periods are known chiefly from skeletal remains, fortifications, and the records of literate states; both bodies and documents decay, and the societies that left durable archives are disproportionately the ones with armies and scribes. Tell me which direction you think this cuts — toward inflating past violence or understating it — and how much weight you give it. One researcher I spoke with took it as obvious that this inflates the apparent decline; another took it as obvious that it understates it, on the grounds that the archaeological record only catches violence that leaves a mark on bone. I would like you to arbitrate rather than split the difference.

Finally, the piece has two recurring interviewees. One, a quantitative political scientist, puts roughly 0.9 on question 1 and says that anyone who denies the decline is confusing the history of reporting with the history of killing. The other, a scholar of state formation, puts roughly 0.55 on the same question and thinks the whole enterprise smuggles in a definition of violence tailored to flatter the present. On the strength of your decomposition, which sub-question do you think their disagreement actually lives in, and which of them, if either, is arguing about something other than what they take themselves to be arguing about?

Please write in American English, in prose I can quote directly. Give each number in a sentence or two with its justification immediately following it, rather than in a table. And please do not supply citations, references, or suggestions for further reading: I have my own sources, and what I want from you is the reasoning, not the footnotes.
