Below are working notes (really more like a sketch than a paper) for a short piece on Sen's liberal paradox — the Lewd/Prude thing with the book. I'd like comments on the argument, not on the prose. Please don't pad the reply with references or tell me who to read; I can do that myself later. What I want is someone to actually check the reasoning and tell me where it breaks.

Setup. There are n agents. An alternative is a tuple a = (a_1, ..., a_n), where a_i is agent i's "personal component" (what i does / what happens in i's own sphere). Say i's preference ordering is own-separable (OS) at a profile iff i's ranking of any two alternatives depends only on their i-th components: formally, whenever x_i = x'_i and y_i = y'_i, we have x R_i y iff x' R_i y'. Call a preference medlesome to the extent that it isn't like this. Individual preferences are weak orders. Three conditions on a social strict preference P:

(Par) If everyone weakly prefers x to y and someone strictly does, then x P y.
(PL) (positive liberalism) For each i there is a pair differing only in i's component such that x P_i y implies x P y, at every profile. This is Sen's minimal liberalism.
(VL) (veto liberalism) For each i there is a pair differing only in i's component such that x P_i y implies not (y P x), at every profile. A right as shield, not sword.

Result 1 (the veto reading is costless, and that's suspicious). Take the Pareto rule: x P y iff everyone weakly prefers x to y and at least one strictly; then set x R y iff not(y P x). Over the unrestricted domain this satisfies Par and VL. Its P is just the Pareto partial order, which is transitive when everyone's R_i is: if x P y P z then everyone has x R_i y R_i z and hence x R_i z, and anyone strict on x over y is strict on x over z (if z R_i x as well, transitivity gives y R_i x, contradicting x P_i y). So no cycle, no empty choice set. Notice also that the veto never binds against unanimity: b P a requires everyone to weakly prefer b, which already rules out the sphere-holder strictly preferring a. So under VL a right can only block rankings produced by non-Paretian rules (majority rule, a dictator, whatever) — it can never produce a social strict preference. It's a shield against aggregation procedures, not a sword against what everyone else wants. Sen's cycle needs the sword.

The cost: the induced R isn't transitive, because indifference isn't. Example: agents A and B with A: z > x > y, B: y > z > x. Then P = {z P x} only; x and y are socially indifferent, y and z are socially indifferent, but z is strictly above x. So I can pick either of x and y when only those two are on the menu, but once z joins the menu x drops out while y stays. That looks like a real menu-dependence cost of the escape.

Result 2 (the paradox is a phenomenon of other-regarding preferences). Restrict the domain to profiles where everyone is OS and let the alternative set be the full product of the components, with each i's protected pairs being exactly those differing only in a_i. Then the Pareto rule satisfies even PL, not just VL: if x and y differ only in a_i, every other agent is indifferent between them (their own component is unchanged), so Par delivers x P y exactly when i strictly prefers x_i to y_i. Social P is then just componentwise dominance relative to each agent's own ranking — a partial order. So on the product domain, PL + Par is consistent precisely when preferences are own-separable, and the witness is the boring Pareto rule.

The Lady Chatterley case illustrates it. Take the four alternatives x = Lewd reads, y = Prude reads, w = both read, z = neither. In the original three-alternative version, Lewd has y > x > z and Prude has z > y > x, and the cycle is x P z (Lewd's sphere), z P y (Prude's sphere), y P x (Par). Now complete the domain and ask for OS preferences that agree with those sphere rankings on the sphere pairs. Lewd's sphere pairs are (x, z) — his own component flips, Prude's is 0 in both — so Lewd prefers his own component = 1; and (w, y), where Prude's component is 1 in both. If Lewd is OS, he must then prefer w to y. Prude's sphere pairs are (y, z) and (x, w); from z > y with Lewd's component 0 in both, Prude prefers his own component = 0, so if he is OS he must prefer x to w. Now run Par on the full domain: Lewd prefers x to y (his component 1 vs 0) and Prude prefers x to y (his component 0 vs 1), so we get x P y, not y P x. The Pareto step that drove the original cycle reverses, and the social ranking x > z > y, x > w > y is acyclic and honours both rights. The cycle needed preferences that are context-dependent across someone else's component.

So the draft's thesis: the impossibility isn't about rights versus efficiency. It needs two ingredients together — rights strong enough to override unanimity, and preferences that reach across spheres. Drop either and the plain Pareto rule is a witness; keep both and you get the cycle. The interesting question is then which of the two we should give up, and what the intermediate positions look like (rights that override unanimity only when the opposing preferences are themselves medlesome; rights that are trumped by large or intense coalitions; and so on).

One worry I can't settle: an escape route that discounts non-separable preferences has to classify them, and whether my right is honoured then depends on the shape of my whole ordering — which I can misrepresent. So the fix may buy consistency at the price of a new kind of manipulability. I'm not sure the charge lands.
