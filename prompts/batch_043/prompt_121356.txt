I spent my career in molecular biology and have been reading philosophy in retirement, mostly in the neighbourhood of self-reference and mechanism. I have run into a pattern I cannot put a name to, and I would like help both with the label and — more importantly — with the carving: what exactly the thing is, what it is not, and where its edges lie. A bare name would not settle anything for me.

The pattern, in the cases where I first noticed it.

(i) A well-known book of the late 1970s (Hofstadter's, I believe; a friend pressed it on me) invents a toy chemistry in which strands of letters are translated, by a fixed table, into enzymes, and those enzymes in turn operate on strands — cutting, copying, moving them about — so that a suitably arranged strand gets itself copied by the very enzymes it specifies. The author offers this as a picture of both DNA replication and the construction of a sentence in a formal system that effectively talks about itself.

(ii) A self-printing computer program, whose output is its own source text.

(iii) An actual cell, where the polymerases and the translation apparatus that read and copy the nucleic acids are themselves specified by nucleic acids.

(iv) The sentence, in a formal system of arithmetic, that somehow contrives to be about its own unprovability.

What struck me is that in none of these is the "self" the whole story. Beside the thing that gets copied there is always a second thing that does the copying or the reading — the enzyme set, whatever executes the program, the cell's machinery, the clause that fixes what the formal sentences mean — and that second thing is simply assumed. It is not produced by the process I am watching. So self-replication, as far as I can see, always means replication *relative to* a background taken as given.

The part I really cannot place is that the background sits at different depths in different cases and seems to be movable. The cell, unlike the toy chemistry, does encode its own reading machinery; I gather a self-printing program can be written so as to swallow more and more of the interpreter that runs it. But something is always left over: in the toy chemistry the translation table; in the cell, the chemistry and the assignment of triplets to amino acids; in the formal case, whatever makes the sentences mean anything at all. (I am honestly unsure how to count the middle one: the cell does manufacture the enzymes that enforce the code, so perhaps the code is internalised after all, in which case my example is badly chosen.) And whenever I try to imagine the last leftover being internalised too — a system that supplies its own interpretation, with nothing standing outside it doing the reading — I seem to run straight into the Liar and its relatives.

So: is there an established name for the dependence I have described — replication relative to an un-replicated reader, where the reader can be pulled inside but perhaps only so far? One name for both halves, or two? Which of the near-misses actually fits? It is plainly not just "self-reference"; I have been told to look near the distinction between using an expression and mentioning it, near the distinction between a program's text and its execution, near the idea of a system that produces its own components, and near the looping hierarchies that the author of the toy chemistry is fond of — but each seems to me to catch only part of it, and I cannot say which is the real one. Finally, is the "only so far" a theorem, or just an intuition dragged over from the paradoxes? I can see why a system could not be the entire source of its own semantics; I am much less sure about the merely mechanical case, and I cannot tell whether these are the same fact.

Please commit rather than survey: tell me which concept or concepts genuinely cover this, which candidates fail and why, and whether there is a hard floor here or only a practical one — with reasons. If the honest answer is that no established term exists, then coin one, define it, and mark the boundary cases; that would be just as useful. No more than 600 words.
