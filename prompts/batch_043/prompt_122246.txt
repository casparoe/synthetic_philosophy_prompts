I'm spending this summer working through formal epistemology on my own — I'm a philosophy graduate student with a decision-theory-adjacent dissertation topic and no more formal training than one logic sequence — and I've built an argument against imprecise credences that I'm fairly confident proves too much. The motivating cases seem to me real: an urn known only to contain a hundred balls, each black or white, in some proportion, from which no sample has been drawn; two meteorologists who respectively announce a 30 and an 80 per cent chance of rain tomorrow when I have no reason to trust either; an Ellsberg-style urn with thirty red balls and sixty others that are black or yellow in an unknown proportion. In each case it feels wrong to write down a single sharp number — to announce 48 per cent for black on the first draw, or 55 per cent for rain — because the number would encode a discrimination my evidence does not support. So I started out sympathetic to representing my state by a non-singleton set of probability functions, and then I talked myself out of it:

(1) Some bodies of evidence are unspecific rather than merely inconclusive: they fail to single out any one probability function as the one to have, and in such cases rationality requires an imprecise state, represented by a set of functions, with my confidence in a proposition given by the spread of values the set assigns it.
(2) The members of such a set are never all on a par. In the hundred-ball urn I do not take all black or all white as seriously as roughly even compositions; I would be more surprised by ninety-seven black than by forty-eight. So I have graded comparative attitudes toward the members of my own set — a second-order ranking of the first-order states I have not ruled out.
(3) It is irrational to hold first-order attitudes while ignoring your own second-order assessment of which first-order states are more likely to be correct. That is a kind of self-fragmentation, and it makes you exploitable: you will post betting prices that you, by your own second-order lights, expect to lose money at.
(4) Where you have graded second-order confidence over a set of hypotheses about what the correct first-order state is, rationality requires your first-order attitudes to be the weighted mixture of the members of that set, with the weights given by your second-order confidence.
(5) A weighted mixture of probability functions is a single sharp probability function.
(6) So my effective first-order credences are sharp. The interval was never a feature of my doxastic state; it was an artifact of writing down the set and leaving the weights out of the description.
(7) And the argument generalizes: any credal set whose members I can rank collapses into its mixture, and if I cannot rank them at all I violate (2) and am incoherent anyway. So imprecise credences are never rationally permissible — they are always either sharpness in disguise or incoherence.
(8) But (7) would mean that an entire research programme is refutable in six lines by someone with one logic sequence behind him, that the three cases I opened with have to be handled entirely inside sharp Bayesianism, and that the distinction I was reaching for between evidence that is merely inconclusive and evidence that is unspecific is not a real distinction at all. I do not believe that.

Please tell me which step to reject and what rejecting it costs. I keep going back and forth between (2) and (4): it may be that the move from a rough comparative ranking to anything sharp enough to be averaged is where the slide happens, or that (4) conflates two different relations a higher-order attitude can bear to a first-order one. I have also been told that (5) only lands back inside the set if the set is convex and that the convexity requirement is itself contested, but I cannot tell whether that is the load-bearing point or a side issue; and I have seen the term dilation used for a nearby worry about intervals widening when you condition, which I take to be a different complaint, though I would like to be told if it is not. If I have to choose, I would rather keep (1) than (3) — I would rather give up some demand of global coherence than go back to announcing 55 per cent for rain — but I want to know exactly what I am paying for that, what the sharp Bayesian who models unspecific evidence with a hierarchy of priors has to say about my three cases, and whether there is an established way of drawing the distinction the argument runs together. A bare label for the mistake will not help me; I want the step identified, an explanation of why it looked innocent, and an honest price for each of the alternatives.
