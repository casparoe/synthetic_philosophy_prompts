I'm a high-school senior writing an independent research paper on the question of where meaning is located, and I've been working through the part of *Gödel, Escher, Bach* where Hofstadter separates a message into a frame message, an outer message, and an inner message. Since I'm working largely on my own, I've been checking my understanding against a set of online study notes for the book. The relevant paragraph runs as follows:

"Every message has three layers. The frame message is the message that tells you that you are looking at a message at all, and it is carried by features of the object itself — the regular shape of a strange thing washed up on a beach, say. The outer message tells you which code the message is written in, so it is the key that unlocks everything else; in the case of DNA, the outer message is the genetic code. The inner message is the actual content, and it is the only layer where meaning is really located: the same inner message, the same piece of music, can ride on a vinyl record, a CD, or a player-piano roll, which shows that meaning does not depend on the medium. Hofstadter's own discussion, however, turns against him. Whether an object has a frame message in the first place depends on who finds it — a crab would not take the object on the beach to be a message at all. But if the frame message is in the eye of the beholder, then everything built on top of it is too, and meaning turns out to be subjective after all; all Hofstadter can really claim is that meaning is intrinsic for creatures like us, which is subjectivism with extra steps."

I would be grateful for an audit of this. Which sentences would someone who knows Hofstadter's account well accept as stated, which are fair simplifications of something he actually argues, and which would he reject? In particular, I want to know whether the conclusion in the final two sentences really follows from the parts of the summary that are correct, or whether it depends on something the notes have quietly changed — my instinct is that the summary has made the frame message sound like a property sitting in the object, where Hofstadter seems to be saying something more complicated, but I can't get from that instinct to a clear account of where the argument goes wrong.

The reason I care is an artifact I built for the paper. It is a strip of card printed with a row of letters and a dotted line along one edge; the intended procedure is to wrap it around a cylinder of the right diameter so that letters from successive turns line up and spell something out, though the card never says what diameter to use. Wrapped around the correct cylinder it reads WRAP ME AROUND A CYLINDER — an instruction you can only have already followed in order to read it — and around any other cylinder it produces gibberish. My teacher says this is simply a degenerate case that the three-level scheme predicts: an object with a frame message and an outer message but effectively no inner message. I think it shows the scheme is missing something. Complicating this, I later found by accident that a second, larger cylinder also yields grammatical English, though a different sentence, and I can't tell whether that means one object carries two inner messages or that one of my two readings is a decoding mistake that merely looks like success.

So, beyond the audit of the notes: on the corrected version of Hofstadter's distinction, is my teacher right about the artifact, is my instinct right, or is neither quite right — and does the answer to that change once the second, accidental reading is taken into account?
