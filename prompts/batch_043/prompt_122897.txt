I am commissioning one document for a volume on the metaphysics of emotion, and I want it written as the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration: the paper that a judgment theorist and a feeling theorist could both put their names to after arguing in good faith for a term.

The two signatories hold the following, in their own words, which you should not paraphrase into their opponent's vocabulary. Party A is a judgment (appraisal-cognitivist) theorist: an emotion is, or is essentially constituted by, an evaluative judgment or belief-like state — to fear is at least to take one's situation to be dangerous, and what makes an episode the emotion it is, is what it takes its object to be. Party B is a feeling (somatic, affect-program) theorist: an emotion is a valenced bodily or affective state of a kind shared with non-human animals and with infants, whose intentional directedness, if it has any, is inherited from the eliciting conditions and the organism's history rather than from conceptual content, and whose phenomenology is not the accompaniment of a judgment but the state itself.

Requirements for the document.

1. A numbered section of agreed claims. Each must be substantive enough that a hostile reader could deny it, but slanted toward neither side. Tag every claim with which of three questions it answers: the constituency question (what an emotion is made of), the correctness question (in virtue of what an emotion is fitting or unfitting), and the individuation question (what makes fear fear rather than anger or anxiety). Claims that answer more than one question should say so explicitly.

2. A disagreements table. Each disagreement is stated twice: once in the judgment theorist's own formulation, once in the feeling theorist's, and neither statement may be a strawman — a check is that each theorist would accept their own row as what they believe. Add a third column, for a non-signatory: where an attitudinal-perceptual theorist (emotions as perceptions or construals of evaluative properties) would place themselves on the same row, and whether they would side with A, with B, or with neither.

3. A verbal-disputes subsection. Identify any row on which the disagreement is about the words judgment, representation, content, or belief rather than about the emotions, and say exactly what question survives once the labels are replaced by neutral ones.

4. A cruxes table. A crux is a sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side. For each crux give: a case, argument, or test both sides accept as bearing on it; the judgment theorist's prediction about that case, written down in advance; the feeling theorist's prediction; and a note on how the losing side would argue that the case was not decisive after all. Include at least these four cruxes.

   a. Recalcitrance. A subject sincerely judges the glass elevator safe and continues to fear it. Both accept this happens. Is it one state with an evaluative content set by something other than judgment, or two states in a divided mind?

   b. Affective decoupling. Stipulate, without appeal to any real experiment or clinical syndrome, an intervention that reliably produces a full fear episode in an adult — phenomenology, autonomic profile, action tendencies, characteristic behaviour — while the subject forms no evaluative judgment, holds no belief that they are in danger, and has no impairment of their general capacity to judge. Both sides must say what they would take this to show, and each must record whether they regard the stipulation as even coherent on their own view; a verdict of incoherent is itself a prediction and should be entered as one.

   c. Calibrated conviction without affect. A subject judges the situation dangerous, reasons well from that judgment, acts on it, reports no feelings of any kind, and is naturally described as fearless rather than afraid. Does this subject have an emotion?

   d. The argument from rational review. Emotions are assessable for fittingness and revisable by reflection; only states with propositional structure are assessable and revisable in that way; feelings lack propositional structure; therefore emotions are not feelings. Both sides must say whether the argument is sound, which premise fails if it is not, why that premise looked secure, and what the argument does establish even granting the failure.

5. Working constraints. Define every contested term before first use. Where a thesis has a strong and a weak reading, say which reading is being signed. Do not cite, allude to, or lean on actual experimental findings, clinical cases, or published arguments by name: the document should stand on conceptual analysis and on stipulation alone, so that a reader with no library can check every step. No signatory is asked to concede their central thesis; if you conclude that some item admits no jointly signable formulation, enter that as a finding and explain why.

6. Format. Section I, numbered agreed claims with question-tags. Section II, the disagreements table with its third column. Section III, the verbal-disputes subsection. Section IV, the cruxes table. Section V, a short closing note on what each side counts as evidence against its own view, and whether there is anything both agree would actually move the debate. Aim for roughly 1,200 words; precision matters more than coverage.
