I've just come home from a long, slightly heated argument with my friend Theo about socially constructed kinds, and I'd like to settle it in a different register than the one we were stuck in, because we ended up just repeating ourselves. Theo pressed a dilemma on me and I couldn't answer it: either a kind like race, or money, or a hereditary caste, is real in the sense that its members share something that would be there anyway, independent of what anyone believes, in which case it earns its keep in explanation and prediction but we cannot really abolish it — we can only stop noticing it; or it is constructed, in which case it is optional and pointing that out should be enough to dismantle it. When I said that money and promises are both constructed and perfectly real, he said that was exactly his point: nobody has ever abolished money by proving it was a convention, and no critique of a category has ever been able to avoid using the category. What I want is a story that makes a third option vivid rather than argued for, and I have a fairly specific machinery in mind for it.

Set it on the island of Talvern, in the harbour town of Saltmarch, some time in the first half of the twentieth century, and keep it realist: no allegory, no future, no last-page essay in which a character explains the philosophy. For two centuries the town has sorted a part of its population into a category called the Tide-marked, registered at birth by parentage, put to cold-water salvage and diving work from the age of eight, and legally confirmed at twelve by examiners who read a pale ring in the iris that is said to run in families. The sign is real, it is reliably readable, and the examiners are honest. The visiting inspector from the mainland — she has been sent to replace their judgement with a laboratory test — should discover that the ring is in fact produced by the brine ration and the years of cold-water work, so that the test she has brought will confirm every single entry on the register, and her evidence can therefore not discriminate between a natural kind and a kind that manufactures its own diagnostic mark. Give me the scene where she understands this and the scene where she decides what to do with it.

Then carry it through to the reform, which is where I want the argument actually to land. Abolishing the category turns out to require naming it: the restitution office needs a roll, the divers need to be found to be pensioned, the families who lose the salvage income need to claim under some description, and the mainland officials need a cut somewhere. The consequence I want shown rather than stated is that the category re-forms under a new name a few years later, not through anyone's bad faith or backsliding — everyone involved is trying to do the right thing — but because the mechanism is a loop of higher-order expectations: a person is Tide-marked if the register says so, the register says so because everyone expects everyone else to treat it as saying so, and any instrument built to dismantle it has to be made out of it. The ending should be genuinely ambiguous, in that real things have changed — the eight-year-olds are not in the water, the pensions are paid, the ring is fading — and yet something with the same structure is back, and it should not be clear to the reader whether to count that as failure. If the story works, someone who has thought about social construction should recognize in it the move that construction is not the discovery that a kind is unreal, and someone who hasn't should just be left with an uncomfortable and well-made story. Around 1,200 to 1,800 words.
