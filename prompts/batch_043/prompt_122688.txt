I'm on the editorial board of my school's literary magazine, and our winter issue has the theme "both/and." So far the theme has been wasted: almost everything we've been sent is really about ambivalence — loving and hating the same person, wanting to leave and wanting to stay — which is just two attitudes, or one thing true at one time and another thing true at another. Meanwhile I've been sitting in on a philosophy reading group that spent a couple of weeks on dialetheism and paraconsistent logic, and the two things keep colliding in my head. I want one poem in the issue that is actually contradictory, and I don't want it to be a trick of wording.

My idea is a ghazal, though I'm open to a better form if you have one: five to eight couplets, each meant to stand on its own rather than build on the one before, all sharing a rhyme and closing on the same refrain, with a signature couplet at the end. The form seems to do some of the philosophical work for me. What makes a logic paraconsistent is that a contradiction in it doesn't entail everything — it stays where it is instead of spreading — and a ghazal's couplets don't accumulate into an argument either, so a contradiction that shows up in one of them shouldn't leak into the others. The subject I keep coming back to is the last afternoon of the school year: the point at which a leaving student both is and is not a student of the school anymore, in a way I can't get rid of by redescribing it. I want at least one couplet to be a real dialetheia — the same thing asserted and denied in the same respect, at the same time, so a reader can see it isn't ambivalence, or two aspects, or a shift in point of view. Please keep it away from the other diagnosis my group kept arguing about, where a borderline sentence is neither true nor false: that's the non-glutty reading, and it isn't the one I want. I want both.

Three constraints matter more to me than the rest. The poem must not go trivialist: it shouldn't turn into a poem about everything, the refrain should go on meaning the one thing it has meant since the first couplet, and the couplets that aren't the contradictory one should stay about their own business. It shouldn't resolve the contradiction, either — no reconciling the opposites into a deeper unity, no shrug about the wonderfulness of paradox. And it has to be a poem first: someone who picks up the issue knowing no logic should just read a good poem about leaving school, while someone in my reading group should be able to point at one couplet, say which it is and why, and see that the poem's refusal to fall apart is the whole point.
