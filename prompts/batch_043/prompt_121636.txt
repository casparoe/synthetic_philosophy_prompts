I work on a small team that writes country-risk briefs on democratic governance, and our internal coding manual has no word for a pattern my colleagues and I keep arguing about. I'd like to know whether the comparative-democratization or constitutional-theory literature has one; if it doesn't, I want a term defined sharply enough that three different analysts reading the same case would code it the same way. Please describe positions by their content rather than pointing me toward papers or authors — I have no way to chase a reference from where I'm working.

The pattern has two halves, and it is their combination that I can't place.

First: a governing party, over two or three terms, redraws the single-member districts, folds the public broadcasters into a single council whose members it appoints, lowers the mandatory retirement age for Supreme Court judges and fills the vacancies, then changes how the election commission is staffed. Ask about any one of these and you get the same reply — that this mechanism, or one functionally like it, also exists in some country nobody calls authoritarian. We can find no way to write down what is wrong that doesn't collapse into "look who benefits," which is exactly the move our critics invoke when they say that "backsliding" is just a partisan label for the other side winning.

Second: the asymmetry. Deterioration is only available in thin, individually defensible increments. Restoration, when an opposition eventually wins, is only available as a thick bundle — enlarge the court, rewrite the media statute, hand redistricting to a commission — and a bundle of that size is precisely the profile outside observers are primed to code as abuse. The same instrument that is nearly blind to slow decay sounds an alarm at fast repair.

Does that asymmetry — erosion payable in small change, repair only in large notes that look like the very thing everyone is watching for — have a name of its own? I don't think I am merely describing incumbency advantage, or path dependence, or a slippery slope, but I want to be told if it collapses into one of those and why. I also want its edges fixed: does it require a sequence, or can a single large reform instantiate it; does it require anyone to intend the outcome, or does unintended decay count — the collapse of local newspapers, the quiet underfunding of election administration; does it generalize to a university senate or a professional body, or is it essentially tied to electoral competition? And if it is real, could it serve as a diagnostic that doesn't depend on whether one approves of the government's policies — which is the only reason I care, since that is what would make the concept more than a label.

Be explicit throughout about what is settled and what is contested among people who actually work on this. In particular, if my composite case is not a fair or agreed description of Hungary or Poland, say so rather than letting a contested characterization sit on the page as fact; and if you end up coining a term, say plainly that you are coining it and whether it may overlap with something already in use.
