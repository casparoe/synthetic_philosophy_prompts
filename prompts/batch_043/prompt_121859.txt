I recently joined the audit committee of a midsize publicly traded manufacturer, and there is a piece of our own institutional practice that I cannot get to make sense. Each year our external auditor issues an unqualified opinion stating that the financial statements present the company's position and results fairly in all material respects. In the same meeting, the auditor hands the committee a schedule of uncorrected misstatements: actual errors the audit has identified, which management has declined to correct and which the auditor has signed off on anyway, because each one falls below a materiality threshold set at a small percentage of earnings. The audit itself never examined every transaction; it tested samples and projected the errors found in them across the untested remainder. The threshold is deliberately set below the headline number so that many individually tolerable errors cannot add up to an intolerable total. And the profession also treats some numerically trivial items as material regardless of size — a misstatement that turns a small loss into a small profit, that would put the company in breach of a loan covenant, or that involves a related-party transaction.

A friend who teaches philosophy tells me this has the shape of the preface paradox, and of its cousin the lottery paradox: the author who endorses every claim in her book while expecting at least one of them to be wrong, and the ticket holder for whom it is reasonable to accept of each ticket that it loses and reasonable to accept that some ticket wins. I would like a careful reconstruction of what our practice presupposes, rather than a verdict on whether auditors are behaving well.

What view of warrant, acceptance, or assertion must be in place for this to be more than professional habit? In particular: is the opinion best read as a great many item-level assertions that happen to be individually below a toleration threshold, or as a single indivisible claim about the statements taken as a whole, so that the appearance of a paradox comes from misidentifying the object of the attestation? Does the practice strictly presuppose that justification fails to agglomerate across conjunction, or does it presuppose something weaker and more pragmatic, such that acceptability is fixed entirely by the decisions of an imagined reasonable investor rather than by the probability of any particular error? Does the treatment of qualitatively material items settle that question, or is it compatible with both readings?

Then the questions of consistency and of underdetermination. Sampling plus projection appears to yield warrant about an aggregate while leaving each unexamined item unwarranted: is that a stable position, or is it simply the lottery paradox relocated one level up? Which rival epistemologies would license exactly this practice, so that the practice does not discriminate between them? And what would concretely have to change if the profession abandoned materiality in favor of a norm requiring that each asserted item be individually known to be correct, or if the unqualified opinion were replaced by a statistical report stating a confidence interval for total misstatement and observed error rates by account?
