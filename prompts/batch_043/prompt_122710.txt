I sit on the value analysis committee at a small regional hospital — nine of us, two doctors, a nurse manager, a pharmacist, an admin, a finance person and me (I do procurement and I'm the one who ends up with the spreadsheets). We meet twice a month for about two hours and we decide what supplies, devices and clinical protocols the hospital adopts: things that cost anywhere from 20 grand to 400 grand and that the floor staff then has to live with for five to ten years. I have no economics or philosophy background at all; I got into this because I noticed we almost always end up agreeing with whoever speaks first, and someone on a forum told me to look up information cascades and rational herding. I sort of get the basic idea — people copy the people who went before them and rationally stop using their own private information — but every explanation I find is about strangers picking between two restaurants, or balls in an urn, and I can't map it onto my situation, where we talk in a room, people say out loud how sure they are, we all know each other's track records, and the thing being decided is usually some ugly mixture of a prediction about the world and a values call about what we care about.

One wrinkle that I think makes us different from the textbook setup, and please tell me if I'm wrong about this. In our meetings people don't only copy each other's picks, they copy each other's level of confidence. Three people who each have a genuinely weak private reason will each sound firm, because each one is reading the other two as firm, and the meeting ends in what I've started calling a "confidence cascade" — nobody has really changed their mind about anything, but the room is radiating a certainty that no individual in it actually holds, and anybody with a real worry suddenly feels like the odd one out and shuts up. There's a mirror version where one respected person says something lukewarm and the whole proposal quietly dies, which I've been calling a "chill." My sense is that a procedure built for binary choices made one at a time in a fixed order won't catch either of these, because with us the action isn't binary, the order isn't fixed and the thing being transmitted is partly a mood.

What I actually want is a procedure I can run, in the twenty minutes I have before a meeting or in the two minutes of floor time I get during it, to decide for myself: is this a case where I should go along because the fact that everyone is converging really is evidence, or a case where I should dig in and put my private read on the table? A short ordered list of questions, each answerable by a non-expert in a minute or two without knowing the theory, with a clear stopping rule for each — stop and defer, stop and escalate, or stop and hand it to a person because the remaining question is about values rather than facts. Please be honest about which steps are real tests and which are just judgment wearing a clipboard. It has to handle: decisions where there is a fact of the matter that will show up in our own data later (does this catheter actually cut infection rates on our wards) versus ones where the right answer is mostly about what we value (do we buy the nicer monitoring system or spend the same money on another night nurse) versus ones where what matters is simply that we all do the same thing (which electronic record system we standardise on); the fact that sometimes I can get a private straw vote before anyone talks and sometimes I can't, because the chair runs the meeting how she likes; the fact that some of what people know isn't the kind of thing they can put into words, like a nurse's gut feeling that a new pump is going to be a misery on nights; and the fact that I am not the chair and I have maybe two minutes. If the answer is dig in, tell me what the move actually is — secret ballot, ask the quietest person first, table it, send it to a subcommittee, whatever. And please don't point me at papers or tell me what to read; our library access is basically nil and it wouldn't help me with fifteen minutes before a meeting anyway.

Then run it on three cases from the last year. First: we were choosing between two IV pump models, everyone drifted toward the one the senior anesthesiologist liked, and I had our own repair-ticket data showing that model had noticeably more failures at our two sites over eighteen months than the other one did. Second: we were deciding whether to switch to a cheaper sterilization wrap before the next inspection; six people with far more relevant experience than me were all relaxed about it, my only objection was a vague worry about the inspectors that I couldn't really back up, and I really did not want to be the person who slows everything down — I suspect the right procedure tells me to shut up here, so show me that if it does. Third: an experienced OR nurse said a new surgical positioning device just felt wrong to her, couldn't articulate why, the rest of the room was enthusiastic, and I couldn't tell whether to treat that as a signal or as nothing. Finally, tell me plainly what the procedure can't do: what kinds of decisions it will misfire on, and how I keep it from turning me into the person who objects to everything.
