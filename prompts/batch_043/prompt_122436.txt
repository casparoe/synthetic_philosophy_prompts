Marion, who runs the seminar, has been going through cases one at a time and giving a crisp verdict on each: does the subject's report get to go into the heterophenomenological record as evidence about what it was like for that subject, or not? She does sometimes say no. Her verdicts so far, with the one-line reason she gave:

1. Forced-choice brightness judgements, and detailed afterimage descriptions given after the stimulus is gone ("greenish ring that moves when my eyes move") — in.
2. Chronic pain patient's "burning, deep, about a seven" — in, but she added that the number is a measurement of the reporting, not of the pain.
3. A synesthete's "the five looks brown" — in, and she stressed it only got in because the interference task backed it up.
4. A blindsight patient saying "I'm only guessing" while performing well above chance — in as a report, but it does not settle whether there was anything it was like for him.
5. Change blindness: "I saw the whole scene clearly the whole time" — in, but reinterpreted: it's a claim about the scene, not about the processing of every detail in it.
6. Phantom limb: "my missing left foot itches" — in.
7. Inattentional blindness, asked only *after* being debriefed, "oh yes, I definitely saw the gorilla" — out, as evidence that she saw it.
8. The Libet-style business, "I felt the urge to flex before my wrist moved" — out, as evidence about when the conscious event happened; she says we have no authority on the order or the causation of our own mental events.
9. Inverted spectrum, "my red might be your green and nothing behavioural would ever show it" — out, flat, no discussion. Nothing could turn on it.
10. A dream report given on waking from REM, "I was being chased down a corridor" — in, and more than in: she said that for dreams the report isn't a clue to the dream, it's most of what there is to the dream.

Next week's case is mine, which is why I care. A group is running a study on long-term meditators and I'm one of the subjects — I've been doing the practice for about thirty years, so I have skin in this game, though I'd rather have the methodological answer than a flattering one. The setup: you sit there alert, and there is supposedly no object — no image, no sound, no thought, no bodily feeling. You signal onset and offset with a button press, and when a buzzer goes you write a report. The reports all come out as some variant of "there was nothing there, but I wasn't asleep or zoned out; I'd have answered you if you'd asked." Three things about the data:

(a) The experienced people can be trained to signal onset and offset, and the presses aren't randomly placed; one group also reported an EEG pattern distinguishing those epochs from ordinary quiet rest, though nobody has replicated it. Undergraduates told to do the same thing produce nothing you'd want to call a state.
(b) In forced choice, the experienced people can tell, better than chance, whether a probe caught them in that state or in ordinary mind-wandering. So they aren't randomly guessing about something.
(c) Otherwise nobody has found anything the state does. No replicated neural signature, no difference on the tasks run immediately afterwards. The only thing that separates "contentless awareness" from "nothing happened at all and afterwards I said it did" is that during the epoch they're responsive — they hit the button, answer the buzzer, and say they could have answered anything.
