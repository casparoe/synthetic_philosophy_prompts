I work as a software engineer (distributed systems, mostly) and have no formal philosophy background. On a recent flight I read a chapter in a popular-physics book that presented an argument I'd like to have audited before I repeat it to anyone else. The chapter is about the Rietdijk–Putnam argument and the "block universe," and its summary runs roughly like this:

'Special relativity kills the idea of a universal now. Whether two distant events are simultaneous depends on your frame of reference, so there is no fact of the matter about what is happening right now anywhere else in the universe. Rietdijk and Putnam showed how extreme this is. Suppose you are standing still and a friend walks past you at about a metre per second in the direction of Andromeda. Your "now" slices the universe one way and hers slices it another, and because Andromeda is two and a half million light years away, that tiny slope in the slice adds up: the events there that she calls happening now are events that, in your frame, lie a few days in the future. If she walks the other way, the events she calls now are events you call past. But every inertial frame is as good as any other, so if those Andromedean events are real for her, they are real, full stop — including the ones you would call future. Run the argument in both temporal directions and you get the block universe: past, present and future are all equally real, nothing comes into or goes out of existence, and the feeling that time flows is just an artefact of how memory works — we remember one direction and not the other, and mistake an asymmetry in our own heads for an asymmetry in the world. None of this is optional: it is entailed by special relativity alone, and general relativity and quantum mechanics only reinforce it. The open future is therefore an illusion, and any philosophy or religion that relies on one has been refuted by physics.'

I am inclined to believe the core of this — the frame-relativity of simultaneity is the part I can actually check myself, since the offset scales as vL/c² and I can do that arithmetic. But the step from "real for her" to "real, full stop" looks to me like it is doing all the work, and I can't tell whether it is a legitimate consequence of there being no privileged frame or an extra philosophical premise smuggled in. Two other things bothered me. First, she cannot observe what is happening in Andromeda now in any sense that would let her check the claim; the light from there is 2.5 million years old when it arrives, so "simultaneous for her" and "knowable by her" seem to be different things, and I can't tell which one the argument needs. Second, in my own field the analogous situation is uncontroversial and doesn't produce this kind of conclusion: a distributed system with no global clock has many admissible "consistent cuts" (global snapshots) through the same set of events, and the fact that no cut is canonical does not mean every event in the run has already happened — it means "the current state" is only well-defined relative to a chosen cut. There is also a partial order, the happens-before relation, that is invariant across all of them, so not everything is up for grabs.

So, please: go through the passage claim by claim and tell me what a philosopher of physics or a physicist would accept, what is a reasonable popularization-level simplification, and what is just wrong. In particular:

- Is there really "no fact of the matter" about distant simultaneity, or is the accurate claim weaker (or stronger) than that?
- Does the argument as stated actually deliver eternalism, or does it need premises beyond special relativity — and what does a presentist or growing-block theorist say in reply? Is there a live position on which "what is real now" is indexical or frame-relative, or is that just relabelling?
- Is the passage confusing the arrow of time (a real asymmetry in the block) with the passage or flow of time (what the block universe is supposed to deny)? Is the memory-based explanation of the illusion part of the view or an optional extra?
- Does the block universe entail fatalism, or is "the future is real" compatible with the future being in some sense open, or with free will, or with deliberation mattering?
- Is my distributed-systems analogy — invariant causal order plus many non-canonical global slices — a fair picture of Minkowski spacetime, and does it undercut the argument or merely redescribe it? If it does track something, is there a name for the view it points to, and what is the strongest objection to it that I should read next?

I don't want a reading list; I want to know which sentences of that chapter I can safely quote at dinner and which ones would make a physicist wince.
