I think that's badly wrong, but I can't yet say why in a way that survives her pushback, and I want the proposal itself to be the thing that works it out.

The half-formed idea is this. Everyone in the debate borrows the vocabulary of worst-case and average-case guarantees from algorithm analysis, and I think the borrowing is broken in a specific way that nobody spells out. In the CS setting, the cases are drawn from a distribution that's fixed independently of what the algorithm's designer does — you analyse a sorting routine against a distribution over inputs, and the input distribution doesn't rearrange itself in response to your choice. But for a deployed AI system the "cases" seem to be (i) partly chosen by us, since the decision to deploy (and where, and into what surrounding institutions) shifts which situations the system actually lands in, and (ii) possibly chosen by the system, if the thing we're evaluating is itself an optimizer with some capacity to find exactly the input on which it behaves worst. If either of those is right, then "average case" may not refer to anything stable enough to take expectations over, and "worst case" may be the case that's most likely to show up rather than the case that's vanishingly improbable. The other piece I can't stop chewing on: in complexity theory there are worst-case-to-average-case reductions, where you show that an algorithm succeeding on a random instance can be converted into one that succeeds on all instances, so that average-case and worst-case difficulty collapse together. Is there any analogue for safety? Could you design a training procedure or a deployment wrapper such that good behaviour on the average of some distribution we control would imply a bound on behaviour in the worst case — a reduction, but pointing the other way? My guess is the answer is no, and that the reason it's no is precisely the adversarial-optimization bit above, but I want that to be a result the project tests rather than a hunch I assert.
