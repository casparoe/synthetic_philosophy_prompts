Two people from my decision theory reading group carried on arguing after the session finished, and I was the only one their who hadn't picked a side, so I got nominated to settle it and I honestly can't.

The two cases they were fighting over, in my own words: (A) Omega flips a fair coin. If it comes up tails it asks you for £100. If it comes up heads it pays you £10,000 — but only if it predicted you would have paid had it come up tails. Since you're being asked, you know it came up tails. (B) A perfectly honest predictor sends you a note saying it sent the note iff exactly one of these is true: you will pay it £1000, or your building has termites. An infestation would cost you £1,000,000.

Ines: these are the same problem. In both, a predictor's behaviour turns on a counterfactual fact about your decision procedure, and in both your choice now has no causal effect on the state that matters — the coin is already down, the termites are already there or not. So the only real question is which output of your decision algorithm does best across the situations it could face: pay in A, refuse in B. And you can watch the theories trip over exactly this. Causal decision theory refuses in both. Evidential decision theory gives precisely my answer reversed in both — it refuses in A, since you know the coin came up tails, and pays in B, since paying is what would make the note good news.

Wes: they're not the same. In B, being a refuser pays off in the branch you're actually in: a refuser only ever gets the note when the termites are real, so refusing buys you a free and extremely valuable warning. In A the entire upside lands in a branch you are not in — you hand over real money here for the benefit of a version of you that never exists. That's exactly why everyone agrees you should precommit to paying in A if you get the chance: precommitment is the only thing that makes the other branch's money reachable at all.

Ines: "which branch benefits" can't be what's doing the work. New case. Omega never asks you anything. Yesterday it ran a full simulation of you, told the sim "tails," asked it for the £100, and handed you the £10,000 or not on that basis. Today it tells you the sim said no, so you got nothing — and then offers you the same choice anyway: pay £100, changing nothing. If your principle were the right one this should feel exactly like A, because in both cases the money is already out of reach and all that ever mattered was what your algorithm outputs on this input. It plainly doesn't feel the same, and i think that's because your principle is the wrong one.

Wes: it doesn't feel the same for a reason my account predicts, and I'll just bite the bullet — I don't think you should pay in A either. If what's normatively relevant is what an agent running my algorithm does, then a simulation that already ran should bind me too, and it obviously doesn't; so your sim case is a reductio of everything that makes A look compelling. What actually makes people want to pay in A is that it's normally told as a game you play over and over, where being a payer earns you the reputation. Take that away and A collapses into the sim case.

Please adjudicate this move by move: who's right about which specific point, who's right overall, and where each of them is quietly changing the subject. I most want to know whether Wes's last move really does collapse A into the sim case or whether it costs him somewhere else — and what both of them are missing.
