ok so my TA in phil of mind says "the self is a center of narrative gravity" (someone's phrase, I forget whose) every single time the topic comes up, and he says it in this tone that's supposed to close the subject.

Last week someone in my section asked an actual question: take a guy with dense retrograde amnesia — remembers basically nothing from before the accident, no episodic memory of his own childhood, but his skills and habits are intact, his personality looks the same to everyone who knew him, and he can tell you a perfectly coherent story about the last three years and nothing at all before that. Is that the same person? Did his self get damaged, or replaced, or what?

The TA goes "well, the self is just a center of narrative gravity," and moved on. I think he meant it to be doing two things at once: (1) there's no deep fact of the matter here, its not a question with a real answer, just a question of which description is handier; and (2) so nothing much hangs on it.

I can't tell whether the slogan gets him either of those, and I'm pretty sure its being used to mean four different things depending on the week.

Can you lay the readings out as a menu, from the boring deflationary one to the wild ones, and for each one say roughly: is it trivial, is it substantive and defensible, is it just false, and does it actually support (1), or (2), or neither?

The distinctions I'm after:

- Is the claim about how we should *talk* about people — positing a self is a useful shorthand, like positing a center of gravity, and there's no more a further question than there is about where the center of gravity "really" is — or is it a claim about what a person *is made of*, i.e. the narrative literally constitutes the self, so a wrecked narrative means a wrecked or absent self? Those give totally different answers in the amnesia case and I think he slides between them.

- Does any reading require the narrative to be *told by* the person — avowed, authored, accessible from the inside — or is it enough that there's a story-shaped pattern that makes their behavior predictable? I made up two cases that I think pry these apart, and I want to know what each reading says about each:

 (a) The lifelog guy. Wears a camera/mic rig for a decade, everything indexed and searchable. After a head injury he can't recall episodes on his own at all, but he can query the archive, and he does, constantly; he plans and acts on what it hands him in a way that's functionally indistinguishable from remembering except that he experiences it as looking something up. Where's the center of gravity — in him, in the archive, spread over the whole rig plus person? And does the boring deflationary reading even care, or does it just say "wherever its convenient"?

 (b) The ghostwritten diary. Every night an app that's read everything he's ever written composes his diary entry for him. He reads it in the morning, finds it completely convincing, and sincerely says yes, that's what happened and that's why I did it. He can't tell which bits came from him. He really endorses it and it really does organize his life — call it a narrative prosthesis. Does the narrative have to be *his* in some stronger sense than "he endorses it and it works," or is endorsement plus causal role the whole story? And where a reading does demand something more, what more specifically: authorship of the sentences? counterfactual control over what the story says? the capacity to notice when the story is wrong?

- Also the analogy itself worries me. A center of gravity isn't vague or negotiable — it's pinned down exactly by the mass distribution. So what plays the role of the mass for a self? The behavior, the memories, the avowals, the brain? If nothing does, or if several unrelated things do and they come apart in cases like the two above, then the analogy looks like its doing less work than the phrase suggests, and I'd like to know which reading it's actually load-bearing for and which ones it's just decorating.

- And separately: how far does this scale? If a self is just a useful posit, does a chatbot with long-term memory of your conversations have one? A family? A dog? Which readings say yes, which ones have a stopping point, and is that stopping point principled or is it just embarrassment?

Don't bother with citations or further reading, I just want the distinctions sharp enough to bring to section. And please say how confident you are in the overall picture, and single out the one point you're least sure about — and I'd rather hear about a point where people who actually work on this genuinely disagree than one where you're personally just guessing.
