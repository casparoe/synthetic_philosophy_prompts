I have been going around in circles on a question I cannot get into focus, and I would like help finding the principle behind my own reactions to it. The question is not whether I would have turned out differently if my beginnings had been different — obviously I would have — but whether the resulting person would have been me at all. Here are the cases, along with what I find myself inclined to say about each.

1. A different one of my father's sperm and a different one of my mother's eggs, from the same two parents: not me. In fact this is my brother. I am more confident of this verdict than of any other, though I notice it may only be tracking the fact that a different gamete means a different genome.

2. My actual sperm and egg, but combined by injection in a clinic rather than by intercourse, and the resulting embryo frozen for thirty years before transfer: me, I think, though I am distinctly less confident here than in case 1, and I cannot say why thirty years of storage should raise a question that nine months of gestation does not.

3. My actual sperm and egg, but the embryo carried by a surrogate and the child raised by adoptive parents abroad, so that I would have had none of the life I have actually had: still me, and fairly confidently. This suggests to me that my entire subsequent biography is inessential while something about conception is not, which I find odd and would like explained rather than merely confirmed.

4. My actual sperm and egg, except that the egg's nucleus is transferred into a donor egg whose own nucleus has been removed, so that my mitochondria and cytoplasm come from a stranger: still me. This one is easy, and its easiness seems informative.

5. My actual sperm and egg, except that a single wrong base pair in the sperm is corrected before fertilization, so that the resulting genome differs from my actual one by one letter: I lean toward saying this is still me, and I lean the same way at ten changed base pairs, but not at ten thousand, and I cannot locate a boundary anywhere in between.

6. My actual zygote, except that it splits at the two-cell stage and both halves develop: my identical twin and — whom? I do not think either of us is me, and I do not think both of us are, and the case seems to resist any answer. Yet it is the same zygote in the ordinary sense, which makes me doubt that the zygote is the right thing to anchor essentiality to.

7. The nucleus of a cell taken from my body at thirty, inserted into an enucleated egg, with the resulting child raised as my son: not me — my twin, or my son, or neither — even though the nuclear genome is the same.

8. Now stipulate something that actual meiosis makes all but impossible: a numerically different sperm and a numerically different egg from the same two parents, carrying exactly my genome. My gut says this child would be me. But that verdict contradicts 1 and 7 unless genome identity is what is doing the work in all of them, and my confidence in 1 and 7 is an artifact of the fact that in the real world different gametes always bring different genomes.

9. Two entirely different parents: not me. But I suspect this verdict is entailed by case 1 rather than supported by any independent intuition of my own, and I would like to know whether the entailment is genuine or whether I have simply been talked into it.

What I want from you:

First, is there a single principle that delivers all nine verdicts? The candidates I can construct are: essentiality of the very gametes; essentiality of the initial genome; essentiality of the zygote or early embryo rather than of the gametes; and the view that some of these cases have no fact of the matter at all, so that my judgments are conventions for extending me across counterfactual scenarios rather than detections of a metaphysical boundary. State each candidate precisely, clause by clause, naming the case that motivates each clause, and say where each one collides with a verdict I have reported.

Second, which of my verdicts has to go? I would rather surrender a verdict than accept a principle with an arbitrary cutoff in it, but I want to be told the price in each case, and I want to know whether the verdict I would be giving up is one I had independent reason to trust or one I was led to by a bad argument.

Third, take case 5 seriously. Is the base-pair series a sorites that shows the identity question is vague? Or does the correct principle draw a sharp line I am failing to see? Or is the right reply that a near-enough genome yields someone else — numerically distinct, with his own equally rigid origins — rather than a vague or borderline me?

Fourth, two cases I have not settled and would like the principle's verdict on, together with the reasoning: (i) at the two-cell stage one half of the embryo is discarded and the other implanted; had the discarded half been implanted instead, would that child have been me? (ii) my actual genome is chemically synthesized — no sperm, no egg anywhere in the story — and grown into a child. Same sequence, no gametes. Is that me, and why does your answer not trivialize the original question?

Finally, a framing worry I cannot shake. Is this really a question about me, or is it a question about how the word I refers in counterfactual contexts — such that a principle could tell me what to say without there being any deeper fact to get right? If it is the latter, say so plainly, and say what, if anything, is then at stake in the dispute beyond consistency in how we talk.
