I'm trying to work out what I actually think about punishment by just going case by case, and I want the principle my gut is running on, if there is one.

- Locking up someone we're 95% sure was about to do it, beforehand: not ok.
- Punishing the guy who pulled the trigger but the gun jammed: ok, though somehow a bit less than if it had fired.
- Fining a corporation for fraud, where the money comes out of shareholders who knew nothing: basically fine.
- Cutting off a criminal's family's benefits: not ok.
- A volunteer doing the sentence instead, offender consents, victims on board, deterrence fully intact: not ok, and honestly it's worse than just punishing the guy.
- Someone who did it and then got hit by a truck, lost everything, nearly no memory of it: punishing him feels pointless, I'd let it go.
- Someone who did it and then won the lottery and is thriving: no reaction either way, punish as normal.
- Civil disobedience against a genuinely unjust law: punishing him is allowed, but something is lost and I feel worse about it than I do about the fraudster.

My own guess at the principle was: punishment may only be aimed at the person who did it, unless the cost lands on someone who voluntarily took on the risk of it. But that seems to let the volunteer case in, which I definately don't want, and I'm not sure it explains the truck guy either.

The pair I can't square is shareholders-but-not-family: both are innocents absorbing the cost. Is the difference that the shareholders were in a role that was supposed to carry that risk? Or am I just being inconsistent?

What I'd like: a principle (or two candidates), stated precisely enough to argue with, with the case that motivates each clause; what it predicts about cases I havent listed — punishing the executives personally, punishing something that was legal when it was done, punishing a system that isn't a person — and an honest account of which of my verdicts it makes me give up. And what it says about the one I keep flip-flopping on: a soldier who took part in a massacre and who, twenty years later, is a completely different person with no memory of doing it.
