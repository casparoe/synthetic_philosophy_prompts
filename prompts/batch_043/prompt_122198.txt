I lead a four-person modeling team at a midsize reinsurance broker, and about twice a month we have to make a call I always find awkward: we have to pick a model class to forecast the frequency or severity of a medium-sized weather-related peril (hail, wildfire smoke damage, flash flood) for a region where we only have a few years of satellite-derived indices and historical claims data. The usual fight on the team is between a very flexible model (gradient-boosted trees with a few hundred features, or a small neural net) and a much simpler index model (say, a published drought or vegetation index plus a linear trend). People keep invoking Occam's razor, or saying that Solomonoff induction means we should prefer the shortest hypothesis that fits the data, or that Kolmogorov complexity gives a principled way to trade off fit against complexity. When I try to turn that into something we can actually run in about ninety minutes, though, I get stuck alot: Solomonoff induction is uncomputable, Kolmogorov complexity depends on the description language, shorter descriptions don't obviously mean better expected loss for the decisions we care about, and our current informal rule (the fancy model has to beat the simple one by at least 0.02 Brier score on rotating-origin backtests or we don't use it) definately feels arbitrary. One teammate even suggested using the gzip length of the model file plus its predictions as a crude proxy for description length, and I can't tell whether that is a legitimate approximation or just numerology.

I was catching up with two friends from grad school the other night, one of whom does theoretical ML and one who works in catastrophe modeling, and they made it worse by disagreeing: the theory friend said Solomonoff/Kolmogorov basically justifies our simplicity bias but only relative to a fixed reference machine, while the cat-modeling friend said in practice you should just use the flexible model because the real world is messy and the simple index models have been wrong before. So here is what I actually want: please write a decision procedure or checklist my team could really run when this comes up, grounded in whatever Solomonoff induction, Kolmogorov complexity, and Occam's razor actually support, but honest about where it has to hand off to judgment or business values rather than pretending to be a theorem. The role is fixed: I lead the team, we make this call roughly twice a month, the deadline per case is about ninety minutes, we have historical data but no data from the future regime, any procedure has to end in one of three ways (a clear recommendation, a stop with not enough information, or a handoff to the chief risk officer because the tradeoff is really about risk appetite), and it has to be usable by someone who took one ML course and won't recompute a universal prior by hand. Give me the questions to ask in order, what should count as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure should just say no, and where it should stop and hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. Please say explicitly how to think about the fact that we cannot compute Kolmogorov complexity, whether the gzip proxy idea is admissible as a rough stand-in or should be thrown out, and which parts of Occam's razor survive the translation into a practical model-selection rule.

Then please run the procedure on two cases. Case one: we have 12 regions, three years of weekly satellite moisture/vegetation indices and claims counts, and we are choosing between a linear index model and a gradient-boosted tree with 200 features; the tree fits the history better but only beats the index model by 0.015 Brier on backtests. Case two: a novel-perils team wants us to forecast losses from a newly observed storm type using only eight weeks of data, and the choice is between a physically motivated simulation model with many tuned parameters and a generic time-series model. I want at least one of these worked applications to give an answer I will not like (for example, the procedure says the flexible model is fine, or it says we cannot decide and must escalate). Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: where it will misfire, what kinds of model-class choices are outside its scope, and which hard cases it will silently get wrong if someone trusts it too much.
