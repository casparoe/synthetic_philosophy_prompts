Please act as an expert philosopher of language and logic who also has experience designing interactive decision aids for museum visitors. Take your time, begin by brainstorming the full logical space of possible answer-patterns before assigning any theoretical labels, and only then produce the final instrument. I am curating a small public-philosophy touchscreen kiosk for a science museum, working title Borderline: The Heap, the Portrait, and the Law, and I need the underlying decision tree that will power it. The tree should start from a commitment I expect almost every visitor to grant: I agree that a standard sorites series running from a clear heap of ten thousand grains down to a single grain has no obvious first non-heap, and I am willing to treat that as a puzzle to be solved rather than as a reason to stop using the word. From there, the tree must sort respondents by what they say about concrete cases, never by asking them which theory they like; each branch node must be a question a thoughtful teenager could answer without knowing where it leads, and the tree should be built by first laying out the possible combinations of answers and only then locating the actual positions in that space.

The diagnostic questions must use at least four concrete cases: the ten-thousand-to-one grain heap already mentioned; a slowly degrading digital portrait I am calling The Receding Pixel Friend, in which one randomly chosen pixel is corrupted every minute and the visitor is asked when the image stops being a portrait of a particular person; a legal case in which a jurisdiction sets the age of contractual capacity at eighteen but the defendant missed the threshold by one hour; and a color spectrum passing from unambiguous red to unambiguous orange with no sharp boundary. The tree should have eight to twelve branch questions in total and six to eight leaves. Named leaves must include epistemicism, supervaluationism, subvaluationism if you think it deserves separate treatment, degree-functionalist or fuzzy-logic vagueness, contextualism, and semantic nihilism. In addition, include a leaf for a nonstandard position I call graduated legal fictionalism, the view that for legal purposes we should act as if there were a sharp cutoff even though there is none, because treating the cutoff as a useful fiction is required by the rule of law, and one further leaf for the combination of answers that denies a sharp cutoff, denies semantic indecision, and denies degrees of truth, yet also refuses eliminativism: if nobody really occupies that combination, diagnose in one sentence whether the combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored, and give it a placeholder name. Where two leaves cannot be cleanly separated by the cases—for instance if The Receding Pixel Friend and the color spectrum pull a respondent in opposite directions—flag the overlap explicitly instead of forcing a choice. Do not provide a bibliography, further-reading list, or citations to authors or papers, and do not attribute any passage to a specific text or author; I need a self-contained instrument that a model without web access could generate from conceptual reasoning together with the stipulations in this prompt, using only standard philosophical labels as part of the vernacular. Present the final decision tree in a clear structured form, such as numbered nodes or indented branches, with a brief key noting that the intended audience is museum visitors with no prior training in philosophy.
