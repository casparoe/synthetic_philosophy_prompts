My brother and I run a small bakery together — well, I do the books, the ordering, the payroll, and I also do bookkeeping for about a dozen other tiny businesses on the side, and that side bit is what pays my half of the mortgage. He isnt worried about AI at all, and his entire argument is one analogy. Two years ago we replaced our old deck oven with a much better one, and for the first year it made basically no difference to how much bread went out the door. The oven could do several times the throughput, but the proofer was too small, the mixer was the wrong size, there was no cold room, the delivery van was already booked, and none of that was the clever bit. It took us getting on for two years of rebuilding everything around the oven before the throughput showed up, and even now we dont sell several times as much bread, because people only eat so much bread. He half-remembers something about factories not getting measurably more productive for decades after they got electric motors, and he folds that in too. His conclusion: the clever part gets cheap first, the boring complementary stuff takes years, demand is satiable, and anyone talking about a short gap between "the model can do your job" and "nobody has that job" has never actually run a business. He's using it to tell me not to spend two evenings a week for the next two years retraining as an electrician.

I want to know how far it actually holds. Give me the mapping explicitly — what's the oven, what's the proofer and the van, what's the two years, what's "we only sell so much bread". Then where it breaks. Two things I can sort of put my finger on and want you to sharpen: the oven cannot redesign the bakery, whereas the complementary work in the AI case is mostly the same kind of office-and-paperwork work the model is supposedly good at, so the bottleneck might be automatable too, which would compress those two years into something much shorter; and bread is a thing people get full of, while whatever AI sells is an input into everything else. But I also think my brother might be onto something I dont have a name for, which is that economy-wide change being slow and my particular job going away fast might not even be the same variable, and I keep not being sure which one I should care about.

Tell me how much of your answer is load-bearing versus contested — I want to know which bits are just empirical bets about the next five years that nobody can currently settle, and how confident you'd be in each. And if the whole fast-takeoff-versus-slow-takeoff framing is the wrong thing for me to be deciding this on, say that plainly rather than politely answering the question I asked. Is there a better analogy than the oven?
