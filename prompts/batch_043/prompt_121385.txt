So last week I couldn't sleep, had a really vivid dream about trying to deploy to production from a kayak, and then made the mistake of describing it at board game night. Two friends got into a full-blown argument about whether dreams are experiences, and I've been chewing on it since. A third person said it's basically a decades-old academic fight between Norman Malcolm and Daniel Dennett and got told to shut up, but nobody explained what either of them actually said, so I'm coming to you. For context I'm a backend engineer, zero philosophy background, and I kept flip-flopping the whole night.

Here's roughly how it went. (1) Mara — she does ML infra — said a dream isn't a thing that happens to you at all: the only thing that actually occurs is that you wake up disposed to tell a story, and the story is the whole phenomenon, so there's no further fact of the matter about whether you "lived through" a kayak deploy at 3am. She insisted this isn't skepticism, it's a point about the concept: with a normal experience there's in-principle room to check what you underwent against something else — what you did next, what other people saw, what you said at the time — whereas with a dream there's no room for any such check, so "but was it really experienced?" has no answer to be right or wrong about. She was careful to say she's not denying the sleeping brain is busy. (2) Dev, who's an ER doc, said that's just moving the goalposts: it's a textbook finding that waking people out of REM sleep gets you dream reports way more often than waking them from other stages, and if that correlation isn't evidence that something dream-like is going on during sleep, then nothing is. He also brought up lucid dreamers signalling from inside a dream with pre-arranged eye movements, and said there've even been studies where sleeping people seemed to answer questions in real time before being woken — he claimed no "story manufactured at the moment of waking" account covers that. (3) Mara replied that the eye-movement work shows a sleeping person can execute a learned motor plan that later shows up in a report, which is not the same as showing that anyone was there to undergo something; and the report always arrives at or after waking, so all it shows is that the sleeping brain was processing language and that this processing shaped the waking narrative. Then she stole his own side's example: she brought up Dennett's idea of the dream as a kind of cassette dumped into memory at the moment of waking, and said if that's coherent for ordinary dreams it's coherent for lucid ones too, so the lucid cases settle nothing. (4) Dev then argued that people with REM behaviour disorder act out their dreams, and that external stimuli — water sprayed on a sleeper, a name called, a cuff inflated — turn up in reports in ways that depend on when the stimulus was delivered, which he said means the report is tracking events during sleep rather than being invented on the spot. Mara said that only shows the sleeping brain registers things and biases a later narrative: a server can log an event at 2am and generate a paragraph about it at 8am without anyone undergoing anything. (5) Finally Dev went sci-fi: suppose we could copy whatever memory trace is in your brain at 3:17am and paste it into someone else, and they wake up convinced they were paddling through a code review. We'd definately say that person experienced nothing — but if that very same trace is what produces your own 8am conviction, what's supposed to be different about your case? Mara said she actually liked the example and thought it cut both ways: it shows how comfortable we are with a memory-like state that has no experience behind it, but it also shows we have no clean way of saying what would have to be added for it to count as a real one.

Can you go through this move by move and tell me who's right about what? I'd like each of the five exchanges rated somewhere from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a short reason, and I especially want to know what (if anything) both of them are missing — like whether "experience" is even the right word to be fighting over, or whether they're each quietly switching between two different questions. Please don't give me reading recommendations, just adjudicate the arguments as actually made.
