The question as set

Question 4 (30 marks). A five-member public-health advisory committee must decide what to recommend about a ventilation standard. Three propositions are on the agenda:

P: the virus in question is transmitted substantially by aerosol.
Q: the proposed ventilation standard would materially reduce transmission.
R: the committee should recommend the standard.

The committee's charter supplies the connection rule: R if and only if both P and Q — that is, R holds exactly when the conjunction P and Q holds. The members' judgments are as follows.

Member A: P yes, Q yes, R yes
Member B: P yes, Q yes, R yes
Member C: P yes, Q no, R no
Member D: P no, Q yes, R no
Member E: P no, Q yes, R no

Assume that no member is misrepresenting anything strategically, that every member's own judgment set is logically consistent, and that the committee must reach a decision one way or the other.

(a) (8 marks) Work out the outcome under propositionwise majority voting. State whether the resulting collective judgment set is consistent with the charter's connection rule, and locate precisely where the inconsistency arises.

(b) (8 marks) Work out the outcome under the premise-based procedure and under the conclusion-based procedure. In one sentence, explain why the two procedures can diverge.

(c) (6 marks) Define universal domain, collective rationality, and independence. For each of the premise-based and the conclusion-based procedures, state which of the three conditions it satisfies and which it fails, with a one-line reason in each case.

(d) (8 marks) The statute under which the committee sits provides that the committee shall publish, together with any decision, a statement recording whether each consideration that the charter makes necessary is or is not endorsed by a majority of its members. Discuss whether this leaves the conclusion-based procedure available to the committee, and say what the clause implies about the power of whoever drafts the charter. Then consider the following proposal from one member: vote on R first, and then, if R carries, hold votes on P and Q in order to generate the required statement. Would this give the same result as either procedure in part (b)? Is it path-dependent?

The answer I received

(a) Counted proposition by proposition, P is supported by A, B and C and opposed by D and E, so P carries 3–2. Q is supported by A, B, D and E and opposed by C only, so Q carries 4–1. R is supported by A and B alone and opposed by C, D and E, so R fails 2–3. The collective judgment is therefore P, Q and not-R. That is inconsistent with the charter, which makes R equivalent to the conjunction of P and Q: a body that accepts both conjuncts is committed to the conjunction, and here it also denies it. Nothing has gone wrong at the level of the individuals — each of the five members has a consistent judgment set, and no one is voting strategically — so the inconsistency is produced by the aggregation rule itself, by taking majority votes on each proposition separately.

(b) Under the premise-based procedure the committee aggregates the premises and derives everything else by the charter's own logic. P carries and Q carries, so R holds and the standard is recommended. Under the conclusion-based procedure the members vote on R alone; R is defeated 2–3 and the standard is not recommended. The two diverge because the majority for each premise need not be a majority for the conclusion: the set of members who accept both reasons — here A and B alone — can be a minority even when each reason separately commands a majority.

(c) Universal domain: any combination of individually consistent and complete judgment sets must be admissible as input. Collective rationality: the collective judgment set must itself be consistent and complete. Independence: the collective judgment on a proposition must depend only on the members' individual judgments on that proposition. On these definitions, the premise-based procedure satisfies independence, since it aggregates each premise separately by majority vote and derives the conclusion by logic rather than by any further aggregation, with no member's vote on one proposition influencing how votes on another are counted; it also satisfies universal domain and, since the two premises here are logically independent of one another, collective rationality. The conclusion-based procedure, by contrast, violates independence, because it discards the members' judgments on P and Q rather than letting each proposition be settled on its own; it secures consistency only by leaving the committee without any collective view on the reasons at all. Because no rule can satisfy universal domain, collective rationality and independence together, the choice is which condition to give up, and the usual judgment has been that independence is the least costly to lose. The lesson is the same one that Condorcet's paradox and Arrow's theorem teach about preferences; in its judgment-aggregation form the result is the more general one, with Arrow's independence condition as the ancestor of the independence condition used here.

(d) The statutory duty to publish reasons settles the matter. A bare verdict is not a statement of reasons, and the statute requires a majority-endorsed statement on each necessary consideration, which the committee cannot produce unless it has actually aggregated P and Q. The conclusion-based procedure is therefore not available to this committee. That shows how much power the framing of the charter carries: had the drafter collapsed P and Q into a single question, whether the standard is warranted, the committee could vote only on that, and the reasons would never be voted on at all. Nor would voting on R first help. That ordering returns not-R, after which the connection rule forbids the committee from endorsing both P and Q, so the later votes are either constrained or idle: it is the conclusion-based procedure with extra steps, and it makes the outcome depend on the order in which the questions are put.

In sum: propositionwise majority voting is collectively irrational in this case; the premise-based procedure is the right response to the discursive dilemma and costs nothing in independence; and the conclusion-based procedure buys consistency by throwing away the committee's reasons, which both the statute and the point of having a committee at all forbid.
