Hey — long one, sorry in advance. I'm in a methods reading group (psych adjacent, mostly grad students plus one statistician who keeps audibly sighing) and I've been asked to write up my own position on the replication mess so the others can poke at it before the next session. I'd rather find the holes myself first, so here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) The crisis is mainly a selective-reporting problem — p-hacking, publication bias, the file drawer, the garden of forking paths — not a problem with the internal logic of frequentist inference. A p-value does what it says on the tin; people just fed it garbage. (2) Because of that, I'm happy to let big multi-site preregistered replications and meta-analyses adjudicate: if a claim has been through that filter I'll believe it, and if it hasn't I'm at maybe 50/50 at best. (3) I'm not a Bayesian in practice. Priors feel like a license to smuggle in what you already hoped was true, and I don't want my conclusions to depend on my guess; long-run error control is the thing a method can actually promise. (4) That said, I like the proposal floating around to drop the default threshold by an order of magnitude (0.05 to 0.005), and I like preregistration as the main institutional fix. (5) I think the field should de-emphasize dichotomous significant/not-significant verdicts in favour of reporting effect sizes and interval estimates. (6) I also think exploratory analysis is where the interesting findings actually come from, and I don't want a culture where nobody is allowed to look at their own data until they've committed to an analysis plan.

And here's the bit I've been pushing in the group that nobody has quite shot down yet — I call it search-adjusted thresholds. The idea: how suspicious you should be of a nominally significant result ought to scale with how searchy the literature it comes from is, not just with the design of the single study. A p = 0.04 from a clean pre-registered test of a prediction nobody had thought to look for is one thing; the same number from a subfield where everyone runs twenty outcomes and four exclusion criteria and reports whichever one cleared the bar is quite another, even if the paper in front of you is itself preregistered, because the whole literature's habits are what generated the hypothesis you're now testing. So the evidential bar should rise with the forking density of the surrounding research area. My statistician friend says this is just a prior wearing a trenchcoat, and that once you start estimating forking density you're doing exactly the thing in (3) that I say I don't want to do. I think it's a correction for a measurable feature of a procedure and it can in principle be read off the design, not off my hopes. Who's closer to right?

What I want is an audit of the whole package, not a lecture on any single item: which of these actually collide with each other, which only look like they do and dissolve once you cash them out, and for each real collision what the cheapest fix would be and what it would cost me. Rank the tensions by how bad they are rather than flagging all of them as equally fatal, and tell me if there's a single revision that would clean up several at once. Also, please seperate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this — flag plainly when something you're telling me is one side of a live argument instead of smoothing it into the consensus view — and where you're reading a commitment into me that I didn't actually state, say what you're assuming so I can disown it if it isn't mine.
