I teach a first-year undergraduate module on formal systems, computation and cognition — 22 students, roughly half philosophy and half computer science, one 80-minute session, no prerequisites past school mathematics. The session is the one where we meet the MIU system (alphabet M, I, U; axiom MI; rules: from xI derive xIU, from Mx derive Mxx, from xIIIy derive xUy, from xUUy derive xy; target MU) and the idea of jumping out of the system. I can already lecture this; what I want instead is a game in which students do the jumping before they can name it.

Design a runnable in-class game in which, for the first part of the session, students are simply inside the system — applying rules in teams of three or four, chasing MU — and in which the move to reasoning about the system is forced on them by the situation rather than announced by me. Constraints I care about: no lecture up front, and the words invariant, meta, proof, and jump out of the system must not be spoken until the debrief; the facilitator keeps a public ledger (board or shared sheet) of every string the room derives, and that ledger rather than my hints is what makes the outside view findable; and there is a points scheme needing no money under which, for the target that cannot be reached, keep grinding is rationally worse than stop and work out why it cannot be done, while a blanket policy of declaring impossibility is not profitable either — one target is derivable in a few steps, and one is a target that the invariant they will find says nothing about.

Give me: the rules of the game and the materials; a minute-by-minute timeline; three or four target strings with an answer key that settles each one by derivation or by impossibility argument; the template you would hand them, once they are stuck, for an impossibility certificate — a property the axiom has, every rule preserves, and the target lacks — together with the clause that rules out vacuous properties such as is derivable; a last-ten-minutes twist in which I alter one rule and their certificate visibly breaks, with what is actually true about MU in the amended system; a debrief that maps specific moments onto the contrast between working inside and reasoning outside, including the asymmetry that an outside argument can establish that something is not derivable while its silence is not evidence that it is; what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each; and an honest statement of what the game cannot reproduce and I will have to say in so many words. Lead with the core mechanism of the game in one sentence, then give the rest.
