I am a professor of philosophy. I have taught social choice for years and I know Sen's impossibility result about a Paretian liberal as well as I know anything — the three-option construction in which each of two people has a protected say over a pair that touches only his own conduct, while everyone agrees about the remaining pair, so that the social preference comes out with no best element. Please do not explain the result to me. What I have never managed to do, and what I am asking for, is to convert it into something I can use in a room.

For three years I have been the only philosopher on the release-governance board of the Aldergrove Health Data Trust, a federation of eleven member clinics that pool de-identified records for approved research. We meet for ninety minutes twice a month, we handle eight or nine release requests a year, our decisions bind the trust, each one must be defended in a single paragraph published in the annual report to the member clinics and to our funder, and a decision can be struck down by the annual assembly of member clinics — so whatever rule we use has to survive being read aloud to the clinic whose claim it overrides. The other eight members are data stewards, two clinicians and two patient representatives; they are entitled to a rule they can run without me in the chair. The charter I inherited contains two clauses that I now read as Sen's two conditions in plain dress. Clause L: each member clinic has a recognised personal sphere, namely the inclusion or exclusion of its own patients' records in a given release, and within that sphere that clinic's own preference is the trust's preference. Clause P: if every member clinic prefers one option to another, the trust may not select the latter. And there is always a status-quo option, no release at all. Last spring the clauses came apart in front of us. A, a large clinic holding the only dataset in the federation big enough to answer the research question, invoked Clause L against the use of its own records, citing commitments made to its patients; B, a small clinic whose researchers need the publication, invoked Clause L in favour of including its own; and every member of the board, A and B included, said it would rather have the release built on A's data than on B's, since a study powered by B's cohort alone would be underpowered and possibly misleading. We then discovered that no option was preferred by the trust to every other, and that when we voted the options pairwise in the order they happened to appear on the agenda we got no release, while in the reverse order we got the release built on A's data. We postponed, twice, and eventually let the request lapse.

What I need from you is a written procedure the board can actually run in ninety minutes, with the theory stripped out of it: ordered steps with explicit stopping points, beginning with a screening test, stated in terms a data steward could apply, for whether a request is in the Sen configuration at all — what pattern of protected pairs, status quo and unanimity has to be present — together with an instruction to stop and apply the ordinary rules when it is not. Then, when it is, a ranked sequence of escapes with triggers, each labelled with which clause it relaxes: narrowing the agenda so that some comparisons are declared not social questions at all; screening out preferences that range over another clinic's sphere, with a test for genuine meddlesomeness that cannot be used merely to disqualify whichever clinic is causing the difficulty; applying Clause P first as a filter and Clause L only within what survives, which I strongly suspect is Clause L retained in wording only, and I want to be told whether I am right; imposing a fixed priority order among the personal spheres, chosen in advance by rotation, lottery or seniority, which I suspect relocates the arbitrariness rather than removing it; fixing an agenda once and for all and conceding that whoever sets it decides; and returning to the status quo, including when that is the correct answer rather than a failure of nerve, and how to write the paragraph so that it does not read as one. I also need to know what the procedure does about enlarging the option space — splitting a release, adding safeguards, releasing into an enclave instead — and what it does under partial information, since in practice we have four or five options and only partial rankings, clinics state their views loosely or strategically or refuse to rank options they regard as off the table, and I need to know what may and may not be inferred from silence, and how a decoy option inserted to break a cycle is to be told apart from a genuine alternative. Mark every point at which judgement cannot be eliminated as such, say which of those the board must avow in its published paragraph rather than bury in the machinery, and say plainly what the procedure cannot do.

Then run the finished procedure on two further cases. First: two of our clinics jointly control a merged registry created from a shared electronic-record instance during a merger, and each claims the Clause L say over the same pair, whether those records go into the release — one protected say too many over one pair, which is not the Sen configuration at all, and I want to know whether the procedure treats it as a charter gap, escalates it, hands it to the priority ladder, or refuses to act, and what the published paragraph must concede in each case. Second: C, a clinic governed by a community data protocol, refuses to allow its own records into any release that also contains D's records, and says it would rather have no release at all than one that includes D's, while D's records sit in the option that every other member most prefers. Here the objection is conditional on what happens inside another clinic's sphere, and I expect your procedure to tell me that the part of C's claim about its own records is protected and the part about D's is not — an answer my two patient representatives will hate, since they think the relational objection is exactly what Clause L was written for. Give me the unpalatable answer in at least one of these two cases rather than smoothing it over, and if the procedure forces a choice between a narrow reading of personal sphere that guts Clause L and a wide reading that makes nearly every preference protected, say which reading it commits me to, what it costs, and flag it as a place where the procedure runs out and a person has to decide. If I must choose, I would rather keep Clause L than Clause P; tell me what that costs me too.
