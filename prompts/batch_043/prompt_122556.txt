I work as a backend engineer at a mid-sized software firm and I have no formal training in philosophy, so I should be grateful if you defined any term of art you need rather than assuming it.

A close colleague of mine — I'll call her Priya — has been a lifelong pacifist for as long as I've known her; she is a Quaker and attends a local meeting. Over roughly fifteen years of arguments over lunch I have collected a fairly stable set of verdicts from her, and I now have a case she has never ruled on, in which I am personally involved. I want your help working out what she is likely to say and, more importantly, what rule she is running.

Her verdicts so far, as accurately as I can report them:

1. She would have refused to bear arms in the Second World War and says she would have accepted imprisonment rather than serve — but she has never once been willing to say that those who did fight did anything wrong.
2. She holds that a German officer who shot Hitler in 1943 would have done nothing wrong, and — the part I have never been able to square with the first — she doubts she would have done it herself, and speaks of that doubt as a failing of nerve rather than as a matter of principle.
3. She believes Western governments were right to supply Ukraine with weapons after the full-scale invasion, and has given money to a fund buying anti-drone equipment; she also says she could not have fired any of it herself.
4. She thinks an armed police officer who shoots a hostage-taker who is about to kill a hostage acts rightly and should face no sanction; she thinks a householder who shoots a burglar who is already fleeing acts wrongly.
5. She is a vegan and holds that killing an animal for food is wrong even when it is painless; she thinks nothing of killing a wasp that comes in through the window.
6. She has argued that economic sanctions which cause widespread hunger are violence conducted by other means, and has opposed them on that basis.
7. She left her previous job rather than write software that generated target coordinates for artillery, but stayed for two years maintaining logistics and payroll systems for the same armed forces; her distinction, as she put it to me, was that the second is a job and the first is pulling a trigger through a computer.
8. She opposes capital punishment even for the worst offenders — punishment, she says, is not defence.

The new case. My employer has been offered a contract to write the engagement software for a fixed counter-drone system defending a city of about a million people. The system detects inbound one-way attack drones and launches a small interceptor that destroys them by colliding with them. No human selects the individual target: an operator designates a defended zone and can switch the system off, but the choice to engage each drone, and the moment of engagement, are made by the software. On the current pattern there is no pilot at risk inside the incoming drones; the people flying them sit in another country and are unharmed. The debris has to come down somewhere, and our own risk model puts it at a small number of serious injuries or deaths among the city's inhabitants over the life of the system — smaller by two orders of magnitude than the deaths expected without the system. A non-kinetic alternative exists: a jamming and spoofing suite, cheaper to build, effective against most but not all incoming types, and useless against the wire-guided ones now being used. The customer will not buy it. The detection component we would write could, under the contract's terms, be reused by the customer in an offensive drone swarm, and we would not be told if it were.

I have been assigned to the team writing the engagement logic. I could ask to be moved to the logistics side of the programme at modest cost to my career.

What I would like from you:

1. State, properly, the two or three rules that best fit her eight verdicts. The candidates I can vaguely make out are something like a personal-vocation view on which she must not kill but will not condemn those who do; a lesser-evil view on which defensive violence is justified when it prevents much more violence, with her own abstention treated as a weakness or as a discipline rather than as a duty; and some rule keyed to imminence and to whether the person killed is culpably threatening. But I don't trust my own formulations, and I suspect there are others I haven't thought of — including rules that fit her verdicts at least as well as mine do.

2. For each rule you identify, say what it predicts about: (a) whether it is permissible for a democratically accountable state to build and operate the system at all; (b) whether it is permissible for Priya to write the engagement logic; (c) whether it is permissible for me to write it, given that I share none of her scruples; and (d) whether any of those answers changes if a human operator must confirm each engagement, if the non-kinetic suite is substituted, if the expected debris casualties were ten times higher, or if the detection component is in fact reused in an offensive swarm.

3. Give your best guess about (a), (b) and (c), with your reasons, and tell me how much confidence I should attach to each.

4. Name the single extra fact or hypothetical change to the case that would best tell the leading two rules apart, and say what each of them predicts about it. I am thinking of variations such as a human in the loop, a machine rather than a person as the target, or foreseen rather than intended deaths on the ground — but tell me which one actually does the discriminating work and why.

5. If her past verdicts genuinely underdetermine her answer, say which way consistency pulls, and be explicit about what she would have to give up in each direction.

Finally, and briefly: is there anything in the rules you identify that ought to move me, who shares none of her commitments, or is the whole apparatus simply irrelevant to someone who never accepted pacifism in the first place?
