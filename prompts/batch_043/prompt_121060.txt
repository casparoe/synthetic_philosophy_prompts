I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of mind and cognitive science: seventeen students, one ninety-minute session, a room with movable chairs and two long tables, and nothing I cannot print in advance. I want to spend one session on whether a collection of agents can itself be a subject — whether the group can believe, want, and mean things that none of its members can, and whether there is a fact of the matter about what it believes. This is the territory of the ant-colony chapter of Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach, where the individual insects are as dumb as can be while the colony they compose is the intelligent and entertaining one; but I do not want the session to be about that chapter. I want the students to spend most of the hour inside a system with that structure and only discover, in the last twenty minutes, what they were inside. No lecture up front, and no one should hear the words holism, reductionism, emergence, supervenience, intentionality, or the name of the chapter until the debrief.

Here is the skeleton I have in mind; replace it if you have something better, but keep the load-bearing feature, which is that the students do something and the puzzle arrives as an experience of their own failure or discomfort rather than as a thesis.

The class is split into two colonies of six or seven ants, plus one reader per colony. The colony sits at a table with a grid of maybe thirty-six printed tiles, each bearing a simple mark. Every ant has a rule card she may not show anyone, saying things of the form: when the tile in front of you and the tile to your left disagree in such-and-such a way, rotate the tile in front of you; when they agree, move one square clockwise. She may see only a two-by-two window of the board at any moment, may touch only the tile directly in front of her, and may not speak, gesture, or look at another ant's card. The colony's utterance is the final configuration of the board when time is called. The reader, who has been outside the room or facing the wall and has not seen the question, then comes in and reads the configuration aloud, translating it into a sentence using a vocabulary of eight or ten permitted messages that I hand her. The colony's job is to answer a question — a quantity, a location, a shape — and success is scored only by whether the reader recovers it. Ants privately wager plastic counters each round on whether the reader will get it, which lets me exploit their overconfidence without any real stakes.

What I want from you:

One. The actual artefact. Write the rule cards, the tile markings, the permitted-message vocabulary, the scoring, and the questions for each round, at a level of detail where I could photocopy your answer and run the session tomorrow. Show me a short trace of one round — board state, which rule fires, next board state — that establishes the local rules really do suffice to produce the answer without any single ant ever representing it.

Two. A sequence of rounds, each of which is a small experiment the students perform on their own colony, arranged so that they generate the arguments themselves before anyone names them. I have four in mind and want your judgement on which are load-bearing and which to cut for time: a shuffle round, in which the rule cards are redistributed at random among the same ants before a fresh question, so that whatever survives is a property of the organisation and not of the individuals; a lesion round, in which I secretly freeze the two ants whose cards have been firing most often, or remove a card from circulation entirely, so that the group discovers for itself whether its competence is localised or spread out and how gracefully it degrades; a dictator round, in which one ant is given full view of the board but may alter only one tile per turn, so the group can feel whether a global controller is genuinely required for agency or merely looks required; and an autobiography round, in which the colony must produce a first-person account of how it reached its answer, one word per ant per turn, each ant writing only from what her window shows her, which the reader then reads aloud.

Three. The round I am most attached to and want you to sharpen: after one utterance has been produced, a second reader with a different vocabulary list reads the very same final configuration and produces a different, equally coherent message. I want the students to be forced to answer, in the moment, whether the colony failed, whether one of the readers failed, or whether the colony never said anything determinate at all — and I want them to notice that nothing they can inspect settles it. Design that round so the two readings are both genuinely supported, not one obviously forced.

Four. What they should come away able to separate, because my experience is that they run all of it together: the claim that the group is intelligent in the sense of getting things right; the claim that its states are about something; the claim that there is something it is like to be the group; and the claim that there is a determinate answer to what the group meant. The debrief should map specific moments in the activity — a particular round, a particular thing someone will say — onto each of these four, and should also deliver the uncomfortable move that a first-personal report can be produced by parts with no access to the whole, so that the students' own introspective certainty cannot settle whether they are colonies.

Five. Honesty about the ceiling. Tell me what goes wrong the first time I run this: the strong student who solves the encoding and stops being an ant, the drift into it is all interpretation so nothing is true, the lesion round landing as a punishment, the group that talks its way around the no-speaking rule, the round that eats fifteen minutes more than it should. Tell me how to handle each. And tell me, plainly, which feature of the question the activity cannot reproduce and I must supply in the debrief — I expect the gaps are the difference in scale and speed between a colony and a brain, the fact that real colony-level function is a product of selection whereas my colony's function is imposed by rules I wrote, and anything to do with whether there is a subject there at all rather than merely a competent arrangement.

Six. A one-minute written exit ticket that is not a self-report: a forced choice or a small task whose pattern of answers tells me the next day whether the hour worked. Also give me a variant of the whole thing for a forty-person lecture section and one for a reading group of eight, and say which part of the design does not survive those changes.

Assume I have no library access and no interest in being pointed at further reading; I want the design, the materials, and the reasoning, not a list of sources.
