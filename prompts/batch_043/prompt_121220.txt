You are a professional philosopher with a second specialization in the philosophy of the social sciences and in causal inference. You are careful, you do not bluff, and you make your assumptions explicit. Before you write anything that looks like an answer, take a short planning pass: lay out the candidate mechanisms you intend to consider, the tests you will apply to them, the respects in which you expect to be uncertain, and only then build the analysis. I would rather have a slower, disciplined answer than a fluent one. Reason carefully, and say nothing you cannot defend.

The topic is the one I teach under the heading of culture as a cause: Weber's Protestant ethic thesis, and the general question of what has to be in place before an outcome can be explained by appeal to values. I want a case analysis running in both directions — the theory illuminating the case, the case testing the theory — not a survey of literature. Please do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations of any kind; and do not rest your answer on factual claims beyond the ones I sketch below. Where you need a further fact, state it plainly and flag it as an assumption you could not check.

The episode is the long East Asian growth episode and its reversal. Between roughly 1965 and the mid-1990s, South Korea, Singapore, Taiwan and Hong Kong industrialized at rates without precedent in the historical record, and a widely repeated explanation credited this to a Confucian inheritance: thrift and deferred consumption, discipline, respect for education, deference to hierarchy, family obligation, preference for group over individual. The same values were invoked politically in the 1990s Asian values arguments associated with Lee Kuan Yew, as a rationale for political arrangements thought to be required by them. Then came the financial crisis of 1997–98: capital flight, currency collapses, IMF programmes, and an equally rapid re-description of the very same traits — deference to hierarchy and family obligation now appearing as crony capitalism, connected lending, guanxi and opacity, and thrift now appearing as demand deficiency. Note also the divergence inside the supposed cultural zone: Korea, with its chaebol and its state-directed credit, was among the hardest hit and went into an IMF programme; Singapore, at least as plausibly Confucian, was not.

Here is the apparatus I want you to use, some of which is Weber's and some of which is mine.

First, specify the cause. Say exactly which value, held by whom and how widely, with what distribution across the relevant population, and how its presence and intensity could in principle be registered independently of the outcome it is supposed to explain.

Second, distinguish four channels by which a value might enter a causal story, and state which one each version of the culture claim actually relies on: (i) the dispositional channel, in which an internalized ethos shapes conduct directly, as inner-worldly asceticism supposedly shaped the conduct of Weber's ascetic Protestants; (ii) the sorting channel, in which the ethos affects who enters, remains in, or is admitted to which roles; (iii) the legitimation channel, in which values are invoked to justify or license conduct already driven by something else; (iv) the coordination channel, in which shared expectations make one equilibrium rather than another focal.

Third, apply Weber's own methodological vocabulary, in your own words: the ideal type, and the counterfactual-probability test he associates with objective possibility and adequate causation, according to which imputing a factor as a cause requires a judgment about how the outcome would likely have gone without it. Say what that test demands here, and whether this case can satisfy it.

Fourth, apply what I call the Janus test: a value that is cited to explain both the success and the failure of the same societies needs to be decomposed into a mechanism plus a context, or else it explains nothing. Say what decomposition, if any, rescues the Confucian explanation.

Then I want the analysis proper, covering at least these: whether the dispositional version or the legitimation version is the more charitable reading of the Confucian thesis, and why; the objection that the values are old while the growth is new, together with an honest comparison to the analogous objection Weber's own thesis faces in the gap between the sixteenth-century ethos and the later industrial breakthrough; how much weight the Korea–Singapore divergence and the 1997 reversal can bear, given that a crisis is one observation and not a controlled trial; and finally whether the episode is a confirmation of culture-as-cause, an anomaly for it, or a case the theory never anticipated — for instance a case where values do causal work as coordination devices or as institutions rather than as dispositions.

Please also steelman the strongest rival family of explanations — the developmental state, export discipline, the security umbrella and access to American markets, demographics, mass education, technology transfer — and then say precisely what would have to be true for a values-based claim to survive alongside it rather than be displaced by it. Be equally clear about where Weber's apparatus is silent: name at least two features of this episode it has no resources to address, and do not force it to speak.

Close with two short items. First: describe one observation, which you would not need a library to imagine, that would discriminate between the dispositional and the legitimation channels in this case, and say which way you would expect it to cut. Second: a single paragraph of verdict — if you had to wager, is there any defensible causal claim about values in this episode, and what is the most specific form of that claim you would be willing to put money on.
