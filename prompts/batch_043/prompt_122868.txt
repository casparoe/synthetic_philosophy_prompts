I sit on the embryo-selection advisory committee at a hospital fertility clinic. I am the non-clinical member, a philosopher by training, and we have been asked finally to write down the rule we are actually applying, because we have been deciding case by case and I suspect we are not being consistent. Here is our track record. We have always approved discarding embryos with a fully penetrant, severe, early-onset genetic condition. We approved discarding an embryo carrying a high-penetrance mutation for a serious condition that would not onset until the fifth decade, over the objection that four good decades is not nothing. We approved, after mandatory counselling, a couple's request to avoid the embryo carrying roughly a fourfold elevation in polygenic risk for a serious psychiatric illness. We approved with conditions the selection of an HLA-compatible embryo for a couple whose existing child needed a transplant, on the ground that the later donation would not set back the new child. We refused sex selection for family balancing. We refused a deaf couple's request to select an embryo likely to be deaf like them, and the minutes give the ground as deliberately closing off a major capacity being different from declining to maximise. We refused a request to avoid an embryo scoring high on a same-sex-sexual-behaviour index, on the ground that the only expected downside was other people's prejudice. We refused a request to choose the embryo with the highest score on a cognitive-and-educational-attainment index in a cycle where no embryo carried any elevated disease risk, and the minutes record three competing grounds without saying which carried the day: that the expected difference was within the score's error, that the advantage was competitive rather than a benefit to the child, and that we do not do enhancement. For completeness: when couples simply name an embryo in an unremarkable cycle, we transfer it and do not ask why.

The case in front of us involves three embryos, none with any diagnosable monogenic condition. Embryo A carries psychiatric risk at about twice baseline, scores in the top decile on a pain-reactivity composite, and is middling on the attainment index. Embryo B carries psychiatric risk at about six times baseline, scores in the bottom quintile on pain reactivity, and is lowest of the three on attainment though nowhere near the abnormal range. Embryo C carries psychiatric risk at about six times baseline, is middling on pain reactivity, and is top-quintile on attainment. The parents request embryo B. Both of them live with chronic pain conditions and say they want to spare a child the thing that has been hardest in their own lives; they accept the higher psychiatric risk and say they do not care about attainment. Their counsellor has told them two things that I think matter more than they realise. First, the true between-embryo spread on all three indices is probably smaller than the laboratory's headline figures, which are derived from comparisons among unrelated people, because siblings share so much genetic and environmental background. Second, if we refuse B they will probably not settle for A: they will pay for another retrieval, so the alternative to transferring B may be no child from this cycle rather than a child with A's profile. The pain-reactivity composite is also new to us. It is not diagnostic of anything, the laboratory markets it as a wellness product rather than a medical test, and what it predicts is suffering rather than disease.

What I want from you is this. Set out the candidate rules that fit our whole track record — at least three, each stated sharply enough to be applied to a new file — and say what each predicts about this request. Then give me your best guess about what we will in fact do, and, separately, what we should do if coherence with our own precedents is the goal: identify the closest analogue among the precedents, say how much weight it can really bear, and be honest about which principle we would be committing ourselves to with each available choice. Three further things puzzle me and I would like them addressed rather than smoothed over. Should the attenuation point and the another-cycle counterfactual change the class of outcomes we are comparing at all, and does that differ depending on which rule we are running? Can a rule that turns on the reasons parents give be stable, given that we routinely transfer a nominated embryo without asking for any? And can the distinction between a trait that makes a life worse intrinsically and a trait that makes it worse only because of how others respond — which seems to be carrying all the weight in our seventh and eighth precedents — bear that weight, or was our refusal in the seventh really a judgement about the kind of people the parents were revealing themselves to be? Finally, give me one further hypothetical case we could put in front of ourselves that would separate whichever two rules you think are most likely to be the real ones.
