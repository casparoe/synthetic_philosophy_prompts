I'm a nurse practitioner at a big safety-net hospital and I've somehow ended up on the committee that has to sign off on any predictive model before it goes anywhere near patient care. I got put on it because I complained, loudly, when I found out our old sepsis alert was firing at noticeably different rates on my clinic's patients than on the privately insured patients upstairs.

Our first real decision lands in two weeks, and there'll be more after it, so I want a procedure and not just advice about this one model. Here's the case. Care management wants a model that scores every patient at discharge on their risk of an "avoidable" readmission within 30 days, so they can hand out the 40 care-coordination slots they have each month to the top-scoring patients out of roughly 900 discharges. The vendor says the model is "demographic-blind" — it doesn't take race or insurance type as an input — but it does take zip code, prior utilisation, number of previous missed appointments, and a comorbidity score built partly from billing codes, and I do not need anyone to explain to me why that's not the same thing as blind.

The thing I actually need: an ordered decision procedure my committee can run on a proposal like this. For each step, what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure just stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a human because the question is one of values rather than facts. Specific things it has to deal with, because these are the bits I keep getting lost in:

- We can only give the intervention to 40 people. It's a ranking with a fixed budget, not a yes/no threshold. I think that changes which fairness constraints are even coherent and which ones quietly stop mattering, but I can't get anyone to say why in a sentence I can repeat.
- The label is contaminated two ways that I can see: readmissions only get counted if the patient comes back to *us*, and patients who got care coordination last year were less likely to be counted as readmitting. So the training target partly records our own past behaviour. If that kills the proposal, I want the procedure to say so early rather than at the end.
- Our compliance people won't let us use race as an input and won't let us collect it even to audit with. We do have insurance type, preferred language, age and zip. So tell me how to audit anything at all when the group you care about is one you're not allowed to look at, and be honest about what that workaround buys and what it doesn't.
- There's apparently a result — someone mentioned it to me and I've been unable to reconstruct it — to the effect that a score can't in general satisfy all of these at once: (a) the score means the same thing for people in different groups, (b) the same share of people who really would readmit get flagged in each group, (c) of the people flagged, the same share really would readmit in each group, (d) the same share of people who would *not* have readmitted get flagged in each group — except in special cases like equal base rates or a perfect predictor. My committee needs to be walked to the point where it has to *choose* which of these to give up, on the basis of who eats which error, rather than being allowed to nod along to all four.

Constraints on the document: eight people on the committee (two clinicians, one data scientist, one lawyer, two patient reps, a finance person and me), so no notation, nothing that requires knowing what a false positive rate is without being told; the whole thing has to be run in a two-hour meeting; and it has to be honest about which steps are judgement calls dressed up as procedure.

Then run it on three things: the readmission model above; a proposal to overbook clinic slots for patients predicted to miss their appointment; and, as a contrast case, a tool that only reorders the radiology reading queue with no fixed budget. I want at least one of those to come out as no, and I'd rather find out now that the no is going to land on the overbooking one than be told so politely.
