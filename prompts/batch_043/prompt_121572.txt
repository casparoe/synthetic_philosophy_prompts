Hey — I'm the PhD-student rep on the program committee for the Meridian Foundation's "Progress & Method" stream (we hand out one three-year grant a year, about $1.4M, and our rules let us split it into two 18-month scoping grants instead if we genuinely can't pick). We're down to two finalists, the full committee votes in twelve days, and I've been asked to draft the memo that goes out with the agenda — with the wrinkle that both applicants get to read it and send a one-page rebuttal before the vote, so it can't be vague or hand-wavy. Proposal A ("Atlas", a formal epistemologist plus two postdocs) wants to build a working toolkit that scores competing models for closeness-to-truth: you fix a finite partition of the space of possible states of the target system, each theory is identified with the set of cells it leaves open, and the score has two components — if the actual state falls inside the theory's permitted set, the theory is rewarded for ruling out more of the remaining space, and if the actual state falls outside, it's penalized by how far the actual state lies from the nearest permitted cell (on some metric over the space that they propose to choose per-case). They'd validate it on a fixed menu of historical pairs — Ptolemaic vs Copernican, phlogiston vs oxygen, Newtonian vs relativistic gravitation — and then apply it to one live case from our own portfolio (two competing research programs we fund on cellular aging, where the renewal decision is genuinely contested). Their pitch is that funders talk about "progress" constantly and have no way to measure it, and this would give them a number. Proposal B ("Cassandra", one senior philosopher, cheap — mostly salary plus a workshop) says the enterprise is bankrupt in principle, for two reasons it claims compound: first, that everything in A's score is carving-relative — redo the partition in a logically equivalent vocabulary with finer or coarser cells and the ranking flips, and the choice of metric on the space is doing most of the work, so the toolkit doesn't measure closeness-to-truth so much as record the analyst's choices; second, the older and nastier objection (the one usually credited to Miller and Grünbaum) that Popper's own cash-out of verisimilitude — roughly truth-content minus falsity-content — never actually worked, because once a theory is false, adding content only adds new ways to be wrong, so the least informative false theory comes out closest to the truth and no false theory comes out closer to it than the trivial one, which means the measure can't do the single job it was invented for, namely underwriting the judgment that Einstein got closer than Newton. B wants to spend the money instead on a project that replaces truthlikeness with purely instrumental and predictive criteria and produces an argument that funding agencies should stop talking about getting closer to the truth at all.

The committee dynamics are making this harder than the proposals themselves. Our astrophysicist says essentially "nobody doubts GR is closer to the truth than Newtonian gravity, so either 'progress' means something or this whole stream is cosplay — just fund the toolkit"; I think that's question-begging in exactly the way the grant is supposed to investigate, but I also can't tell whether he's wrong or just impatient. Separately, our statistician has floated a compromise I keep going back and forth on: drop distance-to-the-truth as unmeasurable and instead fund a proxy — a model's expected predictive accuracy at the limit of inquiry, estimated from its track record under a proper scoring rule — on the grounds that this is all the foundation really needs for renewal decisions and that unlike verisimilitude it's something we can actually estimate. My worry is that a model can match every observation we're capable of making and still be badly wrong about the parts of the space we can't probe, so the proxy and the real thing can come apart, but I have no sense of how wide that gap can get or whether it widens on the cases on their menu. And there's an institutional trap I want handled honestly rather than quietly: B's conclusion, taken seriously, seems to undercut our stream's own founding language ("high-risk projects that get us closer to the truth"), and our program officer has off the record hinted she'd rather we didn't fund a project whose main output is an argument that our founding language is nonsense — I don't know if that's a legitimate thing for us to weigh or just self-protection dressed up.

So: write the memo, ~900 words, addressed to the committee. Give me a bottom line — fund A, fund B, split into two scoping grants, or fund neither and re-open — plus the reasoning, and make the reasoning the kind of thing the losing applicant could actually respond to rather than a restatement of our priors. State the strongest version of the losing side's case, ideally better than the losing proposal states it itself, and tell me which of the three live disputes I sketched (carving-relativity, the Popperian collapse for false theories, the end-of-inquiry proxy) actually carries the recommendation and which is a red herring we're wasting meeting time on. Also give me two or three concrete milestones that would count as the funded project having *failed* — not "papers published", real ones — and a view on whether the live aging-research case should be in scope at all or whether including it poisons the validation. If you think the decision turns on something the committee could take a stance on rather than kick to the applicants (e.g. whether we should require invariance under reconceptualization as a hard constraint, and what exactly that would rule out), say which and how you'd have us resolve it. Please argue it from scratch — the committee knows the literature, so no citations or reading lists are needed, and anything you attribute to a named figure should be something you can defend on its merits rather than on authority.
