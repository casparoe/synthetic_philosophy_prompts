I'm a junior, and in two weeks I'm leading the forty-minute discussion at my school's philosophy club on the distinction between frame, outer, and inner messages from Hofstadter's *Gödel, Escher, Bach* — roughly, the three levels at which an object can be said to carry a message: that it is a message at all, what sort of code or structure it is to be read with, and what it says once that code is applied. Nobody in the room will have read the book, and I don't want to run the session as a lecture. Please don't explain the distinction to me; I want a plan for getting a dozen juniors and seniors to the point where they can apply it to cases I haven't handed them.

The parts I most need help with:

**Order and dependency.** My instinct is that before I name the three levels I have to establish that the same content can ride on completely different carriers — a vinyl record, an audio file, a page of sheet music — and that no property of the carrier by itself tells you how to read it. So I want to know what has to be in place, and in what sequence, before the three labels do any work, and which step collapses if I reorder it.

**The carrying example.** My candidate is that record / file / sheet music trio, with someone in the room who doesn't read music, so that the outer level becomes visible by failing. Tell me whether this is the right spine, and what such an example must have and must lack. In particular: must the example have been produced by a sender with intentions, or does a natural object — tree rings, a fossil, a genetic sequence — make the contrast sharper? And does it need a case where the frame level itself is in doubt, one where a reasonable person could take the object for junk rather than for an unread message?

**The misconceptions, in the order my club will form them, and the exact point in the sequence to head off each.** My guesses: that frame, outer, and inner map cleanly onto object, code, and meaning; that the inner message is what is really there and the other two are only packaging; that if the receiver's machinery supplies half the content, then anything can mean anything; and the slide into imagining the whole thing as three nested boxes, like an onion. I also expect someone to say that the inner message is just the interpretation, and I can't tell whether that is a mistake or the point.

**A distinction of my own, which I'd like you to place or discard.** I've been sorting failures into three kinds: frame loss, where nothing in the object announces it as a message; outer loss, where you know it's a message and you have a key, but you can't tell which part is key and which part is payload; and inner loss, where you extract a clean, well-formed content and have no way of telling whether it is the intended one. Does this track the three levels, is one of the three a different kind of thing entirely, and if you'd keep it, where in the forty minutes does it belong? Relatedly, I want to close with an audio recording of someone reading aloud the operating instructions for a record player, where the content you recover is what you need in order to recover it — but I'm not sure whether that's a genuine case of the distinction looping back on itself or just a cute puzzle.

**The closing check.** One question or small task whose answer would show me they've understood the distinction rather than picked up the vocabulary — ideally something that forces them to decide whether a case has an inner message at all, not merely to label one.

**And what I should decline to do at this level, and say out loud that I'm declining.** I assume that means the book's connections to formal systems and self-reference, and any general thesis about whether meaning is really in the object or in the mind — but tell me what else I should refuse, and how to refuse it in a way that doesn't make the session feel evasive.
