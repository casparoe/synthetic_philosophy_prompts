I spent last night arguing with an old friend — he is writing a dissertation on Kant, I work mostly on epistemology — about whether transcendental arguments do any real work against scepticism. He thinks the familiar objections are evasions. I think the form proves too much, and I tried to show him why. He replied that my argument, if it worked, would take down a good deal more than transcendental arguments, and I am no longer sure where the machinery seizes. I have written it out below as a chain of steps, partly his reconstruction and partly mine. I would be grateful for a diagnosis rather than a verdict: which step do you reject, why does that step look compelling, and what does rejecting it cost?

(1) Call an argument transcendental when it has this shape: F is a necessary condition of the possibility of E, where E is something the sceptic must grant — experience, judgement, self-consciousness, doubt, meaning something by one's words — therefore F obtains. The attraction of the form is that its premise looks immune to sceptical doubt while its conclusion looks substantive enough to answer the sceptic.

(2) The force of any such argument is entirely a function of what kind of necessity is claimed in the premise. There appear to be three candidates: (a) conceptual-constitutive necessity — we cannot make sense of E except by conceiving of things as F; (b) architectural necessity — beings with a cognitive architecture like ours cannot have E unless F; (c) metaphysical necessity — E cannot occur for any possible subject at all unless F obtains.

(3) (a) is dialectically inert. From (a) you get at most that we cannot help representing things as F, and that we cannot coherently judge that they are not F while we are engaged in the practice in question. The sceptic grants both. Her claim was never that we can stop representing things as F; it was that the representation may be systematically false. So a transcendental argument needs more than (a).

(4) (b) looks more promising, because the sceptic's own scenarios — the demon, the vat, the simulation — normally hold our architecture fixed and vary the world, so the conditional detaches: given the architecture, F. But the conditional itself is something we know only by reflecting on our own experience, that is, by exercising the very faculty whose reliability is in question. A premise established through a suspect source cannot be used to certify that source. So (b) is circular in the way that matters here.

(5) So a transcendental argument with any anti-sceptical force must be claiming (c): the necessity is not relative to how we happen to be built, but holds of experience as such, and that is what lets the conclusion detach without any conditional residue.

(6) But (c) has a feature that should worry us. Take any feature F that we find indispensable to experience. From the first-person standpoint there is no possible experience that would distinguish two hypotheses: that F is indispensable because the world could not be otherwise, and that F is indispensable because any subject capable of experience must format its experience in that way. Call this the indistinguishability of format and object. If it holds, then we are never in a position to tell whether a candidate condition of possibility has the modal force (c) requires, and the claim that it does is groundless rather than merely unproven.

(7) And there is positive reason to think the claim is often false. Features that were once offered as conditions of the possibility of experience have subsequently been given up: that spatial intuition must be Euclidean, that experience must present its objects as determinately located and determinately causally related, that the objects of experience must be persisting substances. If any transcendental argument had ever been sound, its conclusion would have had the status of a necessary truth, and these revisions would have been not merely mistaken but inconceivable. Since we treat them as genuine revisions — hard-won, empirical, and in some cases conceptual — no claim of the form experience requires X can carry the modal weight a transcendental argument needs.

(8) Worse, the form looks promiscuous in a way that lets it deliver incompatible conclusions. Consider two arguments of exactly the shape described in (1). First: having experience at all requires drawing the distinction between how things seem and how they are; that distinction collapses unless there is a mind-independent order that constrains how things seem; so there is a mind-independent order. Second: whatever constrains experience must be able to show up within experience as a constraint; nothing can constrain experience from outside all possible experience; so the mind-independent order the first argument concludes to is either experienceable, and not mind-independent in the sense required, or empty. Both arguments look transcendental; their conclusions are incompatible; and I cannot see a non-question-begging principle that admits the first and excludes the second. A form that yields a conclusion and its rival on neighbouring subject matter proves nothing.

(9) So: transcendental arguments are either restricted to (a) or (b), in which case they leave the sceptic exactly where she was, or they aspire to (c), in which case they are empirically refuted by the revisability recorded in (7) and dialectically unlimited in the way (8) describes. On either reading they do no work, and my friend's project is misguided.

(10) My friend has two replies. The first is that (2) offers a false trichotomy: there is a fourth kind of necessity, the transcendental one, which is neither a fact about how we happen to be built nor a fact about being as such, but a condition of anything's counting as a representation of an objective world at all — and the sceptic validates it from inside her own practice, since in raising her doubt she is already representing an objective world and already committed to there being a difference between getting it right and getting it wrong. The second is that (7) conflates two levels: what got revised were empirical specifications layered on top of a framework of objective particulars in space and time, and it is only the framework that transcendental arguments ever claimed as a condition of possibility, so the revisability of the former is no evidence against the latter.

(11) His counter-charge is that my argument proves too much about itself. If (6) and (7) are sound, then no a priori reflection on the conditions of experience could ever establish a modal claim, and that would take down not only transcendental arguments but conceptual analysis, the epistemology of mathematics, and most of what either of us does for a living. He thinks this shows the premise set is self-undermining and that I should retreat to the claim that transcendental arguments prove less than their friends suppose. I think he may only have relabelled the problem in (2).

I do not want the literature's verdict on who won. I want to know where the chain breaks, if it breaks. If it does not break — if the argument is valid and the real choice is among premises — then I would like to know which premise is cheapest to give up and what giving it up commits me to: in particular whether I can hold on to (7), which seems to me to have independent support from the fate of geometry and from quantum mechanics, and whether rejecting (6) forces me to accept that we have some non-empirical capacity to tell the world's necessities from our own format. If forced, I would rather surrender the claim that transcendental arguments are wholly inert than the revisability premise, but I cannot say why that is the right casualty, and I suspect my reasoning in (4) and (8) is where the real damage is.
