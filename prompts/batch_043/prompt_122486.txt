Someone in my EA-adjacent group chat more or less ended a discussion about where our little giving circle's money should go this year by pasting this line, which gets credited to Popper in The Open Society and Its Enemies:

I believe that there is, from the ethical point of view, no symmetry between suffering and happiness, or between pain and pleasure. ... human suffering makes a direct moral appeal for help, while there is no similar call to increase the happiness of a man who is doing well anyway.

The way he used it: there was a proposal to split the pool between a suffering-focused fund (think extreme-poverty surgery or farmed-anionmal welfare) and something whose pitch is basically "make people who are already doing okay happier" — a wellbeing/mental-health upsides thing. He said the asymmetry settles it, that work aimed at raising happiness above zero has literally zero moral value, so every dollar goes to the suffering fund and anyone who disagrees is just running the greatest-happiness principle without noticing.

I can't tell what claim is actually being made here, and he won't say. Can you lay out the menu of precise things that one-liner could mean, and tell me which of them would actually get him his conclusion (all the money, no value at all to happiness-promotion) versus which only get something weaker like "reduce suffering first at the margin"?

Some candidate readings I've half-worked out, please sort them, fix them, and add ones I'm missing:

- a straight axiological one: a unit of suffering is worse than a unit of happiness is good, maybe by a fixed multiplier, maybe lexically (no amount of happiness outweighs the tiniest suffering)
- a "different kind of reason, not a bigger amount" one — suffering generates a directed duty to a particular person, happiness-generating is merely permitted or supererogatory, so the asymmetry is in the shape of the requirement and can't be plugged into a trade-off at all
- an epistemic/measurement one: we can identify, predict and price suffering much better than happiness (adaptation, reporting bias, no stable scale), so the asymmetry is a recommendation about what we can act on, not about value
- an intervention-structure one: relieving suffering is removing a defect with a determinate cause, promoting happiness is adding an optional good, so the asymmetry tracks how reliable the intervention is
- an institutional one: roughly Popper's own use, that fighting avoidable misery is a job for public policy while increasing happiness should be left to private initiative — which seems to be about who acts, not about what's worth anything
- a scope one: the asymmetry might hold within a single life (your own pain vs your own pleasure) without saying anything about trading one person's suffering against another's happiness

Also: which of these would still leave room for "creating new happy beings" mattering, and which commit you to the notorious implication that an empty, painless world beats a world full of happiness plus a bit of suffering? And a separate worry — people in these threads keep sliding between this asymmetry and a totally different one, the asymmetry about coming into existence where absent pain counts as good even if nobody is deprived of it while absent pleasure is merely not bad. Is that the same claim wearing different clothes or a genuinely different one, and is conflating them what's doing the work in his argument?

One more thing I want to bring up in the chat: the quoted passage itself says the point is "not one for rational argument." If that's right, in what sense can it settle a funding decision — and does that disclaimer sink some readings (the ones where it's a factual or conceptual claim about value) while leaving others (the motivational or institutional ones) standing?

Format it as a list I can paste, each reading with: what it precisely asserts, whether it's trivial / plausible-but-substantive / implausible / false, and whether it gets him all-the-money or only something weaker.
