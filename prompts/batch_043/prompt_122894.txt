Hi — I'm an HPS PhD student working on mid-century biomedicine, and I keep using "paradigm shift" loosly and then getting called on it in workshops. Before I write the bit where I score Kuhn, Lakatos and Feyerabend against each other, I want the spec. What would an account of theory change have to deliver to count as capturing what's distinctive about a Kuhnian paradigm shift, as opposed to just "a big change" or "normal science meeting a stubborn anomaly"? Give me the requirement list with the case or consideration behind each. The ones I already think belong on it: it has to say how a successor gets compared to its predecessor when the standards of comparison are among the things that changed; it has to distinguish real meaning change from mere belief change (my archives are full of terms like "stress" and "immune" that keep their spelling and quietly lose their sense); it has to handle granularity, since my cases sit at the level of one lab or one subfield, not a whole discipline; and it has to be diagnosable from evidence a working historian actually has — grant files, correspondence, referee reports — not only in hindsight, once we know who won. I also want the "many anomalies, no crisis" case faced head on, since anomaly accumulation alone plainly isn't enough. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just preferences of one camp — realists wanting cumulative progress, sociologists wanting the social to do the explanatory work, historians wanting local continuity — and flag any pair you suspect can't be jointly satisfied, because that's the part I actually want out of this. Two cases to run against the list as you go: does the replication crisis in psychology count as a crisis in Kuhn's sense or as normal science repairing itself, and is the shift from syndrome-style psychiatric categories toward dimensional, mechanism-first nosology a paradigm shift or a puzzle solution inside one? Keep it on the criteria, skip the scorecard, I'll do that myself.
