I am putting together a one-page handout for an upper-level value theory seminar on the heterogeneity problem for hedonism: what, if anything, does the pleasure of scratching an itch have in common with the pleasure of watching a hard proof go through, or with the pleasure of a hot bath, such that each is a pleasure? I want a taxonomy rather than an essay: a tree with four yes-or-no branch nodes and eight to ten leaves, where each leaf carries a structurally described position (a felt-quality or hedonic-tone view; an adverbial or otherwise intrinsic-but-inseparable view; an attitudinal view on which pleasantness is a stance the subject takes; a desire-based or functional-role view; a pluralist or disjunctivist view; and eliminativism about pleasure as a single psychological kind), together with one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from the list of names: state the branch questions first, say which combinations of answers are live, then locate the real positions in that space, and list the unoccupied cells with a one-clause diagnosis of whether each is empty because it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored.

Every branch node must be a claim a student could take a stand on without knowing any of the labels, and no node may be phrased as a choice between the names of theories. I would like the nodes drawn from four tests: the grafting test (whether the pleasantness of one episode could in principle be detached and reattached to a phenomenology that was originally unpleasant while leaving that phenomenology otherwise unchanged); the absorption test (whether an episode you were wholly absorbed in, with no noticeable pleasant feeling competing for attention, can be pleasant in virtue of something you were aware of at the time); the repudiation test (whether an episode can be pleasant while the subject herself, on reflection and in full command of the facts, refuses to count it as pleasant or as good for her); and the exchange test (whether any two pleasures must be comparable as more or less pleasant, and tradeable against one another, or whether pleasantness comes in incommensurable kinds).

The tree has to sort these four cases: the pleasure of scratching an itch you would rather not have had; the absorbed problem-solving case from the absorption test; the pleasure of laughing at a joke that turns out to have been at a friend's expense, which you later repudiate; and the attempted comparison of a hot bath with finishing a proof. Finally, flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate, and say what further question would be needed to prise them apart.
