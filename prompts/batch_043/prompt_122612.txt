so I'm trying to write down where I've actually landed on population size and pronatalism before a reading group thing on it, and I want to know whether the package hangs together before I stand up and defend it. Roughly what I believe:

1. the asymmetry about procreation — causing someone to exist with a life worth living isn't in itself an improvement, but causing someone to exist with a life not worth living is a worsening. And I'm moved by the person-affecting motivation for it: you can't benefit someone by creating them, you can only harm them.

2. pronatalism as policy: rich countries sitting at 1.4–1.6 births per woman should be doing way more to raise fertility — cash, housing, leave, whatever — and not just to prop up pension systems. Though from what I've read the cash stuff mostly shifts the timing of births rather than the number, so maybe this is more aspirational than I'd like.

3. the reason for 2 that isn't about pensions: more people means more problem-solvers, so population isn't just welfare-units added up, it changes what a society can do. Plus what I think of as threshold goods: below some headcount you simply can't sustain certain things at all — a trauma centre, a semiconductor supply chain, a research university system, a living language — and losing those isn't compensated by the survivors being richer per head. And connectedly: a world with no humans in it at all would be a great loss, not a neutral outcome.

4. I reject the repugnant conclusion. A giant population of lives barely worth living is worse than a much smaller population of flourishing ones.

5. headcount is headcount: if what's doing the work in 3 is the number of people solving problems and keeping institutions staffed, then adding people by immigration is as good as adding them by birth, probably better since somebody else paid for the schooling. So I dont get pronatalism-plus-closed-borders, and I dont see why anyone worried about population should care specifically about the fertility of their own co-citizens.

6. a long slow decline in population is genuinely bad, and I think the dependency-ratio people are onto something real, not just an accounting artefact.

What I want is an audit, not a lecture. Which of these actually collide, how badly, and ordered by how much damage it does to the rest. I suspect 1 and 3 are in trouble together, but only for the boring reason that one says adding people is neutral and the other says it's good, and I feel like there's something deeper I'm not seeing. I also cant tell whether 3 and 4 are fine with each other. And are 1 and 6 really in conflict, or does that one dissolve once you're careful about who the costs land on? For each tension say what would have to give and what giving it up costs me — and please don't tell me all six are in tension with each other, that's not useful.
