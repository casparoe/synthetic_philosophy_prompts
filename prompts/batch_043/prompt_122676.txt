I'm a novelist and I've let myself get talked into doing a fifty-minute session at a writers' festival in the spring — Sunday morning slot, so: a room of maybe thirty novelists, poets and memoirists, half of them hungover, none of them with any maths past age sixteen, and a decent chance that four or five of them already invoke Gödel in their essays the way other people invoke quantum mechanics. The brief I was given is 'what the incompleteness theorems actually show and what they don't', and I said yes partly because there's a character in the book I'm finishing — a retired judge who has gone slightly mystical — who announces on page 214 that every system of rules has to lean on something outside itself, and I want to know whether that line can stand or whether I'm going to get a letter. So don't explain the theorems to me; plan how I would explain them to that room.

Start with the order, because that's what I keep getting wrong. My instinct is to open with a toy system I knocked together myself — two symbols, three rules, and a target string I know is unreachable — and let them push at it for fifteen minutes before I ever say the word 'provable', on the theory that 'unreachable from my starting points' has to be felt before it's named. But a friend who does popular maths says that's a trap: my toy is unreachable only because my rules are weak, and any bright person in row three will find the outside trick (some parity or counting invariant) that settles it, which is exactly the move that is *not* available for a genuinely Gödelian sentence. So: is my toy the right carrier, should I use it and then deliberately break it, or should I drop it and carry the hour on something else — and if something else, what should it have and lack? Relatedly, do I put the three conditions (system is consistent, strong enough to do a bit of arithmetic, and its axioms are checkable by a machine) on the board at minute five as a checklist, or does that drain the room before anything has happened? And where does the second theorem — that such a system can't certify its own consistency — go, given that it's the one that gives my judge's line whatever content it has?

Then the misconceptions, which is the part I actually care about. The ones I expect: that the Gödel sentence is just the liar paradox in a maths costume; that the theorems say arithmetic might be inconsistent, or more loosely that nothing is certain any more; that truth is bigger than proof full stop, so law, ethics and my fictional religion are incomplete in the same way arithmetic is; that a machine can't see what we see when we look at the sentence and go 'well of course it's true'; that Goldbach or Riemann might be undecidable and, worse, that the theorems give us any reason to think they are; that you just add the undecidable sentence as a new axiom and get on with it; and that the obvious fix is to take all the true statements as axioms. For each, tell me where in the sequence I have to head it off and in what order it will actually arrive — which ones walk in with the audience and which ones my own explanation will manufacture if I'm careless, and which single one I should let stand untouched until the last ten minutes because it's the one worth detonating late. Also tell me what to flatly refuse to explain at this level (I assume the coding trick, and the difference between consistency and the stronger condition, and anything resembling a proof of the second theorem) and how to say 'I'm not going to show you this' without sounding like a fraud. Finally, a check: I was going to hand out a dozen slips with claims on them and have the room sort them into 'the theorems show this', 'compatible with them but not shown', and 'ruled out by them'. Give me the dozen — ideally with one or two that are the tell — and, while you're at it, the one sentence about all this you'd let a retired judge say in a novel without wincing.
