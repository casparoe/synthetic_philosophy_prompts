I am writing the cluelessness section of a graduate seminar handout on consequentialism, and I need a purpose-built example rather than one of the standard ones.

The claim I want the example to earn is narrow: that "we have no grip on the long-run effects of our actions" cannot be a blanket reason to fall back on near-term consequences, because cluelessness can discriminate between options. So I need a case in which two options are roughly tied on near-term expected value, both send causal chains out indefinitely far, both chains are equally opaque and equally unquantified, and yet it is reasonable to count the long run in favour of one of them and not the other.

The distinction I want the case to turn on is one I have been calling the sign-structure of a causal channel. Call a channel sign-preserving if the sign of its cumulative effect is stable under composition: each link pushes the running total in the same direction regardless of what else happens downstream, so the long-run total inherits that sign even while its magnitude stays entirely unknown. Call a channel sign-flipping if the contribution of each link depends on the state of other variables that later agents will set, so the cumulative effect resembles a sum of terms whose signs are separately up for grabs and whose total sign is genuinely unsettled. My working hypothesis is that cluelessness bites only on the second kind of channel, and I want a case that tests it.

Constraints on the scenario:

1. Realistic — a hospital, a public-health agency, a university department, a city council. No simulation arguments, no anthropics, no existential-risk-scale stakes; the case must still work if every effect is capped at a few thousand lives over two centuries, so that nothing is carried by fanatical magnitudes.
2. The two options are roughly tied on expected value over the near term (take that as the next ten years), on evidence a real decision-maker in that role could actually have.
3. No asymmetry of evidence. You may not make one option's long-run effects shorter-lived, better studied, or more predictable in size. Both chains are equally long, equally murky, and equally unmeasured; the difference must be structural, a matter of how effects compose rather than of how much is known about them.
4. The verdicts have to be generated by the theories operating on the scenario, not written into it. If your description of the favoured option amounts to "and this keeps helping," the case has failed.

Please answer in exactly five numbered items and nothing else — no preamble and nothing after item 5:

1. The scenario (this item may run to three sentences; items 2 through 5 are one or two sentences each).
2. Why the cluelessness objection has real force against the option whose channel is sign-flipping, and why it does not have that force against the sign-preserving one.
3. Which single feature of the case is load-bearing, and what happens to both verdicts when you remove it by making the sign-preserving option's channel sign-flipping as well.
4. The strongest reply available to someone who insists that cluelessness is symmetric across the two options, and whether you think that reply succeeds.
5. One sentence on what a case like this entitles me to assert in the handout, and one on what it cannot establish.

The reply I keep getting stuck on for item 4 is that calling a channel "sign-preserving" is itself an empirical conjecture about the distant future, and that helping myself to it requires exactly the sort of long-run forecast I said we cannot make. If that is in fact the best available objection, say so plainly and tell me what conceding it costs the example.
