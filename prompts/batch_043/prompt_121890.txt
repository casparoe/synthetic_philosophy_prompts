I write novels for a living and read philosophy much as other novelists do: for pleasure, with rather more appetite than training. I have a working grip on terms like intrinsic and instrumental value, virtue, and social contract, but I could not reconstruct a line of scholarship to save myself, and I do not want you to try to. What I want is a translation, done honestly, into the vocabulary I actually think in.

That vocabulary is the vocabulary of craft: motive, credibility, what a character wants and what she is willing to pay for it; obstacle, consequence, and the way a plot distributes them; point of view, narrative distance, dramatic irony; showing as against telling; what a scene exists to do; the covenant of trust between a book and its reader; and above all the standing distinction between a character's reasons for acting and my reasons for giving her those reasons rather than others. Assume I am fluent in it and that I do not need it explained back to me.

Please restate Glaucon's challenge in that vocabulary, and restate the whole of it rather than the famous part. That includes the story about how justice came to be valued at all, the thought experiment of the ring, and the pairing at the end of the challenge: the perfectly just man universally believed unjust, set against the perfectly unjust man universally believed just, with the demand that justice be shown worth choosing both for its own sake and for what follows from it. For each element, say what carries over cleanly, what you had to bend or stretch to make it fit, and what will not go into my vocabulary at all.

Then I would like you to run a variant that I cannot get out of my head, because in my trade visibility is not one thing. A scene can be witnessed by every other character in the book and withheld from the reader, or shown to the reader in full and unwitnessed by anyone inside the fiction. So consider what I have been calling the half-ring: the wearer is invisible to every character in the book and fully visible to the reader, who sees exactly what she does and why, and who can neither intervene, reward, punish, nor tell anyone. Is this a case of the ring of Gyges, or has splitting visibility in two destroyed the example? Does the argument go through unchanged, does it quietly become a different argument, or does it collapse? And what would an answer to Glaucon have to look like if it had to be written from inside that case rather than from outside it?

Two further things. First, I know that the answer Socrates eventually offers runs by way of a comparison between a city and a soul, with justice as each part doing its own work, and that this is the part I find least translatable — a character is constituted by what is shown of her, whereas a soul is supposed to have a condition that obtains whether or not anyone sees it. Do not quietly drop that element to make the translation tidy; tell me instead whether my vocabulary's inability to hold it is a defect in my vocabulary or a sign that the philosophical claim is doing less work than it appears to.

Second, I should declare my own suspicion so that you can test it rather than humour it. I suspect that the question *why be moral?* cannot be put to a character at all: her reasons are reasons I have given her, and the only genuine question available to me is what makes a better novel — which looks like a maker's question wearing an agent's clothes. If that is a category error on my part, say so, and say what it is in my vocabulary that stops me from seeing it. I would rather be corrected than confirmed.

About 1,200 words of continuous prose, if you would, with the element-by-element translation and the residue report kept clearly apart. No reading lists, no citations, no surveys of interpretation: I want the argument, in British English, at the level of a well-read adult rather than a seminar.
