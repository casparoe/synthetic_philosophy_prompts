I am a retired biophysicist who has come to philosophy rather late and is currently auditing a graduate seminar on Kant's practical philosophy, and I have become stuck on a dispute that seems to me to be going around in circles — my reading group has returned to it four or five times over the past year without anybody's position shifting by so much as a degree. The question is whether Kant's moral theory can accommodate direct duties *to* animals, as opposed to duties merely *with regard to* animals. One side insists that because animals are not rational beings and therefore cannot be ends in themselves in the relevant sense, they fall outside the class of beings that can be wronged; on that reading, the familiar passages in the Doctrine of Virtue about our duties concerning animals are best explained by the claim that cruelty to animals coarsens a susceptibility in us that we need in order to carry out our genuine duties to human beings, so that what makes such cruelty wrong is what it does to the agent's own humanity rather than anything owed to the animal. The other side replies that this makes the wrongness of institutionalized cruelty hostage to a contingent empirical generalization about moral psychology, that it yields absurd verdicts in cases where no human is anywhere in the causal neighbourhood, and that a theory which grounds the special standing of rational beings in something like the capacity to set ends, or in being a self-maintaining organism with needs that matter to it, has room for animals as objects of direct duty after all. What interests me is that nobody in my group disagrees about the text, about the historical context, or about which animals are sentient: everyone grants the same facts and yet the two camps talk past each other completely. I have two cases I keep testing people with, and they produce remarkably consistent embarrassment: first, a lone surviving rational agent on a dying Earth who will never encounter another person and who tortures animals for amusement, where the indirect-duty story appears to have no remaining foothold; second, an alien rational being whose moral psychology is simply insulated, so that in its case cruelty to animals has no tendency whatsoever to blunt its humane dispositions toward persons — is the indirect theorist committed to saying its cruelty is blameless, or to saying it is blameful only because of a statistical fact about creatures like us, or to quietly relying on a corruptness of disposition that no longer seems indirect at all?

My own hypothesis, which I want you to pressure-test rather than flatter, is that this is not really an interpretive dispute about Kant but a dispute about the architecture of a duty: whether a duty must be a *directed* relation that is owed to someone and generates something like a claim in that someone, or whether it is enough for a requirement to be correct and to be correct *because of* the being that is affected. On that hypothesis the two sides are using the phrase duty to differently — one to mark wronging, the other to mark the locus of the justifying reason — and the apparent stalemate is partly verbal, though not merely verbal, because behind the verbal issue sits a substantive one about whether the class of beings that can be wronged has to coincide with the class of beings that can respond to reasons. A second possibility I cannot rule out is that the sides disagree about what *kind* of argument Kant is offering: a derivation from the categorical imperative in its various formulations, versus an application of an independently held moral psychology that Kant never intended to bear that much weight.

Please answer as a professional philosopher who works both on Kant's ethics and on the general metaphysics of normativity, and think carefully before you write: begin by brainstorming several candidate diagnoses of the stalemate — verbal, substantive-but-about-something-else, difference in standards of evidence about what counts as a case, bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for, competing conceptions of what an ethical theory is *for* — then explicitly set aside the ones you reject and commit to the one you think is right. I want a diagnosis supported by what each side actually says, not a survey of the literature, and I want you to tell me what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress: a distinction, a test case, an argument that would force one side to give something up, a reframing of the question — or, if you think no progress should be expected here, an explanation of why the impasse is principled rather than merely stubborn.
