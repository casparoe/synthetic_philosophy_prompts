I teach a final-year undergraduate seminar on consciousness, and I am writing the closed-book essay paper. I want one 60-minute question on epiphenomenalism and the paradox of phenomenal judgment, and I have had trouble writing one that does not invite a canned answer. In previous years candidates have written some version of: experiences cause nothing, therefore our talk about them is unexplained, therefore the view refutes itself. That answer gets a middling mark from everyone and discriminates between nobody. I would like a question whose crux is *which premise is doing the work*. The naive causal version — epiphenomenalism plus a causal theory of knowledge — seems to me to yield scepticism about mathematics, about the future and about unobserved physical quantities just as readily as about pains, so it cannot be the argument that anyone should find disturbing. The interesting version has to be reconstructed differently, and I want candidates to have to reconstruct it.

The device I have been toying with, and which I would like you to build the question around, is a deliberately engineered analogue. Stipulate a quantity that has exactly the two features the epiphenomenalist attributes to phenomenal properties: it is causally inert with respect to everything physical, including the formation of beliefs; and it is tied to the physical by a correlation that is nomologically necessary but metaphysically contingent and brute, so that there are possible worlds with the physical base and the physical side of the laws intact but the quantity absent or different. Then stipulate that investigators have fine-grained, reliable, discriminating beliefs about its values, formed via instruments responsive only to the physical base plus a known bridge law. Call it the 'eta-field' case, or rename it if you prefer. Because it is a structural mirror of the phenomenal case, any argument that generates a paradox in one should generate it in the other; so a candidate must either locate the residual difference between them or explain why the argument is not a reductio when it generalises. My own suspicion — which you should feel free to reject in the model answer if you think it is wrong — is that the residue concerns acquaintance, or presentation, or the way phenomenal concepts are supposed to be grounded, rather than causation or reliability as such. Please make the question self-contained: define any term of art inside the question, do not reward familiarity with particular articles or authors, and set the expected answer at about 1,000 to 1,200 words.

Deliverables, all in British English: first, the question exactly as it would appear on the paper, with whatever setup, stipulations and sub-questions it needs; second, a model answer of roughly 900 words; third, a marking scheme out of 20. I want the model answer to do four things, and the scheme to reward them. It should reconstruct the paradox in its strongest form, which I suspect is a modal or safety-style argument rather than a causal one. It should use the analogue as a diagnostic to isolate what, if anything, is special about phenomenal judgment. It should state the resulting dilemma for the epiphenomenalist crisply and then take a stand on one horn, saying what that horn costs — for instance whether phenomenal self-knowledge must be reconstrued as theoretical or inferential, and what that does to its supposed authority or to the conceivability argument that motivated the view in the first place. And it should say, on that resolution, what becomes of the self-stultification worry: whether the epiphenomenalist can still claim to know, or to have evidence for, the claim that she has experiences, or whether she ends up holding a view she can have no reason to hold. For the scheme, name four or five must-hit moves, allocate the 20 marks across them, and give a one-line note for each on how candidates typically get it wrong and what a B answer does instead of the A move. Add a short note on what counts as a bare pass and what should be failed, and flag the two or three errors that should cost marks even in an otherwise fluent answer — for example conflating causal inertness with explanatory irrelevance, or treating the common-cause reliability reply as though it closed the whole issue.
