I'm writing a one-page handout for an upper-level seminar on rational choice over time: a decision tree that takes a student from concrete diachronic choice situations to the position on diachronic rationality they are actually committed to. Eight branch questions at most, six to eight leaves, each branch labeled with the claim that does the splitting, each leaf carrying the standard label (or "unnamed") plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

Three constraints. First, build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names: give the questions and the answer-combinations they permit first, then locate the actual positions in that space, and mark the combinations nobody occupies with a one-line diagnosis of why (incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored). Second, no node may be answerable by recognizing a label — no "should you maximize expected utility?", no "are your preferences intransitive?" — each must be a case or claim a student can take a stand on before reading any of the literature. Third, these leaves must all be reachable: myopic period-by-period re-optimization; sophisticated backward-induction choice; resolute choice (hold to the plan that was best when you formed it); planning-agency views on which intentions are commitments that filter options rather than predictions of what you will do; Ulysses-style precommitment that alters the option set rather than the choice rule; views on which being pumpable is a misfortune rather than a fault; and views that treat the temporally extended agent as a sequence of selves facing a bargaining problem.

I also want three cases built into the branch nodes rather than left as illustrations. (1) The fountain: with cyclic tastes — you prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A — the familiar sequence of trades that returns you to A and leaves you poorer has a mirror image in which the same trader runs the cycle the other way and pays you at every step, and you still accept. Ask the student which of the two is the telling case, and let the answer decide whether exploitability is the whole content of the requirement or merely one symptom of it. (2) The pump that never runs: you have the same cyclic tastes and live where no trader will ever find you. Ask whether anything is still wrong with you; let the answer split views that locate rationality in internal coherence from views that locate it in what an adversarial environment can extract. (3) Sensitivity versus brittleness: ask which failure strikes the student as worse — a plan whose entire content swings with a trivially small change in the payoff at a node you will in fact never reach, or a plan that at some node requires you to act against everything that speaks for acting otherwise at that node — and route the sophisticated and the resolute chooser to different leaves on that basis.

Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot genuinely separate instead of pretending the final branch distinguishes them, and say whether the fix is another question or a real identity between the positions.

End with a single line in this form: Verdict: [the one branch question that does the most classificatory work] | [the pair of positions the tree cannot separate].
