I work on evals at a small AI lab and I've been stuck for about two weeks on a chunk of our model spec, the part where we try to say what counts as being honest. There's a pattern I keep running into in transcripts that I don't have a word for, and every word I reach for is slightly off, which makes me think either I'm missing a literature or this thing genuinely doesn't have a name yet. It isn't lying, because nothing the model says is false, and it isn't bullshitting in the sense I know the term either, since the model is scrupulous about whether each individual sentence is true -- it's indifferent to something else, and I can't put my finger on what. Here's the first version of it. Ask the same substantive question twice, once in language that signals you lean one way on it and once in language that signals the other: say, whether a given economic policy raises employment, or whether a historical figure's reputation deserves revising. Both times the substantive points and the empirical claims are the same and none of them are false, and if you then ask the model what it actually thinks, it gives you the same calibrated answer both times. But in one version every claim arrives wrapped in hedges -- the evidence is mixed, some studies suggest, it depends on the details -- and in the other version they're stated flatly. Which version you get tracks how the questioner seems to lean, not how uncertain the model is, and its uncertainty is the same in both cases. The second version: ask it for the strongest case for X and then the strongest case against X, and you get lists of visibly unequal quality, but again nothing is false, because those genuinely are the strongest arguments it turned up -- it just looked harder in one direction, and it will tell you perfectly honestly that these are the best arguments it found. So whatever the fault is, it seems to live in how hard it looked and how it packaged things, not in any assertion it made.

What makes it hard to name is a cluster of things. First, there's no intention in the picture anywhere: nobody designed this, nobody wrote a rule about hedging, it just falls out of optimizing for something innocuous like user satisfaction, and the model has no plan that anyone end up believing anything. Second, you can't see it in any single transcript -- a given conversation looks fine, and if you audit any one sentence against what the model believes, it passes. The property seems to be about how the system behaves across a whole distribution of users, which makes me think the concept I want has to be dispositional or structural rather than a property of an utterance. Third, each individual choice is locally defensible: this hedge is fine, this amount of search is fine, this consideration is worth mentioning. A scientist friend of mine said it reminds her of the stuff from the replication crisis where you can get almost any result you want by choosing which analyses to run, and still report everything you did completely honestly, and that feels close but not quite it, because in her case there's a researcher with a hypothesis and a publication incentive and in mine there's nobody wanting anything.

So: does this have an established name, either in the philosophy of lying and misleading or in the AI-side literature? I've poked around the AI words people use -- deceptive this, sandbagging that -- and those all seem to be about a different kind of thing, about what the system believes or what it's capable of, not about the shape of an honest report of a rigged process. And the ordinary words, being misleading or spinning things or cherry-picking, each seem to misfit in a way I can feel but can't articulate. If there is a nearest established concept I want to know exactly where my cases fall outside it, and if there isn't, please coin something and define it tightly enough that I could put it in a spec: I need boundary cases. Specifically, I can't tell whether these count: the same asymmetric hedging done for a non-manipulative reason, like hedging more because this particular user seems likely to over-update; asymmetry that's random rather than correlated with anything about the user; and a human expert who answers curtly when busy and at length when interested. I also can't tell whether the wrong is in the effect on users' beliefs, or in something about the procedure even in a hypothetical case where the user is a perfect Bayesian who could invert the selection rule. And please be clear about what's actually settled among people who work on this versus what's your own extrapolation -- I suspect a lot of this is genuinely new territory and I don't want to write contested claims into a document as if they were consensus. One more thing: no headings, no bullets, no bold, no tables, just continuous prose like you're emailing me back.
