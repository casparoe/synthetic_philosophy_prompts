I convene a small reading group on practical rationality, and this week we stalled on decision-making under normative uncertainty in a way I suspect is really a problem about aggregation rather than about morality — I would like to know whether that diagnosis is right, and if so what exactly is being aggregated. As I understand the standard move: I have credences over several first-order moral theories, each theory assigns my available options some degree of choice-worthiness, and I am supposed to multiply each theory's verdict by my credence in it and sum, exactly as I would under empirical uncertainty. My own attempt at saying what goes wrong is that under empirical uncertainty every state–outcome pair already has a value on one shared scale, whereas here each candidate theory arguably brings its own scale and its own account of what is even being measured, so the multiplication is not well defined until I impose a common unit — and any such imposition looks like a further first-order moral commitment smuggled in under the label of a neutral second-order procedure. Is that the right place to locate the difficulty, and is there a cleaner formulation of it?

What made me suspicious is a case I built to test the framework. Say my options are: (A) take a lucrative job and give away most of the income, (B) stay home and care for an ailing parent full time, (C) a compromise — part-time work, modest giving, partial care. One theory, in which I have credence 0.45, ranks them 100 / 0 / 60; a second, also with credence 0.45, ranks them 0 / 100 / 60; a third, with credence 0.10, does not rank A at all but declares it forbidden. If I rescale each cardinal theory onto a common range using the best and worst options in the case at hand, the compromise wins: A and B each come out at 0.45 and C at 0.54. Two things bother me here. First, no theory I give any credence to recommends C — each of the two cardinal theories thinks C throws away most of what matters — so the framework seems to generate recommendations by averaging rather than by moral reasoning, and I cannot tell whether that is a feature (moral hedging is genuinely reasonable when you are unsure) or a reductio. Second, if option B leaves the menu because a sibling takes over the care, the rescaling changes and C's advantage disappears entirely: A and C now tie at 0.45. So the recommendation depends on an option nobody was going to choose, which strikes me as the same shape as a failure of independence of irrelevant alternatives in social choice, and I wonder whether it admits a diachronic or money-pump-style argument — could someone who controls which options are on offer reliably extract something from an agent who maximises expected choice-worthiness in this way?

I have two half-formed escape routes and I would like them assessed alongside the framework itself. A friend suggests treating the theories as parties to a bargain and aggregating by something like a Nash bargaining solution over their normalised profiles; my worry is that this just relocates the problem, since bargaining needs interpersonally comparable utilities and a disagreement point, and imports its own fairness commitments. The other thought is to normalise on a global rather than a case-relative scale — each theory's values measured against the best and worst outcomes it can express anywhere — which would restore menu-independence but seems to hand a theory with enormous stakes the same say as one with tiny stakes, and to require a moral judgement about comparative ambition that the second-order framework was supposed to avoid. Against both: the so-called my-favourite-theory rule is menu-independent and needs no comparison, but it apparently tells me to do A here even though a theory I take seriously at 10% says A is forbidden, which suggests that deontic status and scalar value cannot simply be fed into the same expectation.

What I would like, in roughly this order: (i) an explanation of why the "just take the expectation" move fails here but succeeds under empirical uncertainty, stated sharply enough that I can tell whether my own formulation above is getting it right; (ii) an assessment of whether the menu-dependence I have illustrated is a decisive objection to case-relative normalisation, a merely cosmetic artefact of my numbers, or a symptom that the whole expected-choice-worthiness picture is misdesigned; (iii) a comparison of the alternatives — my-favourite-theory, variance-style normalisation, a global scale, and my friend's bargaining proposal — on the two worries I have raised, namely menu-sensitivity and the fact that the recommended option is one no theory endorses; and (iv) a view on whether forbidden-versus-merely-bad is a separate problem that a purely cardinal framework cannot represent, or whether it can be handled by the same machinery. Please keep it conceptual and self-contained: I am not asking for references, just for the distinctions that make the disagreement intelligible.
