I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on contrastive explanation — the view that a 'why P?' question really has the form 'why P rather than Q?', and is only well posed relative to a contrast class — and for the past year I've been sitting in on a comparative economic history reading group as the resident outsider, partly for the arguments and partly as field work for a paper I'm trying to write. The group's central topic is the so-called Great Divergence and the question named after the historian of Chinese science who asked why modern science never appeared in China despite its earlier lead: why did sustained, self-reinforcing growth happen in Britain, or in north-west Europe, rather than in China, given that around 1750 or 1800 the most advanced region on each side looked, on many measures, roughly comparable? Roughly a quarter-century after the book that set the modern terms of the debate — Pomeranz's 2000 study, which gives the credit to coal and to access to the New World — the same exchanges keep recurring in the same shape: the coal-and-colonies line, the institutions-and-property-rights line, the culture-of-growth/Enlightenment line, the high-wage-economy-and-cheap-energy line, and the revisionist reconstructions of GDP per head and real wages that push the divergence back before 1800 and deny that the two regions were ever comparable in the first place. Nobody moves. When I ventured at the last meeting that the question itself might not be well posed, I got two opposite reactions: the historians said of course it is, we have data now that we didn't have in 1990; the one other philosopher in the room said it isn't, because 'China' and 'Europe' are the wrong units and there is no single thing to explain. I'd like a diagnosis, not a verdict.

My own hypothesis, which I would rather have tested than flattered, is that at least three distinct questions are being run together under one heading and that much of the apparent staleness is that: first, a contrast-class question — whether the comparison is England against the whole of China, England against the Yangzi Delta, north-west Europe against the Old World's other advanced cores, or Britain against Song-dynasty China (one member of the group pointed out that Chinese ironmasters were already using coke for smelting centuries before the European take-off, so coal cannot be doing the explanatory work relative to that contrast, whatever it does relative to the others); second, a level-versus-rate-versus-timing question — why here rather than there, why then rather than earlier or later, and whether an account of the rate of growth must be the same account as that of the escape from Malthusian constraints; third, a question about what counts as data here, since real-wage series, heights, urbanisation rates, book production and probate inventories can come apart, and since the direction of the answer seems to flip with the scale of aggregation in a way I have started calling the aggregation-sensitivity problem.

So what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it verbal, about the terms of comparison, or substantive; are the sides differing about facts, about which series or which cases count as evidence, about what a historical explanation has to be (a mechanism and a narrative, versus measured variables and a counterfactual), about political or anti-Eurocentric commitments that no amount of data will touch, or about intuitions about what an explanation should look like that neither side can argue for? Is a hidden empirical or formal question doing the work? Is my contrast-class diagnosis right, is it one fault line among several, or is it a philosopher's imposition on a question that historians are prosecuting perfectly well — I would much rather be told that plainly than have it politely accommodated. And given whatever you diagnose, what would count as progress: a distinction I should be drawing, an accounting or decomposition exercise that could settle some piece of it, an agreement to split the question, or nothing, because this is the sort of question a field lives inside rather than settles? Please mark how confident you are in each part of your diagnosis and where you are extrapolating from a partial picture rather than standing on something firm. Please don't pad the answer with bibliographies or references — I have those already and want the argument. British English throughout, please.
