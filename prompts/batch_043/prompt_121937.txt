I am trying to state for myself when I will count a proof of an existential claim, or of a disjunction, as constructively acceptable, and my verdicts on cases will not line up with any principle I have managed to formulate. I work informally in a Bishop-style setting: no fan theorem or continuity principles assumed, no Church's thesis, and I would prefer that my standard not depend on which of those extras I end up accepting.

Let me name the kind of case that started the trouble. Call a proof of an existential claim ∃n A(n), with A decidable, an *escrow proof* if it supplies an explicit search procedure e together with a proof that e terminates, where the termination proof is itself essentially classical — excluded middle, a compactness argument, a well-foundedness or well-quasi-ordering argument, a counting argument that yields no bound. The witness sits in escrow: guaranteed retrievable, but the guarantee is not itself constructive.

My verdicts:

1. Euclid's construction (given n, take a prime factor of n! + 1, which exceeds n): fine, paradigmatically so.
2. The classical proof that some irrational power of an irrational is rational, by case-splitting on whether √2^√2 is rational: not acceptable. I believe the conclusion, but the proof leaves me without a witness, and that is the defect. The repaired version using √2 and log₂9: fine. (Setting aside the further worry about whether a^b is constructively well defined for arbitrary reals — I take the repair to fix that too, by exhibiting specific computable reals.)
3. Erdős-style existence of graphs with large girth and large chromatic number by the probabilistic method: fine. The counting argument yields an explicit finite N, hence a search that terminates, even though no one could run it. I am mildly surprised that I accept this while rejecting case 2.
4. The exact intermediate value theorem for arbitrary continuous f with f(0) < 0 < f(1): not fine. The approximate version (bisection to within any prescribed ε): fine. The exact version restricted to polynomials with rational coefficients, proved by bisection over rational midpoints with exact rational arithmetic: fine. So I do not think exactness as such is what fails in the first case.
5. Brouwer's fixed point theorem: not fine — as I understand it, there are computable self-maps of the square with no computable fixed point, so no uniform construction is to be had. But the finite Sperner statement and the approximate fixed point it yields: fine.
6. Existence obtained by Markov's principle — from ¬¬∃n A(n), with A primitive recursive, conclude ∃n A(n) and run the search: fine. I cannot see what more a construction could demand of me here.
7. Escrow proofs in which the search carries an explicit computable bound: fine. This is case 3 in general form.
8. Escrow proofs whose termination proof yields no bound — termination inferred from compactness, or from the classical finiteness of a set of possible failures: not fine.
9. Case splits on whether a search halts (the limited principle of omniscience) or on the sign of an arbitrary real (its lesser cousin): not fine, anywhere. Classical reasoning whose conclusion is negative or Π⁰₁: fine. Classical reasoning whose conclusion is a bare disjunction or an unadorned Σ⁰₁ existential: not fine, except as in cases 6 and 7.

Here is why I cannot get these into equilibrium. As far as I can see, cases 6 and 9-under-negative-conclusions jointly collapse case 8 into case 7. If a classical theory proves that e halts, the double-negation translation turns that into an intuitionistic proof of ¬¬(e halts); halting is Σ⁰₁; Markov's principle returns (e halts); and then the search itself delivers the witness. Worse, if I have the standard results right, classical and intuitionistic arithmetic prove the same Π⁰₂ sentences, so every true Σ⁰₁ existence claim of the sort I am worrying about was already provable in the intuitionistic theory, and the witness was always extractable in principle. In that case my distinction between 7 and 8 cannot be a distinction about what is constructively provable, or even about what is extractable — it has to be a distinction about what the proof as given hands me. Tell me if this argument is wrong, and where.

My tentative principle is therefore: an existence claim is acceptable to me only if the proof supplies a computable bound on the search, not merely a proof that the search terminates. But this is clearly not a proposal about intuitionistic validity; it is a stronger demand, and I do not know what kind of demand it is — a restriction on the logic, a restriction on which claims I assert, or a requirement that a proof carry extra data the theorem does not mention.

What I want from you:

(a) Two or three candidate principles, each stated precisely enough to be argued with, where you say which of my verdicts forces each clause and which verdict each principle overturns. I would rather have a three-clause principle I can dispute than a taxonomy of my cases.
(b) A verdict on whether I should keep case 6. The bound requirement kills it, since Markov's principle gives no bound; should I drop 6, or weaken the requirement to something like "search over a domain whose finiteness I can certify, bound optional"? Does that weakened version then let case 8 back in through the back door?
(c) Note that cases 6 and 9 are hard to reconcile independently of all this: the usual justification of Markov's principle — the search cannot fail, so run it — looks like exactly the unbounded case-splitting I refuse in 9. Is there a formulation that keeps 6 without smuggling in the limited principle of omniscience, or is that pair the thing I have to give up?
(d) What each candidate says about cases I have not listed: a proof of ∃n A(n) in a theory with the existence property, where I know some numeral works but the proof is too large for me to extract it from; an escrow proof whose termination proof is Π⁰₃ rather than Σ⁰₁, so that the translation and Markov's principle no longer reach it; a witness obtainable from a classical proof only by a heavy metatheorem such as the Dialectica or A-translation, and not by inspection; countable and dependent choice (picking a modulus of convergence for each real in a sequence) as against full choice and as against unique choice; the passage from ∀x ∃y A(x, y) to ∃f ∀x A(x, f(x)).
(e) Whether the whole pattern dissolves if I separate two things I think I have been running together: the claims I am willing to assert, and the proofs I am willing to call constructions. I notice in this connection that I am perfectly willing to assert ¬¬(P ∨ ¬P), since it is intuitionistically provable, while refusing to use P ∨ ¬P as a premise in a construction. If that separation is the right one, then cases 2 and 8 are verdicts about use rather than about assertion, and I would like to know what remains of the bound requirement once it is read that way.

Please be plain about which of my verdicts you think is simply mistaken, and do not try to accommodate all nine.
