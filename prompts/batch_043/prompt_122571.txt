In my comparative political theory seminar, "equality of opportunity, not equality of outcomes" gets used as a conversation-stopper: most recently to argue that Nordic-style sectoral wage bargaining, high union density, and generous social insurance are simply beside the point, because all justice requires is that everyone get a fair shot. I don't want to react to the slogan, I want it precisified. Give me a menu of the distinct claims it could be making, one entry per reading, each with a verdict on whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or substantive and hard to defend. For every reading, please pin down three things I think are doing hidden work: the currency — opportunity for what, and is there even a single scale on which whole lives or whole opportunity sets can be ranked?; the timing — equal at what point in a life, given that opportunities become outcomes and outcomes become the next generation's opportunities; and whether the claim concerns equal chances ex ante or the eventual distribution ex post, since the two come sharply apart when what is competed for is positional and there are only so many places at the top. Then tell me which readings, if any, really license dismissing the European approach. My suspicion — argue me out of it if I'm wrong — is that the readings strong enough to do that work are nearly empty, that the readings worth defending are more demanding than any existing welfare state, and that the two sides may not be measuring in the same currency at all, one caring about mobility and dispersion and the other about security and standing, so that the slogan changes the subject rather than settling it. If some single reading is both worth defending and strong enough to do the work my seminar wanted it to do, that is what I most want to see; and close by saying what, on your best reading, each attitude notices that the other misses.
