Dear Dr Achebe,

May I ask a favour in writing rather than at Thursday's meeting? I have been keeping a private list of cases and verdicts on the view the reading group calls tranquilism — that nothing is good for a person except the absence of craving and of suffering, so that pleasure, achievement, knowledge and attachment are at best instruments for getting the wanting to stop — and I have reached the point where I cannot tell whether my verdicts add up to anything. I would be grateful for your help, and I would rather have a principle I can argue with than a survey of the literature.

Some background, since it explains the shape of my confusion. For most of my career I worked on regulatory physiology: set points, error signals, gain, adaptation, the difference between a variable's being out of range and the organism's being perturbed by it. When I meet a philosophical claim of the form "only this quantity matters," my instinct is to ask what the quantity is a function of, over what interval it is integrated, and whether it is measured in the signalling or in the experience. So the formulation I keep falling back on is this:

(W) A life goes better to the extent that the time-integral of its error signal is smaller — the error signal being any active discrepancy between how the organism is set to be and how it is, together with whatever acute suffering is present. Satisfactions, pleasures, achievements and relationships count for nothing in themselves; they count only by driving the integral toward zero. The integral is sub-personal: it is fixed by the signalling, not by how the signalling feels.

The trouble is that when I test (W) against my own reactions it seems both too strong and too weak, and I cannot see whether the fault is in (W) or in the reactions.

Here are the verdicts, with the cases as I actually noted them.

1. My grandson's three weeks of examination nerves and his hour of relief afterwards. I am happy to say the relief is the entire good and the pass itself adds nothing. Fine.

2. A friend with trigeminal neuralgia, given a drug that leaves the pain signal wholly intact but makes her entirely indifferent to it. I judge her much improved and cannot say "no loss." Fine, but only if something matters beyond the error signal as I have defined it — so (W) as written seems already to be missing something.

3. A retired colleague with an uncorrected congenital abnormality that leaves him permanently, mildly thirsty; he has never noticed, reports complete contentment, and refuses the cheap pill because he does not want to be meddled with. I say he is worse off than he needs to be, though nothing feels bad and he prefers things as they are.

4. Another colleague who was told, erroneously, that her paper had been accepted, and who for eleven months had no want at all on that score. I refuse to credit those eleven months to her life — and yet I also cannot call them bad, and I do not know which of those two reactions to surrender.

5. My wife, whose dementia has taken from her the knowledge that our son is dead. She is calm, wants for nothing, reports no distress. I take this to be a misfortune, and not merely a misfortune for me. On (W) it is an improvement in her condition. I reject that flatly.

6. The case the group returns to: a life with no craving and no suffering and also nothing else — no projects, no interest, no one loved — beside a life of striving, with the wanting and the relief. I find myself judging the second the better life, while being unable to name what the extra credit consists in.

7. My own papers being refuted after I am dead, or my name being attached to a fraud I did not commit. I care about this now; I cannot see how it could matter to me then. Not, I think, a real cost.

8. The night before a difficult climb, or before the result of an experiment comes in: a wanting that was not disagreeable but the opposite. I resist counting it as a cost at all.

9. Wanting to see my brother again, twenty years after his death. It does not feel bad; it feels like the shape my love for him now takes. And yet I think I ought to want it to stop, and I think a life without it would be better.

10. The one I go back and forth on: palliative sedation for a last week, abolishing suffering, craving and awareness together, versus the same week awake and reconciled with her family. I judge the second better. On my own principle the first is at least as good, and I cannot say what I am relying on.

What I would like from you, if you will:

A principle — (W) repaired, or replaced — stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case you have not seen. I would like it clause by clause, with the case that motivates each clause.

A plain statement of which of my ten verdicts your principle accommodates, which it overturns, and — this matters most to me — which it cannot accommodate even after repair, and what you would then do: give up the principle, or claim that the verdict is sentiment. I would rather be told that one of my reactions is mere sentiment than be handed a formula that mimics all ten.

Your verdicts, with reasons, on four cases I have not listed. (a) A child you could bring into the world who would live without suffering, with exactly one strong craving at twenty, satisfied at twenty-one, and nothing else at any time: is there any reason, from the point of view of that life, to bring it about? (b) A drug that halves the intensity of every craving and doubles its duration, taken for life: better, worse, or the same, and what does your answer tell me about whether the integral is the right functional form? (c) A woman who wants something from twenty to sixty, gets it at sixty-one, and dies at sixty-two: is the wanting paid off at the end, or is it a pure loss on any correct view? (d) A being engineered so that every craving is extinguished a moment after it arises, so that its integral is near zero and it never gets anything it wanted: is anything lost?

Two further questions, if they are not impertinent. Does tranquilism have to say that no life can be better than a life with nothing at all in it — that zero is the ceiling as well as the floor — or is there a way of denying that without admitting positive goods? And where, if anywhere, am I using a physiological notion to do philosophical work it cannot do? I am well aware that "error signal" is doing a great deal of work in my formulation, and I would like to know how much of it is legitimate.

Forgive the length of this. I have come to think that the view is either nearly right or badly wrong in a way I cannot see, and I would like to know which before Thursday.

Yours sincerely,
[Name withheld]
