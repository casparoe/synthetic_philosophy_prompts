Hey, I need someone to settle an argument between me and my roommate. We're in a very informal reading group on philosophy of action (one grad student who runs it, the rest of us undergrads, we meet in someone's kitchen) and last week we went through Kavka's toxin puzzle: a billionaire gives you a million tonight on the condition that at some point tonight you form the genuine intention to drink a vial of toxin tomorrow morning. The toxin just makes you fairly miserable for a day, the money is already in your account and you keep it whether or not you drink, and the billionaire is a perfect intention-reader so pretending won't get you anywhere. The question is whether you can intend, right now, to do something you know perfectly well you will have no reason to do when the time comes. I might be misremembering parts of the original setup, so correct me if I've got the case wrong.

We agree the case is strange but we disagree about why, and I think the disagreement actually matters. My read is that the problem is basically epistemic: intending to do X seems to involve, or at least normally to bring along, believing that you're going to do X, and here you know you won't drink, so the state you're being asked to produce is self-undermining — you'd have to somehow both believe and not believe that you'll drink. My roommate says that's a red herring and the real problem is that intentions are supposed to be responsive to reasons: an intention isn't just a prediction you make about yourself, it's a commitment you take up for a reason, and in the toxin case the only candidate reason (the million) has already been cashed in and can't do double duty as a reason for drinking. On her view the belief stuff is downstream of that.

Here's the case I came up with to pry the two apart, and I'd really like to know whether you think it's genuinely a different case or just the toxin puzzle in a costume. Same billionaire, same million tonight, same perfect intention-reading. This time what you have to intend, tonight, is to show up at a lecture tomorrow afternoon. The topic is something you're indifferent to or slightly bored by, and right now you have no reason to go. But the billionaire tells you — truthfully and credibly, he has a track record — that tomorrow morning you'll recieve a message containing a piece of information that will give you a reason to attend which you cannot now anticipate and will in fact find compelling. You believe him. So you believe you will go, but you cannot now say what your reason will be.

So: compare the two diagnoses as accounts of what makes the original toxin case weird. Does my lecture case separate them, and if it does, which one does it support and which one does it break? Which constraint is really doing the work in the toxin puzzle, and is there a version of the case where the other constraint is the one that bites? Please don't just summarise both positions — pick the one you think is more fundamental and explain why the other one only looks like it's doing work (or tell me if you think I'm wrong that they can be pulled apart at all). One last aside if you have room: if the answer is that you really can't form the toxin intention directly, would getting there indirectly — making a side bet with a friend, or taking something that makes you reckless enough to mean it — count as forming the intention, or does that just change the subject?
