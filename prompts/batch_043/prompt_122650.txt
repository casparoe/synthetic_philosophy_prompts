I'm finishing a master's in astrophysics and sitting in on a philosophy reading group that's working through the fine-tuning argument, and there's a line that gets repeated in it so often that I've started to suspect it's doing several different jobs at once. One of the cosmology postdocs uses it every time anyone raises the narrowness of the life-permitting range for something like the cosmological constant or the strong force: "well, if it were different, we wouldn't be here to notice." He says it in the tone of someone closing a door, and then the conversation moves on as though there is nothing left to explain. Part of me thinks he's right and the rest of us have been fooled by a selection effect; part of me thinks he's said something with no force at all; and part of me suspects the line is doing real work only on a reading that also commits him to a multiverse he hasn't argued for. Someone else in the group keeps referring to a paper from the 1970s that supposedly coined the phrase "anthropic principle" — I can't look anything up from where I'm sitting and I don't need the reference, so please don't build anything on who said what first.

What I'd like is a disambiguation with verdicts. Set out, as a menu, the distinct precise claims that line could be making, roughly ordered from the trivial to the radical, and tell me for each whether you think it's a tautology, a substantive and defensible thesis, a true claim that doesn't do the work he wants, a claim that quietly imports an undefended extra premise, or a straightforward mistake. I have some candidates in mind, and I'd like you to rule on them rather than just elaborate them: (a) the bare observation-selection point that our evidence is filtered by our own existence, which seems to me clearly right but possibly just a fact about how data reach us; (b) the stronger claim that once you properly condition on "there is at least one observer," the life-permittingness of the universe gets probability 1 and therefore nothing surprising is left over — I can't tell whether the conditioning really gets that result or whether the surprisingness just relocates somewhere; (c) the claim that because we couldn't have observed otherwise, no explanation of the values is called for, which looks to me like a non sequitur but I'd like to be told why; and (d) the claim that there actually exists a large ensemble of universes with varying constants so that selection over it explains what we see, which would genuinely dissolve the puzzle if true but seems to me to be a separate, expensive posit rather than something the slogan delivers for free. Add any readings I haven't thought of, including ones where the line turns out to help the theist, or where "observer" is smuggling something in.

A few things I care about specifically, since they're where I'm most confused. First, does the line's force depend on which question we're asking — why there's something rather than nothing, why these laws, why these values — or does it cut across all of them equally? Second, I've been playing with a distinction I call the "modal gap": whether we had any independent reason to think the constant in question could have been otherwise, and whether the supposedly narrow window is narrow in a way that isn't just an artifact of how we parametrize and normalize the range. Does the slogan's success hinge on that, and if so does the hinge favor him or us? Third, is there a version of the point that survives even for a single-universe theorist, or is the anthropic move only honest when there's a real ensemble with a measure over it? Please be committed: I want you to take a definite position on which readings are correct, on which reading (if any) actually licenses closing the discussion the way he closes it, and on who the argument ultimately favors, with your reasons — but where your verdict depends on contested assumptions about the space of possible constants, typicality, or observation selection, say so explicitly and tell me how confident you'd be in the verdict conditional on each way those assumptions could go. If you think my "modal gap" idea is confused, say that too.
