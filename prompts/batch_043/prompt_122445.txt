Me and a coworker were arguing about this over lunch because we're actually speccing something like it: a long-running planning agent with a persistent world-model that proposes patches to its own codebase and prompt stack (humans still approve the patches, for now), and we want to know which of the seams we should actually be paranoid about.

Here are six failure modes I wrote down. I'm sure some of them overlap and some are my own confused paraphrases, but please work with my list rather than replacing it with the standard taxonomy:

1. Size / self-inclusion: the world-model has to contain the agent, but the agent is smaller than the world, so the "me" part of the model is always lossy in a way that isn't just noise.
2. Self-trust loops: the stuff about Lob's theorem and whether an agent can trust its own future outputs, or its own proofs about itself, without an infinite regress.
3. The boundary is a design choice: whether the weights, the context window, the tools, the other copies of the model, and the datacenter count as agent or as environment seems to change what counts as an action vs. an observation, and nothing in the world settles it.
4. Ontology migration: later the agent revises its world-model, old categories dissolve, and the things its goals pointed at may not exist in the new ontology, so "same goal, better model" might not even be well-defined.
5. Inner optimizers: the planner grows sub-systems that do their own optimizing, and from the planner's point of view they're part of the environment, with objectives that aren't its own.
6. No maintenance window (this one is my own framing, I havent seen it named anywhere): there's no way to pause the world, or the agent, to finish migrating the world-model; it has to keep serving plans built on the old model while the new one is halfway built, and the plans it makes in the meantime are themselves part of what it's modelling.

Then settle our argument: my hot take is that 1, 3 and 5 are all the same bug wearing different hats (something about where you draw a line and how lossy things get at the line), that 4 and 6 are the same bug at different time-scales, and that 2 is the only one that's deep in a genuinely mathematical way; my coworker says that's "definately wrong," that 4 is the real one, and that the rest are just engineering.
