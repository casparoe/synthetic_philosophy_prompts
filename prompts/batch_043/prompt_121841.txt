I would be grateful for your help with an argument that has been circulating in my graduate seminar, which I can neither refute nor accept, and which I suspect of proving considerably more than its author intends.

Say that two options are *indiscriminable in value* when an agent who had lived through both, in either order, with full information about both, could not tell which was better — the difference between them, if there is one, falls below the threshold of axiological discrimination. I write ≻ for *better than* and ⪰ for *at least as good as*. The argument, as I have reconstructed it:

(1) If *x* and *y* are indiscriminable in value, then neither is better than the other.
(2) There are finite spectra *L*₀, …, *L*ₙ in which consecutive items are indiscriminable in value while the endpoints are not: consider a sequence that first converts, minute by minute, the discomfort-free minutes of a flourishing eighty-year life into minutes of the mildest discomfort, and then raises, one just-noticeable step at a time, the intensity of those minutes until they are agonising. Our settled verdict at the endpoints is that *L*₀ ≻ *L*ₙ: a flourishing life is better than a life of unrelieved agony.
(3) Hence for each *i*, not(*Lᵢ* ≻ *Lᵢ*₊₁) and not(*Lᵢ*₊₁ ≻ *Lᵢ*).
(4) ⪰ is definable from ≻: *x* ⪰ *y* if and only if not(*y* ≻ *x*).
(5) Hence for each *i*, *Lᵢ*₊₁ ⪰ *Lᵢ* — and, by the other half of (3), *Lᵢ* ⪰ *Lᵢ*₊₁ as well.
(6) ⪰ is transitive: a weak *at least as good as* that did not chain would not be an ordering at all.
(7) Hence *Lₙ* ⪰ *L*₀ — and equally *L*₀ ⪰ *Lₙ* — which by (4) means not(*L*₀ ≻ *Lₙ*).

That contradicts (2). Since (2) records a verdict I will not surrender, the argument appears to force us to conclude that the items in the middle of such a spectrum cannot be ordered by any relation that is both transitive and capable of the verdicts we confidently make at the ends.

What makes me suspicious is that the argument seems to prove too much, and to prove it everywhere. Substitute *taller than* for *better than* and *indistinguishable in height by the naked eye* for *indiscriminable in value*: every premise is true, there are spectra of heights with indiscriminable neighbours and clearly different endpoints, and the conclusion — that nobody is taller than anybody, or that *taller than* is not transitive — is straightforwardly false. So whatever is wrong here is wrong in a way that has nothing especially to do with value, and I need to know which step it is and why it looked compulsory. My suspicion wavers between (1) and (6); I cannot settle it, and I cannot reconstruct why the step I suspect looked fine to me for as long as it did.

My colleague has a reply to the height objection that I also cannot evaluate. She grants that the comparison fails for height but denies that it transfers, on the grounds that the height case is rescued only because heights come on a single scale: the threshold treatment requires the relation to be representable, at least on a finite domain, as a semiorder — a single numerical assignment together with a fixed positive threshold, with *x* ≻ *y* just in case the number for *x* exceeds the number for *y* by more than the threshold. Where there is one underlying magnitude, the strict relation stays transitive and acyclic and only the derived weak relation fails to chain. The goods that vary along an axiological spectrum, she says, are irreducibly plural, so there is no such magnitude, so the rescue is unavailable, so the conclusion stands for value even though it fails for height. Is that right? I would particularly like to know whether the pluralist must choose between commensurability and transitivity, or whether she has some other way of breaking the chain — I have in mind the possibility that indiscriminability marks not equality but a gap, so that indiscriminable items are neither better, nor worse, nor equally good — and what that gap costs her, both in the theory of choice and in the face of a chaining argument directed at the gap itself.

There is a further escalation that worries me more than my colleague's version does. The spectra in (2) are linear, but axiological space is not: between any two options there ramify a great many indiscriminability paths. If the move from (3) to (7) were licensed, the conclusion would not be merely that the endpoints of one spectrum are indiscriminable but that nothing is better than anything. I take that as a reductio of the step rather than as a discovery, but I would like to hear whether the ramified version adds any pressure that the linear version does not, or whether it is simply the same mistake repeated.

So, to be concrete about what I am asking: name the step you reject; explain why it seemed safe; and price the alternatives. As I see it there are four places to intervene — reject (1), holding that every difference in value, however small, is a real betterness and that our inability to detect it is a merely epistemic matter; reject (2), denying that the spectra exist and positing a sharp discontinuity somewhere in the middle; reject (4), allowing a gap between *not better than* and *at least as good as*; or reject (6), accepting that the derived weak relation does not chain while the strict relation remains transitive and acyclic, which I take to be the region of the strict partial orders that the measurement theorists call interval orders and semiorders. I care less which of these you recommend than that you say what each costs, and whether any of them is cheaper for a pluralist about the goods than for a monist.

For what it is worth, there are three things I would like to keep if I can: the reliability of our verdicts in clear cases; the transitivity and asymmetry of *better than*; and a claim I have defended elsewhere, that a difference no agent could ever detect is not a difference that could rationally move that agent. I have come to suspect that the third of these is doing either much more or much less work in this argument than I assumed, and I would like to know which.

One last thing, if you will indulge me. The diachronic version of the case — an agent who accepts each marginally worse setting of her life in exchange for a small payment, and who thereby travels the whole spectrum — ends up badly off without ever making a choice that is worse by her own lights at the time she makes it, and without any cycle anywhere in her strict betterness relation. Does that show that the money-pump argument for transitivity proves too little, since it never gets a grip here, or too much, since it would condemn an agent whose strict relation is impeccably transitive and acyclic? I want to know whether exploitability and coherence come apart in exactly this region, or whether I am conflating two things that the argument keeps separate.
