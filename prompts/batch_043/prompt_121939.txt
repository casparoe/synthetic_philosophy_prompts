Her side of the argument came in four pieces. (1) A precautionary thing: the evidence on insect nociception is messy but not nothing, the numbers involved are astronomical, so even a small credence in insect sentience makes insecticide use a serious moral problem rather than a hygiene question. (2) A replaceability point: killing pest insects doesn't reduce the total amount of insect life, because populations bounce back to whatever the habitat supports, so I'm not "saving" anything by scaling back — I'm just choosing which individuals get to live. (3) A principle she made up on the spot, which she called the "distress budget": rank control methods by expected suffering per unit of crop saved, not by how selective they are; her example was that a slow-acting fungal biopesticide that takes days to kill could easily be worse than a fast pyrethroid knock-down, even though the fungal one is the one with the friendlier label. (4) The one that wound me up most: if most insect lives are short and end badly, maybe we should be suppressing them harder, not less.

I pushed back the way I always do — thresholds mean we only spray when it's worth it, non-target effects and biocontrol disruption are the real harms, resistance is the thing that'll actually bite us, and a certain amount of the suffering talk seems to me to be unanswerable in principle. But we kept sliding past each other, and I think it's because my vocabulary has no word for what an individual insect experiences. Her best line was "your whole framework is written from the point of view of the crop", which I still think is unfair and also possibly right.
