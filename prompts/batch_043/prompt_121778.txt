I'm a journalist — I cover science and ideas, mostly astrobiology and physics-adjacent stuff — and I've been trying for weeks to write a piece about the Fermi question, but I keep getting derailed by an argument a cosmologist friend walked me into after a conference dinner last month, which I now cant find the exit from. Relevant context: a preprint has been doing the rounds since then from a group I won't name, reanalysing archival all-sky mid-infrared data, claiming that out to roughly two thousand light years they would have caught anything shedding waste heat at more than something like a millionth of a star's total output in the relevant band, and that they see nothing. Everyone I talk to says this makes the sky quieter than ever, and I want to know whether it actually moves any dial or just adds a decimal place. Here is the argument, as near as I can reconstruct it, with my friend's flourish at the end. (1) Nothing about us is privileged: hundreds of billions of stars in the Milky Way, billions of years of head start available to anyone who came before us, and no step in the chain from chemistry to a species that can build a spacecraft looks so improbable that it would happen only once in ten billion years. (2) A settlement front is fast on galactic timescales: even at a thousandth of the speed of light, with a few centuries of stopover per system, it crosses the galaxy in a few tens of millions of years, well under one percent of the galaxy's age, so anyone who ever started, at almost any point in galactic history, would have finished long before the Sun formed. (3) If such a front reached us, the Solar System and its neighbourhood would show it — hardware, settlements, industrial waste heat — and given the new bound we would now have detected the waste heat out to a couple of thousand light-years. (4) We see none of it. (5) So no one ever starts: either no other technological species has ever appeared in the Milky Way, or every single one that appeared abstained from expanding, permanently, without one exception across billions of years and billions of separate chances. (6) But the chain applies to us too, so if it is sound it also establishes, from an astronomical null result, that our own descendants will never settle beyond the Solar System — a conclusion about engineering, economics and politics drawn from a telescope survey, of exactly the kind we would laugh at anywhere else (no one has built a net-positive fusion plant, therefore fusion is impossible, therefore physics forbids it). (7) And it generalises outward: run (1) through (5) galaxy by galaxy and you get the result that the observable universe contains no engineering anywhere, a claim about a hundred billion galaxies resting on what is really a null result in our own neighbourhood plus an assumption about what expansion has to look like. (8) Then the bit that scrambled me: someone at the table said the mediocrity move in (1) can be pointed at time instead of space. Draw yourself as a random sample from all the observers the Milky Way will ever contain; if expansion happens, the overwhelming majority of them live downstream of it, in a filled galaxy, billions of years from now, whereas we find ourselves absurdly early, with most of the galaxy's star-forming life still ahead of us. That is atypical unless the future is short — unless, in fact, (5) is true. So the very principle I used to argue that we are not special in space now seems to require that we are special in time, and (5) stops being the surprising upshot of the argument and starts looking like what the argument's own first premise demands.

I don't accept that, but I can't locate the mistake, and I also don't want an easy out. I'd most like to keep that interstellar settlement is physically possible for a species at roughly our level, and I'd most like to keep that the sky really is quiet in the way the new preprint describes; I definitely do not want to be pushed into space travel is impossible, or they are all sitting at home by choice, or they are already here and hiding, and I really don't want to be pushed into a Doomsday-flavoured conclusion about how much future humanity has. One of the others at dinner said the real problem is (3): a mature wave wouldn't look like anything, because settlements run cold, infrastructure can be seeded dormant and woken later, and a civilization's detectable energy use might fall as it gets older rather than rise, so the null result only constrains loud, wasteful, early-stage civilizations like the one we would be for a few centuries. My friend says that is special pleading — you can't make every possible colonization invisible by stipulation — and I don't know who is right or whether it matters which of them is. So: which step do you reject, why does that step look fine until you press on it, what does each of the other plausible candidates cost me if I give it up instead, and does the new infrared bound do any real work here or is it noise? And please write it as continuous prose: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, and no numbered list in your answer either if you can manage it — just paragraphs, the way you'd write a long email to a colleague who then has to defend the thing in print.
