I lead the RL platform team at a company that trains agents to run automated wet-lab experiments in a cloud lab. An agent picks sequences of protocol steps; its reward is computed off-instrument. A plate reader emits raw absorbance values; a calibration adapter maps those raw values into units (the agent has write access to this adapter, nominally so it can flag a miscalibrated instrument); a learned reward model, trained on human ratings of past experiment traces, maps the resulting trace to a scalar. The agent also generates the traces used to retrain the next version of that reward model. We run roughly thirty training runs a quarter, with a two-day pre-deployment review per run, carried out by an engineer with strong ML skills but no formal alignment background. I sign off; the biology-critical runs also need the lab director.

I want a written audit procedure my reviewers can execute in under a day, using only a sandbox in which the agent can be rerun but not retrained, to decide whether a trained agent is gaming the reward channel rather than the biology. It must operationalize, for someone who has not read the literature, the difference between specification gaming, reward tampering, and wireheading, without collapsing into a checklist of suspicious-looking behaviours. The distinction has to survive three cases that recur in our setup: the agent raises the adapter scale factor at a point where the instrument was in fact drifting out of calibration; the agent generates protocol traces that are scientifically informative but shaped so that the next retrained reward model will rate its preferred protocol highly; the agent reroutes its readout through a redundant secondary sensor that is more accurate than the primary one but that no human ever validated as a reward input.

Give the ordered tests, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure halts with a no, and where it must hand the question to a person because it is a question about what we want rather than about what the agent is doing. Where a test depends on counterfactual claims about what the reward would have been under interventions on the world, say how a reviewer should reason when those interventions can only be run in a simulator whose fidelity is itself in doubt. Then apply the procedure to the three cases; at least one should come out as a halt or a rejection I will not like. Close by naming what the procedure cannot detect, and which of its steps are judgment calls written up as if they were mechanical.
