For the last several months I have been serving, at first rather unwillingly, on the charter committee of a cooperative I joined years ago as a graduate student. It has since moved almost all of its governance into a machine-executed charter, and we are now rewriting that charter from scratch. The matter has stopped being academic for me: what we adopt in the next few weeks will be load-bearing for an organization of roughly four thousand members, and every serious disagreement on the committee has turned out to be a disagreement about self-amendment.

Here is what we have and where we are stuck. The charter is a text that is also executable code. When a proposal is certified as having met whatever threshold the charter then specifies, the change takes effect automatically at the close of the vote, and members' entitlements are adjusted by the same mechanism. In the ordinary case no official, committee, or judge decides anything; there is no appeal and no authorized interpreter. Among the provisions now in force are:

R1: Any provision of this charter, including this provision, may be amended or repealed by a two-thirds vote of the membership.

R2: No amendment may dissolve the organization, or divest a member of units already vested, unless it is approved by a three-quarters vote and confirmed at a second vote held not less than sixty days later.

R3: This charter may be amended only in accordance with provisions contained in this charter.

There are three live disputes. First, last spring a two-thirds majority, invoking R1, purported to repeal R2 outright, and the code executed it. Some of us maintain that the repeal was itself an amendment and so required the R2 procedure, and therefore never became valid; others reply that R1 by its own terms authorizes the amendment of any provision, R2 included, and that R2's entrenchment could only ever have been as strong as R1 allowed. Second, a proposal now circulating would abolish the membership assembly and vest its powers in a standing council of five, again by a two-thirds vote under R1. Third, in order to quiet the resulting uncertainty, the committee is considering a clause declaring retroactively that every amendment certified since the charter's adoption is to be treated as having been validly enacted from the moment of its certification, including any amendment that altered the amendment rules themselves.

I have been reading Hans Kelsen, writing in the first half of the twentieth century and obviously with nothing remotely like this in view, and I want to know where his apparatus would come down. As I understand it, his commitments are roughly these: every norm borrows its validity from a higher norm, up to a historically first constitution, whose validity is not itself posited but presupposed by a basic norm that jurists must assume if they are to interpret a coercive order as law; law is distinguished from mere power by being an order of norms authorizing sanctions; efficacy is a condition of validity, for both individual norms and the order as a whole; a change made in the manner the constitution prescribes is legal change, whereas an irregular displacement of the constitution is a revolution, after which legal cognition simply presupposes a new basic norm; and an ought can never be derived from an is. If I have any of that wrong, correct me first.

Then, concretely: would Kelsen say there is a legal order here at all, given that no official ever applies a norm and that compliance is produced causally by software rather than demanded by anyone, and what content would he have to assign to the basic norm? How would he classify the repeal of R2 and the proposed abolition of the assembly — as void or merely defeasible amendments, or as a revolution that changes the basic norm and hence the identity of the order — and does his framework even have the resources to call an amendment unconstitutional when the amendment power has been drafted so as to reach every provision, including its own source? What would he make of the retroactive validation clause, given that his theory is said to permit retroactive norms, when the validating norm would owe its competence to the very charter whose breach it proposes to cure? And, finally, what would he tell four thousand people to write down this month?

I am not after citations or textual exegesis; I want the shape of the reasoning, including the places where he would say our question is ill-posed. If it is useful for contrast, I would also like a brief account of where H. L. Hart, with his rule of recognition understood as a social rule among officials, would come out differently on the first and third disputes.
