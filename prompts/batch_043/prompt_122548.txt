Hey — I'm a science journalist and I do a steady diet of short same-day pieces (500–800 words) on language acquisition, infant cognition, and, increasingly, ML papers pitched as finally settling some old nature/nurture argument. I keep writing sentences like "the study suggests grammar is hardwired," "the ability turns out to be learned after all," "this undercuts the claim that we're born knowing X," and honestly half the time I couldn't tell you what im claiming. My copy desk will not catch it, and twice now an author has emailed after publication saying we overstated them.

What I want is a decision procedure I can actually run in ten minutes, on a press release plus an abstract plus one short phone call with the author, with no linguistics background, that tells me which of those sentences I'm allowed to write and what to write instead. Constraints: it has to output something my editor will print (she kills copy that is hedges stacked on hedges), it has to give me one line I can defend if the researcher complains, and it has to tell me when to stop and hand the question to a human — me making an editorial call, the author being asked what their model actually had access to, or an outside reviewer.

Please build it around the real structure of the poverty-of-the-stimulus argument rather than around a vibe: the claim that learners end up knowing something the input they got didn't pin down, the point that kids mostly aren't corrected or told which sentences are impossible, and the inference from that gap to something in the learner. I specifically want:

- The "where is the work being done?" question in a form I can ask a researcher on the phone without sounding like I'm accusing them of cheating: what to ask about a model's training signal, architecture, and pretraining data, and what to ask about a child study. My sense is that a successful learner only relocates the puzzle, and I want the procedure to say what follows when that's true and what doesn't follow.
- A rule that makes me fill in the blanks before I use the word at all: innate relative to *which* input, given *which* starting machinery. If I can't fill them in from the press release, the procedure should tell me to drop the word and give me the replacement sentence.

Hard cases it has to handle: heritability and twin studies; a model that does well on a linguistic benchmark; "a language that lacks X" field reports; newborn and other very-early-infant effects; critical-period claims.

Then run it on three of my actual situations:

1. A group in Zurich trains a neural net on about 40 million words of child-directed speech from an existing corpus plus a few thousand hours of nursery audio, and it does better than expected at forming yes/no questions.
