I am a psychological wellbeing practitioner in an NHS Talking Therapies service, and I run a six-session guided self-help group for people with worry, health anxiety and work-related burnout. More and more clients arrive already carrying a version of the modern Stoic revival — the podcasts, the week-long Stoic courses, the harder-edged founder-bro version — and they bring it into the room as a tool: the dichotomy between what is up to us and what is not, attributed to Epictetus at the opening of the handbook compiled from his lectures, and usually drawn on a flipchart as two circles. In supervision I have been told that the modern word control imports something the ancients did not mean in quite that way: what is up to us is not what we can causally manipulate but what is attributable to our own judgement and choice alone, so that not only the weather and other people's opinions but also our health, our reputation and our own bodies fall on the far side of the line. My supervisor used two Greek terms I only half followed — one for the faculty that is up to us, one for the habit of attaching a reservation along the lines of if nothing prevents it to every aim one adopts — and she also insisted the two circles are not a licence for quietism, since the ancient Stoics expected the person who has made progress to stand for office, marry and take part in public affairs unless something prevents it.

What I need is a procedure rather than an explanation: something I can run in about twenty minutes with a client who has written down six things they are worried about, and that a trainee with our six-week training could run too. Give me the questions in the order they should be asked, what counts as an adequate answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision back to the client because the matter is one of values rather than classification. Three features of the material worry me and I want them built into the design rather than footnoted. First, most of what clients bring is partly influenceable — a promotion panel, a son's drug use, a union ballot, an election — so a strict dichotomy returns an answer that feels wrong, while the three-ring version popular in management self-help (what I control, what I can influence, what I must accept) seems truer to how action actually works and may lose the whole point of the exercise; tell me whether to keep the middle ring and what the procedure loses either way. Second, clients cannot switch their assent at will: panic attacks, intrusive thoughts and grief are not up to us in any sense a distressed person will recognise, so the procedure needs a timescale — what can be trained over months versus what can be chosen in the next ten minutes — and needs to say plainly when the tool should be withdrawn, because I have clients who turn the question is this up to me into a fresh checking ritual. Third, my group finds it appalling that the ancient view apparently counts grief itself as a judgement and therefore as up to us; say whether the procedure absorbs that or brackets it.

Then run it, in the form a session would actually take, on three cases: a client anxious about a promotion panel next week; a client whose adult son is using drugs and who oscillates between I can make him stop and nothing I do makes any difference; and a client who has concluded that a colleague's harassment of her is not up to me and has gone quiet about it. I suspect the procedure will tell me something about that third case I will not want to hear, and I would rather hear it. Finally, state what the procedure cannot do: the presentations on which it must not be used at all, and the points at which no amount of structuring removes the need for clinical judgement. Begin with a single sentence — the one my trainees could memorise and pin above the desk — that states the procedure's core move, then give the procedure and the worked cases; no preamble, and no restatement of what I have asked.
