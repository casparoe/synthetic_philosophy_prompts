I help put together a small photocopied zine with some friends — half of them are comp-neuro / ML people, the other half are just friends who read it because its free — and I want a piece for the next issue. My brother does component teardown videos on YouTube (the kind where they decap a chip and read the die markings) and last month he "reviewed" a washing machine control board as if it were a CPU, which is where the idea came from.

What I want is a hardware evaluation memorandum, dated 1958, in which a team of vacuum-tube computer engineers takes the brain-as-computer idea completely literally and files an assessment of the brain as a computing machine — as though a specimen had been delivered to their lab and they had to decide whether to recommend it for procurement. Deadpan throughout: no winking, no "of course the brain isn't really a computer", no nudge at the reader at the end. Around 700-900 words, formatted as a memo with numbered sections (something like: Summary, Switching Elements, Logic and Memory, Instruction Set, Reliability, Power and Cooling, Recommendation).

Background to build the jokes out of, so you don't need to recall the source: von Neumann's short, unfinished book from the Silliman lectures, published posthumously in 1958, treats the brain as a computing machine but keeps stressing how unlike the computers of his day it is. The points I want the engineers to get exactly right: the elementary switching element is orders of magnitude slower than a vacuum tube (milliseconds vs microseconds), and since the system finishes enormously complicated tasks in a fraction of a second, it must be doing an enormous number of things at once — which also means the computation has to be very wide and very shallow, only a handful of sequential steps deep, so anything needing a long precise chain of serial operations is exactly what this machine should be bad at (and is). There's no separate memory and arithmetic unit, no clock, no readable stored program — it violates the architecture the senior engineer on the memo himself helped invent. The elements look partly digital (all-or-none pulses) and partly analog (firing rates, thresholds, chemical modulaton), which offends him. Nobody can find the adder, and his worry — von Neumann's own — is that the language the thing uses internally isn't mathematics or formal logic at all. Components are individually slow and unreliable, yet the system works, by redundancy and statistics; and the failure mode isn't an error code, its the machine degrading, or quietly filling in the missing part. Runs on about twenty watts.

Constraints that matter to me: the laughs have to come from the engineering being right — a computational neuroscientist should be able to read it and concede the objections are real, and a non-specialist should get the joke anyway. No quotations or epigraphs from any real book, and no named real people in the memo itself — invented staff and an invented company are fine. The final Recommendation should follow the memo's own logic to a conclusion the reader recoils from: my instinct is that the team ends up recommending the device be re-engineered with fast, reliable, properly digital components and a separated memory unit — that is, that we build a computer instead — and never notices that this removes the one property that made the machine worth evaluating in the first place. If you have a better reductio than that, use it.
