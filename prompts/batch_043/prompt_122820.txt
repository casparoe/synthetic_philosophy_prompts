I spent most of my career fitting models to field measurements of stomatal conductance, and in retirement I have been reading about the problem of verisimilitude: whether one false theory can be genuinely closer to the truth than another, and whether the succession of theories in a mature science can be described as an accumulation of truth rather than merely of predictive success. I keep returning to a case from my own field, and I would like it used as the test case for a position I find attractive but cannot state precisely enough to defend.

Two models of the same leaves. Model A gets the mechanism right in outline: guard cells actively regulate aperture through turgor and ion flux, with hydraulic feedback from the leaf to the soil, so that conductance is the output of a control system rather than a passive consequence of evaporation. But it badly mis-specifies the functional form, treating conductance as a linear function of vapour pressure deficit and of soil water potential, and it fails qualitatively at the dry end of the range. Model B postulates no active regulation at all, treats the leaf as a passive resistor–capacitor network, and fits a non-rectangular hyperbola to the same data; it reproduces measured conductance to within a few percent across the entire range my instruments could resolve, including at the extremes where A breaks down. Every physiologist I know would say that B is the better model and A is the better piece of biology, and I want to know whether that double judgement can be cashed out as a claim about closeness to truth.

Please argue for the following position. Verisimilitude is not a two-place relation between a theory and the truth but a three-place one, holding between a theory, the truth, and a specification of the questions about the system that are in play: what I would call an agenda, that is, a partition of the space of possible states of the system into cells whose members we are not yet prepared, or equipped, to distinguish. On this view B is closer to the truth than A relative to any agenda coarse enough that the question 'what is the conductance at this vapour pressure deficit?' is the finest question asked, while A is closer than B relative to an agenda that also asks what the guard cells are doing; and the familiar impossibility arguments for content-based definitions — those showing that if closeness is measured by comparing truth-content with falsity-content, then no false theory is ever more truthlike than another, and no false theory ever closer than a true one — lose their grip, because they tacitly require a single question-invariant ordering.

In making the argument, please do four things. First, say what an agenda is in terms a working modeller could apply to her own equations, and what makes one agenda strictly finer than another. Second, explain why agenda-relativity is a claim about what closeness to the truth consists in, and not merely an expression of humility about which false theory we should prefer. Third, confront the objection that this makes verisimilitude interest-relative in a way that lets any theory count as progress given a suitably chosen agenda, and say what constrains the admissible agendas; my own suggestion is that the true theory must come out maximally truthlike at every admissible agenda, so that agendas cannot be gerrymandered to flatter a falsehood. Fourth, say what becomes of the thought that the history of a science is a history of getting closer to the truth, if closeness is always closeness at some resolution. Keep the discussion concrete: where it helps, use cases such as the ideal gas law as a limiting case of a more exact treatment, or Newtonian mechanics against relativity, and mark clearly where you are stipulating a definition rather than reporting an established usage.
