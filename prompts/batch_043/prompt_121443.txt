(a) [6 marks] Distinguish the argument from illusion from the argument from hallucination. Which premise of the argument from illusion does the disjunctivist reject, and how exactly does the rejection work?

(b) [8 marks] The disjunctivist holds that a veridical perception and a hallucination subjectively indistinguishable from it are mental events of fundamentally different kinds: there is no "highest common factor" that the two share. Consider the following attempt to get a common factor back. Call it the staircase case. A subject wears a headset through which she sees a real red cube. Over the course of a minute, in six hundred steps, the headset gradually replaces the light arriving from the cube with a computer-generated image of a qualitatively identical cube, and the real cube is quietly removed somewhere in the middle of the sequence. No single step is discriminable by the subject from the step before it. The argument then runs: the subject's evidence in the first case is the same as her evidence in the second, and so on down the chain; so her evidence at the start is the same as at the end; but the start is a veridical perception and the end is a pure hallucination; therefore a perception and a hallucination can share all of their evidential force, and disjunctivism is false. Does the staircase argument do any work that the plain argument from hallucination does not do? If the disjunctivist should reject one of its premises, which one — and is rejecting it ad hoc, or does the disjunctivist have independent reason to reject it?

(c) [6 marks] "If disjunctivism is true, then a hallucinating subject has no reason at all to believe what she seems to perceive." Is this a consequence of the view, an objection to it, or a misreading of it? Explain.

Here is the answer, verbatim, from a candidate I'll call 2207.

The argument from illusion and the argument from hallucination tend to get run together, but they start from different cases. The argument from illusion starts from cases where something looks other than it is — the oar that looks bent in the water, the two lines that look unequal — and argues that what the subject is immediately aware of cannot be the physical object, since the physical object does not have the property it appears to have. The argument from hallucination instead starts from cases in which there is nothing there at all. The second is the cleaner argument, because it needs no claim about how the physical object is; it needs only the bare possibility of an experience subjectively indistinguishable from a veridical perception in which nothing is perceived. And that is a possibility the disjunctivist already concedes. Their claim is not that the subject can always tell the difference; it is that the indistinguishability of the two cases gives you no common nature. So the premise the disjunctivist rejects in the argument from illusion is the step from "the subject cannot tell these apart" to "there is a single kind of mental event, or a single kind of object of awareness, present in both."

The staircase case is supposed to force the common factor back by walking you down a chain of cases rather than asking you to compare two. But the disjunctivist has a good reply. The argument needs indiscriminability to be transitive, and it is not: small differences can accumulate into a large one without any single step being noticeable, so the disjunctivist can say that the final case differs in kind from the first even though no step in the sequence marks where the change happens. There is a second point available too. The cases in the middle of the staircase are not pure perceptions or pure hallucinations, they are mixtures of the two, so the sequence does not even compare a perception with a hallucination at its endpoints. Nor is any of this ad hoc. The failure of transitivity in sorites-style sequences is a general phenomenon that has nothing to do with perception, and the disjunctivist already had independent reason, from the nature of perception as a relation to particular objects, to deny a common kind. The staircase argument therefore hides the same disputed premise inside a lot of small steps; it does not add anything to the plain argument from hallucination.

Where disjunctivism really does come apart, though, is on illusion, and here I think the argument from illusion is the stronger of the two arguments rather than the weaker. In the bent-oar case there is no object present with the properties the subject is aware of, so the subject must be aware of something that is not the physical object — a sense-datum. And that is precisely what disjunctivism cannot allow. Since it holds that in veridical perception the object itself partly constitutes the experience, it is committed to denying that perceptual experience has representational content at all, which is the whole point of the view. So it has no vocabulary for what happens when things merely look some way, and no way of allowing that what is immediately present to the subject in an illusion is the same sort of thing as what is present in a hallucination. A theory of perception that cannot handle the bent oar is not a theory of perception.

One last consequence is worth stating, because it is what makes the position feel so expensive. Because the disjunctivist refuses any common factor between perception and hallucination, she is committed to saying that the hallucinating subject has no justification whatsoever for the beliefs she forms on the basis of her experience — not merely less justification than the perceiver, but none — and indeed to denying that the two states share any property at all, since any shared property would be a common factor. She is also thereby pushed toward just the sceptical conclusion that disjunctivism was supposed to help us avoid: that the hallucinating subject is in no way in the same evidential position as the perceiver. That, I think, is the strongest single reason to reject the view.
