I am in the lower sixth and have been writing up the first chapter of an Extended Project on how consciousness is distributed across animals. My supervisor is going to read it next week, and before she does I would like to know whether the position I have ended up in is actually one position, or whether I have quietly borrowed from two or three incompatible ones. Below is where I think I have landed. I would be grateful if you could treat it as an object to be audited rather than as a set of questions to be answered: what I want is an account of what commits me to what, argued from the commitments themselves. Please do not give me a reading list or point me at published authors; I cannot easily get hold of journal articles, and in any case what I need to know is whether my own claims fit together.

1. Consciousness is a matter of functional organisation rather than of what a creature is made of. Anything with the right organisation would be conscious, including in principle something running on silicon. In particular, no anatomical feature can be strictly required: it cannot be that a cortex, or a thalamus, or any particular piece of tissue is what consciousness needs. I have started calling this the no-anatomy rule.

2. The only evidence about another creature's experience that we could ever actually have is its behaviour, and the behaviour that carries the weight is flexible learning: not habituation or sensitisation, but the animal revising a learned response after a single unreinforced or punished trial, and then carrying that revision into a situation in which the original response was never trained. Call this the revaluation-and-transfer test. Anything that passes it, I want to say, has some experience or other.

3. My verdicts: mammals and birds, conscious; cephalopods, probably; fish, probably; insects, almost certainly not; spiders, no.

4. Where the evidence is genuinely unclear, we should organise our practices — what we eat, how we deal with pests, what we are willing to do in laboratories — as though the animal were conscious, while not claiming that it is. Acting on a risk is not the same as believing that the risk has been realised.

5. Consciousness comes in degrees: a mouse has a thinner, dimmer version of what I have. But moral standing does not trail along behind it in the same graded way. Below some line an animal's pain counts for little or nothing; above it, it counts in full. I do not think a fish's pain matters a tenth as much as a human's merely because its mental life is a tenth as rich.

6. A future artificial system could be conscious, and the way we would find out is by examining its architecture — whether it has the right sort of global integration, a single arena in which information is made available to many otherwise separate subsystems — rather than by putting it through learning tests.

7. Two cases I invented in order to test myself, with my actual reactions to them. First: a burrowing wasp no larger than a grain of rice, whose nervous system we have mapped completely and can simulate end to end on a laptop, with fewer than a thousand neurons and nothing in it that looks remotely like a central arena, but which passes the revaluation-and-transfer test reliably and in more than one sensory modality. My reaction is that it is not conscious, and that if the test says otherwise then the test is too easy. Second: a sluggish deep-sea fish with a large, layered brain whose overall organisation resembles a simplified bird's, and which fails every version of the test we can devise. My reaction is that it probably is conscious, and that the test is simply the wrong instrument for an animal that slow. I notice that I am prepared to overrule my own test in both directions at once, and I cannot tell whether that shows the test to be defective or me to be.

What I would like from you is this. Go through the seven and tell me which pairs are in real tension and which merely look as though they are; I suspect at least one of the apparent conflicts dissolves if you are careful, and I would rather you told me so than manufactured a difficulty. Rank the genuine tensions by how severe they are rather than listing them as equally bad, and for each one say what exactly would have to give and what giving it would cost me elsewhere in the set. Please do not rank them by how unfashionable they make me. Finally, if there is a single revision that would clean up more than one of the tensions at once, say which it is — but do not assume that I would rather have a tidy position than a true one, so tell me plainly what the revision would commit me to.
