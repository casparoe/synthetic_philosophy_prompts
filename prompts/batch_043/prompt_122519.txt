My father sustained a severe brain injury eight months ago. He was initially described as minimally conscious, then as emerging from that state; there were several weeks in which he followed simple commands, and one afternoon in which the team asked him to imagine walking through his own house while they scanned him, and the images came back looking the way they would in a healthy volunteer. We are now being asked to make decisions about long-term care. I am the one with the authority to make them. The question I keep being pulled back to is whether there is still anyone there.

I read some philosophy as an undergraduate and have started reading again since this began. I have built the following argument, and I can neither accept it nor find the exit.

(1) Hold fixed everything that could in principle be observed or measured about my father: the microphysical state of his brain, its causal and functional organization, his behavior, his dispositions to behave, his history. I can conceive of all of that obtaining while there is nothing at all that it is like to be him. The standard conceivability argument treats this as a genuine modal gap rather than a failure of my imagination, and I cannot see my way to denying it.

(2) If the gap is genuine, it is not only a fact about whole worlds. For any single system, its physical and functional description does not entail that it is conscious; being conscious is not something the physical-functional facts about a system bring along with them.

(3) So whether such a system is conscious is a further fact, and the physical-functional facts impose no constraint on which way it goes. For any distribution of consciousness across physical-functional duplicates — all of them conscious, none, every third one, present between noon and midnight and absent otherwise — there is a metaphysically possible world in which things are distributed that way.

(4) In particular: there is a possible world identical to ours in every observable respect up to the morning of the injury, in which the lights went out then and have stayed out while everything since unfolded exactly as it has. There is another in which they came back for the six weeks of his rehabilitation and went out again in October. There is another in which everyone alive went dark at midnight last Tuesday and I did not.

(5) If (4) is right, then nothing I can observe is evidence either way about whether there is anyone there: no scan, no squeeze of the hand, no answer to a question, nothing about his brain or his behavior. And the same holds for everyone else, and for my own past, since the memories I have of being conscious are precisely the memories my counterpart in those worlds has.

(6) But I will not accept that. I take the scan and the hand-squeeze to be good evidence, and I am not willing to say that consciousness can blink on and off with nothing observable changing.

My sister says the reasoning is valid and the only real question is which premise is cheapest to abandon. A friend who has read more of this literature than I have says one of the middle steps does something the earlier premises do not license, but refuses to say which. I would rather keep (1) and (6) than any of (2) through (5) — but I would rather know the price than protect them.

So: which step do you reject, and why did it look as though it followed? If there are several places to cut, price each of them for me, including the two I would least like: accepting (5), and giving up (1). And tell me what I would be committing to in each case — about causation, about what is held fixed in a possible world, about possibility versus evidence, or about other minds — since I have to go back to that hospital room with whatever I decide.
