Our coach told me to look up Scanlon's contractualism because it says something interesting about cases where one person's loss is huge and everyone else's is tiny. "Scanlon's contractualism says that an act is wrong if there is some principle for the general regulation of behaviour that no one could reasonably reject and that forbids the act. The 'no one could reasonably reject' bit puts it in the social contract tradition: morality is what self-interested people would agree to in a bargain, with the word 'reasonable' added to make it sound less brutal than Hobbes or Gauthier. The important upshot is that contractualism cannot add up benefits and burdens across different people. You have to look at each person's complaint one at a time, from that person's own point of view, and ask which complaint is strongest — you can never pile up a thousand weak complaints to beat one strong one. So numbers basically drop out entirely: one person losing a hand outweighs any number of people getting a stubbed toe, and one death outweighs any number of mild headaches, no matter how many. This means contractualism quietly collapses into Rawls-style maximin, since you always end up asking which single individual is worst off under each option. And because what matters is whether people actually object, anyone who complains loudly enough can effectively veto a principle, while burdens nobody bothers to complain about create no complaint at all. That last feature is what sinks the theory: no sane moral view says that one person's stubbed toe matters more than a million people's stubbed toes."
