Hi — I'm one of three people writing the text for a small permanent gallery on the history of medicine at a regional science museum. My background is exhibit design, not history, and our de facto historian is a board member who mostly communicates by writing sigh in the margins of my drafts. We've got roughly forty wall panels, a twelve-foot timeline, and an audio-guide script to get done before we open in eight months, so this is a call I'm making two or three times a week, not once. Panels are about ninety words, no footnotes, read mostly by school groups and occasionally quoted by the local paper; the director signs everything off, and anything that looks like picking a fight goes to our lay advisory board. I get maybe fifteen minutes per panel and I can't run to the historian everytime.

What I want is an actual procedure: the questions in order, what counts as an adequate answer to each, where it stops with a this needs a rewrite, where it stops with a this is a values call, send it upstairs, and where it says leave it alone because the honest version costs more than it buys. I've read a little about the whig interpretation of history — Butterfield's 1931 book, and the general complaint that telling the past as a march toward us flatters the present and falsifies the past — and it's easy to nod at in the abstract and weirdly hard to catch in my own sentences, so I need it operationalized into something I can run on a draft. A few things I want the procedure to handle, and please tell me which of my home-made distinctions are real and which are junk: the martyr frame, where someone was right, was laughed at, and died unappreciated, which I suspect is just whig history with a victim in it; the they almost got it habit, describing past actors as groping toward our answer; my own favourite move, the myth-busting panel, no, people in the middle ages didn't think the earth was flat, which I'm now worried is itself a progress story in which we're the enlightened ones and the past is a crowd of rubes; the timeline, which is nothing but milestones and might be the most whiggish object in the room; and panels where what was progress for one group was straightforwardly loss for another. I've been using a rough test I call the swap test: if the alternative that actually lost had won instead, and my panel still reads basically the same with the names changed, then I haven't written history, I've written a just-so story. Is that the right test, is there a better one, and what should I do when a true correction would eat forty of my ninety words and lose a ten-year-old?

Then run the finished procedure on three things. First, my draft panel on the doctor in an 1840s Vienna maternity ward who made the staff wash their hands, was ignored, and ended up in an asylum: I want it to be moving but I think it's currently the martyr frame, so tell me what it should say instead. Second, the twelve-foot timeline: should it exist at all, and if it does, what does a non-whig timeline look like in chunks that short, given I can't run a second rail of roads not taken the whole way without loosing the school groups entirely. Third, the one I'll like least: the flat-earth myth-busting panel. If your procedure says it's smug in the same way the whig panels are smug, say so plainly and tell me what to do with it, because it's the panel I'm proudest of. And there's a fourth one I want flagged rather than solved — a panel on a local surgeon who pioneered a technique in the 1930s and also operated on people without asking them — where I want the procedure to say out loud that this stopped being a question it can answer.
