I've been going in circles on whether dreams are experiences — I skimmed the two famous papers (Malcolm says no, Dennett says the question gets settled by decision rather than discovery) and honestly they both sound right to me in places, which is a bad sign. So I want to stop reading and just go with my gut, case by case, and see if there's a principle underneath. Here are my verdicts.

1. I dreamt I was flying over a city, very vivid, woke up grinning. Yes, that was an experience.
2. Lucid dream: partway through I realised I was asleep and deliberately decided to fly. Yes — if anything more of an experience than 1, because I was in charge.
3. Woke at 4am out of nothing at all — no images, no story, no me — just flat dread for ten seconds. Yes. Weirdly this is the one I'm most sure about, even though there was nothing I saw or heard.
4. Dreamt I was reading a page of Russian (I speak none) and understanding it perfectly. No. I did not have an experience of understanding Russian. I did have something — I'll say an experience, but not one of understanding.
5. Dreamt I saw in one glance why a theorem I've been stuck on for years is true, and woke with the whole proof. Also no. I don't want to say I had an experience of understanding; at most an experience that seemed like one, and I'm not even sure "seeming" is available to me in sleep, since seeming is a kind of judging.
6. Someone woken out of deep slow-wave sleep who says "nothing, I wasn't anywhere." No experience. Presumably the same for most of the night.
7. Anaesthesia awareness: fully paralysed, no sign of anything to the surgical team, afterwards reports the surgeons' conversation and pain. Yes, obviously a real experience — and the fact that nobody could tell at the time is irrelevant.
8. A dream with a seam in it: I'm in a kitchen, and then I'm on a motorway, and at the time there was no sense of a jump and no sense of travelling between them. My verdict: no experience of getting from one to the other, and also no fact of the matter about the "gap," which I know sounds like a cop-out.
9. The composed-on-waking case: I surface from sleep already inside a dream-narrative, I'm fairly sure I'm making it up as I go, and it's about something I'd never dream about. Gut says: not a dream. But whatever is happening at waking is itself an experience of some kind. So maybe my "no" is only a verdict about whether it was a dream, not about whether there was an experience.

The pairs I can't reconcile. 2 vs 5: in the lucid dream I judge, infer and decide while asleep, but in 5 I supposedly can't understand — both are cognitive things happening in sleep, so what separates judging from understanding? And 7 vs 6: the only difference I can find is that one got reported later and the other didn't, which sounds like a fact about memory rather than about experience. Throw in 3: objectless dread counts as dread, but I don't want to say dreamt pain is really pain, and I can't say why feelings differ.

My own attempt: something is an experience iff there is a true answer to the question what it was like for the subject, where that answer is fixed by what the subject would say if asked at the time. I think that gets 1, 3 and 7 and maybe 6, but it also says yes to 5, which I don't want, and I can't work out what it says about 8 or 9.

What I'd like: the principle, or two or three candidate principles stated precisely, with each clause tied to the case that forces it; what each predicts about two cases I haven't listed — (a) a dream I've had maybe fifty times, word for word, since I was eight, (b) a dream in which I dream that I'm remembering a dream from earlier that same night, where there was no earlier dream; and an honest list of which of my verdicts it can't keep and which one you'd overturn. If you think 2 and 5 can both be kept, tell me what the difference between them actually is.

End with the principle you'd defend, in one sentence, wrapped in \answer{...}.
