I have spent most of my working life as a machinist and, for the last six years, as the person who keeps a small fleet of CNC machines running. For about two years now I have been reading Marx on my own in the evenings, with no one to check my understanding against and no library to work in. What I keep returning to is his account of machinery in Capital, and in particular the pair of distinctions that seem to hold the whole story together: constant capital versus variable capital, and dead labor stored up in a machine versus living labor performed by a worker. As I have come to understand him, the machine can only hand back on the value that went into building it, while new value — and therefore surplus value — can come only from living labor. That is what makes the capitalist who replaces workers with machines into someone who, on Marx's own account, is sawing off the branch he is sitting on: he wins a temporary advantage over his competitors while helping to erode the source of profit itself.

The case I cannot fit into this is automation that is not merely a replacement for muscle or for a routine, but is built out of recordings of what skilled workers actually did. A system is trained on thousands of hours of the movements, corrections, and judgments of the people who are best at a job — the ones who can hear a bearing going, or who know which cut will chatter before it chatters — and once the system has absorbed that, those people are let go and the system does the work. It seems to me that what is inside the machine here is not quite like the value stored in a stamping press. It is skill: someone's living, practiced judgment, extracted, frozen, and then set running without the person attached to it. I have taken to calling it fossilized skill.

My worry is this. If fossilized skill is genuinely dead labor, then a firm whose capital is mostly fossilized skill is a firm with almost no living labor left, and Marx's machinery argument should apply to it in full. But if fossilized skill keeps something of the character of living labor — if it is still, in some sense, somebody's judgment operating rather than a stored-up quantity — then it looks as though the firm can go on producing new value, and new surplus value, with fewer and fewer workers, and the tension Marx relies on never arrives. And it gets worse if you consider a later system trained largely on the output of earlier systems, so that whatever human skill was in there has been diluted across several generations and nobody can point to whose labor it was.

What I would like from you is one concrete example of a case of this kind, worked up carefully enough that I can test my worry against it. Please describe an actual firm or production process — realistic, whether or not it exists yet — with enough detail that I can picture the floor, the machines, the people, who owns what, and where the money goes. It should not depend on anything that contradicts what Marx says about value and profit; the point is to build the case inside his assumptions and see whether it strains them.

Please set your answer out in the following five sections, under these headings and in this order:

1. The case — about 450 words. The concrete example itself, including the ownership arrangement and roughly what happens to the displaced workers.
2. Where the dead labor / living labor line buckles — about 350 words. Which of Marx's claims about machinery stops doing the work he needs it to do in this case, and what downstream conclusion of his is put at risk (the source of surplus value, the pressure on the rate of profit, the reserve army of labor, or whichever it turns out to be).
3. The strongest reply on Marx's behalf — about 300 words. How someone who wants to keep his framework intact would redescribe the case so that the distinction survives, stated as charitably as you can.
4. Is fossilized skill a real addition or a new name for something he already has? — about 250 words. An honest assessment of my own idea: whether it picks out something Marx's categories genuinely cannot handle, or whether it collapses into something he could already express. If you think my framing is confused, say so plainly here rather than quietly correcting it, and tell me what the confusion is.
5. What this leaves unresolved — about 150 words. What someone in my position, reading alone, could take as the live question, and what kind of consideration would actually settle it.

Two practical constraints. First, I have no way to look anything up, so please argue from the concepts themselves and define any term of art as you go: do not send me to chapters, page numbers, commentators, or further reading, and do not rest anything on a passage I would have to check. Second, do not flatter the setup — if the worry in this letter is muddled, I would much rather be told that than have a clever example built on top of it.
