I am writing a chapter on contractualist decision procedures for population-level policy, and I keep running into a structure I cannot find a name for. I would be grateful for help identifying it — or, if it has no established name, for a name and a definition I could introduce and defend.

The version of the view I am working with ranks options by individual complaints: for each option, identify how badly off the worst-off individual under that option would be, and rank options by that. Only where the worst-off individuals on the two sides are burdened to roughly the same degree is the number of burdened people allowed to matter, and then only as a tie-breaker, in favour of the option that burdens fewer people. If the standard statement of the view is not structurally like this, please say so; but the reconstruction is what I need to discuss either way, since it is what the applied literature I am working through actually employs.

The pattern I keep finding is that this procedure goes in circles. Two instances, both stylised but not, I think, gerrymandered.

A health authority must choose one of three closures. Option A withdraws funding for one named patient's last-line therapy: one person loses 10 units of health. Option B closes a satellite dialysis unit: forty patients lose 9.7 each. Option C ends a screening programme: two thousand people lose 9.3 each. Read roughly the same degree tolerantly — say, a gap of up to five per cent of the larger burden. Comparing A with B: the gap is 0.3, within tolerance, so the tie-breaker applies and A wins, one burdened person against forty. Comparing B with C: the gap is 0.4, within tolerance, so B wins, forty against two thousand. Comparing A with C: the gap is 0.7, outside tolerance, so the tie-breaker is switched off and the smaller individual burden wins — C beats A. A beats B beats C beats A. Both cycles survive any tolerance between roughly four and seven per cent, so this is not knife-edge. Note also that total burden rises at every step of the circle: 10, then 388, then 18,600, and then the comparison that closes the circle is between the first and the last.

A research funder faces the same structure at another scale. X: cancel a trial with two enrolled participants, each losing 20 units. Y: suspend a field station on which 900 people rely, each losing 19.3. Z: close a national monitoring programme on which 90,000 people rely, each losing 18.6. At five per cent, X beats Y on numbers, Y beats Z on numbers, and Z beats X on the size of the individual burden.

What I think is doing the work is the slack in roughly the same degree. The relation that governs whether numbers may count is tolerant, hence non-transitive, and it gates a switch between two different decision rules: ignore numbers, or count them lexicographically. So the ranking inherits the non-transitivity of the gate.

What I cannot work out is whether this is a known object, and if so, which of several nearby things it is. It is not, I think, the aggregation problem in the usual sense, nor the familiar complaint that contractualism cannot count numbers at all: in every pairwise comparison here the procedure behaves exactly as its defenders say it should, and the difficulty appears only when the comparisons are combined. It may be an instance of the sorites or chaining worry about vague comparatives, but that worry is normally about the comparative term itself, whereas here the vagueness sits in a threshold that turns a lexicographic switch on and off. It resembles the classic argument that imperceptible-difference or indifference relations are not transitive, but that argument gets its force from assuming transitivity and deriving absurd indifference across a wide range; here nothing assumes transitivity, and the output is not absurd indifference but a definite circular ranking. It also resembles Condorcet-style cycling, except that no one is aggregating several people's orderings: it is a single procedure applied to a single pair at a time. If the structural diagnosis from social choice or from decision theory about thresholds and similarity carries over, I want to know that; if these really are four different phenomena and mine is a fifth, I want to know that too.

So I am not after a label. I am after the carving: what exactly this is, what it is not, and where its edges lie. Three boundary questions I especially need settled. First, if one compares every option against a fixed baseline such as the status quo rather than pairwise, the circle disappears, but the winner then changes when the baseline changes in ways I cannot see as morally relevant — is that a solution, or a relocation of the problem? Second, I have been assuming burdens are interpersonally comparable on a single cardinal scale in order to isolate the phenomenon; does the structure survive if all we have is rough ordinal comparison of complaints? Third, there is a nearby effect where adding or removing an option that will not be chosen can change which of the remaining options is permissible — is that the same phenomenon in another guise, or a separate one that I should keep distinct?

Finally, I would like an assessment of the repairs: making numbers enter continuously rather than as a tie-breaker, denying that the procedure must yield a transitive ranking and adopting a different choice rule over cyclic options, letting the tolerance threshold vary with context, or going baseline-relative. Which of these actually addresses the phenomenon, and which merely relocates it to a different comparison?

I should say that pointing me at literature to read will not help; I need the analysis, since I have to decide whether to argue for this from scratch or to situate myself relative to something already settled. Assume familiarity with the contractualism literature and with the standard cases.
