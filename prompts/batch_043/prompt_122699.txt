I've been working through the literature on infinite aggregation with two friends from a decision-theory reading group — none of us is a professional philosopher, so please explain rather than gesture — and I've ended up committed to a position that I'd like stress-tested rather than supported. Setup: model a world as an assignment of a real number (local value) to each location in a countably infinite grid of locations, with every finite region having a finite total. My view, which I'll call Two-Tier Frame-Relativism, is this. Fix a frame: an increasing, exhaustive sequence of finite regions W₁ ⊂ W₂ ⊂ …. Let Dₙ be the total value in Wₙ under world A minus the total value in Wₙ under world B. Say A is better than B relative to that frame just in case Dₙ is eventually bounded below by some positive amount — equivalently, the lim inf of Dₙ is positive; if Dₙ converges to zero I call the worlds equal; if it swings unboundedly in both directions the pair gets no verdict at all. The two-tier part: when A and B differ only inside some bounded region, Dₙ eventually stabilizes at a constant, every frame agrees, and I get the ordinary, reassuring answer that you compare the net value in the region where they differ and ignore the infinite background; I call that localized comparison, and it is the part of the view I actually rely on when I think about real choices, since my own effects are presumably bounded. When the difference is unbounded, I bite a fairly large bullet: the verdict is allowed to depend on the frame, and I deny that there is any frame-free fact about which of two unboundedly divergent worlds is better. I hold this because it seems to escape infinitarian paralysis for the cases that matter while refusing to pretend that a privileged ordering of locations has been handed down from nowhere.

What I want is a map of what would count against it, ranked, and I want you to be blunt about which of my clauses is load-bearing. Four things I can see looming, and I'd like you to tell me which are fatal and which merely awkward, and to add the ones I've missed. First, frame-gerrymandering: take a pair of striped worlds that partition space into slabs of linearly growing magnitude, with A ahead in the even slabs and behind in the odd ones by equal and growing amounts. A frame that sweeps up even slabs first makes A better; one that sweeps up odd slabs first makes B better; one that takes slabs in their natural order yields an oscillating Dₙ and hence no verdict. Is the choice of frame here doing substantive normative work under a technical name, and if so, is that worse than simply admitting paralysis? Second, the smeared-loss case: let B differ from a bland baseline by one unit of gain in a bounded region plus, spread across all of infinite space, losses at location n of size 1/n. The cumulative loss diverges, so my divergence criterion says B is worse than the baseline by an unbounded margin, while any average-density or limiting-average comparison says the two are equal; the tier boundary, not the frame, is what decides this, and I don't have a principled account of why divergence rather than density should win. I'd especially like to know whether the density-friendly answer can be squeezed by some chaining or step-by-step argument that turns a sequence of ties into a big difference. Third, a worry about localized comparison itself: local value may not be intrinsic, since a person's welfare inside a bounded region can depend on how things stand outside it — someone whose deepest desire concerns how infinitely many distant strangers fare — and if local value has to be defined conditionally on the rest of the world, then tier 1 either goes wrong or secretly presupposes the global axiology it was supposed to replace. Fourth, the main rival I take seriously is nonstandard or hyperreal utilitarianism, which counts value with infinite and infinitesimal numbers and gets frame-free-looking verdicts once an ordering of locations is fixed; I'd like to know whether my frame is an ultrafilter under a friendlier name, whether the two views are genuinely different or merely notational variants, and if they are variants, whether I've inherited hyperreal utilitarianism's sensitivity to where in the ordering you place a unit of value. I also have a half-memory of a family of results in the economics literature on ranking infinite utility streams — pairing some permutation-invariance or anonymity condition with a strong Pareto condition, to the effect that no ordering satisfying both is representable by a real-valued welfare function, and that the orderings which do satisfy both are non-constructible in a way that depends on the axiom of choice. If I've got that roughly right, say so and tell me whether it bears on my view at all; if I've garbled it, correct me.

Two further things, and then the format. I want to know where the view sticks its neck out: what it predicts that its rivals don't, and which observations or arguments would be decisive rather than merely inconvenient — including whether anything about cosmology matters here, since if the actual world is finite, or if our effects are bounded, the disturbing half of the view may be idle machinery, whereas if our effects are unbounded the frame-relativity may swallow every choice I care about. And I want an honest verdict on insulation: my tier-1 verdicts are frame-invariant and so in principle falsifiable, but my treatment of unbounded divergence looks worryingly absorbent, in that a counterexample can perhaps always be met by redescribing the frame; please tell me whether that absorption is a degenerating move or a reasonable refusal to answer a question with no answer, and whether a view whose whole motivation was escaping infinitarian paralysis has a problem if it reintroduces paralysis for exactly the pairs where it declines to speak. Tell me also, at each point, which objections a committed defender of a view like mine would themselves concede as counting against it, and which they would dismiss as resting on a rival's assumptions — I can't tell the difference from the inside. My own sense is that I'd be most moved by an argument that frame-relativity is unstable, in that once verdicts are allowed to depend on a frame for unbounded cases, some redescription or relocation trick makes them depend on the frame for bounded cases too, and the view collapses into the thing it was built to avoid; second most moved by a case where localized comparison gives the wrong answer because of the infinite background it was told to ignore. Please write in American English, keep the ranking explicit — decisive, serious, merely awkward — and don't send me to a reading list or a set of papers, since I'm working somewhere without library access and would rather have the arguments than the references.
