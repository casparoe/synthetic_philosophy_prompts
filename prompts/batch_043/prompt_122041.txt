I've just come away from a long argument with a friend who works in machine-learning theory, and I'd like you to adjudicate it from an angle we never got around to trying. We were both at a workshop on algorithmic information theory, and his line — which he repeats in every conversation of this kind — is that Solomonoff induction finally closes the problem Hume raised. The pitch is that if you put a prior over computable hypotheses weighted by the length of their shortest description, you get a predictor that converges to the truth for any computable data source and dominates every other computable predictor to within a constant factor, so that preference for simple theories stops being a piece of metaphysical taste and becomes a theorem. He then uses this to read contemporary AI: a large model trained to minimise description length over a corpus is a crude finite approximation to Solomonoff induction, so the fact that it works is evidence that compression really is prediction, and any further worrying about whether induction can be justified is, in his phrase, pre-Solomonoff griping that the formal theory has already retired.

What I pushed back with is the reference-machine worry. The universal prior is universal only relative to a choice of universal machine, and the result that is supposed to make the choice harmless says only that any two machines' priors differ by a multiplicative constant — a constant which, as far as I can tell, can be made arbitrarily and grotesquely large, since for any finite body of evidence you like there is some machine on which your pet hypothesis is the shortest description of that evidence and something ugly is the shortest description of everything after it. So the convergence is asymptotic, and over any quantity of data we will actually live to collect the machine-relative constant can outweigh the likelihood term entirely; my friend's reply was that this is just the standard complaint you could lodge against any prior whatsoever, and that nobody has exhibited a case where it bites. We went nowhere.

So here is what I want instead of a verdict on our argument: reconstruct Hume's. Imagine you could hand him the apparatus — a universal machine, a prior over finite strings weighted by shortest description, the convergence and dominance results — and explain it without expecting him to be impressed by the notation. Where exactly would he say the question has been begged? Would he take the length-first weighting to be the uniformity principle restated in a formal vocabulary, so that the theorems show only what follows if the world is computable and simple, and give no reason at all to think it is? And how would he handle the fact that his own positive story is that induction rests on custom and habit rather than reason, and is meant as a description of our practice rather than a vindication of it: would he welcome Solomonoff induction as a rigorous model of habit-formation, or attack it as a rationalist attempt to extract necessity from a definition? One further thing I'd like you to use, because it seems to me the sharpest test: his principle that every idea is copied from some antecedent impression. Is algorithmic simplicity — being generated by a short program — the sort of feature that could ever be copied from an impression? If not, what status would he assign the simplicity ordering: one of the natural relations among ideas, a habit, a sentiment? And would that classification change whether he thought the razor was doing genuine epistemic work or merely describing our psychology? There is also a claim of my friend's I couldn't answer: that Solomonoff is simply Ockham's razor, the slogan about not multiplying entities, finally made exact — I don't know whether that identification is a real continuity or a pun on a shared name, and whether Hume would care either way.

Give me his probable answer in the strongest form you can, and be explicit about what he would concede rather than treating him as a pure nay-sayer: my suspicion is that the dominance theorem would genuinely interest him, and I'd like the best case for thinking it gives him what he asked for set against the best case for thinking it cannot, since after three hours my friend and I were only repeating ourselves.
