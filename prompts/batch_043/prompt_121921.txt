Topic is Lewis's triviality results for indicative conditionals. They're fine with conditional probability, the law of total probability and expected value, and they've met the Ramsey test — suppose the antecedent, then judge your confidence in the consequent — and they like it. Nobody's heard of triviality, and I don't want anyone to hear the word, or the name Lewis, until the debrief.

What I want is a betting market, no real money, chips or points only. Teams post prices for bets on conditionals about a single die roll, priced by the Ramsey recipe, and then find that their own recipe applied a second time to the very same bet hands them a different number. The case I've been playing with: A = "the roll is 1 or 2", C = "the roll is 2 or 3". The Ramsey price for a bet on "if A then C" is 1/2; split the same bet across C and not-C, price each half by that same recipe (suppose C, then suppose A: certain. Suppose not-C, then suppose A: hopeless), and total probability gives 1/3. I want them to feel that gap as the house taking their chips, not as a line of algebra — so I need real trades with stakes, at their posted prices, that lock the difference in. By the end they should see that the Ramsey recipe plus "a conditional is an ordinary bettable proposition" plus ordinary price coherence forces every correlated pair of propositions to come out independent, and that every way out costs something.

Three things I'd like built in if they're workable:

- The opposing team picks the partition the house splits on, and the house only certifies splits where both A-with-B and A-without-B are possible. When I sketched this myself I noticed splitting on "the roll is even" gives back 1/2 while splitting on C gives 1/3, so don't pretend every split bites — say which ones are inert and why, and make hunting for one that bites part of the game.
- A cost to changing your mind: teams may revise a posted price, but each revision widens the spread they trade at or costs a chip. Abandoning an assumption should feel like it has a price, because it does.
- An exit auction at the end. Each team declares which move it's giving up: (1) the Ramsey equation itself, (2) the thought that a conditional is an ordinary proposition you can bet on unconditionally, (3) the Ramsey test inside a supposition, (4) price coherence / total probability. Their declared exit decides which bets they're allowed to refuse in one final nested round (bets on things like "if A then if B then C", paying double or triple). Each exit should let a team get through that round without being arbitraged, and each should cost them something visible.
