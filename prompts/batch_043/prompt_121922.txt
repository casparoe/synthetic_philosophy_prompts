Over the past year I wound up a small business that I had run for more than a decade. For roughly the last eighteen months of it I told my co-founder, my staff and my family that things were tight but fine — while, as I can now see, not opening the monthly management accounts that would have told me otherwise. A friend, meaning to be kind, said afterwards that I had not been lying to anyone: I had been deceiving myself. I have been trying to work out whether that is even a coherent thing to say, and the more of the philosophical literature I read, the less certain I am that the people arguing about it are arguing with one another.

The shape of the dispute, as I have it: deception seems to require a deceiver who aims to get someone to believe what the deceiver takes to be false. Make deceiver and deceived the same person and you appear to need a subject who simultaneously believes and disbelieves, or who intends to produce in herself a belief whose falsity she already registers — a bootstrapping version of Moore's paradox rather than an ordinary explanation of anything. One camp accepts the paradox and explains it by adding structure to the mind: divisions, sub-personal mechanisms, sequences in which the intention is formed and then allowed to lapse, self-deception as a trait rather than an episode. A second camp simply denies that self-deception needs an intention to deceive oneself at all; motivationally skewed gathering and weighing of evidence issuing in a false belief is enough, and the paradoxical requirement is an artefact of an over-literal reading of the word 'deceive'. A third response keeps a demanding notion of belief and concludes that the self-deceived person does not really believe the comforting proposition — she accepts it, or avows it, or is committed to it in some non-doxastic way, or has simply never settled the question.

My tentative diagnosis is that the sides are answering different questions under one heading, because they are working from different pools of cases. The paradox-driven camp seems to be looking at literary and clinical paradigms — the lover who will not see the affair, the patient who will not hear the diagnosis — where the subject has the evidence in front of her and still holds on. The deflationary camp seems to be looking at laboratory populations and at the large, well-known body of work on motivated reasoning, where people tolerate avoidable discomfort, avoid self-relevant tests, and drift into false belief without ever doing anything that looks like deceiving. Once you distinguish a state of believing against the evidence, a process of motivated evidence-treatment, and a policy of not inquiring at all, a great many of the apparently competing claims look compatible — and my own case seems to belong to the third category: I never formed the belief that the business was fine, I simply failed to check. Call this the audit-aversion account, if that helps.

Before I settle for that, I would like a diagnosis with reasons. Which of the disagreements here are substantive, which turn on how 'deceive' and 'belief' are being used, and which are really disputes about which cases count as data? Is there a hidden empirical or formal question doing the work — something about what introspection could in principle reveal, or about whether a policy of not inquiring can be distinguished from mere ignorance? Two further things I would value. First, a test: my own suggestion is to ask what happens when the evidence is made vivid and unavoidable — if the comforting belief collapses instantly, perhaps it was never a belief, and perhaps that tells us something about which account is right. Second, an extension I have not seen discussed: my co-founder and I maintained a shared story, and neither of us was the deceiver or the deceived, yet the firm as a whole seems to have done exactly what an individual self-deceiver does. Does the concept extend to pairs and institutions, and does the answer bear on the individual case or merely offload it?

Given whatever you think the disagreement actually is, what would count as progress — a distinction, a reclassification of cases, an argument that the folk term should be allowed to fragment into several phenomena — and, honestly, should any be expected, or is this a question on which a century of very careful people have failed to converge because there is no single thing to converge on?
