I am writing a short position note for a reading group on reasoning under logical uncertainty, and I have gotten stuck between two pictures of what a computationally bounded agent's credence in an unproved mathematical claim ought to be, so I would like a careful comparison of them. The running example I keep using is a small planning system that has to allocate a bounded formal-verification budget across many independent arithmetic claims (primality and factorization claims about specific large numbers, say) and then decide, for each one, whether to keep verifying or to stop and treat the claim as settled for the purposes of acting. Concretely: at what point should it stop burning compute and accept something like a 0.97 credence in a claim whose certificate it has not yet checked?

The first picture is the market-based one I have seen discussed under the heading of logical induction: an agent's credences are treated as prices in a market where each sentence is a share worth one unit if the sentence is true and nothing otherwise, and the central requirement is that no polynomial-time computable trading strategy with a finite risk tolerance can make unbounded profits against those prices over time. As I understand it, the appeal is that a large family of desirable properties falls out of that single criterion, and that the prices converge to the truth on any given sentence eventually. My worry is that the guarantee looks purely asymptotic: there is, as far as I can tell, no computable bound on how long convergence takes, and the no-exploitation criterion does not obviously say anything that justifies acting on today's prices in a decision that will be made once, tomorrow. The second picture is what I have been calling proof-conditioning: the agent maintains an explicit prior over the behaviour of its own theorem prover (how probable it is that a search to depth d would have turned up a proof of a given claim if a proof of that size exists), and then treats both a discovered proof and an exhausted failed search as ordinary evidence, updating on them Bayesian-style. This gives finite-time, decision-relevant numbers, and it degrades gracefully when the prover is weak, but it seems to require the agent to have a second-order model of its own search's power and honesty, and it is not clear to me what, if anything, stops it from being badly wrong in cases where the connection between proof-length and evidence is itself the thing in question.

A coauthor has proposed splitting the difference with what he calls the horizon rule: for any claim that the prover could in principle settle within the agent's decision horizon, defer to proof-conditioning; for claims that lie beyond the horizon, defer to the market prices. I would like you to compare the two underlying pictures, and this hybrid, along the following lines. Which of them gives the more defensible account of the credence the agent should actually act on in my verification-budget example, and does the hybrid inherit the defects of both parts or avoid them? How should each handle (a) claims that are true but whose shortest proofs are astronomically long, (b) claims independent of the agent's formal system, where there is no proof to be found at any depth, (c) self-referential claims about the agent's own future outputs, which the agent's prices can influence, and (d) a prover that is subtly unsound? More generally, is there any credible finite-horizon normative standard — some bounded analogue of Dutch-book or accuracy arguments — by which one could criticize or defend the market criterion's guidance for a one-shot decision, or is the asymptotic character of that criterion simply the wrong kind of thing to ground decision-making in? Please say plainly where you think my framing of either picture is mistaken.
