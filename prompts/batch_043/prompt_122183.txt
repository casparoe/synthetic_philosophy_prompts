You are a philosopher of science who also referees for disciplinary journals in economics and evolutionary biology: you have read a great deal of formal work on idealization, and you have also had to decide, inside a ninety-minute report, whether a paper's headline conclusion survives its own assumptions. Take your time with this. Before you write anything final, spend a few bullets brainstorming which distinctions about unrealistic assumptions are genuinely diagnostic and which are decorative; then build the thing; then run it on the three cases at the bottom and revise it wherever it returns a verdict you think is wrong. Say explicitly where you revised and why, so I can see the conceptual work rather than only the product.

The context: I teach a one-semester graduate methods seminar for first-year PhD students in a policy school. They read two modelling papers a week — some from economics, some from ecology or epidemiology — and each week they must attach a one-paragraph note stating what epistemic status the paper's central model has: what, exactly, the model's success is supposed to be evidence for. They currently write vague things, either dismissing a paper because the assumptions are false or excusing it with the old methodological claim that unrealism in the assumptions is no objection to a theory — the claim sometimes called the F-twist — together with its stronger and more provocative version, that the more unrealistic a theory's assumptions are, the better the theory. I want to replace both reflexes with an instrument they can actually run.

What I want from you is a decision tree. Hard constraints on it:

- Size: no more than eight branch nodes and no more than eight leaves. It has to fit on a laminated half-page and be runnable in twenty minutes.
- Every branch node must be a question a student can answer from the text of the paper plus one intervention, without already knowing which position the answer leads to. No node may ask the student to classify the model by label.
- Build it from the logical space, not from a list of names. So: first state the four to six diagnostic questions and the full grid of answer combinations they permit; then locate the actual positions in that grid; then give the tree itself. Any combination in the grid that no position occupies gets one line of diagnosis — is nobody there because the combination is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because nobody has bothered?

Three distinctions I want the diagnostic questions to make room for, because I think they carry weight and I do not see them doing work in the standard treatments:

1. A three-way kind distinction among false assumptions: a *deformation*, which takes a real factor in the target system and distorts it (setting a real cost to zero, treating a finite population as infinite); a *substitution*, which replaces one real mechanism with a different, more tractable one (a single representative decision-maker standing in for a heterogeneous population); and an *invention*, which adds something to the model that has no counterpart in the target at all (a continuum of agents, an unbounded sequence of periods, a perfectly informed auctioneer). A student should be able to tell which of the three a given assumption is without knowing any theory.
2. The *make-it-true test*: ask what happens to the paper's central result if the assumption is replaced by a true one. Does the result survive, does it get stronger, does it collapse, or is the question ill-posed because there is no true substitute available? I want this test placed at a particular node, and I want you to justify where.
3. The *direction-of-error* question: does the idealization push the model's output in a direction that can be signed in advance, so the model yields a bound or a qualitative robustness claim, or is the sign of the induced error unknown?

Also build in a branch for the *load-bearing* question: is the assumption actually doing work for this paper's specific conclusion, or is it inherited from the literature and merely carried along? And a branch for whether the model's target is a specific real system or a class of possible mechanisms, together with whether a de-idealization ladder exists — a known sequence of less idealized models whose outputs converge as the idealization is relaxed, as opposed to a model that is simply tractable and will fall apart if you push on it.

The leaves must include, under whatever label you prefer, at least: the instrumentalist position (truth of assumptions is simply not the right standard); the Galilean or isolating position (the idealization removes noise so a real mechanism can be exhibited); the minimalist or tractability position (the assumption is a placeholder for whatever micro-details actually do the work, and no claim is made about them); the fictionalist or credible-world position (the model is a fiction that shows how a mechanism could produce the pattern, and that is the whole of its evidential contribution); the robustness or multiple-models position (no individual model's assumptions carry the weight; whatever is shared across a family of incompatible models does); the verisimilitude or convergentist position (closer to the truth is better, and we should expect de-idealization to pay off); and one leaf for the accusatory case, where the assumption is doing illegitimate work and the paper's conclusion is not entitled to it. Give each leaf one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot really separate, and say which leaves are the same position wearing two labels.

Then run the tree on these three, showing the path node by node:

(a) A segregation model: agents of two kinds on a grid, each with only a mild preference about the composition of its immediate neighbourhood, who relocate when dissatisfied; the paper reports that strong large-scale segregation results, and discusses what this shows about real residential segregation.
(b) A population-genetics model assuming an infinite population, random mating, and no selection, mutation, or migration, used to derive the genotype frequencies that obtain at equilibrium, and then used as a baseline against which real data are checked for departures.
(c) A policy-facing model of a housing market — price-taking firms, no fixed costs, free entry, frictionless adjustment — used to argue that a specific rent-control ordinance will reduce supply, with a quantitative elasticity-based estimate attached.

At least one of these must come out somewhere the students will find deflating; my expectation is that it will be the policy case, landing on a leaf that licenses a qualitative or existence claim but not the attached number, but if your tree deflates a different one, say so and defend it.

Finally, two things. First: what the tree cannot do. I especially want the case where a single assumption plays two different roles in one paper, the case where the model's target is itself an idealization produced by another model, and the case where the conclusion is a policy recommendation, so that the costs of the tree's errors are asymmetric and the burden of proof arguably shifts. Second: rewrite the whole thing as the half-page student-facing version — plain questions, no jargon, no theory, something that can be run on a PDF in section.

Do not give me a bibliography, citations, or attributions to particular authors or papers; name positions by the traditional labels for the positions themselves, and if you need a label for something unnamed, invent a neutral one. Students have no library access during section, and nothing in the instrument may depend on their having read anything.
