Bit of background: I did thirty-five years in isotope geochemistry, and now on Tuesday nights I go to a philosophy reading group where I'm usually the only person who has ever dissected anything. Lately we've been stuck on whether selection acts on genes, on individuals, or on groups.

One member — a philosophy postdoc, very much a selfish-gene man, Dawkins and all that — keeps closing the question with the same picture: DNA is a recipe, the organism is the cake. Recipes get copied and handed on; cakes get eaten. So, he says, the gene is the unit of selection, the organism is only a survival machine or a vehicle, and when people talk about group selection they have confused the cake with the recipe.

He also says the picture is earning its keep rather than just being a slogan. Because a recipe is a set of instructions, you can ask what the instructions are for and get a crisp answer; because recipes are copied rather than consumed, the thing that persists and accumulates changes is the recipe, so questions about what a trait is for and whose benefit it serves are supposed to land on the gene and nowhere else. That move is what actually wins him the arguments, and I cant tell whether it's legitimate.

So give me an audit. Lay out the mapping: what plays the recipe, the cookbook, the cook, the kitchen, the oven, the photocopier, the typo, the cake — and whatever else the picture needs in order to run. Then the places the mapping breaks, kept seperate: the ones that matter for the job he's using it for, and the ones that don't. I especially want to know what the picture quietly imports that the biology doesn't have — an author, a reader, a clean split between instruction and material, instructions that are discrete and additive. And does anything in the picture so much as distinguish a recipe for one cake from a recipe for a whole meal that only comes out right if several cakes are baked together?

Two things I want taken seriously rather than waved away. First, a case I made up and brought to the group, which he never answered: a colonial marine animal — call it a ladder colony — that buds off daughter colonies, where the heritable thing isn't a stretch of DNA at all but a small self-reconstructing chemical state in the egg: say a redox or pH pattern plus a self-templating metabolite loop, rebuilt every generation out of whatever happens to be in the seawater. It varies, the variants change how many daughter colonies a colony leaves, and the variants persist for thousands of generations. Descent with modification, heritability, differential success — everything the picture needs — but nothing that looks like stored symbolic code. It's closer to a seed crystal or a standing wave than to a text. Is there still a recipe and a cake here? If not, does that show the picture was smuggling in the claim that the replicator has to be code?

Second: the cake doesn't print the next edition of the book. His answer is that this is just what a vehicle is. Is that enough, or does the asymmetry hide how much of the causal work the cake is doing — the reading of the recipe, the development, whether there's any separate cook at all?

And say flat out whether his conclusion survives: does recipe-versus-cake settle anything about genes versus individuals versus groups, or does it only rename the question as which recipe? If it's beyond saving, give me a better picture to take to the next meeting — I'd rather have something usable than a list of complaints.
