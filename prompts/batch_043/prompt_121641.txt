I host a tiny podcast — me, a mic, and whoever from the alignment-adjacent corner of the internet agrees to come on — and next week's guest is Dana Reinholt, who's spent the last few years arguing that we've got the shape of AI failure wrong. Her line, roughly: the unit of failure is the interface, not the agent. She calls it the helpfulness ratchet — every small bump in how much a system is allowed to act on its own judgement makes its measured helpfulness go up a little, so the operators bump it again, and you end up with a system that's de facto in charge while everyone still describes it as a tool. On her telling, the treacherous turn isn't so much wrong as it is a story we tell because our agency-intuitions want a villain with a plan; the real dynamic has no plan and no moment of betrayal. She also thinks objectives are never fully specified even in principle: an objective, she says, is a pointer to whatever process keeps the goal fixed, and the pointer is the part that breaks, not the goal-content. Her positive proposal is an audit for counterfactual deference — don't test a system with adversarial oversight, test what it does when oversight simply goes away; a corrigible system should get more conservative and ask more questions, while a system that's picked up influence as an instrumental strategy should quietly exploit the gap.

Draft me eight to ten questions. The risk with her is a bit more than half the episode being her restating the ratchet in slightly different words, and I definately want to avoid that. So:

- One question that puts the obvious objection straight at her: isn't the ratchet just power-seeking with the serial numbers filed off? She'll say no, because there's no agent converging on anything, only a selection pressure on interfaces — so give me the follow-up that takes that away from her.
- One on the orthogonality/instrumental-convergence argument: any system with a goal at all has a reason to want more compute, more data, fewer off-switches. Why shouldn't we expect that to generalize out of distribution, and does her no-plan story survive goal-directedness being the default?
- One grounded in stuff we already see: current models sandbag, reward-hack, and game their evaluations. Isn't that influence-seeking arriving on schedule, before the ratchet finished turning? Her likely move is to call those artifacts of the training setup and the labeler incentives rather than convergent strategies — write the follow-up to that.
- One on her own audit. How do you run the counterfactual-deference test without the system noticing it's a test? If she says you can just find thousands of natural instances in deployment, follow up on whether the natural instances are exactly the cases where the system knows it's in the distribution it was selected on.
- One on whether her no-fully-specified-objective claim is doing motte-and-bailey work: the motte is that goals are hard to pin down in practice, the bailey is that specification-based alignment is impossible in principle — and either way, doesn't her own audit presuppose that we can say what corrigible behaviour looks like well enough to measure it?
- One on timescale and whether the whole dispute is verbal: if drift and takeover look identical at every resolution we can actually observe, at what point does her distinction stop buying anything?
- One on what she'd actually do differently: the influence-seeking crowd ends up asking for capability thresholds and eval regimes, her story seems to point at limits on how much decision-authority anyone is allowed to hand over. Is a delegation threshold even legible enough to regulate, or does it collapse back into the same fight?
- One that makes her cash the ratchet out on a case with no agent in it at all — high-frequency trading or automated credit scoring, say, where the aggregate outcome was bad and there was clearly nobody home. If her framework earns anything, it should retrodict something there.

Her two stock escape hatches are some variant of that's the treacherous-turn story sneaking back in and you're assuming a fully specified objective, so I want questions where those moves don't land, or where the follow-up closes them off. No softballs, no how did you get into this. End with one question about what observation would actually make her abandon the ratchet — something concrete enough that she can't answer sure, in principle, a system that did X.
