For a graduate seminar on the equality-of-what question, I need a case that separates the capabilities critique from the examples that normally carry it. Construct a case involving two people in which the resourcist finds them equally well off, the welfarist also finds them equally well off, and the capability theorist finds one of them clearly worse off, in a way a capability theorist would count as a genuine loss rather than a mere difference in option sets. Constraints: a realistic setting (a workplace, a clinic, a school, a housing estate); one paragraph of scenario; and none of the stock mechanisms may do the work — no disability or illness, no difference in talent, skill, or effort, no expensive or adaptive preferences, no difference in internal constitution of any kind. The gap must also be one that no transfer of resources to the worse-off party closes, and that cannot be traced to an identifiable wrongdoer who could be taxed or punished; if it can be closed at all, it closes only by altering the setting. Assume further that the worse-off party is contented and would not trade places, so that no welfarist appeal to frustration or unmet desire is available.

Then do the check: state what each of the three metrics is comparing and show why it returns the verdict it does; name the single feature of the case doing the work; say what happens to the capability verdict if that feature is removed; and give the strongest reply available to the resourcist or the welfarist — not that capabilities are hard to index or measure, but that the capability comparison is tracking something distributive justice should not be in the business of equalizing. Finish by telling me honestly whether the case generates these verdicts from the metrics or quietly stipulates them, since what I want to know is whether the critique has any force once the usual examples are off the table.
