I'm in a reading group that has degenerated into a two-person argument — me and a game theorist — and we were supposed to turn it into a paper and have now been stuck for about a year on the centipede game. Setup: two players alternate, at each node you can take (you get more than half the pot, the other guy gets less) or pass, which grows the pot by a fixed factor and hands the choice over. The bite is that if you pass and he immediately takes, you end up worse off than if you'd taken one move earlier; but if you both keep passing you both end up far better off than either of you would have by grabbing at the start. Backward induction says take at the very first node. He thinks that's basically the whole story and the only interesting question left is why lab subjects don't do it. I think the argument is self-undermining: at node six, the bare fact that you've reached node six is already evidence that your opponent isn't the kind of agent the premise describes, so you can't use the premise to work out what to do here. That feels like the same self-referential structure as the surprise exam, but he says the analogy is bogus because the surprise exam is about knowledge and this is about action, and I genuinely can't tell whether that's a real disanalogy or him swatting it away.

So I want a diagnosis, not a verdict. Two things we tried on a whiteboard that I think matter. First: put a small tremble in — each player, when she means to take, passes by mistake with probability ε. Then passing early looks like reputation-building and you get a cutoff rule, pass for a while then take, and the cutoff depends on ε. We couldn't agree which way it slides as ε shrinks, or whether for a fixed number of nodes it collapses back to take-at-node-one while for a game that grows longer it doesn't. Second: we made the ending random — a coin flip each round instead of a known last node — and then the induction has nothing to grab onto, and it looked to us like always-pass becomes sustainable if the pot grows faster than the hazard rate, which if right would mean the paradoxical conclusion is an artifact of the fixed finite horizon rather than of common knowledge of rationality itself.

Here's my hypothesis, which I don't trust: that we are not disagreeing about the formal facts at all (I think we'd agree about both variants once we actually did the algebra), and not really about the lab data either, because a tremble model already predicts partial cooperation, so the experiments don't discriminate between us. I think we're answering different questions with the phrase "what should you do": he's asking what the model predicts about a game, I'm asking what you should do if you find yourself mid-game having already been surprised. But I can't tell if that's the real fault line or if I've just found a polite way to describe my own intuition.

What kind of stalemate is this — verbal, a difference about what counts as data, a hidden formal question doing the work, a disagreement about the job of a solution concept, or bedrock intuition about rationality that neither of us can argue for? Which of our two variants would actually separate the diagnoses, and what would count as progress: a theorem about robustness to trembles, a distinction between prediction and advice, dropping the fixed horizon, or changing the question?
