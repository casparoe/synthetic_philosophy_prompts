So, bit of context: my family run a mussel and oyster farm, and about four months ago I let myself get talked onto the industry association's committee that's writing the animal welfare code for the whole sector — which means I'm now one of the people deciding which animals the code actually covers. I'm not a philosopher and I'm not a vet, and I'm obviously conflicted about the shellfish part, so I'd rather have a procedure I can defend then keep going by gut.

The shape of the job: a new species or a new supplier application comes up every couple of weeks, we've got maybe forty minutes each, the people who'll actually run this are auditors with one day of training plus our own vets, every decision needs a half-page written justification, and anything that restricts a practice has to be signed off by the retailer's technical person. We can't send samples to a lab or commission studies — we work from what's already out there in general summaries and from what you can observe on a farm.

What I've been doing by instinct, and want turned into something I can hand to other people:

- a flat "no centralised brain, no coverage" cutoff;
- treating motivational flexibility as the marker I trust most — the animal changes what it will put up with, or work for, when the stakes change, instead of just withdrawing from a stimulus in a fixed way — and discounting both plain reflex responses and the cases where all that's been shown is that it learns to avoid the bad thing, since that might just be conditioning;
- when I'm genuinely unsure, weighting the possible suffering heavier than the possible cost, by a factor I basically made up.

Please turn that into a real decision procedure: the questions in the order they should be asked, what counts as a sufficient yes to each one, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it has to stop and hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. Be explicit about which distinctions from the philosophy have to survive the simplification and which can be dropped without much loss, and about where the procedure will misfire — one guy on the committee keeps saying that all the marker stuff is just proxies and what we care about is the thing itself, and I don't have a good answer to him. It also needs to be revisable: something should be able to move up or down a tier when evidence comes in, with a trigger for when we look again.

Then run it on five cases: a decapod supplier someone wants to add; the oysters and mussels we already farm; black soldier fly larvae grown for feed; a honeybee operation where the argument is that the unit that matters is the colony and not the individual; and one species nobody has ever run a single test on. Tell me which of those comes out in a way I'm not going to like, and why the procedure is still right to say it.
