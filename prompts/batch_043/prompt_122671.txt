Hey — I need help drafting something, and I'd rather you just wrote it than told me how you'd go about it.

Set-up: two camps about consciousness that I think have been talking past each other, and I want the paper they'd produce if they actually did an adversarial collaboration instead of writing past each other for another decade.

Camp U, call them the interface fictionalists: conscious experience is a user illusion, like a desktop. The system does a pile of messy, parallel, partly subpersonal processing, and it renders a simplified, action-guiding summary of itself to itself. What it's like for you is a description of the contents of that rendering, and there is no further fact, over and above the rendering being produced, about what it's really like. They're not saying nothing is happening and they're not saying nobody is conscious — they're saying the thing that's happening is the rendering.

Camp R, call them the render realists: fine, the rendering is a simplification, it isn't a picture of the neural goings-on, and it isn't inspected by anybody. But the rendering is a real occurrent event with intrinsic qualitative character, and that character isn't exhausted by the functional and dispositional facts about what the system does with it. There's something it's like to have this rendering, and that's an extra fact, not a re-description of the same one.

I'm stipulating up front that both camps have already signed onto: no Cartesian theatre, no homunculus, no inner eye that looks at the rendering, and the rendering isn't for anybody in the sense that would need one. Reports are the primary evidence base but they're data with error, not verdicts. The question how rich or sparse experience is, is at least partly empirical. And neither side gets to win by saying we all know what it's like, or by treating the word illusion as if it were an argument.

What I want:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims — things both could actually sign. Nothing vacuous: consciousness exists, or the hard problem is hard, are useless to me. I want claims with teeth that a hostile reader from either camp would still have to grant, including claims about what would count as evidence and what the methodology is.

2. A table of the residual disagreements. Columns: the issue; the formulation in wording U would accept for its own view; the formulation in wording R would accept for its own view; whether you think the disagreement is genuine or merely verbal; and which crux below it hangs on.

Then a cruxes section. For each crux I want one concrete case or argument that both sides would agree bears on it — not a gesture at future neuroscience — plus each side's prediction written down in advance, so neither can wriggle later. The cruxes I want handled:

- The lag crux. Reportable experience seems to lag discriminable processing by a couple hundred milliseconds, and masked or unattended stimuli get discriminated well above chance while not being reported at all. Does that support the experience being a retrospective construction, so the lag is an artifact of the rendering, or only that reports have a coarse temporal grain, so the lag tells us nothing about when experience occurs? Find the case that would separate these and write down what each side predicts.

- The substitution crux. People blind from birth who train for years on a camera-to-tongue or camera-to-sound device get genuinely good at shape and motion discriminations. As their behaviour converges with sighted subjects', do their spontaneous reports converge too — and if they do, does that favour R, because a shared real structure is getting rendered, or U, because both groups are just describing the same task-general interface vocabulary? Be specific about what report-content would count as convergence.

- The render-invariance crux, which is my own invention so you'll have to work it out as you go. Suppose two systems are probe-identical: same discriminations, same reports, same voluntary behaviour, same everything measurable from outside, but they differ in whether anything is rendered at all. Is there a difference in whether they're conscious? U presumably says no difference or no fact of the matter; R presumably says maybe yes. Where does that bottom out, and is there any way to turn it into something testable, or is it a stalemate by construction? I suspect a large part of this one is verbal — tell me exactly which part, and what reformulation would reveal whether any factual disagreement survives.

- The user crux. If it's an illusion, who is the user? Both sides need a story about the user that doesn't smuggle a homunculus back in. Give me each side's story, and either a formulation both could sign or a clear statement of why none is available.

- The efficacy crux. Is the rendering causally efficacious as a rendering, or only as the computational state that happens to implement it? Does whichever way that goes actually change anyone's mind here, or is it orthogonal?

And be committed. I don't want a survey of the options with the choice left to me. Tell me which cruxes are genuine and which are verbal, which side's wording is doing more work than it admits, which side you'd expect to be moved first if these tests were run tomorrow, and what you'd definitely predict about the substitution case. Give your reasons. Where you're guessing rather than arguing, say so — but still commit to the guess. Also, if you think a crux is genuinely undecidable by any means either side would accept, say that rather than manufacturing a fake test; I'd rather have the honest version.
