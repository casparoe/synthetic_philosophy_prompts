I've come to accept a strong claim about collective decision-making, and I want to know how much of it is actually at risk.

The claim has two parts. The structural part: for any agenda whose propositions are linked in the relevant way — p, q, and a conclusion r that obtains just in case both p and q do — no procedure for aggregating individual yes-or-no judgments can satisfy all of universal domain (every profile of individually consistent judgments must be handled), collective rationality (the group's output is itself consistent and complete), and systematicity (the group's verdict on any one proposition depends only on individual verdicts on that proposition), unless it is dictatorial; add anonymity and no such procedure exists at all. The illustration I have in mind is a three-member court deciding liability, where liability requires both that a valid contract existed and that it was breached: one member accepts both premises, one accepts only the first, one accepts only the second, so a majority accepts each premise and a majority rejects the conclusion.

The significance part: this is not a quirk of one profile. Any group that aggregates judgments rather than mere preferences is forced to choose between being collectively incoherent and surrendering one of equality of input, agenda-independence, or the requirement that a group's decision follow from reasons the group itself accepts. I read that as a genuine cost to pictures of collective reasoning — courts as reasoners, deliberative democracy, committee epistemology — in the way Arrow's result is a genuine cost rather than a parlour trick.

Tell me what would count against the significance part, and separately what would count against the structural part (in case I have the conditions, or the scope of the agenda requirement, wrong in a way that changes the picture). Rank your defeaters from potentially fatal to merely awkward. For each, say what would have to be true for it to bite, whether it is merely logically possible or actually live, what it would take to check it, and — this matters most — whether a defender of my position should concede it as counting against them or may dismiss it as changing the subject.

Some things to take into account, since they are already in play: the truth-tracking reply (a procedure can be more accurate overall while output that no consistent judge could hold, and premise-based aggregation may dominate on accuracy); the deflationary reply (there is no group mind, so collective incoherence is at best shorthand — but then say what, if anything, a collective decision is answerable to); the repair-rule reply (produce the inconsistent set, then revise by distance from the majority or by maximal consistent subsets, and tell me whether the revised rule reimports the problem at the meta-level); and the fact that real bodies already have workarounds such as sequential voting, agenda control by a chair, and separating the vote from the written rationale.

Three points I would particularly like handled. First, each of the two available procedures betrays a majority somewhere: premise-based aggregation decides against the majority verdict on the very question asked, conclusion-based aggregation reaches a verdict the majority endorses but for reasons the majority rejects. Is there any principled priority between a majority on the question asked and a majority on the reasons, or is that choice just where the arbitrariness reappears? Second, is the path-dependence of sequential voting a second and independent defect, which my diagnosis has simply failed to mention, or the same defect in another guise, which my diagnosis is double-counting? Third, given that deliberation tends to dissolve precisely the profiles that generate the dilemma, is a robustness-across-all-possible-profiles requirement the right one for a body whose job is to reason itself toward consensus?

Be straight with me about insulation. I am tempted to say that empirical frequency is irrelevant here, because legitimacy requires a guarantee across profiles, and that nobody defuses Arrow by observing that cycles are rare. Tell me whether that is a legitimate structural immunity or an evasion, and if the theorem part is unfalsifiable by construction, where exactly the exposure lives.

For what it is worth, the thing I find hardest to shake is the contrast with individual reasoning: someone who settled a conflict among her own beliefs by tallying them would be irrational in a paradigmatic way, and I do not see why coherence stops being a requirement when the believer is a committee. The thing I find hardest to answer is that every member remains individually coherent and can endorse the procedure in advance, so perhaps nothing irrational occurs at any level that exists.

Please argue in your own terms: no names, dates, papers, or reading lists. Lead with a single sentence giving your bottom line — where the exposure lies and how deep it goes — then work down your ranked list, no more than ninety words per item and 700 words in total.
