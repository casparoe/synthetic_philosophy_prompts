Okay, this has gotten weirdly personal for me. Since about Febuary my therapist has me keeping a dream journal — there's a nightmare I've had in different versions for years, the gist is always the same, and she wants me to write it down each morning and then rewrite the ending. So every morning I sit there writing something down and treating it as a report of what happened to me in the night, and the longer I do it the less I know which thing I think I'm doing: recording, or making up.

Then two friends got into it about exactly that at my kitchen table on Sunday and I've been chewing on it since. I won't tell you who I agreed with because I honestly couldn't tell.

Marcus started. He said my journal isn't obviously a record of anything, because there are three stories that fit exactly the same first-person data. One: the ordinary picture — you have a stream of experiences during the night and in the morning you remember them. Two: the experiences happen during the night but they're recorded and then fast-forwarded into you at the moment of waking, so the only thing you ever actually undergo is the replay. Three: nothing dreamlike happens during the night at all, and at the moment of waking the whole thing gets composed on the spot — the images, the story, and the feeling of remembering it, all of it, like a confabulation with no lag. He said no amount of how-it-seemed-to-me, no amount of vividness or detail in the report, tells you which of those three is true. So the thought "I know what I dreamt because it seemed to me on waking that I dreamt" buys you nothing at all.

Sasha said that's a parlor trick you could run on any memory, and that it's just false that there's no evidence independent of the report. She pointed at the sleep-lab work with lucid dreamers: they agree in advance to make some specific eye movement when they realize they're dreaming, and the movement shows up on the recording at a datable point in the middle of REM, before anyone has woken them, and then the dream they report has them doing that exact thing at that point in the dream. That's not a correlation with later reports, she said — that's the subject acting from inside the dream at a time the lab can date for itself. And there's the plainer fact that waking people up out of some sleep stages gets you dream reports far more often than waking them out of others.

Marcus said the lucid case is the best one there is and it still doesn't do the work. For one thing, a narrative composed at waking can be composed to fit the record: the subject knows in advance what signal was agreed, and dropping a segment where I do the eye movement into the right place in the story is exactly the kind of thing a post-hoc construction is good at. You're assuming the report is a memory in order to prove it's a memory. For another, you can only time-stamp the bits the subject chose to signal — the rest of the night's story stays as unverified as it was. What you'd have is islands of verified consciousness floating in a sea of narrative, not evidence that the narrative is a record. And as for the sleep-stage stuff: even a perfect correlation between some brain state and later reports is a correlation with reports. It wouldn't tell you whether the report is retrieved or fabricated.

Sasha then said, fine, take it out of the first person entirely. Say that in a few years we're good enough at decoding imagery out of visual cortex that we can pull a rough movie out of someone's brain overnight without asking them anything. Two cases. If the decoded movie matches the morning report, then you've got a check on the report that doesn't run through the subject at all, and the whole debate is settled. And if the movie and the report come apart — decoders get a beach, the person insists on the same old nightmare about the stairwell — then you have to choose. Either the dream is the movie, in which case dreams are real experiences occurring during sleep and the report is a confabulation about them; or the dream is the report, and you owe an account of why a night-long movie in someone's visual cortex doesn't count. She said either way he loses: the first option concedes the thing he's denying, and the second one he hasn't got an answer to.

Marcus said he'd take the first option and it costs him nothing. If the movie is the dream, then the thing I've been writing in my journal every morning is mostly not a report of my dreams at all — it's a story generated next to them, or after them. And then, he said, you have to say in what sense a movie running in my visual cortex that nobody can get at is something I experience; if the answer is that it just is, whether or not anyone can access it, then I've quietly dropped the idea that a dream is something that happens to me, which was the whole point. Then he turned it back on my journal: if the reports are generated fresh each morning, then "the same nightmare again" isn't the same event recurring at all — it's the same script being re-run. My therapist is treating it like a memory I'm revising, and maybe what I'm actually doing is retraining a habit of composition.

Sasha said that last part is a bullet he has to bite and he just swallowed it whole. If the reports aren't memories, then my journal isn't evidence about anything that happened, and "I've had this nightmare for years" comes out meaning something like "I have produced this kind of text on many mornings," which she'd of thought is a reductio of his position rather than a consequence he gets to help himself to.

They asked me to referee and I couldn't. (I mentioned it to someone later and she said oh, this is basically Malcolm versus Dennett, there's a whole literature — I haven't read any of it, so don't assume I know the background.)

My one uneducated guess is that they're both assuming dreams have to be one thing across the board, and that the morning report has to be either a faithful memory or a total fabrication with nothing in between, but I could be way off.
