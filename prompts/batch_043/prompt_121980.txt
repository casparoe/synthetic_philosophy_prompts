I sit on the allocation committee of a mid-sized health and development fund, and I draft its rulings. When two applications are both fundable and the money covers only one, we have to record whether the choice between them is settled by the reasons we already accept or is a hard one that we close by judgment or by chance. Our verdict so far takes one of three forms: rank them and fund the better one; decline to rank and decide by judgment; decline to rank and randomize. The precedents, with the reasons minuted:

1. A malaria-net distribution trial against a philosophy-in-schools programme, both large and multi-year: declined to rank, decided by judgment, funded the nets, and minuted that either choice would have been permissible.
2. Two deworming proposals in different countries, same outcome metric, 4.2 against 5.1 units per dollar, intervals not overlapping: ranked.
3. Two cash-transfer proposals, same metric, best estimates differing by about a third, intervals overlapping: ranked by expected value, minuting that our uncertainty was ignorance rather than incommensurability.
4. Two proposals in the same field whose estimates differed by a few tenths of a percent, far inside measurement error: deadlocked three to three and never resolved.
5. Two small pilots in different domains, a poetry workshop and a bicycle-repair workshop: declined to rank, randomized.

Last quarter the fund adopted a new instrument: a single cardinal scale of wellbeing equivalents, fitted to survey data, that assigns one number to any programme's expected outcomes with a stated resolution of roughly plus or minus 0.2 units. It was adopted as a common reporting metric; no one recorded whether it is meant to govern allocation.

The pair I have to move on Thursday: a mental-health programme at 6.1 units and a vocational-training programme at 7.4 units. Different kinds of benefit, as in case 1, but now separated by many times the resolution of a shared scale, as in case 2.

Please give me, in this order:

1. Candidate rules, about 150 words: the distinct rules that fit all five precedents and could be generating our verdicts.
2. What each predicts here, about 120 words: each rule's ruling on the new pair, where they diverge, and whether any rule's prediction turns on the unresolved status of the new scale.
3. Recommendation, about 100 words: the ruling I should move for if the aim is consistency with our own record, with the closest analogue among the precedents identified.
4. What we would be committing to, about 80 words: the substantive principle each available ruling would commit the fund to, whichever way we go.
5. The case that would settle it, about 80 words: the further pair of applications whose ruling would discriminate between the rules that agree on this one, and what each result would tell us.
