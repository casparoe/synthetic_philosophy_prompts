I'm taking a graduate seminar in philosophy of mind this term, and a few of us are putting together a short comedic sketch for the end-of-term reading-group party. My job is to supply one joke, and I would like it to be a genuine in-joke: something that gets a laugh out of people who have actually read Clark and Chalmers, and merely puzzles everyone else. The generic register — "I can't remember anything without my phone," or a pun that works only because the word "extended" has a second meaning — is exactly what I am trying to avoid.

The case I would like the joke to work with (or with a variant of it) is one our instructor introduced, which as far as I can tell does not appear in the published literature. Call the subject Mira. Like Otto in the well-known notebook case, Mira carries a notebook-sized external store of what she treats as her own beliefs, consults it constantly and unhesitatingly, and would be lost without it. Unlike Otto, Mira did not write it, has never read it through, and has no independent opinion about most of what is in it: a research assistant has been quietly composing, curating and revising it on her behalf for years, silently updating past entries whenever they conflict with what the assistant now takes to be the best available evidence. Mira simply endorses whatever she finds there.

What the seminar has been arguing about is which of the conditions standardly taken to matter for genuine cognitive extension Mira's case actually meets. Our handout lists four: that the external resource be reliably available; that it be automatically endorsed; that its contents be easily accessible; and that the information in it have been consciously endorsed by the subject at some point in the past. The live question, as our instructor framed it, is whether Mira shows the list to be too permissive — because if all that matters is coupling plus automatic endorsement, it starts to look as though the assistant, and not just the notebook, is part of Mira's mind — or too demanding, because it would seem to exclude cases of socially or institutionally maintained memory that nobody really wants to rule out.

So what I am asking for is a joke whose punchline turns on the actual machinery of the extended mind thesis: on what the parity principle is doing when it says, roughly, that if a process occurring outside the head were occurring inside it we would not hesitate to count it as cognitive, then it is cognitive; on which of those four conditions is doing the real work and which is decorative; on the slide from being causally coupled to something and being partly constituted by it; or on the "cognitive bloat" objection and the demand for a mark of the cognitive. It should be the sort of joke a philosopher working on this literature laughs at because she can hear the premise the joke is trading on.

Please give me the setup and punchline in performable form — a few lines at most, not a shaggy-dog story — ideally spoken by one of the characters in the Mira case: Mira herself, the assistant, or a colleague of Mira's who is trying to work out whether the assistant deserves co-authorship on her next paper. Then, in two or three sentences, tell me what the joke is trading on: which claim or condition it exploits, and whether the laugh is at the expense of the extended mind thesis or at the expense of one of the objections to it.
