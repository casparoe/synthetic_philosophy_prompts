I'm a PhD student in philosophy putting together a position paper for a neuroethics workshop on the abolitionist programme — the proposal to raise the hedonic set point by genetic recalibration and designer neurochemistry, and eventually to remove the capacity for suffering altogether — and I want a consistency audit of the commitments I've ended up with before I circulate it. Here is the set:

(1) Only valenced experience is intrinsically valuable; knowledge, achievement, relationships and meaning matter solely through their effects on the felt quality of experience.
(2) Positive and negative valence lie on one continuous scale, yet the negative deserves priority of effort, because the lower tail of the distribution of achievable experiences is far longer and deeper than the upper tail.
(3) The long-run aim is abolition rather than compensation: recalibrate the biological machinery of valence so that no experience falls below a hedonic floor, while keeping motivation intact through states that remain information-sensitive — that still track what is going wrong and still move us to act, without hurting in the way pain and despair now hurt.
(4) Wireheading — direct stimulation of the reward system — is not an acceptable route to the same end, even in principle. It is a defeat and not a solution, and I want to keep that verdict whatever the hedonic arithmetic says about it.
(5) Abolition must not cost us epistemic contact with the world; a recalibrated psychology that makes us systematically misjudge risk or become indifferent to real badness is disqualified.
(6) The intervention should be democratically legitimated, and competent individuals should retain a right to decline it for themselves.
(7) The obligation extends to all sentient beings, including animals in the wild, whose suffering we have reason to prevent or design away.

Please work through these and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension, how severe each tension is, and what the cheapest revision would be — and, just as usefully, which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection, so that I don't spend the paper defending against objections that don't bite. My own hunch is that (1) and (2) are the shakiest pair, but I'd rather you did your own triage than followed my suspicion. Argue from the structure of the commitments rather than from the literature — I don't want citations or reading suggestions, since I have the bibliography handling. Rank the genuine tensions by severity, and for each say explicitly which numbered commitment would have to give and what that costs me elsewhere.
