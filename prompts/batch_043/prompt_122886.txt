I spent a career in the physical sciences — numerical modelling of geophysical fluids, for the most part — and since retiring I have been reading my way into the philosophy of mathematics. I came to it already holding most of the views below, and they now look to me like they may not all fit together. I would be grateful for an audit rather than an introduction.

What I believe:

1. I am a scientific realist, and I got there abductively: the no-miracles argument that gives me electrons and fields also gives me numbers and functions, since our best theories quantify over both. I accept the Quine–Putnam indispensability argument, and specifically its explanatory form — the point is not that mathematics is needed to *state* our theories but that it does *explanatory* work in them.

2. I am a structuralist about mathematical objects. I take Benacerraf's identification problem to be fatal to any object-based platonism: there is no fact of the matter as to which sets the natural numbers are, and nothing in mathematical practice turns on it. What mathematics describes are structures and positions within them, not objects with intrinsic natures. I do not think this threatens the determinacy of ordinary arithmetical truth.

3. I answer the other Benacerraf problem — the access problem — by going plenitudinous. If every consistent mathematical structure exists, then our mathematical beliefs are automatically reliable: any structure we coherently describe is one we are describing truly. This is, for me, the main attraction of platonism over its rivals, since it buys the ontology without a causal theory of mathematical knowledge.

4. About explanation I hold what I suppose most working scientists hold: to explain a phenomenon is to exhibit the mechanism that produces it, or to display the pattern of counterfactual dependence you get by intervening on the causes. What made me trust a model was never that it fit; it was that perturbing it behaved the way the real system behaves.

5. I also accept that there are distinctively mathematical and non-causal explanations in science — cases where a physical outcome is explained by a purely mathematical fact such as primeness or non-divisibility, with no causal story running from the mathematical fact to the outcome (the prime-length life cycles of periodical cicadas are the standard example, though I do not care about the biological details). I take such cases to be the strongest available support for the explanatory indispensability argument in (1).

6. I am a uniquist about set theory: there is one intended universe, the continuum hypothesis has a determinate truth value despite independence from the usual axioms, and independence results are evidence about the weakness of our axioms rather than about the subject matter.

7. I think the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics is largely a selection effect. We retain the mathematics that fits and quietly discard what does not, so I do not find applicability surprising, and I do not want to rest anything on it.

8. I reject the deflationary turn in this debate. I do not think the dispute between platonist and nominalist is merely verbal, and I do not think that the existence of numbers follows trivially from ordinary true claims about chairs and planets via some logical or semantic machinery. I take mathematical existence to be a substantive metaphysical claim, and I amunsympathetic to positions that answer the ontological question by making it cheap.

Two requests about method. First, I would rather be told that one of these has to go than be told that all eight are defensible after suitable refinement; please rank whatever tensions you find by severity rather than listing them as equally serious, and for each say what would have to be given up and what that costs. Second, I suspect at least one or two of the apparent conflicts are not real — my guess is that (7) does not in fact damage (1), and that (2) does not in fact damage the determinacy of mathematics — but I would like that argued rather than assumed, since I may be wrong about where the real trouble is.

Where does the set actually come apart, which single revision would clean up the most, and what would I have to stop saying?
