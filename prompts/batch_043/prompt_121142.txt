I'm a science-and-ideas journalist working on a piece about what dramatizations owe the real dead, and I'm using the HBO/Sky Chernobyl as my main case, since everyone's seen it and it's about as self-consciously true-ish as television gets. I've listened to Mazin's episode-by-episode podcasts where he goes through each bit of artistic license, so I know the defenses already. What I want is the harder version of the question: not "is it accurate," but what exactly is being taken, from whom, and whether anyone is owed anything back.

The crude distinction I've been leaning on in my draft is "borrowing" vs "forging." Borrowing is compressing, moving, or consolidating what the record supports: three real investigators folded into one composite physicist, timelines squeezed, Legasov's suicide pulled forward so it lands right after the trial when really it was a couple of years later (he did kill himself, in April 1988, after a long slide rather than a single dramatic day). Forging is putting into a real person's mouth or hands something the record contradicts, or filling a silence where the silence is itself part of the story.

The case that breaks my own distinction is the trial in the final episode. As I understand it — correct me if I'm wrong — the trial was real but Legasov's testimony in the show, where he explains the graphite-tipped control rods and the fatal shutdown button, was basically written for television, and at the one real occasion we can compare it to, his Vienna presentation in 1986, he did not tell the world about the design flaws. So the show hands him the courage of a speech he never gave, at precisely the kind of moment where the record says he kept quiet. That's not a gap-filler, it's closer to a reversal of a documented act — and unlike most biopic sins it flatters him rather than smearing him. Is giving a real dead man a heroism he didn't display a theft, a gift, or just a lie with better publicity? And does it matter that he seems to have been moving toward going public in his last years, on tape?

Then there's the composite, where my two-column frame feels useless. Khomyuk is nobody, so nothing can be taken from her. But credit that belonged to a scattered group now has a single owner, and a fictional woman gets to be brave on screen while the actual people she's made of don't. Is there a wrong here that isn't a wrong to a person — a wrong of erasure, or of making the audience feel they've met someone who never existed? Is a composite a lie about who did the work, or a lie about what the room looked like, or both, and can any composite avoid being at least one?

There's a layer under that too. The firefighter-and-his-wife storyline comes out of Svetlana Alexievich's Voices from Chernobyl, and I've since read that researchers argue she inserted her own words into her subjects' testimony and revised it heavily between editions. If that's right, the show's most intimate scenes are dramatizing somebody else's literary construction. Does that loosen the duty, because there's no stable record to be faithful to, or relocate it, because now you're answerable to a living-feeling person rather than a transcript? My suspicion is it means my borrowing/forging line needs a third category and I can't see what it is.

I also want the anonymous cases. The scene on the bridge with the man and his baby watching the fire is, by most accounts, an urban legend, not an event. What is owed to dead people who are never named? Is inventing them more or less forgivable than inventing things about Legasov?

On disclosure: my editor says the closing title cards settle it. You're told Khomyuk is a composite, you're told what happened to the real people, Mazin has been openly explaining himself in public, so nobody is deceived. I call this the reciept problem: you get the reciept after you've already left the shop, and everyone I know who watched it believes Khomyuk was a real woman who was there. Does a duty of accuracy include an obligation to make the correction as sticky as the invention? Is that even an obligation to the audience as opposed to the dead, or are those the same thing wearing different hats?

And my editor's bigger objection, which I think is too fast but can't defeat: Legasov has been dead since 1988, dead people can't be harmed, so who exactly is the victim. I want to say you can wrong someone without harming them, but I'd need to say it without sounding like I'm doing definatley circular academic hand-waving. Is there a version of that move which survives contact with a skeptic who wants a victim and a damage figure?

Last thing: tell me where the show resists this whole reading. Mazin seems to think there's a higher-order truth — that lies cost lives, that credit under a system like that is deliberately scattered and a composite can be truer than the scattered fact — and that this licenses a lot of invention. Is "compression makes it truer" a real defense or a get-out? And is there anything the show understands about the real dead that the philosophy doesn't have a category for? I'm thinking of the way it handles Dyatlov, a real man depicted as insisting the core can't have exploded while standing in the debris, and whom plenty of engineers think got turned into a villain.

What I need is a reading I can actually use in about 1200 words, not a survey: where my frame holds, where it collapses, and one or two things I'd be wrong to leave out. Please don't give me a reading list or footnotes — I'll do that legwork myself, and half of what you'd name would be paywalled anyway.
