I convene a two-hour evening workshop for a university "AI and society" reading group: 16 participants, mostly computer-science graduate students with a handful of philosophy and psychology students, one 110-minute session in a room with a whiteboard, index cards, and no reliable projector. Before the session I will not have lectured, and I do not want the session to contain a lecture. Design a runnable activity that makes the group feel, in their own hands and before anyone names it, what is difficult about the family of views that defines the target of an AI system as what people *would* want under better conditions — more information, more coherence, more reflection, less muddle — rather than as what they actually want here and now.

I have a specific structure in mind for the mechanism, and I want the activity built around it. Give every team the same invented subject (a short profile on a card: messy, ordinary, quotidian first-order concerns about career, location, family, health, money and time; nothing political, nothing that invites participants to disclose anything real about themselves) and the same three operations to apply to that subject, stated as plain instructions:

1. supply the subject with information she currently lacks about herself and her situation;
2. remove incoherence among her attitudes (things she wants that defeat things she wants more, aversions that survive the facts, means she refuses though she wants the ends);
3. let her reactions to the results of (1) and (2) settle, and follow where they go.

Then split the room into teams of four, and vary — secretly, by card — only two things: the *order* in which a team must apply the three operations, and the tie-break rule it must use when two operations pull in different directions ("keep the result recognizable to the subject as she is now" versus "keep the profile maximally coherent, whatever that does to the resemblance"). My hypothesis, which the activity should be built to test, is that the visible divergence in outcomes will be driven almost entirely by the tie-break and only secondarily by the order, and that the teams themselves will insist it was driven by the operations.

The three things I want participants to collide with, in this order of felt discovery:

- **Non-commutation.** Adding knowledge and removing incoherence are not guaranteed to be order-independent, and the group should construct two profiles where the order changes the answer, so that "what she'd want if she knew more and were more coherent" turns out not to denote a single destination.
- **Laundering.** A team can make a desire vanish by re-describing the situation the subject is in, or by dissolving the self that has it, rather than by ever testing whether it survives improvement. I want the instructions arranged so that at least one team is strongly tempted into this and can see afterwards what they did, and so that "we removed it because it was an artifact" is visibly a way of deleting a person rather than refining her.
- **The successor problem.** The result of enough extrapolation can be an agent whose standpoint the current subject cannot occupy and whose concerns need not include hers, at which point "this is what she really wants" quietly becomes "this is what her successor wants." The activity should end with at least one team holding a profile they themselves would describe as well-integrated and that the subject, reading it, would refuse.

For the ending I want an observable stake rather than a prize: each subject card is authored by a participant who stays silent during the exercise, and afterwards each team reads its profile aloud while that author rates, in writing and without discussion, whether she recognizes it as hers. No money, no real personal disclosure, no votes on other people's values.

Give me: the setup and materials; the roles with their private information; the three operations in a form precise enough that two teams given the same card cannot reasonably disagree about what they were asked to do; a minute-by-minute timeline of what participants do and what the facilitator says (the facilitator must not use the words *idealized*, *extrapolated*, *coherent*, *preference* or *volition* before the debrief); the hidden-parameter assignment; a debrief that maps specific moments participants will have lived through onto the three difficulties above, in the order they will want to talk about them; an honest account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it in the room; a frank statement of which feature of the idealization debate the activity cannot reproduce and which I must therefore supply in words (I assume one of these is that real extrapolated preferences are supposed to *converge* across persons, and that my invented cards diverge partly because they are thin); and how to know at the end that it landed rather than merely confusing people. Tell me also how to compress it to 8 participants or stretch it to 30.

After the design, return a single JSON object with this exact schema:

- "title": string — a short name for the activity, usable on a handout.
- "materials": array of strings — one per item, with quantities.
- "roles": array of objects with fields "role" (string), "number" (number), "private_information" (string), "incentive_or_constraint" (string) — what that role knows that others do not, and what binds them.
- "operations": array of objects with fields "name" (string), "one_line_spec" (string), "what_it_models" (string).
- "timeline": array of objects with fields "minutes" (string, e.g. "0-10"), "phase" (string), "participants_do" (string), "facilitator_says_or_does" (string).
- "hidden_parameters": array of objects with fields "parameter" (string), "values_assigned" (string), "expected_effect" (string).
- "predicted_moments": array of objects with fields "when" (string), "what_you_will_see" (string), "maps_onto" (string).
