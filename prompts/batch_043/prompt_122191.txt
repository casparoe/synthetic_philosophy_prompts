I'm 71, retired a few years ago after a career in hydrology (rivers, not the glamorous kind of science), and in the evenings I've started reading philosophy, which my daughter thinks is a phase and I think is the first thing I've read since university that actually bites. Anyway, this isn't homework, it's a real mess I'm in. My oldest friend Pete — we climbed together for fifty years, we were in each other's pockets — took a bad fall eight months ago, three weeks in a coma, and came out a different man. Not confused: he remembers dates, routes, conversations, everything. But he's gentle where he was caustic, he's got religion, and he's on what he calls an amends tour, apologising to people for things the previous version of him did, including to me for something fairly unforgivable he said to me in 1987. He also wants me to hand over the letters he wrote me over the decades so he can burn them, and he'd rather my granddaughter not be told anything about who he used to be. His own daughter says flatly that her father died in that hospital and this is a nice man wearing his body. Here's the detail I can't get past: Pete tells stories about our shared past that are factually impeccable and, to me, simply not about the same events — the trip where we nearly came to blows is now the story of how close we always were, and when he tells it I feel like I'm being gently erased.

I've been trying to think about it the only way I know how, which is to look for the invariant, and I've got two half-baked ideas I want you to take seriously and then knock down. First: an old friend of mine, Marion, changed every bit as much as Pete did, but slowly, over thirty years, and it never once occurred to me to doubt she was the same person. So it isn't the size of the change that bothers me, it's that there's no path between the two Petes — I've started calling it the corridor test, whether I can walk from one version to the other without stepping off into nothing. Second: I read somewhere that the self is supposed to be a bit like a centre of gravity, not an extra thing in the world, just a useful abstraction you can talk about. That sounds clever until you notice that a centre of gravity is pinned down by where the mass actually is — you don't get rival centres of gravity, one per family member — whereas there are at least three stories here, Pete's, his daughter's and mine, and I can't see what would settle which one is the real one. So either the comparison is doing no work at all, or there's something that anchors the story that I'm not seeing, and I suspect part of my discomfort with the apology is that I can't tell whether I think it's misdirected, or just cheap because the man saying it doesn't feel the thing he's apologising for.

So: should I hand over the letters, should I let him apologise to me, and what do I actually say to a nine-year-old who asks who Pete was? And don't send me to a reading list, I've got a shelf of them and no patience left. Lay it out for me in these sections, roughly: "What's really being asked here" (about 150 words, the shape of the problem under my story, not the story itself); "Testing my two ideas" (about 300, take the corridor test and the centre-of-gravity business separately and tell me where each one is pointing at something true and where it collapses); "The three decisions" (about 250, the letters, the apology, the granddaughter — and yes I want actual verdicts, not both sides); "What I'd have to give up either way" (about 150, what stops being true for me if I take one line rather than the other); and "The question I should have asked instead" (about 100). Write it for someone who knows his way around a mass balance but not around the literature.
