I sit on the disorders-of-consciousness committee at a large teaching hospital, and I've been asked to review our written protocol for patients who show no behavioural response at the bedside. It says, roughly:

(i) A patient counts as conscious only if they can produce a willful, command-following response — either overtly, or "covertly", by modulating their brain activity in a scanner or EEG paradigm in the way healthy volunteers do when they are instructed to perform two different imagery tasks, with the result required to clear a pre-set statistical threshold and to be reproduced on at least two of three attempts.

(ii) Evidence that is not voluntary is given explicitly zero weight: cortical responses to noxious stimulation, preserved thalamocortical metabolism, pupil dilation, sleep–wake architecture, startle. These are recorded as subcortical or reflexive — non-conscious neural processing.

(iii) When the two kinds of evidence come apart — intact, differentiated responses to painful stimulation, but failure of every active paradigm — the protocol tells us to classify the patient as not conscious.

(iv) The same committee, ruling on analgesia for neonates undergoing surgery and on non-human animals in the hospital's research protocols, treats behavioural and physiological responses to noxious stimulation as sufficient evidence of conscious pain, and requires anaesthesia or analgesia accordingly.

A colleague has now proposed an amendment: patients who fail every active paradigm but show the responses listed in (ii) should be recorded as "possibly in pain" and given analgesia, while remaining, for the purposes of the treatment decision, not conscious.

Someone on the committee said that what we are doing is "basically a higher-order theory of consciousness," and I want to know whether that is right and how much of it we are actually committed to.

Please reconstruct the implicit theory and then tell me the following. Does the protocol strictly presuppose that a mental state is conscious only in virtue of being accompanied by some suitable higher-order representation, or availability to the subject, or does it only presuppose a higher-order *evidential policy* — a rule about what to demand as evidence in a population whose behaviour is badly damaged — that a first-order theorist could accept just as well, on grounds about base rates, unreliable first-order signs, or the asymmetry between wrongly withdrawing treatment and wrongly continuing it? Which rival views does the protocol quietly rule out, and which would license exactly the same rules for different reasons? Is clause (iv) genuinely inconsistent with (i)–(iii), or does it dissolve once the protocol's evidentiary standard, rather than its metaphysics, is what is doing the work — and does that reconciliation remain available if clause (i) is read as a metaphysical claim?

Then the amendment: does this family of theory really deliver "unconscious pain" as a stable category, or does the idea of a pain that no one undergoes collapse into mere nociception? If it collapses, what happens to the case for the protocol and to the amendment? Finally, where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, and what specifically would have to change in it if we adopted a first-order view instead — would clause (ii) evidence have to be given weight, and what would we do with the two-of-three reproducibility rule, given that a patient's capacity to produce a higher-order state might fluctuate while the underlying sensory state does not?

I want the argument rather than the literature; please don't spend the answer on who has defended which view.
