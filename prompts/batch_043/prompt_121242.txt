Hey — I'm building a tutorial session on the knowledge argument and I need a Mary's-room variant constructed to spec, not just an illustration. The thing I want students to actually feel is that she gains a new fact on release does not by itself get you to there are non-physical facts, and the move they keep missing is the self-locating one: a complete physical description of the world is uncentered, and you can have all of that and still not know which physical state you are currently in or what it is like from the inside. So build the case to isolate that.

Specs:

One paragraph, realistic. Ordinary humans, ordinary objects, no surgery, no implants, no futuristic tech, no demons. The only licence you get is ordinary human variation — born without colour vision, or a deficit roughly like prosopagnosia but for colours, or no visual imagery at all. If you want a recognition deficit, describe it; dont tell me it's a real clinical diagnosis.

The subject must be stipulated (via the case, not via narration) to lack all three abilities the ability hypothesis standardly points at: imagining the experience in advance, recognizing it later, remembering it afterwards. Build that into the situation.

Do not tell the reader she learns something. Set it up so the intuition shows up on its own — if a physicalist can read your paragraph and reasonably say she gains nothing, the case fails.

Given that, here are the verdicts I want, and I want each one generated from the theory rather than asserted:

- The ability hypothesis: forced either to say she gains nothing new, or to stretch ability until it stops doing explanatory work. Say which, and what the stretch costs it.
- The phenomenal-concept / old-fact-new-guise move: should come out worse here than in the standard case. With no durable recognitional capacity, there is no ordinary recognitional concept for her to acquire, so the defender has to either fall back on a one-off demonstrative that arguably isn't a concept, or hand the problem over to the self-locating diagnosis. Show that dilemma.
- The self-locating diagnosis: gets the clean verdict. New fact, but a centered one, whose uncentered content she already had, so physicalism survives. Spell out what the centered fact is and why completeness of her prior physical knowledge doesn't include it.
- The knowledge argument: keeps the full intuition but has to be shown to need an extra premise to get from new fact to non-physical fact. Name the premise and say how a physicalist denies it.

Two more design details. Her prior knowledge should be complete about colour vision rather than about the entire universe — say whether narrowing it that way helps or hurts any of the four verdicts. And nothing in the case may stipulate a verdict, e.g. no narrator aside to the effect that of course she now knows something new.

Then, after the paragraph: a short block for each of the four views in the order above, each showing the reasoning that produces its verdict. After that, tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work; what happens if I remove it (I expect it definately collapses back into the usual stalemate where the ability response and the phenomenal-concept response both look perfectly fine — tell me if that's wrong); and the strongest thing an ability theorist would say to escape, where the escape isn't just relabeling having the experience as an ability.

Audience is second-years who have read the standard case once, so keep the walkthrough concrete and skip the literature map.
