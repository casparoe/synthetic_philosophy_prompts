I'm setting the final for my mixed upper-level / MA metaphysics seminar — about 14 students, and it has to work both as a 90-minute in-class exam and as a take-home, so nothing that needs a library or the internet. The last third of the term was verbal disputes and metaontology: the usual Hirsch-style quantifier variance, Sider-ish carve-nature-at-the-joints realism, Chalmers-style tests for whether a dispute is merely verbal, Thomasson-style easy ontology. What I definately don't want is a question they can kill by writing "it depends what you mean by exists" or by reciting the subtraction / paraphrase test back at me.

The ingredient I want at the centre is something I've started calling a stipulation-proof dispute. Both sides pass every standard diagnostic for verbalness: they accept each other's full paraphrases in the shared neutral vocabulary, they agree on every non-ontological fact, each would assent to the other's sentence read in the other's idiolect. And still the dispute won't dissolve. They keep fighting over which vocabulary goes in the textbook chapter / the joint paper / the policy doc, each thinks the other's preferred way of talking is actively misleading, and neither will accept a stipulation that settles it. So there's no residual factual disagreement for the tests to detect, but there's also no peace.

Write me ONE essay question built around that, a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 20. The question should force them to say whether "verbal" is a verdict about the dispute or about what the disputants are doing with their words; whether a fully verbal dispute can still be a disagreement worth having, and if so what kind of disagreement it is; and — the part I care most about — whether the verbalness verdict can be turned on the metaontological disagreement itself. If their fight about what fixes the meaning of the neutral vocabulary is verbal in the same way, is that a regress, a fixed point, or neither, and does the answer change what we should say about first-order ontology? The model answer should commit to a position rather than laying out the options.

For the marking scheme: five must-hit moves with marks each, and one line on how people standardly screw each one up. I want the A/B boundary sharp, so say what seperates an answer that just redescribes the stipulation-proof case from one that actually does something with it. And flag in advance which line of the question is most likely to get misread, so I can rephrase it.

Titles, dates and citations matter not at all — I say so on the front page — so please don't build anything that depends on a reference being right, and don't make the model answer name-drop instead of arguing.
