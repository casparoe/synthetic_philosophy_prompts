hey — I run a tiny reading group with two friends where we've been working through rational choice theory informally (no supervisor breathing down our necks, which is maybe the problem), and I promised to hand them a one-page "where I stand on diachronic rationality" sheet before Thursday. I've been going around in circles on it, and I'd rather get it poked at before I commit to something that quietly contradicts itself. I'm after whether the package hangs together, not whether any of it is fashionable, and please skip reading suggestions or citations — I already have more than I can read and I'd rather you just argue with me.

Roughly where I've landed, in five claims:

(1) The money-pump argument is the best reason we have for thinking any structural condition on preferences (transitivity and the rest) is a requirement of rationality: a preference structure is defective exactly when it lets someone extract resources from you for nothing. Call it the no-free-lunch reading. I want the "for nothing" to do real work: if the trader ends up taking your apple and handing your orange back while you're two cents down, you haven't been pumped unless there's some standpoint from which the whole sequence leaves you worse off and you got nothing out of it — and I've started to suspect that for an agent whose preferences genuinely change over time there may simply be no such standpoint, which is the version of the problem I keep getting stuck on.

(2) There's no distinctively diachronic requirement of rationality. Your preferences can change completely and nothing is irrational about that: I loved terrible metal at fifteen and can't stand it now, and neither the fifteen-year-old nor I was making a mistake. Rationality is a property of the choice you make against the preferences you have when you make it.

(3) Given that, the right way to handle your own future instability is the "sophisticated" approach: at every point, hold your current preferences fixed, work out what you'll actually do later, and pick what's best by your current lights given that prediction. Corollary: don't form plans you know you won't keep; if you know Thursday-you will blow the deadline regardless, plan around Thursday-you rather than pretending otherwise.

(4) But I also want to say that sometimes you should do what you planned even when, at the moment of acting, you'd honestly rather deviate, deviating is free and available, and there's no downstream cost — no reputation hit, no self-deception, no hangover. My friend's example is acquiring a taste: you drag yourself to the beginners' cello lesson on a wet evening when you'd much rather stay in, because the person you'll become is glad you went, and you'd defend that as the thing to do even though at 6pm it definately isn't what you want. I don't want to cash this out as "you secretly value being a plan-keeper", because that feels like cheating — but maybe it is cheating and I should just own it.

(5) And I like the idea that rational choice theory stays neutral about the content of preferences: it's never irrational to have the preferences you have, however weird (being indifferent to any pain that happens to land on a future Tuesday, say); the only thing that can be irrational is choosing badly given the ones you have.

What I want from you is an audit of these against each other. Which pairs genuinely pull against each other and which only look like they do, and for the real ones, what exactly has to give and what does giving it up cost me? Rank the tensions by severity instead of flagging them all as equally bad, and tell me which single revision would tidy up the most. I really don't know where the fault line is — I keep flip-flopping between (4) being the odd one out and (2) being it — so don't assume I've got a favourite I'm protecting. And if one of the apparent conflicts dissolves under pressure, explain why rather than just adding it to the pile.
