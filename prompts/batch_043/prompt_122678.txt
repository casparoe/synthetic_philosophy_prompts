I am trying to settle what, if anything, survives of the familiar complaint that the Great Divergence question is ill posed — the question, in one of its standard forms, of why sustained, self-reinforcing economic modernization began in Britain rather than in China: the puzzle that descends from Needham, and that Pomeranz's *The Great Divergence* (2000) reframed as a comparison between advanced regions of northwestern Europe and the Yangzi Delta.

The complaint, as I understand it, has three parts. "China" was not a single acting or decision-making unit of the right kind. "The Industrial Revolution" is a sprawling process rather than a datable event, so the topic of the question is not well individuated. And there is no determinate counterfactual against which a difference-maker could be isolated.

My problem is that this seems to prove a great deal too much. Contrastive why-questions are routine in the natural sciences, and on the standard account such a question is fixed by three things — a topic, a contrast class, and a relevance relation — with the answer read off as a difference-maker against a causal background shared by the actual case and the foil. Nothing in that machinery demands that the topic be a datable event, or that the foil have been a live option for some agent. Ask why a particular nucleus decayed at *t* rather than surviving past *t*: the question is in good order even though nothing prior settled which way it would go, and the contrast class can be left almost wholly indeterminate. And the unit worry looks like a problem of granularity rather than of principle. If "China" is too coarse, ask instead why Lancashire rather than the Yangzi Delta, or why this district rather than that one, and keep subdividing until the relata are genuinely comparable.

Let me sharpen the issue. Call a contrastive question *comparable* when its relata are of the same kind, and *detachable* when the foil can be specified as a counterfactual state of the world without thereby perturbing the causal background of the topic. It seems to me that decomposition is exactly the procedure for securing comparability, and that comparability is the only thing the ill-posedness objection could seriously have been asking for. But that leaves me with a dilemma I cannot resolve. Either the objection is merely that the original question was vaguely worded — in which case there is no interesting methodological lesson, and contrastive explanation in history is in precisely as good standing as it is anywhere else — or the objection survives every increase in precision, and then I do not see how any large historical outcome could be explained at all, which I take to be absurd.

Where I want help is with the dilemma rather than with the history. Is it exhaustive? In particular: does the shift from "Britain versus China" to "Lancashire versus the Yangzi Delta" do anything at all for detachability, or does it only make comparability look better while leaving the deeper problem untouched? Does the decay case really support the claim that contrastive explanation is indifferent to the structure of the background, or does it satisfy conditions — repeatable event types, an invariant background, a well-defined probability distribution — that the historical case conspicuously fails? And if something does survive, what is the positive moral: that questions of this shape must be answered by comparing whole causal structures rather than by isolating a difference-maker, that the contrast class should be redrawn within a single unit, that the topic should be re-individuated, or something I have not thought of?

I am not asking for a survey of the historiography, a reading list, or citations; assume I know the usual coal-and-colonies story and the usual institutional story. What I want is the methodological argument, and, where my reconstruction above has gone wrong, a diagnosis of exactly where it goes wrong.
