I write novels, and I'm revising a manuscript in which I need to settle a line of dialogue before I can move on. A neuroscientist character tells a recently widowed patient, convinced she has seen her dead husband standing in the doorway, that her experience is the same process as ordinary seeing, only nothing is correcting it. He gets there by way of the line that perception is controlled hallucination — I first heard it at a festival panel, where it was being used to explain why eyewitnesses get things wrong — and my editor says it has become a tic of popular neuroscience and that I should know what it commits my character to before I keep it in his mouth.

Could you set out, as a menu, the distinct precise claims that slogan might be expressing: the near-trivial ones (experience goes beyond what arrives at the sensory surfaces; expectation shapes what one sees), the genuinely contested ones (the difference between perceiving and hallucinating is a matter of degree, or turns on how much weight the system gives to sensory error signals rather than on whether anything is out there), and the radical ones (experience never puts us in contact with mind-independent things, so the widow lacks nothing the rest of us have)? I've seen predictive processing and talk of precision-weighting invoked in the same breath; I don't want a survey of who argued what, and I can't check sources — I want the claims themselves.

For each reading, say whether it is essentially a platitude, a defensible but contested thesis, or false, and above all whether it would actually license my character's continuity claim or whether that claim trades on sliding between senses of "hallucination." He is meant to be intelligent and partly right rather than a strawman, so flag which reading, if any, lets him stay that way.

I'd rather have a menu with its weak joints marked than a tidy one: for each verdict, say what would change your mind, and note where you are extrapolating past what the neuroscience actually settles.
