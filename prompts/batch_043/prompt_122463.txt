I have to teach a single ninety-minute seminar on the guilt/shame contrast to first-year undergraduates in a moral psychology course. They will arrive with nothing assigned and with two things already in hand: the slogan that guilt targets what was done while shame targets the person who did it, and a strong intuition that shame is the suspect emotion because it depends on what other people think. Both of these need to be complicated rather than confirmed. I do not need the distinction explained to me; I need a plan for producing it in the room, and I need the plan to be an argument about dependency — what has to be true in their heads before the next thing can be said without distorting it.

Here is what I want from you.

1. The ordering, with the dependency stated at every step. I am torn between two opening moves and I cannot tell which the rest of the session rests on. The first is to make them see that shame survives the absence of any actual witness: the agent who is alone, unobserved, and still flinches. The second is to make them see the discharge asymmetry — that guilt arrives with a procedure for ending itself, namely confession, repair, punishment, and being forgiven, whereas shame has no such procedure and offers only concealment, counter-attack, or exit. I suspect the discharge asymmetry has to come first, because the closing question about which emotion a culture should cultivate only becomes askable once it is visible that one of these emotions can be retired when it has done its work and the other cannot. Tell me whether that is right, give me the full sequence in whatever order you defend, attach a time allocation, and under each step write one sentence saying what later step would fail if this one were skipped or reordered.

2. One carrying case, with its specification. I want a single case introduced early and re-described two or three times as the apparatus thickens. My candidate is a small, deliberate, wholly hidden sabotage of a colleague's work, done years ago, never discovered, with nothing left at stake: no witness, no consequence, no repair that would change anything, and still an inability to think about it without wincing. Defend this or replace it. Either way give me the must-have and must-not-have list. I think it must contain no public exposure, since exposure would reattach shame to an audience and undo the first move, and it must not involve a grave wrong, since then the room will spend its energy arguing about whether the agent deserves to feel anything at all. Tell me what else belongs on both lists, what the case must be silent about, and mark the exact moments in the sequence at which it should be reopened and with which new description.

3. The misconception map, keyed to the sequence. These are the ones I expect: that shame is fear of being found out; that guilt is the mature, private, liberal one and shame the childish, social, illiberal one; that shame is really about status and therefore not a moral emotion at all; that a shameless person merely lacks a social anxiety, when what may be missing is any grip on there being a standard; that guilt is safe because it attaches only to acts and so cannot metastasize; and that if shame is bad, a culture can simply decide to have less of it. Some of these I suspect I should use rather than defuse — tell me which, and at which point in the sequence. Two further items: someone will raise the observation that shame tips into humiliation and rage while guilt tips into repair, and I want that asymmetry given its place without the session turning into a verdict; and someone will have half-heard the anthropological contrast between cultures of guilt and cultures of shame, which I would rather deal with explicitly than have surface as a confused interruption in the last ten minutes.

4. The closing question, which is where I think the session either earns its keep or collapses. My structuring thought is that cultures do not select emotions; they select practices — forms of public naming, exposure, apology, confession, anonymity, honor, and the architecture of punishment — and the emotional profile of a population is a byproduct of those practices rather than a choice anyone is in a position to make directly. If that is right, the teachable question is not which emotion we should prefer but which practices generate which emotional ecology, and what the substitution effects are. Build the final third of the session around exactly one substitution effect, stated as a mechanism rather than as a claim. My own candidate is that public exposure destroys the standing an offender needs in order to make the amends that would discharge guilt, so that a practice intended to intensify shame can end up manufacturing the shamelessness it deplores; take this, state it precisely enough to be argued with, or replace it with a better one and say why mine is wrong. Then tell me what I should openly decline to settle in ninety minutes. I assume that includes a verdict on which emotion is better, but I suspect there is something else I will be more tempted to settle and should not.

5. One closing question to put to the room that separates a student who has the distinction from a student who has only memorized the slogan, together with a description of what a right answer that sounds wrong would look like, so that I do not mark it wrong in the moment.

Format the response as numbered segments with timings and a dependency sentence under each, then the misconception map keyed to segment numbers, then the carrying case with its reopening points, then the closing question. Do not include readings. Keep the whole thing under a thousand words.
