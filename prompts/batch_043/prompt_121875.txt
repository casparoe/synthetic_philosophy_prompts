My flatmate's brother Roscoe helps out at a tiny grantmaking fund that pays for biotech work aimed at abolishing suffering — the whole hedonistic imperative scene, which I had never heard of until he explained it to me over Christmas and which I've been arguing with him about ever since (I work in a care home, so I probably take it more personally than I should). I definately can't work out what rule he's actually running, because depending on the day he sounds like two different people. His decisions so far: he funded, almost without discussion, a non-opioid, non-sedating analgesic for chronic pain; he funded a lab trying to rebuild motivation so it runs on graded positive states that still carry information about what's going on, instead of on the threat of feeling bad; he declined an implant that lets people stimulate their own reward system directly, on the grounds that it short-circuits the person rather than fixing anything; he funded an oral, self-spreading construct to take the sting out of starvation and injury in wild rodents even though he admitted the ecological knock-on effects are basically unknown, though he kept saying the version he actually wants would keep the signal and drop the sting; he declined a proposal to add a mild mood-lifter to a town's water supply without telling anyone, even though he agreed the modelling showed a big fall in depression and suicide, saying only that it wouldn't survive daylight; and he funded a reversible adult gene therapy that raises the hedonic floor, while saying out loud that he hopes nobody ever does the heritable version, and then, when I pushed him on why not, that that was politics rather than ethics.

The new one on his desk is a cheap one-dose neural intervention that works like a governor: nothing can feel worse than about six out of ten, and everything below that feels exactly as it does now, with the same gradations, so people still notice what's wrong and still do something about it — but because of the way it works it also flattens the top of the range, so the best moments in someone's life now top out at mildly pleasant rather than at the intense highs people report from finishing something hard or falling in love. The applicants want money for the first large trial, and they want to run it first on people whose lives are already going badly: long-stay psychiatric patients, prisoners on long sentences, terminal patients, and residents of understaffed care homes like the one I work in. It's adults only with informed consent, and it's reversible in principle, though the reversal is a second procedure nobody has designed yet. The fund has no plan to change the conditions any of those people are living in.

What I want isn't really the yes or no, it's the machinery: give me the small set of rules someone could be running that would generate his entire track record — I can think of a couple, something like plain minimising of suffering with everything else counting only through the consequences, versus something more architectural about suffering having to be replaced rather than masked or flattened so that the signal still does its job, versus something about not adjusting people to situations they'd otherwise reject — and tell me what each of them predicts about this proposal, which of them you think he's on and why, which of his past decisions is the closest analogue, and what variant of the proposal he'd have to be shown to separate the rules that end up agreeing here. And please don't just send me off to read things, I want the argument itself.
