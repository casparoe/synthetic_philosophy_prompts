my partner's doing a linguistics module and she lent me the handout from her pragmatics week. It summarizes Grice roughly like this:

'Grice's starting point is that meaning isn't a property of words but of speakers: for a speaker to mean something by an utterance is for her to intend to produce some effect -- standardly, a belief -- in her hearer. Communication is therefore a matter of the hearer working out what the speaker is up to. Sentence meaning is the conventional, context-free part; whatever the speaker means over and above that is conversational implicature, i.e. implied meaning that isn't part of the literal content. We work these out using the Cooperative Principle and the four maxims -- Quantity (say as much as you can), Quality (be truthful), Relation (be relevant), Manner (be clear) -- which are really just rules of cooperative, considerate conversation. When someone appears to break one, they are "flouting" it: irony, metaphor and hinting are all cases where a maxim gets broken and the hearer has to repair the damage by guessing what was really meant. Because implicatures aren't part of the sentence, they can always be cancelled, and you can always get rid of one by rewording what you said more literally. That's also how Grice handles words like "but": the contrast you get from "she's poor but honest" is a conversational implicature worked out from the maxims, not something the word itself carries. The upshot is that a great deal of apparent semantic ambiguity is really pragmatics: the "and" in "she took off her boots and got into bed" isn't ambiguous between a logical and a temporal reading; the temporal order is just an implicature, which is why Grice says you should never multiply meanings beyond necessity.'
