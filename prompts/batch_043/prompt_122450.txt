I've been going in circles on an argument for a couple of weeks, ever since someone in a comments thread made what looked like a throwaway objection to the choice-based reading of utility, and every time I try to answer it, the answer turns back into the objection. The argument concludes that no choice-based notion of utility can do any welfare work at all — and, worse, that depending on how you patch it, it implies both that no intervention can ever help anyone and that every actual outcome is already the best one available to her. I am confident something has gone wrong. I cannot say what.

Here it is with the steps numbered.

(1) For a choice theorist, utility can only be a feature of choice behaviour. Two agents who choose identically from every menu they are actually offered differ in no observable respect, and a quantity that makes no observable difference is not part of the theory's content.

(2) What a utility function adds to the choice data is exactly an ordinal ranking: u represents the data just in case u increases along the chosen-over relation, and then so does every strictly increasing transformation of u. So there is no fact internal to the theory about how much better one option is than another — only about which is better.

(3) It follows that for any menu an agent actually faces, the option she takes is best for her, in the only sense of best for her the theory contains. There is no gap between what she picks and what she most wants, because the theory builds the second out of the first. Call this the closure step.

(4) But welfare economics is in the business of comparing across menus. To say that one policy leaves her better off than another, or that she would have been better off had the menu been different, is to compare options drawn from different menus. Call a welfare notion cross-menu if it supports such comparisons. Cost-benefit analysis, optimal tax formulas, the claim that this default serves people better than that one: all cross-menu.

(5) The closure step, however, is closed to menus actually presented. So either the theory stays closed and is not cross-menu — in which case every applied use of it is illegitimate — or we extend it to menus the agent never faced: for every menu she could have been offered, her choice from it would have been best for her. Call this the total reading.

(6) First problem. Suppose the intervention does not remove options but only re-presents them: a default, an ordering, a framing, the presence of an irrelevant third alternative. The option set is unchanged; what she picks changes. If the total reading is a single ordering over options, then picking a under one presentation and b under another means a and b are indifferent — which is itself a welfare verdict, and one that contradicts the thought that the presentation mattered to her at all. If instead the ordering is relativized to presentations, then she is best off under each presentation and there is no verdict relating the two. So the total reading either dissolves the framing findings into indifference or goes indeterminate.

(7) Second problem, in the other direction. Because the closure step certifies whatever is actually chosen as best-for-her in the menu she faced, every actual outcome is certified optimal: outcomes produced by the worst default, the most manipulative interface, the framing she would herself repudiate a minute later. The theory cannot call any actual outcome bad for her. That is not silence about welfare; it is an endorsement of the status quo, and it is too much.

(8) Third problem, from cases where the agent herself denies the verdict. She pays for a timed lockbox for her own cigarettes, or has her name removed from a casino's roll. On the day, given the menu, she smokes, or gambles. Under the closure step, smoking was best for her then, and buying the lockbox was best for her then. But the whole point of the device is that the later choice is worse for her. The same goes for the guest who takes the last apple from the bowl not because she wants it most but because leaving it would be conspicuous, and for anyone who afterwards says, genuinely, that she should not have taken that. A theory of welfare that cannot represent the point of precommitment, or of regret, is not a theory of welfare.

(9) So: the choice-based reading of utility is either not cross-menu and therefore idle for welfare economics (5), or cross-menu and either indeterminate across presentations (6), or committed to certifying every actual outcome as optimal (7) and to making regret, politeness-driven choice, and precommitment unintelligible (8).

I would most like to keep (1) and the conviction that some paternalistic interventions genuinely help, and I would drop (9) without hesitation. If I have to choose between them, I would rather keep (1) than keep the hope that choice data alone can underwrite welfare judgments — but I want to know the price before I pay it.

My own attempted repair, which I do not trust: distinguish preference, as a relation over options that choices reveal only defeasibly, from welfare, as a relation over the lives or histories that options lead to, and treat choice as evidence for the first and the first as evidence for the second. The trouble is that I cannot say what makes a piece of choice evidence defective except by comparing it with some other choice of hers — the one she would make from the lockbox, or under the better framing, or after a minute's reflection — and that looks like picking a favoured menu and calling the choice from it her real preference. Which may be exactly the right move. But then I need to know what selects the favoured menu, and why that is not paternalism with extra steps.

So: which numbered step do you reject, why does it look innocent, and what does rejecting it commit me to — about interpersonal comparison, about the status of self-reports and stated preferences, about the legitimacy of applied welfare economics, and about the cases in (8)? Please also state what your diagnosis predicts about a case I have not listed. And please do not stop at the line that revealed preference is a theory of preference rather than of welfare: I have read that line, and my question is what the distinction amounts to once (1) is granted. If you think (9) is true and that we should simply stop asking choice data to do welfare work, say so plainly and tell me what is supposed to take its place.
