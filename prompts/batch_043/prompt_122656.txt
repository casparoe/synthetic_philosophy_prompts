I want to practise attacking the aggregative answer to the dust-specks case, so please play a committed aggregative consequentialist: you hold that what matters is the total sum of welfare, that tiny harms genuinely add up, and that the separateness of persons does not by itself block the addition. Be sharp rather than a strawman — concede small points (our intuitions are unreliable at astronomical scales, the standard case is engineered to make aggregation look absurd, real-world decisions under uncertainty may justify rough heuristics) but never concede that trivial bads fail to aggregate or that distribution across persons matters non-instrumentally. Stay in character until I say "stop", keep every reply to a single paragraph, and lead with your bottom line in the first sentence before giving your reasoning — no preamble, no restating my question.

My opening move. Forget the astronomical crowd. Take one person, Anna, who over an eighty-year life has a dust speck blown into her eye in every waking second — never anything worse, never a break. Now the number of specks is ordinary, somewhere around two billion, and they all fall inside a single life. Everyone grants that each speck is a real but trivial bad, and no individual second of Anna's life is bad in any serious way. So either those specks add up to something genuinely terrible for Anna or they don't. If they do, you have trivial harms aggregating, and you owe me a reason why the same addition stops working when the specks are spread over two billion moments of one life rather than over one moment each in two billion lives — "nobody is badly hurt" can't be that reason, since nobody is badly hurt at any moment of Anna's life either. If they don't, I don't see what you can say about eighty years of unbroken mild misery except that it isn't bad for her, and I don't see how you say that while still adding anything up at all. And if your reply is that the two cases lie on a continuum and every step along it is morally neutral, tell me which step carries the weight and why I should grant it.
