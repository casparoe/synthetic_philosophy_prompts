In my philosophy class we've been reading Descartes, and my teacher keeps repeating "animals are machines" as if it settles things — she used it to brush off my question about whether my dog feels anything when it whimpers at the vet, and then again to explain why people once dissected live animals without anaesthetic. I'm sure one slogan can't do both of those jobs, but I can't say what exactly it does mean. Could you set out, as a list I could take to class, the distinct precise claims "animals are machines" might be making: that animals have no immortal soul, no feelings or conscious experience at all, no reason or language, or merely that their behaviour can be fully explained without attributing thoughts to them? I'm also stuck on the word "machine" itself: my teacher said that in Descartes' time machines meant clockwork and minds were supposed to be non-physical, so calling an animal a machine ruled out a mind by definition, whereas we now call computers machines and happily debate whether they might think — so is the slogan making any substantive claim at all today, or just inheriting its force from an older meaning of the word? For each reading, tell me whether it is trivial, defensible, or probably false, whether Descartes' two tests (that animals cannot use signs to express thoughts, and that they cannot act appropriately in every situation a body can face) actually establish it, and which reading, if any, would really support the claim that animals cannot feel pain — especially since our textbook says he denied them thought but not life, a distinction I didn't follow and which seems to matter here.
