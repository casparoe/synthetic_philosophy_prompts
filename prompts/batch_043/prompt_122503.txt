I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I'm working on a feature about Isaac Newton's theology. I need help with a passage from the General Scholium that Newton appended to the second (1713) edition of the Principia. The passage reads:

"This most beautiful system of the sun, planets, and comets, could only proceed from the counsel and dominion of an intelligent being. And if the fixed Stars are the centers of other like systems, these, being formed by the like wise counsel, must all be subject to the dominion of One. [...] This Being Governs all things, not as the soul of the world, but as Lord over all: And on account of his dominion he is wont to be called Lord God Παντοκράτωρ, or Universal Ruler.[1]"

I follow the surface argument: the order of the heavens points to an intelligent governor. What I don't understand is the contrast in the final sentence. In its early modern context, what would "the soul of the world" have meant to Newton's readers, and what exactly is he denying when he says God governs "not as the soul of the world, but as Lord over all"? Is he rejecting a Stoic or Neoplatonic world-soul, a pantheistic identification of God with nature, or something else? A related puzzle: it has been claimed that this passage indirectly signals Newton's anti-Trinitarianism, on the grounds that "Universal Ruler" pictures God as a single monarchical Lord rather than a triune deity. Is that a fair reading of the words themselves, or am I importing a later theological agenda that the passage will not bear? Please explain the concepts and the likely contrast without assuming I have read the Scholium in Latin or the surrounding theological polemics, and don't give me citations to chase.
