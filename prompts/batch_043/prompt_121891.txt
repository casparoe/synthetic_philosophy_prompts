I'm retired now — spent most of my career in experimental particle physics, mostly detector work, which meant an unreasonable amount of my life went into arguing about acceptance corrections and how much of what we saw was the physics and how much was the instrument. A couple of months ago I started sitting in on a philosophy of probability reading group at the university, which is my first philosophy since I was nineteen, and this week they did Bertrand's chord business and the principle of indifference. I came away thinking the whole thing is a muddle about which machine made the chords, but the people in the room clearly thought there was a real question there, and I couldn't see what it was. So here's what I want: take that debate and restate it, as honestly as you can, in the vocabulary I actually have, and then tell me what didn't survive the trip. The vocabulary is: a generating mechanism that produces a sampling distribution over data; the forward model versus the inverse problem of reading a mechanism back off the data; identifiability and degeneracy; nuisance parameters; symmetry groups and invariance under them; priors as model assumptions that go in alongside the likelihood; detector efficiency and resolution smearing. I'm fluent in all of that and in nothing philosophical — words like 'credence' and 'epistemic' I've only met in this reading group and they don't connect to anything for me. Don't bother with references or reading lists, I can't get at journal sites anyway.

Some of the translation I think I can already do myself, and I want you to check it and then go further. Take a circle of radius R and the usual question about whether a random chord beats the side of the inscribed triangle (that's R√3, if I have it right). The three textbook reciepies look to me like three different pieces of apparatus, not three answers to one question: an isotropic point source sitting on the rim, emitting in the plane, gives the 1/3 answer (track length = chord); a broad uniform-flux beam hitting a long cylindrical target side-on gives the 1/2 answer, because the impact parameter is uniform; and I cannot think of any machine that produces the 1/4 answer, where the chord's midpoint is uniform over the disk — that one smells like a coordinate choice rather than a mechanism, and I'd like to know if that's right or if I'm just unimaginative. And then I made up a fourth machine that isn't in the book: put the isotropic emitter at a uniformly random point *inside* the disk and let it fire in a random direction. Doing the integral badly on the back of an envelope I get something like 0.61 for that one, which is none of the three. I also computed the mean chord length for the three classical recipes and got three different numbers — 4R/π, 4R/3 and πR/2 — which if correct means the three aren't even describing the same measurable distribution and an afternoon in the lab would settle which apparatus you've got.

Here's where I want you to be careful, and it's the part I actually care about. I have a suspicion that if I do this translation I'm quietly helping myself to a thesis instead of translating, and I want to know where. Two places especially. First, I half remember that some physicist called Jaynes argued in the seventies that if you demand the answer be indifferent to where the circle is and how big it is you get a unique answer, the 1/2 one — but requiring invariance under a symmetry group is not the same thing as translating a claim, it's adding an assumption about what counts as the same problem, so I want to know whether that belongs in the translation or is a contestant. Second, and more important: in my vocabulary there is no such thing as no information at all. There is always some mechanism, even if it's badly known. So what I can't express is the thing the principle of indifference is supposed to deliver — a probability, or a degree of belief you're rationally required to have, in the total absence of a chance setup. Take the case I actually meet in real life: I know the beam profile is broad and smooth and roughly centred but I don't know its width, or my detector's efficiency depends on track length in a way I've only bracketed, so I can't tell from the data whether a shift came from the source or from the efficiency. That's a partly specified mechanism, and indifference or a prior is supposed to earn its keep there. Tell me what translates, what you had to fudge to fit, and what has no counterpart at all on either side — and then whether the residue is a hole in my way of talking or a sign that the philosophers' question was never as sharp as it looked.
