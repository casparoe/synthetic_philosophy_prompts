I build and release infrastructure for a living and have no philosophy background. A friend in grad school described two puzzles to me over dinner, and I want to know whether they are really saying something I don't already know from my day job. As I understood them: in a fair million-ticket lottery it seems reasonable to believe of each ticket that it will lose, yet you also know that exactly one ticket will win; and an author can reasonably believe every individual claim she makes in a book while also believing, from her track record, that the book surely contains at least one error. Both are supposed to be problems about whether reasonable belief can be combined.

Please restate each of them separately, in the vocabulary I actually use: per-check pass/fail gating, flaky tests and their observed flake rates, false-positive and false-negative rates, error budgets and service-level objectives, graded severities like warn versus fail, and the aggregation of many independent service checks into a single green build. Then give me the residue report: which parts map cleanly onto how I already reason about pipelines, which you had to distort to make them fit, and which have no counterpart in that vocabulary at all.

Two things I want in particular. First, my team is debating whether to auto-merge a deploy when each of roughly two hundred independent checks passes with 99.5% reliability; one engineer says this is just the lottery paradox and our confidence that this deploy is fine should collapse, another says the error-budget approach already accounts for it. Does either description survive your translation, or is the disagreement somewhere else? Second, we distinguish hard gates that block a merge from soft signals that only rank or annotate. Does that distinction do any work in these paradoxes, or is it orthogonal to what is actually at issue? If it is orthogonal, tell me what the real issue is, in terms I can test against my own systems.
