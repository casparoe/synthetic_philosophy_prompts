We have a small prediction market running in my local rationalist group on whether the Born rule is a genuine problem for the many-worlds interpretation, and I've been arguing the "no" side with a construction that I'd like stress-tested before I stake more on it.

The standard objection, as I understand it: if every outcome of a measurement really occurs, then the only thing left for a probability to track is how many branches there are, and a naive count weights every branch equally. A qubit prepared with amplitudes √3/2 and 1/2 along the measured axis would then be a 50/50 matter of self-locating uncertainty, which is empirically false. The usual Everettian replies — decision-theoretic derivations, envariance-style arguments, indifference over an infinite ensemble of copies — all seem to me to be doing something harder than necessary, and I want to know whether the shortcut below is legitimate.

My claim is that the counting objection gets its teeth only from treating a branch as an unanalyzed unit, when in fact a branch has internal structure that a uniform count throws away. Take the state above. Write the higher-amplitude branch as a uniform superposition over three orthogonal sub-branches, each carrying amplitude 1/2, and leave the lower-amplitude branch as a single branch of amplitude 1/2. The norm is preserved (four cells of squared modulus 1/4), and every cell now has the same weight. Counting cells then gives 3:1 for the higher-amplitude outcome, which is exactly |ψ|², and the empirical disagreement disappears. The 50/50 result was an artifact of coarse-graining, not a prediction of the theory. Generalize: always refine the sample space down to equal-amplitude cells, and let the weight of any coarse outcome be the number of cells it contains. On this view there is no extra postulate to add and no need to derive the Born rule from rational choice — the squared-amplitude measure is what you get when you count the structure that was already there.

My interlocutor raises two objections and I don't think either lands, but I want to be sure.

First, he says the refinement is available only when the squared amplitudes are commensurable, so the construction covers a measure-zero set of cases. I think this is answerable by a limit argument: the rationals are dense, so any real ratio can be approximated arbitrarily well by a sequence of commensurable ones, and the weights should converge.

Second, he says I have assumed what I was supposed to derive: I chose to split into three cells rather than two or seven, and I chose cells whose sizes are fixed by squared moduli rather than by moduli or by anything else, so the Born rule enters through the back door when I decide what the natural units are. This strikes me as question-begging on his part. Equal-amplitude cells are the natural units for exactly the reason that the construction works: any other refinement fails to reproduce the observed statistics, and a measure that gets the frequencies wrong isn't a candidate in the first place.

So, concretely:

1. Is the limiting argument for incommensurable amplitudes sound? Does appealing to the density of the rationals actually deliver a well-defined limit here, or does something break — continuity, uniqueness, the physical meaning of the approximating sequence?

2. Does the circularity objection have real force? If it does, I want the step identified precisely: where exactly does the construction presuppose the thing it is supposed to recover, and is that presupposition avoidable by a different choice of refinement?

3. My larger claim is that if the construction holds up, the Born rule problem is not substantive for many-worlds at all — that the literature treats it as deep only because it takes branches as given and then tries to attach probabilities to them from the outside, which is a mistake about what the wavefunction is. Is that right? If it isn't, I want to know what residual question my construction leaves completely untouched.

Please don't give me a literature review or pointers to papers; I can find those myself and I'd rather argue about the structure of the thing than about who said what. What I want to know is whether the construction is circular, and if it is, whether that circularity kills it or just relocates the assumption somewhere a many-worlds advocate is entitled to stand anyway.
