I have been going back and forth with a guy in a comment section for weeks about the torture versus dust specks thing — you know, is there some number of people each getting a speck of dust in the eye that adds up to something worse than one person being tortured for fifty years. I am on the no-amount-of-specks-beats-the-torture side, or at least I was. He has an argument I cannot find the hole in, but I am pretty sure it proves way too much, and I want to know exactly where to push back.

His setup: take a quantity of suffering big enough to equal fifty years of torture, call it Q. You can chop Q up and hand out tiny shares to a huge number of people. If there are enough people, each share is smaller than the smallest change in sensation a person can detect — nobody feels anything, nobody's day goes any differently, nobody could ever tell they were part of the experiment, and it doesn't matter to him whether the shares go to people alive at the same time or to people born centuries apart. Call that the smeared version.

Then:

(1) If two outcomes differ only in ways that nobody could possibly notice — nobody feels any different, nothing in anyone's life goes any differently — then neither outcome is worse than the other.
(2) So the smeared version is not worse than a world where nothing bad happens to anybody at all.
(3) But you can also get from the smeared version to the concentrated version, one person getting all of Q, by a very long sequence of steps, where each step moves one little sliver of suffering from one person onto one other person, and each sliver is far too small for either of the two people involved to notice.
(4) Each of those steps is morally neutral, by (1).
(5) No worse than carries across the steps: if A is no worse than B and B is no worse than C, then A is no worse than C. Run it along the whole chain and the smeared version and the concentrated version come out equally bad.
(6) So, combining with (2), one person being tortured for fifty years is not worse than nothing happening to anybody. Which is insane.

He agrees (6) is insane and says he does not need it. He says steps (1) through (5) already get him what he actually wants: run the chain the other direction, from a gigantic pile of specks to one person being tortured, and since nothing noticeable happens at any point along the way, the speck world and the torture world are equally bad — and with a big enough pile of specks there is more than enough suffering in it to cover fifty years of torture and then some.

So the argument gets him his conclusion and also gets him something he refuses to accept, and I do not think you can have it both ways, but I cannot say which step is the rotten one. Here is where my head is at:

- Step (1) sounds obviously right when I think of it as being about people, like nobody is made worse off. It sounds much less obviously right when it turns into a claim about outcomes, like the world isn't worse. Maybe that is the whole trick and maybe it isn't.
- Step (4) feels wrong too: too small to notice is not the same as weighs nothing. If each sliver actually weighs some tiny amount, then a chain of however many steps could weigh an awful lot in total. But then I don't see why that doesn't sink my side as well, since the only reason I ever wanted to say specks don't add up is that each one is unnoticeable. If I say unnoticeable harms still have weight, I seem to have become him.
- Something is bothering me about the middle of the chain. At some point in it some particular person is recieving something that is definately noticeable, and somebody has to end up being the one who gets all of Q. I can't turn that into an actual objection.
- And (2) needs a ridiculous number of people. If you hold the population at what we actually have, the shares stop being unnoticeable long before you get anywhere. Does that matter, or is he allowed to help himself to as many people as he needs?

What I care about keeping, in order: that torturing one person for fifty years is bad, and that there is something right about the thought that a zillion unnoticeable annoyances don't add up to something as bad as torture. I care much less about the exact version of that thought which says there is no number of specks that ever wins.

So: which step is doing the work, why did it look innocent, and what would each of the candidate rejections cost me? Pretend I am someone who has read a lot of forum posts about this and no philosophy papers. And please answer in American English.
