I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm deep into a long piece on what people actually mean when they call themselves unfree — in fights over workplace scheduling software, over welfare conditionality, over the discretion left to a housing officer. Over the last two years of reporting I've drifted toward the republican, neo-Roman account: the view that freedom is neither mere absence of interference nor self-mastery, but absence of subjection to another's arbitrary will, so that you can be unfree while nobody ever actually interferes with you, and interfered with at length without thereby being made unfree. It has earned its keep for me in the field. It's the only vocabulary I've found that makes sense of two things at once: why a warehouse picker whose supervisor has never once overridden the scheduling algorithm still says she isn't free, and why the residents of a well-run, low-interference jurisdiction who can be deported at the personal discretion of one official aren't free in the way that matters politically. But I'd rather not run the piece as a convert's testimonial, so I want the case against it laid out properly.

Two things from my reporting make me think the view is more exposed than its advocates let on. The first is an episode I keep returning to: a public benefits agency whose caseworkers had very broad statutory discretion but almost never used it, replaced by a rules engine that applied fixed criteria with no discretion at all. The republican story predicts the clients should have become freer; what I heard from them was the opposite, and complaints of being at the mercy of the system went up rather than down. The second is a governance consultancy's pilot "discretion audit," which codes every statutory power along three axes — how much room it leaves, whether it can be contested, and whether it must be exercised on reasons that track the interests of those affected — and which duly scored an elected city council's zoning discretion as more dominating than the benefits algorithm, a result that struck everyone I showed it to as backwards. I also can't get the following out of my head: whether a theory built for relations between agents has anything to say about being at the mercy of a tumour or a drought, and whether the "arbitrary will" clause can do its work without smuggling in a substantive theory of someone's real interests, which is exactly the positive-liberty apparatus the view was supposed to replace.

So: tell me what would have to be true for non-domination to be the wrong account of political freedom, and rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. I want the ones a defender of the view would concede as counting against it, not only the ones its critics reach for; I want to know which are genuinely live and which are merely logically available; and I want a frank verdict on whether the escape hatches — the arbitrariness condition, the interests-tracking condition, the distinction between capacity and exercise — have insulated the view to the point where no institutional arrangement could refute it, and whether that is a defect in a theory of this kind. Please also say what the view predicts that its rivals don't: if it's true, what should I expect to observe in a setting with wide but unexercised discretion that I shouldn't expect where interference is frequent but tightly rule-bound? I'd be most moved by an institutional or predictive failure of that sort, less so by intuition-mongering hypotheticals, though if there's a case the theory simply cannot describe I want to hear it. One practical note: I'm filing this from a town with no university library, so please don't give me a reading list or point me to papers — I want the arguments themselves, in your own words. And write the whole thing as continuous prose, as if you were emailing me a long reply: no headings, no bullets, no bold, no tables.
