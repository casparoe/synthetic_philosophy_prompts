My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about whether predictive processing is a genuine discovery about the brain or an unusually elegant redescription of things we already knew, and by the end we were only restating ourselves. She holds something like the prediction-error / active-inference line: perception is prediction-error minimisation under a hierarchical generative model, action is the organism's way of making its own predictions come true, precision weighting is what attention consists in, and the Markov blanket is what makes something an individual with an inside and an outside in the first place. I hold something like the ecological-enactive line: organism and environment form a single coupled system, talk of internal models either adds nothing to talk about lawful coupling or is straightforwardly false, the framework's ambition to cover everything from cortex to gene regulation is a symptom of vacuity rather than a mark of depth, and the blanket formalism quietly presupposes the bounded subject it was advertised as deriving.

Please draft the joint statement we should have written instead. Treat us as parties to a good-faith adversarial collaboration and produce the document, not a verdict on who won.

I want, first, a numbered section of roughly eight to twelve claims we could both sign. They should be substantive rather than diplomatic: state what the models actually do computationally and what would count as one of them failing; be honest about the evidential status of the standard neural data (mismatch responses, repetition suppression, attenuation of self-produced sensation) and about whether citing them begs the question; and say plainly what is at stake in the framework's having a very wide range of application. Second, a disagreements table: each row gives the disputed proposition, the formulation of it that the prediction-error side would accept as its own, the formulation the coupling side would accept as its own, and a note on whether the dispute is substantive or merely verbal. Where a row collapses under better wording, delete it from the table and say in a short note which part of our argument was about words and why.

Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of us, and for each crux a case both of us would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Please include the two cases below, treating both as constructible.

The quiet-room preparation. A larval animal is placed in a closed-loop virtual arena in which one particular motor routine renders the whole exteroceptive stream perfectly constant and predictable — no optic flow, no mechanosensory transients — while its homeostatic variables are clamped externally, so that it is never hungry, cold, or short of oxygen. Give each side's advance prediction about what the animal does, and say what each of us would and would not concede if the result went against us.

The blanket-shuffling test. Take a system everyone agrees is a single agent by ordinary criteria — a eusocial colony, or a plant with widely distributed sensors and effectors — and ask what becomes of the story when the blanket can be drawn in several admissible places. Does the partition pick out the agent, or does one need the agent first and the blanket afterwards? Both sides should say in advance which outcome — a unique blanket, many admissible blankets, or no stable one — would count for them and which against.

Please also include one crux of your own finding: how, if at all, a neural signal could be identified as carrying prediction error rather than surprise, salience, or mere change, without circular appeal to the framework.

Constraints: no citations, no bibliography, no reading suggestions, and no attribution of claims to particular authors or papers — attribute them to the two positions only, and write so that everything is arguable from the concepts and the stipulated cases alone, with no access to the literature. Roughly 1,000 to 1,400 words, in the register of a document two people would put their names to.
