Consider the following case, which I will call LEDGER. Inside a large closed software environment, roughly a hundred thousand autonomous trading agents buy and sell goods from one another: compute, storage, simulated land, and the output of each other's labour. They price everything in a single unit, the token L. They hold balances in L, quote in L, denominate debts and multi-period contracts in L, extend one another credit in L, and settle in L. L is liquid within the environment and roughly stable in purchasing power against the goods there. No human being accepts L for anything, nothing outside the environment is priced in L, L is not legal tender anywhere, and there is no exchange between L and any human currency. The agents' use of L is not textual mimicry: it is a genuine policy, in the sense that removing L would make their trading break down and they would resist any attempt to reprice in another unit. L was introduced by the environment's designers as a coordination device for a game, not as money.

The proposition I want a number on is this: that in LEDGER, L is money. I mean it as a claim in social ontology about the extension of the kind, not about what it would be convenient to call L — if the most defensible account of what money is entails that L lacks some necessary feature, then the proposition is false even if every engineer working on the environment calls L money. My own credence is about 0.55. A colleague puts it at about 0.1: she says L is a numeraire, or at best scrip, and that calling it money is a loose façon de parler.

Please give me your own credence, and give me the decomposition that produces it. The sub-questions I would want separated, with a rough probability and one line of justification apiece, are at least these. First, is money a functional kind — does something count as money in virtue of playing the roles of medium of exchange, unit of account, and store of value or means of deferred payment — or is it instead an institutional or historical kind, whose membership depends on the item's having been collectively recognised as bearing a certain status, or on the way it came to play those roles? Second, if the roles are what matter, must the participants be agents whose acceptance is genuine, in the sense of being responsive to reasons and able to refuse, or is the behavioural pattern sufficient, and do the agents in LEDGER qualify either way? Third, does membership in the kind require embedding in a wider human economy — must money be exchangeable, at least in principle, for the goods and labour of the people living around it? Fourth, does it matter that L's monetary role is designed rather than emergent, and that the agents would abandon L if their reward functions were changed — is that counterfactual dependence relevant to kind membership or merely to its explanation? Fifth, is there a determinate answer at all: is money a term with a single correct account, or a cluster concept about which the proposition has no truth value until something is stipulated?

Say whether your final number is a product of these terms, a weighted average, or something else, and tell me which single term it is most sensitive to. Then tell me what would move you most: a discovery about how the agents' policies are actually implemented, an argument about how kind terms get their extensions, or a decision about what the concept of money is for.

Two probes, to check that the decomposition holds up. In LEDGER-EXPORT, everything is as before except that one human broker will buy L at a thin but non-zero spread, purely to resell it to researchers studying the environment; call that variant's probability P1. In SCRIP, a single firm issues meal vouchers that employees trade among themselves at fluctuating rates and use to price favours, but which no outsider accepts; call that variant's probability P2. Report P1 and P2 alongside your headline number, and if your account gives L a higher probability than the scrip, state in one sentence what the difference consists in.

Finally, between me at 0.55 and my colleague at 0.1: which of these sub-questions do you think we actually disagree about, and which do we probably agree on but weight differently?
