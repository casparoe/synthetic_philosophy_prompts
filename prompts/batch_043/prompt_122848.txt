A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about Christopher Nolan's film The Prestige, and we reached a point where neither of us could say anything the other found responsive. He holds that it is a period melodrama about obsession and revenge, and that the machine Angier commissions from Tesla is simply a stage illusion that happens to be real, with a body count in the final reel. I hold that the machine is, formally, a generator of self-locating uncertainty, and that the film therefore takes up a position inside the Sleeping Beauty dispute — a position my friend thinks nobody could sincerely hold, and which, he adds, would make Angier's behaviour unintelligible rather than merely monstrous. I would like a reading that tells me which of us is using the film and which is using it as a clothes horse for a puzzle he happens to like.

The terms I put to him were these. In the Sleeping Beauty case a coin is tossed; if it lands heads you are woken once, if tails twice, with the memory of the first waking erased; on waking, unable to tell which day this is, how confident should you be in heads? Those who answer one third do so because there are twice as many subjectively identical wakings on the tails branch, so a principle of indifference over centred worlds favours tails; those who answer one half say you have learned nothing you did not already know and so should not move. The dispute, as I understand it, is really about whether extra candidate-selves have extra credence-weight.

The film's mechanics, as the plot spells them out, put the question on stage. Angier's machine leaves the original where he stands and produces a duplicate at a distance; Angier rigs the staging so that the original drops through the floor into a locked tank and drowns while the duplicate reappears elsewhere in the theatre and takes the bow. Every performance adds another submerged corpse to the cellar, and the closing shot travels down the ranks of these tanks. And the dying Angier confesses that he is no longer sure who he is.

So: is this a self-locating puzzle at all? The film's arrangement is not symmetric — one candidate is designated to die — and one might argue that where there is a designated victim, there is a further fact about who survives, and the puzzle dissolves into ordinary ignorance about a mechanism. I would like you to say whether the reading needs the machine to be a case of symmetric fission, whether the film gives us that, and what changes if we stipulate it.

Then the part I could not get my friend to engage with. Suppose the machine has two settings, equally likely and undetectable from inside: on the low setting it produces one duplicate, so two candidates exist and one of them drowns; on the high setting it produces four, so five exist and one drowns. Angier runs it once and finds himself alive, taking the bow. I got either four fifths or eight thirteenths depending on whether the extra candidates are weighted before or after conditioning on survival; my friend insists the answer is one half, because he says there was always going to be somebody taking the bow and so nothing has been learned. I would like you to settle the arithmetic, say which of these answers is the distinctively Sleeping-Beauty one, and — this is the part I care about — say which one Angier is implicitly relying on when he commits himself to a run of a hundred performances. Note the pressure point: there is guaranteed to be a survivor with all of his memories after every show, so in one ledger Angier cannot lose; there are a hundred corpses, so in another he loses a hundred times over. Is the film's horror located precisely in the gap between those two ledgers, and does that make it a contribution to the debate rather than an illustration of it?

I would also like you to read Borden against Angier. The twins share a single identity, matching each other's mutilations, each living half of one life, and his wife senses that something about him changes from day to day without being able to say what. Is their arrangement the dramatised refusal to count extra centres — the anti-thirder stance — and does the film reward or punish them for it?

Finally, be honest about where the reading fails. Where does treating the film as a Sleeping Beauty case distort it, what does the film know that the puzzle literature does not, and which two or three scenes would you put in front of a seminar to make the point?
