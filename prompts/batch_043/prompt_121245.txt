Hi — I'm a retired geochemist, spent about thirty years doing mass-balance and residence-time calculations on groundwater systems, and since I packed it in I've been going to a philosophy reading group at the local library. We're on population ethics at the moment and I keep having the reaction I used to have in department meetings: everybody is making a stocks-and-flows argument without ever writing down the control volume. Half of the pronatalist material I read is about the *size* of the population — national scale, more brains means more ideas, the old 'more people means more innovation' line — and half of it is obviously about *structure*: who pays the pensions, who does the caring. Those are seperate problems, and as far as I can tell the pension one is really a one-off transition cost rather than anything about the steady state, but the two get run together constantly. The other thing I can't let go of: nobody ever says *whose* welfare is supposed to go up. The extra child can't be the beneficiary in any straightforward sense, because the child you have once you've been offered a baby bonus isn't the child you would have had otherwise — so nothing was done *for* that child — which means the benefit, if there is one, accrues to the rest of us. That makes the child infrastructure. I said as much to the group and they told me to get it out of my system, so here we are.

So what I want is a piece of satire. Please take the role of a professional philosopher who specialises in population ethics and who also, privately, writes very dry policy satire — think the most competent person in the room at a ministry consultation. Think carefully before you start, spend a few lines brainstorming which *real* feature of this literature each joke is exaggerating, and don't invent a feature that isn't there: I mean the repugnant conclusion and the mere addition paradox, the person-affecting restriction and the fact that 'we owe it to future generations' is a strange thing to say to people whose existence depends on whether we say it, critical-level views that are trying to have both more people *and* good lives, and above all the way 'average' worries about congestion, wages and housing get quietly swapped for 'total' worries about national scale whenever the swap is convenient. The piece itself is an internal memo from the Office of Demographic Continuity, a government body under a statutory mandate to "maintain and where possible increase the national population," proposing a package of five measures. Deadpan civil-service voice throughout: everything costed, everything reasonable-sounding, nothing ever conceded, nobody ever breaks character to wink. Push real pronatalist logic exactly one step further than it wants to go — children as public infrastructure with a depreciation schedule, a statutory critical level of expected lifetime welfare below which a birth is logged as a demographic-neutral event rather than an addition, tradable birth credits so the childless can buy demographic compliance from large families, and the Office's flat insistence that the people in question be domestically produced even though its own spreadsheet says admitting them would be cheaper. Keep the internal logic consistent: the memo should never slide into an argument its authors would reject.

Format: exactly five numbered recommendations, each two or three sentences, no title, no preamble, no sign-off, no conclusion — just the five. Each one should be performing a different axiological slide, so that afterwards someone who knows the literature can point at a line and say which of the total, average, or person-affecting framings is doing the work there. Somewhere around 300 to 400 words all told is right; a made-up line from the founding statute or an invented unit of measurement is very welcome if it helps.
