I run a volunteer "big ideas" half hour at the community centre where I work — mostly other staff, a couple of drivers, some of the admin people, one guy who writes code, noone with any philosophy or psych background — and next month I want to try the idea from Hofstadter and Sander's Surfaces and Essences that analogy isn't the fancy thing you do on purpose now and then, it's literally what categorizing is: every time you see something as a something you're making a tiny analogy you don't notice, and a concept is basically the scar tissue left behind by a pile of analogies nobody remembers making. I've been chewing on this on my own for months and I think I get it, but I have no idea how to hand it to somebody else in thirty minutes with a whiteboard and no slides.

So don't explain it to me — plan how I explain it.

What has to land first, and why. My instinct is to open with an "A is to B as C is to D" puzzle, because that's what everyone in the room already thinks analogy means, but I worry that starting there bakes in the wrong idea and I spend the rest of the half hour digging out of it. Would you open with it and then break it, or start somewhere else entirely and bring the puzzle in later as a thing to be suspicious of? I want the dependency logic, not just an order.

The carrying example. I want one example that does the whole session, and I want it to not be a word or a phrase or a metaphor, because the second I use a language example everybody concludes this is just about how words get stretched and I've lost them. So it needs to be a case where the two things share no visible features and no obvious story in common, and where what carries over is a pattern of relationships rather than objects or properties. I have a candidate I like and am definitely too attached to: a forklift driver who's never used a spreadsheet being shown one and within a minute calling the columns rows and then going hunting for the thing that does what a filter does. Tell me if that can carry the session, what it's missing, and what I need to have ready for when it doesn't fire.

The misconceptions, in the order they'll actually show up, and the point in the sequence where each one gets taken on. The ones I'm braced for: (a) the shrug version — so analogy is just a literary device and you're telling me it's more common than I thought; (b) "we think in metaphors" — one of the admin crowd read something about that years ago and will be very pleased to have already known this; (c) the coder, who will say something like so it's basically similarity in a feature space, nearest neighbours, and I dont actually know why that's wrong and I'd like to before I'm standing up there; (d) if literally everything is an analogy then nothing is and you can't ever be wrong, which I think is the serious one and I want it to have teeth rather than me just nodding and agreeing it's a fair point.

Where the bit about concepts stretching to fit a new situation goes — I think they call it slippage? — and how I get people to see the stretch as the productive part and not as an error, without it sliding into anything-goes.

What I should flatly refuse to try to cover in thirty minutes and say out loud that I'm skipping, including what not to get pulled into if the coder pushes.

And how I'll know it landed. Not "does anyone have questions". My idea is to end by giving them something genuinely unfamiliar and asking them to extend it somehow, but I don't trust my own instinct about what counts as getting that right versus just being agreeable, or what I'd even do with the answers I get.
