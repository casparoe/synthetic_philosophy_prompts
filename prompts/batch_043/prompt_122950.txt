My thesis, which you should attack, is that the money pump smuggles in an execution model and then reports a bug in the data.

Concretely. Take an agent who prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A. The pump starts the agent at A, offers C for a penny, then B for a penny, then A for a penny; the agent ends where it began, three pennies poorer, and the cycle can be repeated indefinitely. I concede the cost. What I deny is the diagnosis. The agent pays only because at every state it re-runs its choice procedure from scratch against the local menu, the way an interpreter re-evaluates an expression at each call. An agent that instead compiles once — that selects a plan over the entire decision tree at the outset and then executes it without re-deciding, re-planning only when an option arrives that was not in the original tree — pays nothing at all. On that architecture the cycle is inert. So the pump is, on my view, a reentrancy bug in the execution model: it shows that re-deciding at every node is costly when preferences are cyclic, not that cyclic preferences are irrational. Rationality is a property of the program; cost is a property of the runtime; and the pump only turns because you insist on running the slow runtime.

Here is my opening move. You say the pump exposes an incoherence; I say it exposes a scheduling policy. To get from "this agent bleeds money under repeated local re-evaluation" to "this agent is irrational," you need a premise I do not grant: that the local, re-deciding architecture is the one rationality is answerable to in the first place. Supply that premise, or produce an argument that does without it — one that still bites against an agent who compiles a plan once and holds to it, who resolves in advance never to accept any of the penny trades, and who is under no illusion about what it would choose if it ever re-decided from scratch.

Second, no definitional stops — do not tell me that rationality just means having a coherent utility function, and do not tell me that no real person has cyclic preferences; I am asking what the norm is for, and I want the argument rather than the label.
