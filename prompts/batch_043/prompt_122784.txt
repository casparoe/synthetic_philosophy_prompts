I'm in a small reading group on decision theory — two economists, a cognitive scientist, a philosopher, and me — and last week one of the economists made an argument that seems to dissolve the bounded-rationality debate rather than win it, and I can't tell whether I should be convinced. His version of it: take the standard sequential search problem, the kind where you inspect one apartment per weekend at some cost, can't go back to an apartment you've already turned down, and have to be moved in by September. The optimal policy, he pointed out, already looks exactly like satisficing: compute a reservation utility from the distribution of offers, the inspection cost, and the number of weekends remaining, then take the first apartment that clears it. Satisficers stop at the first acceptable option too; they just call the threshold an aspiration level and set it less carefully. So either satisficing thresholds coincide with the optimizing reservation value — in which case Simon's program is true but empty, a redescription of what the optimizing model was already doing — or they don't, in which case satisficers are simply making errors that the optimizing model can measure, predict, and explain. Either way, he says, there is no room left for a distinct normative or explanatory notion.

He also has a second argument that I find harder to shake, which I've started calling his collapse lemma: for any satisficing rule with a fixed aspiration level and any environment, there is some prior over the options and some cost of inspection for which that rule is exactly the optimal stopping policy — so every satisficer is an optimizer in disguise, and satisficing has no content of its own. His conclusion is that the only genuinely interesting question left is empirical: how far do people's actual aspiration levels sit from the reservation values an optimizing model would prescribe? I want to resist both arguments, but I can't locate my resistance precisely. My own attempt: the shape of a stopping rule and the procedure that generates the threshold are different things. Simon's aspiration level is supposed to drift with experience — up after a run of good options, down after frustration — and it doesn't obviously require options to be rankable along one shared scale, whereas the optimizer's reservation value is read off a distribution and the remaining horizon and falls as the deadline approaches. Is that distinction doing any real work, or is it only the observation that people set their thresholds by a different, worse method?

To test it I tried to build a case where the satisficer seems to me to do something sensible and the optimizing machinery can't even get started: I've just moved to a city where roughly half the places I'll look at are of a type I have no experience with — converted industrial lofts with strange layouts — so I can't put a single number on an option before I see it, and my sense of what counts as good keeps shifting as I see more of them. My instinct is that an adaptive, multi-dimensional aspiration level is well suited to this and that a reservation utility isn't even defined here, but I also worry that I've just redescribed a misspecified-model problem in the vocabulary of a sympathy for Simon. So: is my economist friend's argument, in either version, a fair way of framing the issue, or does it rest on a conflation I should be able to name? And does anything in this neighborhood actually license the stronger claim I keep meeting in popular summaries — that deliberately ignoring information can make you a better predictor than using all of it — given that an ideal agent can always simply throw information away?
