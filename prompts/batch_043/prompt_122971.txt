I tune pianos for a living and run a fortnightly philosophy evening in the back room of a second-hand bookshop, eight or nine of us, none with a philosophy degree, working through whatever has been nagging at whoever is buying the coffee. Last month we spent two hours on the surprise exam paradox and it has not let go of me. I ran my own version on the group: at the first of six sessions I announced that I would spring an unannounced short reading on them at some earlier session than the last, and that they would not be able to work out which one in advance. One member said at once that if we can rule out the last session we can rule out the one before it, and so back down to the first, so the announcement must be nonsense; I sprang the reading at session three and everyone, including her, was genuinely surprised. What I cannot stop chewing on is that the proof and the surprise both feel perfectly real to me simultaneously: each step of the argument sounds airtight as it goes, and yet there the reading was. Someone with an actual degree muttered something to me about sentences of the form it will rain tomorrow but you do not know that, and about an announcement that is supposed to be true and yet unusable, but he was vague and I would rather have the thing shown to me than described.

So I want to close the next session with a poem I can read aloud, and I have got it into my head that it should be a sestina. Set it in the ordinary classroom version: a teacher tells her class one morning that there will be exactly one exam the following week and that they will not know which day it is until the morning of it. Give me the six end-words as the five weekdays plus the word know, and let the word surprise appear exactly once in the whole poem, as the very last word. I would like the first five stanzas to take the week apart backwards in the way the students argument does, Friday first and then Thursday and so on down to Monday, while the sestina's fixed rotation keeps dragging the day that has just been eliminated back to the end of a line; do not hide that, let the poem feel the pressure of it. The sixth stanza can be whatever is left once the proof has eaten its own week. Then in the three-line envoi let the exam actually land on Wednesday, a day the poem crossed off in its third stanza, and end on surprise. I would like it in the teacher's voice, and I would rather the poem never tells us outright whether she was bluffing, whether she knew what the students would conclude, or whether her announcement was even coherent.

Two things I care about, since it has to survive being read out loud to eight people with coffee. First, accuracy of a particular kind: someone who has actually worked through the paradox should be able to tell from the poem which step it treats as the load-bearing and slightly fishy one, whether that is the first step off the back of the week or the slide from I cannot deduce the day to I cannot be surprised by it, and I would rather it locate the crack than wave at self-reference and call it a day; someone who has never heard of the paradox should still just hear a poem about a teacher, a class, and a week that does not behave. Second, sound: loose iambic pentameter, nothing sing-song, and please no word shoved into a line-end for the sake of the rotation. Thirty-nine lines including the envoi, and I will be reading it slowly, so it needs to work in the mouth as well as on the page.
