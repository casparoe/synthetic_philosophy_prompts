Hi — I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I file two or three pieces a week off single papers, usually around 800 words, usually with about a day between the embargo lifting and my deadline. Most of what crosses my desk lately is modelling work: some formal or computational model of households, or cells, or a climate subsystem, or people on a network, and then a headline claim on top of it. And i keep hitting the same wall.

The models obviously rest on assumptions that everyone in the field knows are false — fully optimizing agents, well-mixed populations, costless adjustment, no measurement error, one representative everything — and the standard reply, in the Friedman vein, is that unrealistic assumptions are fine, even a sign of a good theory, as long as the predictions come out right. Fine in principle. But I have to decide, in ten minutes, without reading the appendix and mostly without being able to follow the math, whether the false assumptions in *this* paper are the harmless kind or the kind where the headline result is basically baked into the setup before anyone runs anything.

I've got a rough distinction I half-use and can't apply reliably, and I want it turned into something I can actually run. Four moving parts, as I see them:

1. Load-bearing vs. scaffolding. Some assumptions are there to make the thing solvable; others are doing the actual causal work in producing the result. My test so far is: would flipping the assumption to roughly its opposite flip or kill the result? But I can almost never tell that from the paper, so I need something readable off the text.

2. Direction of error. Every unrealistic assumption errs in some direction, not just "some amount". If the direction it errs in is the same direction as the paper's headline claim — a model that assumes adjustment is frictionless and cheap, finding that a policy is cheap — then part of the result looks built in rather than discovered.

3. Target-matching. Is the model meant to be about one specific real system (a prediction), about a whole class of systems (a mechanism), or only a demonstration that something *could* happen (a how-possibly claim)? I keep turning possibility results into "here's why X happens" stories and my editor catches maybe half of them.

4. Laundered assumptions. Some of these things are just field conventions that get repeated from paper to paper until the intro reads as if they were measured. Twenty years of "assume homogeneous mixing" and it stops sounding like an assumption. I can't tell the conventions from the findings, and I'm wary of my procedure just rubber-stamping whatever a subfield always does.

What I want from you: a short decision procedure I can run on a PDF in ten minutes. Questions in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure stops with a no; and where it hands the decision back to me or my editor because it's a judgment call rather than something the paper settles.

Constraints on what I can actually check: the abstract, the intro's framing of what the model is for, the model section's structure and headings, the tables, and anything the paper says about robustness, extensions, relaxing assumptions, calibration or sensitivity. If I have a day I can email the author one question. What I can't do: read the math, verify whether other groups relaxed the assumption, or chase up references — so please don't give me a reading list or point me at papers, I won't be able to follow them.

The output should end in three things: one of a few concrete hedging levels I can hand my editor; a single sentence of plain English describing the assumption that I paste into the piece somewhere; and the one question actually worth emailing.

Then run it on three cases, including at least one where I won't like the answer:

— A macro-ish paper using a dynamic model with fully optimizing forward-looking households and frictionless reallocation, finding that a large new subsidy roughly pays for itself within a decade. Great story, my editor wants it, I want it, and I suspect your procedure tells me the answer I don't want.

— A compartmental disease model with homogeneous mixing and no behavioural response, projecting a wildlife pathogen under two management options two years out, where the projected numbers differ a lot between the options and the state agency is deciding next month.

— A minimal model of social learning where agents copy neighbours more when the neighbours are similar to them, finding that even mild similarity bias produces near-total fragmentation. I want to write "here's why we're so polarized." I have a feeling this is a how-possibly result that is one step short of being true by construction, but I can't say why.

Finally: be straight with me about what the procedure can't do. The cases it can't settle, the ways it'll misfire (I'm defintely worried about it being lenient toward conventions just because they're conventional, and about me confusing "I don't get it" with "the assumption is doing work"), and the point where over-hedging stops being honesty and starts costing me stories that were fine. Where do I just have to make a call about what readers deserve?
