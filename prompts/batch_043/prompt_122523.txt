My father died in March, eight years into a book he had been writing: a substantial manuscript, perhaps three-quarters finished, by someone who was not famous and whose book nobody was waiting for. As the executor of his estate I keep encountering the same reaction from everyone I deal with — his old department, two editors who have looked at the papers, his friends, and, to my surprise, me. Everyone treats the fact that he spent eight years on the thing as a strong reason for someone to finish it and publish it, and treats the alternative, leaving it in a box, as wasteful or disloyal in a way that seems to go beyond the loss of a possibly good book. The pull appears to scale with how much of himself he put in: nearer to completion, yes, but also more so the more the years cost him. It is not only that a good book might come of it. People would be disappointed in me if I let it go, and I would be disappointed in myself, and I cannot say what that disappointment is about.

The same people who feel this are, in their professional lives, entirely committed to the doctrine that sunk costs are rationally irrelevant: a grant is renewed on its prospective promise, a failing project is shut down, the estate itself is to be valued at what it will fetch, in which his eight years of labour count as nothing at all. So the practice seems to run directly against the rule I have always taken to define rational choice in these matters, and I would like to know whether it is really in conflict with it or only looks that way.

Four features of the practice seem to me to be the ones a reconstruction has to fit, though you may think I have described them wrongly:

1. The reason appears to be tied to the person and not merely to the work. No one proposes that an equally advanced, equally good manuscript abandoned in a drawer by a living stranger be completed at institutional expense; if someone did, we would think it eccentric. Yet the state of the two manuscripts and the value of the two finished products could be the same.

2. It appears to matter who finishes it and in what spirit. The natural candidate is a former student who knew what he was after and who will try to write what he was trying to write, not the best available writer improving the book into the best book it could be. If the point were the value of the completed product, any competent completion would do, and a better one would be better.

3. In his last year he seems to have lost faith in the book and told two or three people he would never finish it. I find this hardly dents the pull at all. If the reason were fidelity to what he wanted, it ought to destroy it.

4. If I leave it in a box, it is hard to say who is wronged. He is not there to be harmed, disappointed, or benefited; the scholarship loses little; his family is not distressed by the box.

So: what view of reasons, or of value, or of agency, does this practice only make sense under? Reconstruct it as charitably as you can, and tell me which parts of it the practice strictly presupposes and which it merely sits well with. I would especially like to know whether several quite different theories — something about the completional value of works, something about shared or extended agency, something about what we owe the dead, something merely about the good it does the living — would each license the same practice, and if so what would settle which one is actually operating. And I want to know whether the conflict with the sunk-cost rule is genuine, or whether the practice quietly redescribes the good so that the past investment is not sunk at all — a book with that history, by that person, finished in that way, being a different thing from the same text produced another way. Finally, tell me what would have to change if we replaced whatever the practice assumes with a strictly prospective view that counted only the value of completed products: would we then fund the anonymous manuscript, welcome anonymous completion, and stop caring about fidelity — and would anything of real value be lost by doing so? I should add that I find the suggestion that the dead have interests obscure, and I would prefer an account that does without it; but if the practice cannot in the end do without it, say so and say why.
