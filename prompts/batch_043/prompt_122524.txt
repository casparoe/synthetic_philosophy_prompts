I direct the evaluation unit of a government behavioural insights team. We run roughly twenty field trials a year — changing defaults, the wording and ordering of forms, the salience of information, and the friction attached to a decision — across workplace pension enrolment, organ donor registration, energy tariff switching, attendance at cancer screening, and high cost short term credit. For each trial I must issue a one page welfare finding: a statement, defensible to our board, to the department that commissioned the trial, and if necessary to a parliamentary committee, of whether the people affected were made better off. I have three analysts, around six weeks per trial, and the finding has to be reproducible in the sense that a second analyst working from the same data reaches the same verdict.

The constraint that makes this hard is a rule our board adopted, which I will call the Choice Rule. It requires that (a) any finding about whether people are better off be grounded in what those people actually chose among the options actually available to them; (b) self reported life satisfaction, happiness, or stated willingness to pay not be used as evidence, though it may be quoted as context; and (c) where the evidence does not settle the matter, the finding say so rather than fill the gap. Clause (b) was adopted because the board distrusted wellbeing questionnaires, not because anyone had worked through what a choice based notion of being better off actually commits us to.

We have been applying the rule by instinct, and it is producing findings I cannot defend. So I want a procedure my analysts can actually run.

My own sense of the problem is this. In the revealed preference tradition, utility is not a feeling or a quantity of satisfaction; it is a numbering of the bundles a person picks, and it is pinned down only up to any relabelling that preserves the ordering. Being better off on that view comes to something like: reaching a bundle that was affordable before and was not taken. That licenses a verdict only for a single person, over a stable set of options at unchanged prices, and only in the weak form at least as well off — never as a magnitude, and never as a total across people. Almost none of that survives contact with what we do. Our interventions typically work by changing presentation or effort cost rather than the feasible set; several work by changing what people believe rather than what they want; and every finding we issue is about a population.

What I want from you is the translation. Give me the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure stops with no welfare finding possible, and the points at which it hands the question to a named person because it is a question of values rather than of fact — and tell me what the one page finding should say in each of those cases. It has to handle at least the following.

Defaults and framing, where the feasible set and the money prices are untouched but behaviour shifts substantially: does the procedure require reversal to count as evidence, does sustained non reversal over a follow up period suffice, and does it matter that a default carries an implicit recommendation from the state?

Friction, where every option remains formally available but one becomes harder to take — a mandatory delay, an extra form, a phone call instead of a click. My analysts are split. Converting hassle into a price equivalent makes the procedure silent about nearly every tool we have; refusing to convert it makes the procedure treat our most effective interventions as though they cost nothing. Tell me which way the procedure should go and what each choice costs us.

Information and debiasing, where an intervention corrects a mistaken belief about the terms of a product. Post intervention choices are choices; pre intervention ones were made against a budget set the person misdescribed. Say what the procedure does here and why.

Cycles and time inconsistency, where people enrol and then lapse, or reverse themselves when the same pair of options is presented in a different order. One analyst proposes that we compute an Afriat style number: roughly, how much of each person's budget one would have to discount before their choices could be described as maximising anything at all, with a threshold used as a pass fail gate. Tell me whether that is a good idea, what such a number would actually be measuring, and how I should read it when it comes out at something like 0.9.

Adaptive and defensive choice, where the choice itself looks like a product of the deprivation — the underserved group that does not complain, the long term user of a harmful product who reports liking it. Clause (b) stops us from asking; it does not tell us whether to read the behaviour as evidence or as a symptom.

Aggregation, since every finding we issue is about a group that contains both gainers and losers. If the theory's currency is one person's ranking, the procedure has to say what it does at the step from persons to populations, and mark that step as owned by the board rather than by us.

Then run it on three of our trials. First: a switch from opt in to opt out enrolment in a workplace pension, moving enrolment from 41 to 87 per cent, with twelve months of follow up showing a low and flat opt out rate, and with no data on whether participants offset by cutting other saving or consumption. Second: a rewritten invitation letter that raises cervical screening attendance in a persistently low attendance group from 34 to 39 per cent, with no change in the feasible set, no follow up data, and a live reading on which attendance expresses anxiety rather than preference. Third, the one where I expect to dislike the answer: a mandatory twenty four hour cooling off period on high cost short term credit that reduced borrowing by about a third in the treated group. Our prior is that this is a clear welfare gain, while a strict choice based view looks likely to return either silence or a loss, since the option became harder to take and those who persisted paid in time and hassle. I want to see what the procedure honestly returns, and how that gets written up for a board that wants the opposite conclusion.

Finally, say plainly what the procedure cannot do. I need to know which parts of the mandate it can never satisfy — interpersonal sums, magnitudes, the intensity of a gain — and whether the deeper question, whether better off should be read as preference satisfaction at all rather than as something thicker, is one the procedure can flag but never settle. Write it so that an analyst with a good undergraduate training in economics or psychology and none in this literature can run it in the time we have, and mark every step where the answer depends on a judgement I have to make rather than one the data make.
