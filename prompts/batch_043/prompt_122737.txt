Last spring I was appointed to the board of a small family foundation whose corpus is large enough to matter to a handful of causes and small enough that no one on the board has professional training in this kind of decision. Our live question is whether to spend most of it down over the next decade or to preserve it and give only at the required annual minimum. Two concrete options are on the table: roughly a decade of grants to a well-measured near-term global health charity, or a transfer into a patient philanthropic vehicle that will hold and disburse on a horizon of fifty years or more, when both the recipients and the state of the world are largely unknown to us.

Trying to think about this seriously, I ran into the cluelessness objection to consequentialism: because our actions feed into chaotic causal systems, our credences over their long-run effects are so diffuse that we cannot tell whether any given act makes things better or worse overall. I have read the textbook presentations, and I do not need them repeated. What I do not understand is the fine structure of the objection, and I have been unable to work it out on my own.

In particular, I want to understand a distinction I have started using privately and am not sure is coherent. Call it the difference between level-cluelessness and difference-cluelessness. Level-cluelessness is cluelessness about the total long-run value of an option taken on its own. Difference-cluelessness is cluelessness about the value of one option relative to another. It seems to me that most of the force of the standard argument comes from level-cluelessness, while what my board actually has to decide is a difference. And it also seems to me that the two can come apart in both directions: much of the chaotic background is shared between the options and should cancel in the contrast, and yet because the options eventually branch into different causal trajectories, the difference itself eventually becomes a chaotic quantity whose sign we cannot estimate. I would like that tension explained: when, and on what grounds, does taking a difference rescue us from cluelessness, and when does chaotic divergence reintroduce it at the level of the difference itself?

There is a related move I would like examined. One of my fellow trustees argues as follows: for effects beyond some horizon, we are about as likely to be right as wrong about the sign, so those effects contribute roughly nothing to expected value, and we should therefore decide on the near-term effects we can actually measure, which favours spending down now. I suspect this inference is invalid, because being roughly indifferent about the sign of a quantity is not the same as that quantity having an expected contribution of about zero, and because the magnitudes involved may be distributed in a heavy-tailed and asymmetric way. But I also suspect my co-trustee is onto something that I cannot quite state correctly, since truncating our attention at the point where our estimates stop carrying information may still be the right decision procedure even if it is not the right account of what makes an act better or worse. I have been calling the point at which our estimates stop being correlated with outcomes the correlation horizon, and I would like to know what happens to the expected contribution of effects beyond that horizon, and whether that notion can bear any weight.

Please explain, as precisely as you can, the conceptual mechanics here: why sign-uncertainty does or does not collapse into a zero expected contribution; whether the criterion of rightness and the decision procedure can come apart in the way I have gestured at; whether cluelessness of this kind gives a positive reason to prefer options that preserve optionality, reversibility, or the ability to learn, rather than options that merely maximise an estimate we do not trust; and what, if anything, follows for the choice actually in front of us. I am looking for the structure of the problem rather than a survey of positions, though I am happy for you to use concrete miniature examples to make the distinctions vivid.
