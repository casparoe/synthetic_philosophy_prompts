hey — I'm writing something on hedonism and I keep going in circles on the heterogeneity problem: what do all the pleasures have in common? the cold beer, the proof finally working out, the warm bath, sex, a dumb joke, the moment a headache stops. Before I go back to the actual theories I want the spec written down. What would a theory of what pleasures share have to do to count as adequate?

Purpose, since I guess adequacy is relative to it — I want it for two things, and I suspect they pull apart. (a) I want pleasure to carry weight as the currency of welfare, so whatever it turns out to be has to be the sort of thing that can be aggregated across a life and compared, at least roughly, between people. (b) I also want it usable by someone who isn't the subject: I'm messing about with an evaluation protocol for a care home, most of the residents have advanced dementia, self-report is basically worthless, so the theory has to say what a third party should be looking at. Tell me if (b) is an illegitimate demand — but then tell me why.

So: the requirements list. For each one, the case or consideration behind it, and mark it non-negotiable vs. a preference of one camp. Flag any pair you think can't be jointly satisfied — that's the bit I most want, because if the list itself is impossible that's a result. Then say which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidates. A short scorecard at the end is fine — few lines each, no more — for four options: the felt-quality / hedonic-tone view; the desire-or-attitude view (you enjoy something iff you're drawn to continuing it, take a pro-attitude to it, whatever the best version of that is); the adverbial-ish view where pleasure is a way of undergoing an experience rather than a bit of one; and the cluster / family-resemblance / straight-up disjunctive view. Add any contender I've left off.

Some things I want you to take a position on, not just list: is the demand that there be ONE feature shared by every pleasure itself a camp prejudice? What would a cluster or disjunctive account have to deliver to count as an answer rather than as giving up? Does the theory owe us an explanation of why pleasure is good, or is that a separate question we shouldn't load onto it? And does it owe us a unit — a hedonic degree that makes aggregation and interpersonal comparison non-arbitrary — or can it punt on measurment and still be adequate?

Distinctions it shouldn't collapse: feeling pleasant vs. enjoying vs. taking pleasure in something; intensity vs. duration vs. how much it contributes to a life; whether the property belongs to experiences or to a person at a time; and being a pleasure vs. being good for you.

Cases it has to handle — some of these I'm making up, so tell me which ones aren't doing any real work:
- the afternoon absorbed fixing a bike: you'd say you enjoyed it, but no moment of it felt pleasant in any distinctive way, and you only notice afterwards
- scratching a sunburn: the same patch of skin, at the same time, feels good and bad, and it gets worse the more you do it
- the third slice of cake, which you keep eating after you stopped enjoying it (and the neuroscience-flavoured stuff about wanting and liking coming apart — grant it for the sake of argument if you don't trust it)
- the long pleasures: writing a book, a friendship, "I enjoyed my twenties". Where in the stream of experience is that pleasure located?
- the pleasure of a headache stopping, and the "pleasure" of never having had one. Is the absence of pain itself a pleasure, and what does the answer cost you?
- a diffuse good mood, with no object and nothing in particular you're noticing
- the disowned pleasures: the giggle at someone elses misfortune, the joke you laugh at and then hate yourself for. Pleasures, clearly; welfare value, questionable
- the inverted case: someone whose pleasant experiences feel to them the way a persistent itch feels to you, but who pursues them and is averse to what you'd call pain. What decides whether they're feeling pleasure — the quality or the motivation? I think this case is doing real work but I'm not sure against whom
- the resident who smiles at music, can't report anything, and has no memory of it thirty seconds later. Is the smile evidence of pleasure, and under which theory?

no citations or reading lists please, I've got those and I'm nowhere near a library. just the spec.
