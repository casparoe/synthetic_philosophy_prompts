I build and train reinforcement-learning agents for long-horizon control problems (multi-year inventory, grid-scale storage dispatch), and I read economics only when my team has to borrow a discount rate. I am fluent in the RL vocabulary: returns and value functions, γ, the contraction property of the Bellman operator, episodic versus continuing formulations, the reading of a γ-discounted return as an undiscounted return under a geometric termination hazard of 1−γ, average-reward (gain) criteria, Blackwell optimality, overtaking and Cesàro criteria, and the awkward fact that as γ→1 discounted optimality becomes insensitive to any fixed finite prefix while the average-reward criterion ignores finite prefixes altogether.

I want the debate about pure time preference and discounting the future restated in that vocabulary, piece by piece. The pieces in front of me are: the decomposition of the social discount rate into a pure time preference term plus a term for growth in per-capita consumption weighted by the elasticity of marginal utility (so that most of the disagreement about the social discount rate, for instance between Stern and Nordhaus, is a disagreement about the pure term); the claim that discounting a generation's well-being merely because it comes later is morally indefensible and reflects only weakness of imagination; the reply that with a zero pure term the implied optimal savings rate becomes implausibly high; the attempt to justify positive discounting by the risk that humanity will not survive to receive the benefit; and Diamond's 1965 result that no continuous Paretian social ordering over infinite utility streams can treat every generation alike.

Give me the translation, then a residue report: which of these translate cleanly, which survive only by being distorted (say where you had to fudge), and which cannot be expressed in my vocabulary at all. Two mismatches I want flagged rather than smoothed over: in RL the discount applies to the agent's own future rewards, whereas social discounting applies to the welfare of distinct people, so the single-agent return may be the wrong target — consider restating it with each generation as the agent and the inherited value function as its bequest; and in RL the reward function is simply given, whereas here what counts as well-being is itself contested. Do not smuggle RL's assumptions in and call the result a translation; tell me each time you do it.

Finally, my own suspicion is that Diamond's impossibility is just my familiar problem in another guise — no stationary, total, convergent criterion can be genuinely sensitive to the far tail — and that RL handles it by living with partial orders (Blackwell optimality, overtaking) rather than by choosing a discount rate. Is that a real translation, or am I flattening a moral claim into a numerical one? About 800 words.
