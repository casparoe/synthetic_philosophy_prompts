I'm a retired physical oceanographer — thirty-odd years of current meters, error bars, and arguing with modellers, and about eighteen months of going to a continuing-education philosophy group on Tuesday nights, which has been a bigger shock to the system than anything the North Sea threw at me. Our tutor, Miriam, is good but she won't answer a question until you say what you'd count as an answer. One thing we do that I like: we keep a forecasting ledger. Every week someone writes down a probability and the reference class they got it from — literally, the set of cases they're treating this one as drawn from — and at the end of term we pool everybody's forecasts inside their declared class and check whether the outcomes were calibrated. Miriam's line is that a class you can't audit is a class you can't use. It's my favorite part of the whole business, because it's the only bit that has error bars on it.

So I've been logging her verdicts, because I want to know what she'll say about a case I actually care about. Her rulings so far:

- Coin from the till, never tested, one flip: 0.5, from the class of coin flips generally. She would not let anyone define the class as this coin for a single toss.
- Same coin after a hundred tosses, 71 of them heads: 0.65, not 0.71. She narrowed to this coin's tosses but pulled back toward the broad class.
- My own chance of dying in the next five years (I'm 74, cycle a lot, live in a well-off postcode): she used the national life table for men 70–74, ignored the cycling, and flatly refused the class men in this postcode district, even though there's plenty of data behind it. She said it's only a stand-in for things we already know more directly.
- A friend's hip replacement lasting fifteen years: she refused all hip replacements on the grounds that it mixes bearing surfaces and age bands, and insisted on the subclass by bearing surface and age, even though that subclass only had about eighty cases in it.
- Whether the district council approves the rock armour scheme in March: she refused to give a probability at all. She wrote no class in the ledger and said it isn't the sort of thing that comes in repeatable batches, though she'd happily give odds as a personal judgement.

Now my real one. There's a 300-metre stretch of concrete stepped revetment, built 1985, protecting about sixty houses at Sandle Bay, one of which is my sister's. Part of it was repaired in 2004 after a partial failure. The council's consultant has put a number into an expected-loss spreadsheet to decide about £2.3m of rock armour, and I don't trust the number. As I see it there are three candidate classes, and they don't agree:

A. All UK concrete stepped revetments of that general type: roughly 1,600 structures, pooled failure rate about half a percent a year, so call it 10% over twenty years. Lovely statistics, hopelessly mixed — different foundations, wave climates, ages all thrown together.
B. Same design, glacial-till foundation, high-energy exposure band, same vintage: nine structures, two of which have failed in the last twenty years, the rest observed for varying periods. Crudely that's about 2% a year, which compounds to knocking on 30% over twenty years — but two events in nine means you can't say much, and with nine members I can't audit calibration inside the class either.
C. A consultant's physics-based model run for this exact structure: overtopping, scour, toe stability, a scenario spread for sea-level rise, giving 3% over twenty years. Except the scour parameters were partly fitted using data from class A, the modellers admit the honest range is 0.5% to 15%, and they left out the settlement crack the 2004 repair was supposed to fix and maybe didn't.

What I want from you:

1. Roughly what number does Miriam give for the twenty-year failure probability, and how wide an interval does she put around it?
2. What rule is she actually running? Give me two or three candidate rules that fit all five of her earlier verdicts, and say what each one predicts here. I can think of a few myself — narrowest class you can still count in, mechanism and causal homogeneity first, go to the physical model and treat the class frequencies as just evidence about the model, or her own ledger rule about auditable classes — but I can't tell which one survives all five cases, and they definately come apart on mine.
3. Your best guess, and the reasons for preferring it.
4. What further case, or what single extra piece of information, would separate the rules? I want something to put to her on Tuesday.
5. Does the 2004 repair move it into a different class — is a once-failed structure a member of structures that have failed before, which plausibly has a much higher rate and no national data at all, or does a repair reset it? Which of the rules does that question bite on?
6. And practically: I have to give the council one number on Tuesday to go in their expected-value sum. What should I tell them if I want to be as consistent with Miriam as possible — and is there a decent chance the honest answer is that no number belongs in that box?

Please write in British English — metres, realise, emphasise, that sort of thing. And don't give me references or a reading list; I can't use them and I don't want them. Just the argument, a few hundred words is plenty.
