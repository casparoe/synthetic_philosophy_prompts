Bit of an odd one. I recently became my brother's guardian after a traumatic brain injury, and the team around him has split into two camps that haven't moved in six months. One side — the health economics guy, mostly — says the only defensible way to tell what's good for my brother is to look at what he actually picks when we put options in front of him: his choices are the data, and utility just is whatever those choices reveal. The rest of us say no, what matters is how things go for him from the inside, and choices come apart from that in all sorts of ways (he takes whatever's infront of him, whatever he took last time, or whatever the aide happens to nod at).

My working theory is that this isn't a disagreement about what his welfare consists in at all — nobody in the room really thinks his welfare is nothing but his behavior — but purely about what counts as evidence, since his self-reports are unreliable and his choices are at least public. Two things make me doubt that. First, when I push him, the choice guy says that taking preference to mean what-would-be-chosen isn't a theory about my brother's mind at all, it's just the only version of the word that's measurable, which sounds like a verbal dispute rather than an evidential one. Second, his choices aren't even consistent with each other: he takes A over B when just those two are on offer, and B over A when a third option is on the table as well — so there may not be one single revealed preference in there to read off.

So what kind of disagreement is this, actually — verbal, evidential, or a real fight about what preference and utility mean — and is my diagnosis definately wrong? I want a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of the positions. And given whatever you think it is, what would count as making progress: some distinction I'm missing, some piece of data about him, a different question entirely? Would the answer be any different for a fully competent adult than for my brother? Please answer in American English.
