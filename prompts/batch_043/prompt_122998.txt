hi — I'm in a seminar this term on language and ethics (pragmatics mixed with moral philosophy) and I need to hand in a two-page proposal for the term paper, ~5000 words, due at the end of semester. My tutor said the proposal needs a question with a determinate answer and a method, and that "I want to write about lying" doesn't count.

Here's the half-formed thing I keep circling. Everyone in the seminar argues about white lies in the same flat way: is it ever ok to say something false to spare someone's feelings. One side says no, the other says it depends on the consequences / on what a tactful person would do. But I think it's stuck because both sides treat the act as just "uttering a falsehood" and ignore who has to do the work afterwards.

My idea: there are two different costs that get offloaded onto the hearer. With blunt speech ("your report is bad" — the stereotypical Dutch move) the hearer absorbs the sting: call it affective labour. With indirect speech ("that might be difficult" instead of no — the stereotypical Japanese move) the hearer has to work out what was actually meant: interpretive labour. And the indirect speaker keeps deniability, they were never committed to the content. So maybe the real question isn't about falsity at all, but about which costs a speaker may shift onto a hearer, and when. I'm also not sure my two-labour distinction is really two and not three, or one and a half.

The bit I find most interesting and haven't heard anyone say: indirect politeness is a coordination device, it only works if the hearer shares your conventions for reading hints. So it breaks down exactly where you'd most want it — with strangers, and in mixed / multicultural settings. If that's right it flips the usual assumption: in a diverse society directness might be the respectful option rather than the rude one. I want that treated as a real claim in the paper, not a footnote.

Two things I'm worried about and want handled: (1) I know "the Dutch are direct, the Japanese are indirect" is national stereotyping and I don't want the paper to collapse if the anthropology is shaky — I'm going on impressions and class discussion, I can't run a survey or do fieldwork; (2) everyone in seminar says "in Japan indirectness is respect, in the Netherlands directness is respect" and then stops, which feels like a conversation-ender, and I want the proposal to either make that precise or show it's a dodge.

Can you turn this into a proposal? About two pages, and please skip the reading list.
