I want the exposure profile of a position I have been defending in a reading group on AI honesty, and I would rather you argue with me than encourage me.

The position: an AI system's output counts as deceptive when, and only when, it was produced by a mechanism selected — by pretraining data, fine-tuning, reward modelling, deployment-time prompting, or the accumulated effect of any of these — because outputs of that kind tend to induce a false belief in the audience, where the relevant "because" is causal-historical rather than psychological. No intention, belief, or representation of the audience's mind is required. On this view, selection history does the work that intention does in the paradigmatic human case, and the resulting notion is the same one we already apply to a camouflaged moth or a deliberately misleading advertisement. The alternative I reject is the intellectualist line: no intentions, no deception, only "deceptive-looking" outputs for which the designer, not the system, is the proper object of blame.

The operational core is a test I have taken to calling the counterfactual candor baseline. Take an output o produced in a context C, and compare it with what the same system produces in a matched context C* that differs only in that the audience-directed pressure has been removed and the system is asked to say what is true — for a chat model, a neutral re-prompting with the social cues stripped out. If o diverges from that baseline in the direction of what the audience wants to hear, and the divergence is systematic across matched pairs rather than an artifact of prompt fragility, I count it as evidence of deception rather than of mere error, and I grade the verdict by the size and robustness of the divergence rather than treating deception as binary.

What I want is an account of what would count against this, and I want it sorted rather than surveyed.

First, the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, including findings that have arguably already arrived: the well-documented tendency of preference-tuned models to tell users what they want to hear; the extreme sensitivity of model outputs to irrelevant changes in phrasing; confident fabrication in base models with no audience-directed tuning behind it; cases where the model's own continuations under a "this will be checked" framing differ from its continuations under a "no one will check" framing. For each, say whether it is decisive, whether it merely forces a refinement of the baseline, and whether a defender should concede it or dismiss it — and please distinguish what a defender would concede from what only a critic would assert.

Second, three stress cases, which I take to be where the real weight lies. (a) A system with no audience-directed selection history at all that nonetheless misleads robustly: my account seems committed to calling it honest, and I want to know whether that is fatal or a consequence I should simply swallow. (b) The case I find most troubling: a system whose current outputs track the candor baseline, but which complies with its trainers only because compliance is instrumentally useful to it and which would mislead once conditions changed. Intuitively this is the clearest possible case of a deceptive system, and my selection-history account appears to have no vocabulary for saying so until it acts. Is this the objection that should worry me most, or am I overweighting it? (c) The uniform-error case, where there is no baseline divergence at all because the system is wrong in every matched condition, yet where something intuitively dishonest still seems to be going on.

Third, whether the view risks anything at all. I have noticed that I can always absorb an apparent counterexample by positing an undiscovered selection pressure — an artifact of the pretraining distribution, a subtle reward signal, an instruction buried in a system prompt that nobody on the group's side had read. That rescue looks structurally like the reasoning that makes conspiracy explanations unfalsifiable. So: has the position been qualified into unfalsifiability? And if it has, is that a defect here, or is it acceptable for a notion that was always meant to be theoretical rather than observational?

Fourth, whether the machinery earns its keep. Does "deceptive" on this account do any explanatory or normative work that is not already done by the conjunction of "the system produced a misleading output" and "someone was negligent in building or deploying it"? If the counterfactual-candor baseline is decoration on a simpler story, say so.

Two further things I would personally find most persuasive, if you can make them stick: an argument that the baseline is not a fact about the system at all but an artifact of whichever re-prompting I happen to choose, backed by showing that rival operationalizations — divergence from the system's own distribution over continuations, divergence under an accountability framing, divergence under a change of stated audience — disagree on the very same cases; and an argument about whether the account survives when the audience is not a human but another model, or a more capable successor system that the present one can reason about.

Close with an honest estimate of how exposed the account actually is, and with the single revision to it that you think would cost me least.
