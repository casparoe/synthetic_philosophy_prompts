I'm a senior at a fairly large public high school, and I run our physics-and-philosophy club — about fourteen regulars, mostly juniors and seniors, and we get one seventy-five-minute block after school every other Thursday, with a faculty advisor who mostly lets us run things ourselves as long as we don't turn it into a lecture. Next month it's my turn to lead a session, and I volunteered to do something on the block universe, partly because I spent part of the summer reading about it on my own and partly because I think a lot of people in the club half-believe it without knowing why. The risk is that I do what previous sessions did: draw a light cone on the board, say that relativity makes simultaneity frame-relative, and then have everyone conclude either that the future is already fixed by the laws or that free will is an illusion, and end up forty minutes away from the actual physics. I want the session to separate three claims I keep mixing up in my own head: that the future is determined by the past together with the laws, that future events exist in the same tenseless sense that present ones do, and that there is no objective, frame-independent fact about which distant events are happening now. My sense is that only the third is really a scientific claim, that the second is a metaphysical reading of it, and that the argument from relativity of simultaneity to eternalism — the Rietdijk–Putnam style argument — lives or dies in the move from the third to the second. Almost nobody in the club has had special relativity formally, though a couple have heard of time dilation, so nothing can presuppose the formalism.

What I'd like from you is a runnable activity for that room and that time budget. We have the gym or a stretch of the football field, a long measuring tape, cones, whistles, index cards, and phone stopwatches — no computers and no lab equipment. My idea, which you should improve on or replace if it's wrong, is to slow the speed of light down to something like a brisk walk by having "signals" carried by designated messengers who always move at one fixed pace, so that the tilt of different observers' simultaneity slices becomes something people can literally walk through and disagree about at human scale rather than something they take on trust. The constraints that matter to me: no explanation up front, so that participants discover on their own that two people in relative motion sort the same distant events into already-happened, happening-now, and not-yet differently and cannot both be corrected; real roles and real small choices rather than a thought experiment narrated at them; and a structure where they generate the move to "so all of it is equally real" themselves before anyone names eternalism. Tell me the rules, the roles, the setup, a timeline with rough minute counts, and what I should hand out on the index cards.

Then I want a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the three claims above, and that deliberately stages the resistance to the argument rather than only the argument. Two things in particular. First: I want a segment where I reveal that the whole game could have been run with a hidden master clock — an unobservable true simultaneity that none of the observers' procedures can detect — and ask what difference, if any, that would make to the conclusion; I think this is the point where the argument has to earn the claim that existence isn't frame-relative rather than just helping itself to it, but I'm not confident I can say that in a way sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds will feel rather than just hear. Second: the argument strikes me as suspiciously cheap, since apparently any nonzero relative velocity at all — someone walking past you, or even moving a finger — is supposed to deliver the block universe, and I'd like help turning that into a live question in the room: is it a virtue of the argument that the conclusion follows from something so ordinary, or is it a sign that the conclusion was packed into a premise about what it means for an event to be real? I'd also like an honest note on what will predictably go wrong — I expect at least one person to insist that the whole thing is about light reaching our eyes and is therefore just an illusion or a perspective, and I'm not sure how to handle that — and a frank statement of which part of this the activity simply cannot make anyone feel, so that I have to supply it in words and can be upfront about doing so. If it helps, the concrete outcome I'm after is that people leave able to say which of the three claims they accept, which they think they were talked into, and why the other two don't follow from it.
