My second supervisor read the chapter I'm writing on formalising the presumption of independence (it's for a handbook, short, and he's the kind of number theorist who thinks heuristics are just stamp collecting) and left me with an argument I can't find the hole in, and whose conclusion I'm sure is false. Roughly as he put it:

(1) The only version of the independence presumption that's actually a theorem is local and finite: for n uniform on {1,…,N}, any *fixed finite* set S of primes, and ν(p) forbidden residue classes mod p, the density of n avoiding all of them tends to ∏_{p∈S}(1 − ν(p)/p). Chinese remainders, equidistribution, done.

(2) An integer m > 1 is prime iff no prime p ≤ √m divides it. So the primes up to N are exactly the survivors of a sieve at level z = √N with ν(p) = 1 everywhere.

(3) So apply (1) with S = all primes up to √N. Get P(n prime) ≈ ∏_{p≤√N}(1 − 1/p).

(4) Mertens: ∏_{p≤x}(1 − 1/p) ~ e^{−γ}/log x. At x = √N that's 2e^{−γ}/log N, i.e. about 1.123/log N.

(5) So π(N) ≈ 1.123 N/log N. But the prime number theorem says N/log N. So (3) is false: the bare independence product is refuted in the one case where we have a theorem to check it against.

(6) The only place it can have gone wrong is the slide from fixed finite S to a family of conditions whose size grows with N (about 2√N/log N of them). Each prime contributes a small error but there are unboundedly many, so no uniformity in |S| is available. Call this the uniformity gap.

(7) Every heuristic anyone actually uses — Hardy–Littlewood for k-tuples, Bateman–Horn for k polynomials — needs a product over *all* primes, i.e. over a set of conditions that grows with N. The only way to make such a product converge is to divide out (1 − 1/p) once per condition: C = ∏_p (1 − ν(p)/p)(1 − 1/p)^{−k}, then predict C ∫_2^N dt/(log t)^k.

(8) That denominator is not derived from (1). It's an extra posit: a choice of baseline. And the only output of the machinery we can compare with a proved theorem is k = 1, where it gives C = 1 and so reproduces the PNT by construction. So k = 1 isn't a test of the correction, it's what the correction was calibrated on.

(9) For k = 2 the corrected and uncorrected products differ by a factor of about (2e^{−γ})^2 ≈ 1.26 — and since the infinitude of twin primes is open, there's no theorem to choose between them. Same for every k ≥ 2, and the gap widens as k grows.

(10) So the whole programme has zero checkable content in the cases anyone cares about. It survives step (5) only because a normalisation was inserted whose entire job is to make step (5) come out right, and it's never been tested where it says something we don't already know. We have no more reason to expect about 1.32 N/(log N)^2 twin primes than 1.66 N/(log N)^2.

The conclusion is mad, for two reasons I can gesture at but not make precise. First: those two constants differ by a quarter, which is the sort of gap that shows up in tabulated counts, and the counts go Hardy–Littlewood's way — so the normalisation is tracking something real rather than being picked. Second, products of exactly that shape, over all primes with local factors, provably turn up as the main term in asymptotics for other additive problems (the number of ways to write a large odd number as a sum of three primes, say); there the local congruence data really is in the answer, and nobody thinks a fudge is doing the work.

But I can't turn either into a diagnosis. So: which step do you reject? I'd rather keep the Hardy–Littlewood constant than the claim that the machinery is untested, and I'd keep (1) above everything since it's the one bit that's a theorem. My own attempt was to restate the presumption as a claim about conditional probabilities — independence holds only after you condition on coprimality to the primorial up to the sieve level, so that all the bias is local — but he says that's either (1) again or a new posit in a costume, and I can't tell which. If the right fix is that the presumption has to be a ratio of two sieve outputs rather than a probability, tell me a ratio of what to what, and what would actually test it. And price the alternatives: what do I lose if I reject the calibration complaint in (8) instead?
