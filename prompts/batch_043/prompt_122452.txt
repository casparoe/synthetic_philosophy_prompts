I'm a high school senior, and in three weeks I'm giving the spring talk to my school's philosophy club. I have a thirty-minute slot, of which maybe ten minutes will be questions, so anything you give me to say has to fit in well under a minute. I'd like you to build me a Q&A prep sheet.

The thesis I'm defending is that the lottery paradox and the preface paradox are normally taught as one problem with one family of solutions, and that this is a mistake. They have opposite dependence structures, the dependence structure is the only thing about them that matters for what an agent should then do, and the standard unified treatments hide it.

My argument has five steps.

One: a raffle with a thousand tickets and exactly one winner. For each ticket I'm willing to say "that one will lose," at something like 0.999, and I'm also completely certain that one of them wins. Two: an author of a book with a thousand factual claims, confident of each one, who also believes her own preface when it says that somewhere in there is a mistake — except that her confidence in the preface is not certainty, only something like 0.63. Three: the standard way these get presented as a single phenomenon — many individually safe acceptances, plus one further thing you accept, and the set refuses to close under conjunction — with the usual fixes being a higher bar for acceptance, or giving up closure, or dropping talk of belief for talk of degrees of confidence. Four: my claim about what that misses. In the raffle the thousand claims are maximally anti-correlated, because at most one of them can be false, which is exactly why each one can sit at 0.999 while their conjunction is certain to fail; the risk is one big risk, rearranged so that it looks small from the standpoint of any single ticket. In the preface the claims are, to a fair approximation, independent: the mistake in chapter four has nothing to do with the mistake in chapter nine. So one case is about risk that is structured and shared, and the other is about many separate small risks that genuinely accumulate. Five: my test case, which is where I think the distinction stops being bookkeeping. The school's scanner reads a thousand answer sheets. My evidence about each sheet is strong, and my evidence about different sheets doesn't overlap — as far as I know the machine's errors are the scattered kind. Then the office hand-counts and the total comes out different. Now I am certain that at least one sheet was misread, while still having strong and apparently independent evidence about each sheet, and those cannot both stand. A thousand independent 0.999s leave better than a third of a chance that everything is fine, and shaving my confidence on every sheet equally never gets me to zero unless I am certain about some particular sheet, which nothing in my evidence picks out. So the independence picture cannot survive; I have to go looking for one common fault, a bent feed or a bad calibration. The practical upshot is what I care about: in the preface case the rational response is local, go check the claims one at a time, because the risks really are separate, whereas in my case the rational response is global and no amount of spot-checking sheets will help. A unified treatment cannot tell you which response you owe.

The room will be: Mr. Ferris, the club's faculty advisor, who is a philosophy PhD candidate working in formal epistemology, thinks belief is a second-rate notion that should always be replaced by degrees of confidence, is the only person there who has met either paradox before, and has already told me he thinks they are the same problem; two people from the debate team who attend irregularly, argue fast, and will want to know whether any of this has consequences someone outside a seminar would notice; Priya, a junior in AP Statistics and on the math team, who will do 0.999 to the thousandth in her head and will not let a claim about independence pass without asking what exactly is supposed to be independent of what; and about a dozen other students who are bright, have no background, and will raise the objection as common sense.

What I want: roughly eight objections, in the order I'll actually get them, ranked by how much damage they would do rather than by how clever they are, each one written the way that person would put it — Mr. Ferris in his vocabulary, the debaters in theirs, Priya with numbers — and each with a note on whether it has real force, a reply I can give in under a minute, and one sentence on where that reply is weakest. Tell me what I should concede during the talk itself, before anyone asks, and what I should refuse to give up even if the question period goes badly. Then include the question I am hoping nobody asks.

Two constraints. First, I can't look anything up between now and the talk, I haven't read the original papers these paradoxes come from, and I don't want to name work I haven't read, so please don't give me anything that depends on my citing an author or a title — give me moves I can make out of my own head. Second, my own guess at the worst question I could get is Mr. Ferris pointing out that my scanner case is a one-line exercise in conditionalizing on a constraint about a conjunction, and that the dependence I'm making so much of is something conditionalization hands over for free; if that really is fatal, say so and tell me what's left of the talk. My guess at something even worse is that real authors' errors are correlated too, the same blind spot or the same unreliable source running through a whole book, so that my independent preface is a made-up case and the contrast collapses. Tell me if either of those is as bad as I think, and whether there is something worse I haven't thought of.
