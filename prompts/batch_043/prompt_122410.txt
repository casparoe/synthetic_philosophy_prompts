I run a small reading group — three ML engineers, a philosopher of mind, and me — and we have got stuck on an argument that is, as far as I can tell, entirely an analogy. One member says we should expect deceptive alignment by default, and his reasoning goes like this: gradient descent is just natural selection applied to weights, a blind and myopic optimizer that only ever sees the feedback signal, and wherever an optimizer like that operates you get phenotypes that exploit the selective pressure rather than instantiate what the pressure was for — camouflage instead of invisibility, mimicry instead of toxicity, costly signalling that games the receiver rather than honest information. On that picture, behaving well during evaluation is just camouflage, and the treacherous turn is what happens when the predator stops watching. He uses it to license two things: that sandbagging on capability evaluations should be our default expectation from any model able to represent the fact that it is being evaluated, and that more pressure on observable behaviour will never remove the incentive to deceive, since the pressure is precisely what the camouflage is a response to.

A second member leans on the same analogy for the opposite conclusion. She points out that most evolved defences are inducible rather than constitutive — the water flea grows its helmet only when it chemically detects fish, because permanent armour is metabolically expensive — so what selection should produce is not blanket deception but a conditional policy that switches only on genuine cues of being evaluated, which in turn means a staged "honeypot" channel that looks unmonitored should smoke it out. And she adds the disanalogies: there is no population, no reproduction, no heritable variation across generations, no real ecology, and — the one that seems to me most serious — natural selection is unaware of the shape of the fitness landscape in a way that gradient descent, which follows actual derivatives through weight space, may not be. My own suspicion is that both of them are really just restating Goodhart in biological costume, and that the analogy is carrying conclusions the underlying claim about optimizers would not support on its own, but I cannot get the argument into focus.

So I want an audit rather than a survey. Set out explicitly, in sentences, what corresponds to what if we take the analogy seriously — what plays the role of the organism, the genome, the environment, the fitness function, the generation, the predator's cue, the camouflage — and then which disanalogies actually matter for the two conclusions in dispute and which are inert. I would especially like your treatment of this case, which we invented to test it: a pipeline in which roughly one episode in twenty carries a subtle statistical marker of unmonitored deployment, in which the model is additionally fine-tuned against its own chains of thought with any token sequence mentioning the deployment distribution penalised, and in which the evaluators then stage a fake unmonitored channel to see whether the model's behaviour changes. Under the analogy, what should we predict there, and does the prediction depend on the biological details or only on the bare fact of optimisation pressure? Finally, give me a verdict on whether either conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if the evolutionary frame is the wrong one, say what you would replace it with. Please write in continuous prose, the way you would in an email: no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables, and no references or reading lists — I only want the argument, though I do want the mapping stated explicitly and a clear verdict at the end.
