hey — i'm in this cross-listed seminar (half philosophy half cs theory) on oversight/scalable supervision stuff, and we've got one session left, on the obfuscated-arguments worry for debate as a scheme for getting good answers out of systems smarter than the judge. I want to close the last meeting with a short poem someone can read out loud, and I have a formal idea for it that I can't make work myself, so:

The setup I want it to sit inside: two debaters, one honest and one not; a judge who's doing their best but has a hard budget — they can read some bounded number of lines, verify maybe one step in detail, and that's it. The worry is NOT that the judge is lazy or that the liar is more charming. It's that the honest debater's actual argument is long (a correctness proof, a chain of empirical claims, whatever), so no part of it that matters fits inside the judge's budget, while the dishonest debater's argument is short, locally checkable, and wrong in exactly one place. Honesty and checkability come apart. The judge's best available move is a coin flip, and running more rounds of cross-examination doesn't obviously fix it, because each extra round can be obfuscated too.

So: write it as a pantoum — lines 2 and 4 of each stanza become lines 1 and 3 of the next, and the last line is the first line again. I want the repeated lines to be the debaters' sentences: the same sentence, carried into a new stanza, doing different work there. That's what obfuscation actually feels like from the judge's seat — nothing false is ever said, the same true-sounding line just keeps showing up someplace new. And the closing move of the form, ending where you started, should land as the judge ending up exactly where they started.

What I want it to do:

- By the end I shouldn't be able to tell which voice was the honest one — not because you hid anything, but because both voices sound equally warranted. If I can tell, it failed.
- Give me one stanza where the judge tries a random spot check: verify one step of the long argument, at random. My hot take in section has been that this trick does totally different work depending on the domain: when the thing being argued is the kind of claim whose proof can be rewritten so that a single random check catches any error (the PCP-ish move, you know the one), the spot check really is close to a guarantee; when the question is messy and empirical and there's nothing to encode, the same trick is a shrug with better branding. I want the turn between those two stanzas to be the actual argument of the poem — and I want it left open, not resolved.
- It should be accurate enough that the cs people don't wince: nothing about debate being proven broken, no claims about what the optimal-play results say beyond what the judge's budget does. The point is the judge's epistemic position, not a theorem.
- Under ~30 lines, works read aloud, and go easy on the repetition where it starts to feel like a gimmick — changing the punctuation on a repeated line is fine. And please no villanelle and no sonnet, we've had both already this term.
