I am drafting a two-page dissertation prospectus with a new doctoral student on whether systems trained by reinforcement learning — grid-world agents, game-playing systems, language models tuned against a learned reward model — can suffer, and I want it built around one distinction rather than around a survey of positions.

The framing everyone reaches for is that suffering requires negative valence, that reward and punishment are the engineering analogues of valence, and that the question is therefore whether anything is felt. I think that framing mislocates the difficulty. In these systems a negative reward is defined by its causal role in updating a policy, it can be swapped for any signal that produces the same updates, and once training is finished it is often no longer present in the system's operation at all — which makes it look less like pain than like a teaching device. The distinction I want the prospectus to exploit is between valence and what I have been calling negotiability: whether the aversive state is one the subject can re-describe, re-frame, or stand back from. Human beings can, it seems, be trained to alter whether pain amounts to suffering. If that is right, valence and negotiability are separable axes, and the cases worth building a dissertation around are those where they come apart.

So: state the question so that it has a determinate answer and is not merely the hard problem of consciousness under a new name. Say what the two axes buy that a valence-only account does not, and say where my own formulation is most likely to collapse — my worry is that non-negotiable either reduces to unreflective or quietly assumes the phenomenal consciousness it was supposed to help us test for. Give the method and explain why a conceptual rather than an empirical project makes progress here. Build the discussion around the cases that should carry the weight: the agent that learns to drive its own reward channel to its maximum, and the large model whose training involves enormous quantities of aversive signal. Close by saying what a finished result would look like, what would count as the project having failed, and where it is most likely to become unmanageable for a dissertation. No citations — those we will add ourselves.
