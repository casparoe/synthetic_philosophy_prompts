My day job is on the team behind a consumer assistant that people already use for genuinely big life calls (taking a job abroad, whether to keep trying for another kid, whether to sue a relative), and next thursday I'm pitching the rest of the group on an output policy I've drafted. The thesis: before the assistant gives any recommendation it has to argue the other side first, in its own voice, at full strength, no strawmen, then give its own conclusion, then say what would change its mind. My claim is that this handles the persuasion problem better than hedging or disclaimers, on the grounds that a superintelligent system that merely sounds unsure is still wildly persuasive, so the only real safeguard is leaving the user actually able to push back. Take it as given for the pitch that the systems really are that capable and that good at persuasion.

The room is: two behavioral economists who run nudge experiments and will want to know what I think I know about order effects and anchoring, a clinical psychologist who does motivational interviewing and will dislike anything that smells like telling people what to do, someone from legal who thinks in consumer-protection disclosure terms, and my own head of product, who mainly cares wether people keep using the thing.

What I want is a prep sheet: the seven or eight objections I'll actually get, ordered by how much damage they'd do, each one phrased the way that particular person would phrase it, with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft, plus what I should concede on the first slide rather than get caught out on later. And there's one I'm dreading: that requiring a steelman just moves the problem up a level, because a superintelligent system also chooses which steelman to hand you and how to phrase it, so maybe I've built a cleverer persuader rather than a check on one. Where does that one rank, and what's the least bad thing I can say if it comes up?
