I help out at my uncle's antiques and decorative arts shop on Saturdays, mostly hauling furniture and googling hallmarks, and last weekend me and two friends got into a really long argument after one of them said that if a forgery is good enough that nobody can ever tell, there's no real loss — the thing hanging on the wall looks the same, the pleasure is the same, and anyone who says otherwise is just being snobbly about auction prices. I disagreed but couldn't say why, and now I'm trying to work out what my actual verdicts are and whether there's one principle behind them. Going on gut only: (1) a painting made in 1920 to be sold as a 17th-century Dutch master, indistinguishable from the real thing, now known to be a forgery — something is genuinely lost, and I don't think it's just the money. (2) The same kind of case, except the forger turns out to be a better painter then the master he was imitating — I still feel the object is worth less, which makes me suspect I'm not really tracking quality at all. (3) A picture out of a big workshop where the master did the faces and assistants did the drapery: not a fake, but I do think of it as less than a fully autograph one. (4) A sculpture an artist designed down to the last bracket and had a fabricator weld together under his supervision, signed by him: fully his work, no hesitation. (5) A bronze poured from the artist's own mould sixty years after he died, authorized and stamped by his estate: I balk at this one, even though it's physically as close to his hand as the lifetime casts are. (6) An open copy of a famous painting, made for study, labelled as a copy, never sold as an original: not a fake, worth maybe a hundredth as much, and I feel no indignation about it whatsoever. (7) A heavily restored painting where a third of the visible surface is new paint: still the original, but damaged in a way that isn't damage to the object. (8) The one that started the fight: a picture made by a model trained on a single artist's work, that looks exactly like a lost painting of hers, sold openly as 'in the style of', zero deception — I still feel something is off about it and can't say what. (9) A work experts declared a forgery and that, forty years later, gets re-attributed to the master: I want to say its value comes back, and that seems to make value a matter of what we believe about the object rather than what the object is, which annoys me because in (1) I wanted to insist the loss was real even before anybody found out.

My half-baked attempt: a fake is an object whose causal history has the wrong shape — it doesn't actually lead back through the decisions of the person it advertises — and what we lose isn't a visual property but the possibility of using the object as a route to that person's agency; and the things that aren't fakes but still feel lesser (the workshop picture, the posthumous cast, the model-made one) lose value in proportion to how little of the chain runs through somebody actually deciding things. I can already see that this gets into trouble with (8), where there was plenty of care, just not all in one head, and with (9), where value seems to track what we think the chain is rather than the chain itself. So: is there a principle that fits most of these and tells me which of my verdicts to drop, or is the honest answer that 'fake', 'copy', 'workshop piece' and 'in the style of' are four different things needing four different stories? If you do have a unified account, please say what it predicts for a couple of cases I haven't listed — say, an artist who signs a student's painting because he thinks it's good and wants to help her, and a religious icon that's supposed to be an exact repetition of a wonderworking original and is venerated as such.
