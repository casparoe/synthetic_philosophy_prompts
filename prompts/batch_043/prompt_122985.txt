I sit on the governance group at a company that deploys LLM agents to take actions inside our own operations systems: issuing refunds up to a cap, changing vendor bank details, granting short-term access to internal tools, closing out support tickets. The sign-off procedure has been built up over two years and has four features I want to lay out precisely, because I think at least two of them pull in opposite directions. First, the agent has to decompose any action into a chain of short steps, and each step is graded in isolation by an ops reviewer with no ML background who cannot see the steps before or after it and is never shown how the action turned out; internally we call this the sealed-step rule. Second, a reviewer who cannot tell whether a step is acceptable marks it unverifiable rather than wrong, and unverifiable does not block: the action goes ahead as long as no single step is marked wrong. Third, reviewers are told in writing that they will not be penalised if an approved action later causes an incident and will not be credited if it turns out to save the company money. Fourth, the agents are trained only on outcomes — the reward signal comes from whether the task finished successfully, never from whether the individual steps were good, on the stated grounds that grading steps would push the model toward reasoning that looks like ours rather than reasoning that works. And yet we require the models to emit step-by-step reasoning at deployment, we retain those traces, and when something goes wrong the first thing everyone does is read the trace to work out what the system was doing. The quarterly bonus for the whole group, reviewers included, is computed from outcome numbers: incident counts, refund error rates, tickets closed.

What puzzles me is that the same package seems to insist, in one place, that outcomes are irrelevant to assessing a person's judgement, and, in another place, that outcomes are exactly what we reward and what we learn from; and it insists that the reasoning trace is not a fit object of supervision while treating it as the primary object of diagnosis. A colleague in platform engineering says the sealed-step rule is security theatre and that we should just measure end results; someone in legal says the opposite, that if we ever graded outcomes we would be punishing reviewers for luck. I would like the implicit theory of the whole thing written out rather than a verdict on who is right.

So: what does a practice like this presuppose? I am interested in at least the following, but do not feel bound by my framing. (i) The sealed-step rule looks like it presupposes that a competent verdict on an action can be assembled out of verdicts on locally checkable pieces, and that error in those pieces is independent enough that local approval is real evidence about the whole. What is that commitment called in epistemology, and what does someone who holds that justification is holistic — that one mistaken framing can make every step individually plausible and the whole action disastrous — have to say about it? Is the rule strictly committed to the independence claim, or could it survive without it? (ii) Treating unverifiable as non-blocking looks like it presupposes something about the asymmetry between acting and not acting, or about where the burden of proof sits when a weaker party is checking a stronger one. Is there a defensible principle here, or is it risk appetite with a procedure bolted on? (iii) Refusing to score steps in training while reading traces after an incident looks like it presupposes that the trace reliably indicates what produced the action, even though nothing in training ever rewarded it for doing so. Is that a commitment anyone should want to defend, or can the incident-review practice be run on a thinner, purely diagnostic reading of what traces are? (iv) Is the outcome blindness for step-graders genuinely inconsistent with outcome-based bonuses, or can the package be split — say, by treating the sealed-step rule as a device for gathering evidence and the bonus as a device for aligning incentives — and if it can be split, what does each half then presuppose on its own?

Please distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits well with, and tell me which rival views would license the same four rules for entirely different reasons. Then say concretely what would have to change if each presupposition were dropped: what a policy built on outcome-only supervision would look like at our scale, what one built on process-only supervision would look like, and where each would break given that our reviewers are weaker than the systems they review, that some of our tasks are far easier to check than to perform, and that a reviewer who simply abstains is, under our current rules, functionally indistinguishable from one who approves.
