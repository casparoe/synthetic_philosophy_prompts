I'm in an upper-level philosophy of psychiatry seminar, and our study group is dividing up the practice final; I've been given Hacking's looping effects and interactive kinds. The difficulty is that everyone in the class can already state what a looping effect is and reproduce the two or three standard examples we were assigned, so I need an essay question that separates people who have actually understood the argument from people who have memorized the label — and I'd rather it not rest on those familiar historical cases again.

Here is an invented case I'd like the question built around. A task force drafting the next revision of the diagnostic manual proposes a new category, 'attenuated social withdrawal syndrome': persistent, distressing withdrawal from face-to-face interaction sustained by a marked preference for mediated contact. Within five years of the category becoming public: self-description and online communities form around it; clinicians report that new patients present with textbook symptom profiles and are far easier to assess than the original cohort, and inter-rater agreement on the diagnosis rises sharply; prevalence rises more than tenfold; insurers and universities extend benefits and accommodations to people who carry the label; and the task force, alarmed by the prevalence figures, proposes tightening the criteria to require documented impairment with onset before age 25.

Please write me a 45-minute in-class essay question on this case. It should force anyone answering it to do at least the following: distinguish first-order looping (the classification changing the self-conception and behaviour of the people classified) from what I'll call second-order looping (the classification changing the classifiers, the criteria, and the institutional incentives through which the label travels), and take a position on whether the two are really the same phenomenon; say what the rise in inter-rater reliability is evidence for, given that patients may simply have learned the criteria, and why rising reliability need not mean rising validity; say in what sense, if any, the tightened criteria and the original ones pick out the same kind, given that human kinds are supposed to be moving targets; and settle whether looping gives the task force any reason at all to doubt the category, since a classification that was both true and clinically useful would also change the people it applies to, so it is not obvious that looping is a defect rather than a cost of getting things right.

Along with the question I'd like a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 built around five must-hit points, specifying the marks each is worth and a one-line note on the error that typically costs a student that point — for instance, announcing that the category is socially constructed and stopping there, or treating looping as a reason to think the kind is not real. Please end with a short note on what distinguishes an A answer from a B answer.
