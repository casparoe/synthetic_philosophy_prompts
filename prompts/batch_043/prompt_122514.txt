I'm putting together the discussion questions for a philosophy-and-film seminar I co-teach with a colleague in economics, and we are pairing Christopher Nolan's The Dark Knight (2008) with Douglas Hofstadter's experiments in superrationality from his 1985 collection Metamagical Themas, especially the Platonia dilemma and the Luring Lottery. The scene I want to focus on is the late-ferry experiment: two evacuation ferries, one carrying convicted prisoners and the other ordinary citizens, are each given a detonator wired to the other boat; they are told that whichever boat destroys the other by midnight will be allowed to live, while if midnight passes with neither boat acting the Joker will destroy both. In the film, the prisoners refuse to use their detonator and the civilians, after voting to blow up the other boat, cannot bring themselves to do it, so neither boat fires and Batman intervenes before the deadline. My students have seen the movie many times, but most of them come in with only the vague sense that the scene is "about the prisoner's dilemma" or "about people being basically good," and I want to press them harder with the actual structure of Hofstadter's games.

As I understand it, Hofstadter's superrationality is meant to handle exactly this kind of symmetric situation: a superrational agent, knowing that the other agent faces the same problem and will reason identically, looks for the strategy that, if adopted by all superrational agents, maximizes each one's expected payoff, and in the two-player Platonia dilemma (where a prize goes to the sole player who sends a telegram and is lost if nobody or more than one sends) that means acting with probability one-half rather than either refraining or acting for sure. Hofstadter's related Luring Lottery, in which a fixed cash prize is divided by the number of people who enter, makes the same point on a larger scale: the egoist who thinks she can quietly be the only entrant ignores that every other egoist is thinking the same thing, so the reward is diluted or destroyed. Here is my confusion. If the Joker's ferry game is really a two-player Platonia-style anti-coordination problem—life to the sole boat that pushes, death to both if neither or both push—then mutual restraint is not a cooperative solution that beats mutual defection; it is exactly as fatal as mutual destruction, and the only superrational escape is for each boat to randomize, pushing with probability one-half to maximize the chance that exactly one boat pushes. Yet the film treats the refusal to push as a moral vindication of ordinary people over the Joker's cynical game theory, and my colleague says this means the scene is really about how real human utilities are not just survival, since the passengers would rather die than kill. I would like a reading that takes both the movie and the superrationality material seriously: map the ferry scene onto the Platonia dilemma and the Luring Lottery, say where the analogy holds and where it breaks, explain whether the film is dramatizing a Prisoner's Dilemma, a Platonia anti-coordination game, or a different game once moral preferences are added, and tell me whether the prisoners' and civilians' refusal is best understood as superrational cooperation, ordinary moral restraint, or a failure of game-theoretic imagination. Where does the scene's use of the experiment trade on a confusion the way a bad popularization of quantum mechanics does, and does the film know the difference, or does it think it is refuting rational choice when it is actually just changing the payoffs? Finally, does the scene add anything to the philosophical debate about superrationality that Hofstadter's bare thought experiments miss—for example, about group agency, voting, the impossibility of a collective coin flip, or the way a public deadline changes things?
