I'm a novelist — three books out, all of them literary-ish historical fiction, the kind where you do a year of research on a period you didn't live through and then try to make people cry about it — and I've gotten myself into an argument I can't get out of. A friend of mine, who reads a lot more theory than I do, got me onto Tolstoy's treatise on art, the late one where he says art is basically a means of infecting other people with feeling, and that most of what gets called art is a counterfeit manufactured by professionals for a class audience. The infuriating thing is that it mostly works on me as a description of how reading actually feels, and it also pretty clearly convicts everything I write, and he himself turned on his own big novels late in life. So I've patched it into a version I can live with, and I want to know how badly the patch holds up.

My version has three moving parts. (1) The transmission part: a work is good to the extent that it transmits a feeling to a reader who didn't come looking for it — craft isn't the opposite of sincerity, it's the technology of transmission, and noticing technique is just what happens when transmission fails. (2) The irrelevance part: what matters is the feeling that arrives, not the feeling the author had; the author's sincerity is a fact about her moral life, not about the work, so the Tolstoyan sincerity condition should be dropped. (3) Where I actually put my own money: I think his real target isn't insincere art but what I call borrowed feeling — art that transmits a feeling nobody ever had, a feeling you caught from other artworks, so the room is full of emotion and the circuit is closed but nothing was ever plugged in. And here's the part I'm least sure about: I want to say the test has to be run on an unprepared reader, someone with no context for the work, on the grounds that prepared readers are just being cued, and I can already tell this is the move that makes the whole thing unfalsifiable, because any failure can be blamed on the reader not being unprepared enough. Meanwhile my own third novel is set in a mining town in 1913 I've never been anywhere near, and I have no idea whether the grief in it is borrowed or not.

So: play it straight and tell me what would count against this. I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to separate the ones a committed holder of my position would concede from the ones she'd just swat away — I can already generate the obvious objections myself, so don't spend much time on "but Tolstoy said X." Specifically: what does my view predict that the standard versions don't, that could actually come out false? Is the borrowed-feeling distinction doing real work or is it just a way of restating "I know it when I see it"? Is there any way to tell the unprepared reader from the merely bored or hostile one, or have I insulated myself into meaninglessess? And if the transmission and irrelevance parts are in tension — if dropping the author's feeling makes "borrowed" an empty category — say so and tell me which of the three I should give up. Skip the citations and quotes entirely, I'll go find those myself; I want the argument, and if the honest answer is that the whole construction is unfalsifiable because of the unprepared-reader move, say that and tell me whether that's fatal for a view of this kind or whether views like this are allowed to be immune.
