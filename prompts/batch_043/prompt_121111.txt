I help to organise a volunteer litter-pick in a park near where I live, I sit on the committee of a small food co-operative, and last autumn I spent an irritating fortnight arguing with my brother-in-law, who is an actuary rather than a philosopher, about whether there is any point in voting in a constituency that has returned the same party since before I was born. In all three settings I keep meeting the same shape of argument, and what puzzles me is not the argument itself but a feature of it for which I have no word. The argument that my own contribution to some collective outcome is too small to matter seems to get stronger, not weaker, the more certain it is that the outcome will happen anyway. Dropping one crisp packet in a park that is already thick with rubbish feels harmless in a way that dropping it on a pristine lawn does not, although the total mess is greater in the first case. In the safe seat my vote has no chance of deciding anything, and the familiar reply that nothing would ever change if everyone reasoned that way seems to me to miss the point, since it is not true that the result would have been different had I stayed at home. And at the co-op, once the buyer has placed the order and the mince is sitting in the fridge, whether or not I take it makes no difference to how many animals were killed, however much it may affect what she orders next month. On the way of thinking that asks how much difference my act actually makes, then, my reason to hold back shrinks towards nothing exactly where the harm is largest and most settled, and is at its strongest when the group happens to be balanced on a knife-edge. That consequence cannot be right, and I cannot see where the reasoning goes wrong.

What I have also noticed, and this may be the same phenomenon or a different one, is that people who defend individual obligations by saying that one does still make a small difference quietly abandon that line and reach for a quite different one — something about not wishing to be among the people who did it, or about what joining in expresses — precisely at the point where the first line fails, that is, precisely in the settled cases; and they never mark the switch, which makes me suspect they have not noticed it themselves. My brother-in-law says the whole difficulty is illusory, because one is never genuinely certain that the outcome is settled, and so there is always some chance of being the one who tips it. But that reply seems to me to work only if there is something really chancy about where the group's behaviour lands, and not if there is merely a fixed threshold somewhere that I happen not to know, and I cannot tell whether that distinction is doing any real work or whether I have invented it.

So my question is whether what I have described has an established name in philosophy, and whether the two things I have described are one thing or two. I should say that I am not after a label: what I want is to be told what exactly the phenomenon is, which of the familiar nearby ideas it is closest to — the general argument that a single person's contribution is too small to matter, the case where many tiny contributions add up imperceptibly into a large harm, the case where the harm would have come about anyway because somebody else would have produced it, and so on — how it differs from each of them, and where its boundaries lie. If there really is no established concept here, then please say so plainly, and coin one: give it a definition precise enough to separate it from the neighbouring cases, and say what would and would not count as an instance of it. Please write your answer in American English, as the notes I am preparing are for a reading group in the States.
