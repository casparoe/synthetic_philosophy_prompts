I run a monthly film-and-philosophy night at my local library with a friend who's very into Advaita Vedanta, and last month we watched Everything Everywhere All at Once — the Daniels' 2022 film with Michelle Yeoh as Evelyn, a laundromat owner getting audited by the IRS, who learns she can verse-jump into the skills and memories of her counterparts in other universes, while her daughter Joy, as the nihilistic Jobu Tupaki, has built an everything bagel that threatens to collapse the whole multiverse; Ke Huy Quan plays her husband Waymond, whose answer to all of this is basically to keep being kind, and there's the famous scene of two rocks with subtitles. My friend insists the whole thing is a popular version of what Schrödinger was on about in his later philosophical essays, where he argues from the fact that consciousness is only ever given to you in the singular — you never experience consciousness in the plural, you only ever have this one — to the conclusion that the plurality of persons is an appearance, and that there's really just one conscious subject, the Upanishadic point. I'm not convinced, and I want to run a session where we actually test the reading rather than just nod along.

Here's the distinction I've been trying to get the group to use. Call access-monism the claim about epistemic situation: consciousness is only ever available from the inside, to one subject at a time, so nobody can count minds from the outside the way they count bodies. Call subject-monism the metaphysical claim that there is literally one conscious subject and the many persons are appearance. I read Schrödinger as sliding from the first to the second by way of something like: you can form no conception of a plurality of consciousnesses, so the plural isn't merely unknown but somehow unintelligible — my name for the suspicious step is the counting-from-nowhere problem, since it looks like it moves from I can't count minds to there aren't many minds. Now here's where the film seems to me to be a genuinely interesting data point rather than an illustration. In the movie, huge amounts of experience leak between apparently distinct Evelyns: memories, skills, even whole emotional histories. So there's an argument available — call it the leakage argument — that starts from cases where experience is shared or transferred and concludes that the sharers aren't really numerically distinct. My own hunch is that the leakage argument is a dud, for roughly the reason fission cases in personal identity are: content can overlap one-to-many without subjects being identical, so what verse-jumping shows is that psychological continuity can be branching, not that there's one subject. But I also suspect there's a weaker view the film really does dramatize — porous pluralism, the idea that the boundaries of a person aren't where common sense puts them, without any monism at all — and I'd like to know whether that's the more honest reading. Meanwhile the film also hands the anti-monist a club: experience stays stubbornly partitioned by bodies. Jobu Tupaki's mind is shattered by being made to experience everything at once, verse-jumping requires the little earpiece and the right neural funny-business, and the damage to one brain tracks one stream of experience. That looks like the standard correlation objection to the one-mind view — if there's really one subject, why does wrecking this brain wreck this stream and not that one? — and I want to know how a Schrödinger-style monist would answer it, and whether the film gives them anything to answer it with.

So what I'd like from you is a reading of the film against this material, faithful in both directions: which scenes you'd actually put in front of the group and what each is doing philosophically (I'm thinking the bagel, the rocks, the hot-dog-fingers universe, the final laundromat confrontation), and whether Jobu's despair is best understood as the Māyā thought run as nihilism, or as a straight misreading of it — my friend keeps saying the Advaitin isn't committed to nothing matters, since the world's dependent status isn't the same as unreality, and I'd like that sorted out. Say too whether Waymond's kindness is the kind of ethical upshot the monist can claim (if the other person just is you, compassion is self-regarding) or whether it's a purely local, non-metaphysical answer that would be available to someone who thinks the multiverse is exactly as plural as it looks. And be explicit about where the film resists the reading: where the honest critic has to say no, this is just a movie about a family and the Vedanta framing is being imposed on it. Finish, if you can, with what the film adds to this argument that the arguments alone don't have — my sense is that it's something about what it's like to be shattered by too much experience at once, and about why the right response to infinity might be an ordinary, local love rather than either despair or transcendence, but I can't quite make that into a claim yet.
