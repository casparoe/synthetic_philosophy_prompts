I'm putting together a handout for my second-year epistemology seminar on the regress problem, and I want the centerpiece to be a decision tree that starts from the one commitment students already arrive with — a belief is justified only if you have a reason for it — and walks them to a named position.

Don't build the tree out of a list of isms. Build it out of the logical space first: the obvious branch points (does the chain have to stop, may it loop, may it go on forever, and if it stops what sits at the bottom — another belief, an experience, or just a reliably produced state), then drop the actual views into the boxes that are occupied, and tell me which boxes are empty and why nobody is sitting in them: incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.

Leaves I want covered: strong and modest foundationalism split by whether the terminus is a belief or a non-doxastic state, a reliabilist terminus as its own leaf even though it may not be playing the same game as the rest, holistic coherentism, the hybrid view that lets experience in as input but still requires mutual support among everything else (foundherentism? if that's the word), infinitism, and the give-up-on-structure skeptical leaf.

Every node has to be phrased as a question a student could answer before knowing which ism it leads to — no "do you think there are basic beliefs?" style nodes.

There's also a view I half-invented and want either placed or diagnosed rather than quietly ignored: the chain never ends and never loops, but each further reason adds a smaller increment of justification, the increments summing to a finite limit, so a finite mind ends up with real but incomplete justification. Where does that go — is it stable, or does it collapse into infinitism or into the skeptical leaf?

Two last things. Flag any pair of leaves your tree can't really seperate (my guesses: modest internalist foundationalism and coherentism converge hard once experience is allowed to count, and infinitism and skepticism are hard to tell apart for a being with a finite memory). And flag any axis that cross-cuts the tree instead of branching in it — whether the subject needs access to the chain at all looks to me like something that cuts across every leaf rather than splitting anywhere.

Keep it to roughly a dozen nodes, and put one line under each leaf about its ugliest commitment so students see the price of each box.
