For the last eighteen months I have been building long-horizon LLM agents, and over the last few I have acquired a checking ritual of my own: back to the door three or four times after I have already locked it, with full awareness, every time, that nothing about the lock has changed since the last look. What unsettles me is not the ritual but the resemblance between it and a failure mode I watch all day in my agents. An agent hits an error, reverts to a checkpoint, and replays the same plan token for token — call it *replay perseveration* — because the failure notice is now in its context and the replay is simply the highest-probability continuation. Both of these look like the digger wasp in the story I grew up on: paralyse the cricket, leave it on the threshold, go in to inspect the burrow, come out, drag it in; and if someone moves the cricket a few inches while she is inside, take it back to the threshold, inspect again, and again, for as long as the experimenter cares to keep moving it.

Write the desiderata that any theory of sphexishness — of what it is for behaviour to be mechanically rigid rather than flexible — would have to meet, with the case or consideration behind each requirement, and say what the theory is meant to be good for. Mine is this: it has to be able to tell me whether the rigidity of my agents, the rigidity of my own checking, and the rigidity of the wasp are one phenomenon or three; whether any of the three is a defect in the thing that has it; and whether any of them bears on the free-will question rather than merely gesturing in its direction.

The cases I want the list to earn its keep on: the wasp; a random-number generator and a thermostat with hysteresis, which vary behaviourally but are not flexible; a rigid controller that calls a stochastic search subroutine, so the whole system passes any variability test while no part of it ever "reconsiders" anything — *delegated flexibility*; a fixed action pattern with a temperature parameter bolted on, i.e. *flexibility-washing*, which I take to be what my own team's "add randomness and retry" patch actually is; a system that rigidly applies the rule "be flexible," such as an agent with a hard-coded reflex to spawn a planning sub-agent on every failure, or a manager's mandatory brainstorming ritual — *second-order sphexishness*; a concert pianist's cadenza and a pilot's pre-landing checklist, which are automatic and yet, unlike my checking, not obviously the worse for it; and my own ritual, where I know the check is pointless and can sometimes be talked out of a given instance but usually cannot stop the sequence once it has started.
