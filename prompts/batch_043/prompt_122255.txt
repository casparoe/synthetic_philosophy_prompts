I am a novelist, and my most recent book has brought me an invitation I am not sure I should have accepted. There is a scene in which the narrator — cut off in a storm, coming to suspect that every belief she has about the world may have been planted in her — works her way to a version of the cogito and takes it to settle more than it does. Her reasoning runs roughly like this: she can be wrong about anything, but that very liability, the fact that her believing can misfire, is not something she can be wrong about; therefore she is, and is the single persisting someone she remembers being. Readers have written to me about that passage more than about anything else in the novel, and a sixth-form college has now asked me to spend fifty minutes with a group of eighteen-year-olds who have all read it. They have no philosophy teaching to speak of, no Latin, and one shared text: my book. I take that to be part of the problem rather than part of the solution, since I have come to think the passage is doing two quite different things at once, and only one of them belongs to Descartes.

So I do not want an explanation of the cogito. I want a plan for teaching, in a single fifty-minute session, what the cogito actually establishes and — more to the point — how much less it establishes than my narrator supposes. Please give me: the order in which the pieces must arrive, and why each has to be in the room before the next can land; the one example or case you would build the session around, including a judgement about whether my narrator's version can carry that weight or will actively mislead, and what a case would need to have and to lack in order to carry it; the misconceptions this particular audience will bring — bearing in mind that they have already watched a first-person voice perform the move confidently and be rewarded for it — and the exact point in the sequence at which each should be headed off; one question I could put to the room at the end that would tell me whether they have the distinction rather than the atmosphere; and an explicit note on what I should decline to explain at this level and say plainly that I am declining, since I would far rather leave something honestly unnamed than gesture at it.

Two constraints. First, I am a novelist and not a philosopher, and I will not be able to field any question about what Descartes's own text says: the plan has to work without appeal to any passage, and without my claiming an authority I do not have. Second, I do not want to spend the hour convicting my own character of a fallacy. If her reasoning fails, I would rather the room finds that out than is told it, and I would like to know where in the sequence that discovery could be arranged to happen on its own.

Format: at most six numbered steps, each headed by the one sentence you would want the room able to repeat back to you at the end of it, followed by a single closing paragraph on what I should refuse to explain. The whole thing under 700 words.
