I host a podcast on formal epistemology, recorded with an audience of graduate students and researchers in the room. My next guest is a philosopher I'll refer to as R, who defends what she calls likelihood-ratio updating: her view is that a learning event does not directly fix a new probability for each cell of a partition, the way Jeffrey conditionalization requires, but instead fixes the Bayes factors linking the experience to the hypotheses at issue, so that the invariance condition in an update should attach to ratios of likelihoods rather than to the conditional probabilities of hypotheses given the cells. She is fluent, well rehearsed, and has a stock reply ready for every standard objection, so I don't want questions that let her restate the view or warm up with her favorite motivation.

Draft me exactly five numbered items. Each item is one question plus one follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to make, at most two sentences per item, with no preamble and no closing summary line. The five items should take on, one apiece, the following pressure points.

1. Partition sensitivity. Jeffrey conditionalization is standardly charged with needing rigidity for a partition whose credentials look like a metaphysical accident. Press her on why invariance of a likelihood ratio is any less hostage to how finely the experience is carved up; if she replies that likelihood ratios are prior-free and so not indexed to a partition at all, follow up on what then settles which proposition the experience is, since a ratio still has to be a ratio for some particular one.

2. Order-dependence. Two successive soft updates will not in general commute. Ask her whether that is a defect of the rule or a signal about the case; if she gives the usual answer that non-commutativity simply encodes what the later experience tells you about the earlier one, follow up with a case where the two experiences are stipulated to be informationally screened off from each other, so that the encoding reply is blocked by construction.

3. Constraints that are not on a partition. There are learning events that fix something other than a new probability for each cell of a partition: a new conditional probability, or the expected value of some quantity, for instance. Ask how likelihood-ratio updating so much as applies there; if she answers that she selects the distribution closest to her old one, in some divergence measure, among those meeting a constraint on the Bayes factor, follow up on why closeness in that measure rather than another selection rule, and on whether the rule she ends up with still has whatever property she used to argue that rigidity had to be abandoned.

4. The reconstructive strategy. A familiar defense of soft updating is that it can be rewritten as ordinary conditionalization, on a richer space, on a proposition about which experience occurred, followed by marginalization. Ask her whether she wants that reconstruction; if she does, follow up on whether it smuggles back the very certainty she rejected at the start, and if she does not, follow up on what her own positive justification is, given that the accuracy-based arguments in this area are stated for conditionalization on something one is certain of.

5. Abandonment conditions. Ask what would make her give the rule up, in a form that cannot be answered by saying that any case where it yields the wrong answer would do; I want her committed in advance to the shape of a counterexample. If she points to cases in which learning changes how the evidence bears on the hypotheses at all, so that every invariance condition fails, follow up on whether those are counterexamples or simply outside the rule's intended domain, and on who gets to draw that boundary.

Keep every question answerable in about ninety seconds, and do not ask her to name papers, authors, or further reading.
