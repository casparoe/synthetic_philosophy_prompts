I have spent the last few years working on minimalism about truth, and I have reached the point where I can no longer tell whether the debate is stuck or finished. Let me describe the impasse as it looks from inside it.

The deflationist's case, at its strongest, is not merely that the equivalence schema — every instance of *it is true that p if and only if p* — is correct, but that the schema, together with the expressive work a truth predicate does in blind ascriptions such as *everything the Pope says is true*, exhausts everything that needs saying about truth. There is no underlying nature to uncover, because the true propositions have nothing interesting in common: correspondence runs out on negative, mathematical and evaluative truths, and pragmatist or verificationist analyses collapse into some version of idealized justification that happily delivers verdicts we would rather not accept. The substantive theorist's reply, also at its strongest, is that truth is not an idle device but does explanatory work we have not shown to be dispensable: it defines validity, states the norms of belief and assertion, underwrites a theory of content, and explains why inquiry that aims at truth tends to succeed. And a schema, or an infinite list of its instances, arguably cannot do that work, because explanation seems to demand a general property rather than a list, and a finite inquirer cannot premise an argument on infinitely many instances.

Three things make me doubt that this is one disagreement.

First, a fork about scope. Some deflationists hold that if the thin treatment of truth is right it should spread to reference, representation and content; others hold that truth can be thin while content and reference are not, so that a theory of meaning still has substantive work to do. These camps are plainly arguing about different things, and I cannot tell whether their dispute is a consequence of deflationism or an independent disagreement about meaning that has been folded in under the same heading.

Second, a case I keep returning to. Imagine two communities whose truth predicates agree on every instance of the equivalence schema for all sentences not tangled up in self-reference, but diverge on the paradoxical ones: one treats the Liar and its kin as neither true nor false and gives up certain classical inferences for sentences containing the predicate; the other treats them as both true and false and gives up different inferences. Both predicates do the disquotational and expressive work; neither community can plausibly be convicted of failing to grasp what truth is. If the deflationist says this is merely a difference of logic, the interesting question has relocated to the philosophy of logic. If the deflationist says it is a difference in which predicate is really the truth predicate, then the schema does not exhaust truth after all, and the substantive theorist has won something. I do not know which answer is better, and that makes me suspect the schema was never the whole quarrel.

Third, the one place where the debate looked as though it might simply be settled. It is often said, I think correctly, that a disquotational truth predicate added to a base theory is not the merely abbreviative device deflationists expected: it lets one derive, in the base language, claims about the soundness or consistency of the base theory that the base theory alone cannot derive. Deflationists have replies. What matters to me is that a formal result arrived at exactly the point where a verdict seemed possible, and did not produce one, because the sides disagreed about what the result showed.

So here is what I want, and it is a diagnosis rather than a verdict or a survey. Tell me what kind of stalemate this is: how much of it is verbal, how much substantive, and how much displaced onto a different question — and support the diagnosis by engaging with what each side actually argues, not with a catalogue of positions. My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the nature of truth is three questions wearing one name: a question about the function of the predicate in language and cognition, which the schema may well answer; a question about what a theory of meaning must posit, which is an argument about metasemantics rather than about truth; and a question about the logic of a self-referential predicate, which belongs elsewhere. If that is right, very little substantive disagreement remains except a methodological one about what counts as an explanation: whether a schema plus its instances can explain a regularity such as the tendency of true beliefs to guide action successfully, or whether explanation requires a single property shared by all the instances. But I cannot tell how much of the dispute that methodological component really carries, or whether it is itself the original question in disguise, and I would like you to say so if you think my three-question diagnosis is wrong.

Finally, and this is the part I care most about: given whatever diagnosis you arrive at, what would actually count as progress — a formal result about conservativeness that both sides accept as relevant, a completed deflationary or anti-deflationary account of content, a settlement of the paradoxes, a distinction that dissolves the question — and which of those is a realistic hope? Or is the honest answer that no progress should be expected, and that the question is ill-posed rather than hard? I do not need references or a bibliography; I know the literature. I want the structure of the disagreement.
