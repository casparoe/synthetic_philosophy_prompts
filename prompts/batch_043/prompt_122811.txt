I have been working through the two-envelope paradox using a finite, explicitly specified prior, because I want a version in which every expectation converges and every number can be checked by hand, rather than one where the resolution can be blamed on infinite expectations or on an improper prior. My setup: a host draws k uniformly from {0, 1, …, 9}, places 2^k dollars in one envelope and 2^(k+1) dollars in the other, and hands me one of the two at random. I open my envelope before deciding whether to switch.

My calculations, which I would like checked, are these. If I see any amount v between 2 and 512 dollars, my posterior is exactly even between holding the smaller and holding the larger amount, so switching wins v with probability one half and loses v/2 with probability one half: a conditional expected gain of +v/4, positive for each of those nine amounts. Seeing 1 or 1024 tells me outright which envelope I hold. Despite this, always switching is worth exactly nothing, as the symmetry argument requires: the ten favourable observations contribute a combined expected gain of +25.6 dollars, and the one-in-twenty chance of seeing 1024 contributes exactly −25.6 dollars. Meanwhile the policy of switching unless I see 1024 has, on my numbers, an expected gain of +25.6 dollars over always staying.

What I cannot get straight is what this case shows. The diagnosis I have been carrying around is that the naive argument smuggles in a flat prior over pairs — it treats my holding the smaller and my holding the larger as equally probable whatever amount I see, which no proper prior can make true at every amount. But in this version that assumption holds at nine of the eleven amounts I could possibly see, and I still find the result counterintuitive, so the diagnosis looks at best incomplete.

Please answer in exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no concluding remark. Between them, I would like the five to cover the following: whether my conditional-gain calculation is correct and whether the naive 5v/4 reasoning is therefore locally sound in this version; the mechanism by which switching can be the right call on ninety-five per cent of the probability mass while always switching is worth nothing at all; where precisely the naive argument fails, if not at the step my diagnosis targets, whether in the assumed posterior or in the slide from switching given what you see to switching regardless; whether the symmetry argument that always switch and always stay tie rules out anything beyond unconditional policies, and so leaves room for a policy like mine to be genuinely profitable; and whether any of this generalises, in the sense that for an arbitrary prior over pairs with finite expectation there is always some observation-contingent policy that strictly beats always staying, or whether that depends on the shape of the prior, and what the answer implies about whether the paradox has really been dissolved.
