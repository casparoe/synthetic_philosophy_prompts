I'm working on a piece for the magazine I write for — long-form stuff on science and ideas — and this one has gotten away from me. A fishing village in Nova Scotia has been fighting for about six years over a wooden boat. Short version: the harbour club has owned a wooden fishing boat called the *Alder* since the 1930s, and for fifty years they replaced its planks one or two at a time as they rotted out — never more than a couple in a season, always launched in the spring, always raced in the Labour Day race under the same name. They kept every plank they took off in a shed behind the maritime museum. Then in 2019 there was a nasty split, half the members walked out, took the pile of old planks out of the shed, had a boat rebuilt from them to the original lines in a boatyard two towns over (they had to use about a fifth new wood, since the frames were shot), and launched it — also called the *Alder*, also wanting the heritage plaque and the race entry. So there are now two boats: one with all the history and almost none of the origional matter, one with most of the original matter and none of the history, and a heritage grant plus the summer charter fees riding on which one gets to be the *Alder*. Two details nobody in the village cares about but that I think matter: for about six years the old planks were just a stack of lumber in a shed, not arranged boatwise at all; and in 1998 the club replaced the stem and the keel of their boat in the same month, so there was a point where the continuously-used boat and the original shared maybe fifteen per cent of their wood.

I know the joke, my editor made it already: Ship of Theseus. But that isn't quite what grabbed me. I got a philosopher on the phone — friend of a friend, would only talk on background — who said the interesting question isn't which boat is the *same* boat but whether there was ever a *boat* there at all, as opposed to just planks arranged boat-wise, and then started going on about parts and wholes, which sounded useless at the time but I now think is the actual story. I'm also filing a shorter piece for the same issue about the living root bridges grown from fig roots in Meghalaya: roots trained across rivers over decades, individual roots dying and being replaced by new growth, the whole thing alive and growing, and nobody built it. And it struck me that if the boat question is "when does a bunch of parts add up to one thing," the bridge is the case where the answer is obviously yes and the boat is the case where it's murky — even though the boat is the one we made on purpose. That contrast can't be an accident and I can't say what it means.

So: what do I actually write? I owe readers a verdict, or at least an account of what *kind* of verdict is available, and I can't just print "there is no fact of the matter" without explaining what kind of non-fact it is and why anyone who doesn't read philosophy journals should care. One name-drop in one sentence is fine if it earns its place, but no footnotes — the desk will kill them. Please write it up in these sections, in this order: "The real question" (~200 words: what the fight is about underneath the Theseus stuff, and why the six years in the shed are the detail that breaks the usual framing), "Three ways it could come out" (~500 words: the three different things someone could think about when parts seem to add up to a whole, what each one says about the two boats, and what each one costs you to believe), "What I'd tell the two skippers" (~200 words: the practical version — who should get the plaque and why — without pretending the philosophy hands down a ruling), "My lede" (~60 words, two sentences, the way I should open the piece), and "Where this could go wrong" (~150 words: where a philosopher reading my piece would say I've definately oversimplified, and whether I should care).
