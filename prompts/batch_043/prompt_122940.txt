I work as a backend and systems engineer, mostly in Rust and Go, with a working knowledge of type theory, compilers, and distributed systems, and no formal philosophy training whatsoever. A colleague in robotics and I have gotten stuck on the philosophical dispute about whether knowing how to do something is really just a species of knowing that something is the case, or whether it is a different kind of state altogether. I want the whole debate restated in vocabulary I actually think in, and then I want to know exactly what gets lost. I am not asking for references, a reading list, or who said what first; I cannot chase citations and I do not care about priority. I care about the shape of the disagreement.

Here is the vocabulary I have on tap, and you should feel free to use distinctions adjacent to these if they help: declarative versus imperative artifacts; specification versus implementation; compile time versus run time; type checking, type inference, and proof-relevant types (I have a hazy grasp of propositions-as-types from type-level programming, so use it only if you can use it precisely); handwritten versus generated code; interpreters, virtual machines, and runtimes; solvers that turn a declarative description into an execution, in the way an SMT solver or a logic program does; test suites, property-based tests, mocking, and test harnesses; Heisenbugs and other failures that vanish under observation; documented procedures and runbooks; competence stored in a system rather than in a person, such as a compiler, an autoscaler, or a trained policy; and the question of whether a running process's behavior is traceable to some specific artifact in the repository.

As I currently understand the two sides: the anti-intellectualist says that knowing how to do something is not the same kind of thing as knowing that some proposition holds, and offers a regress argument, roughly that if every intelligent performance required a prior act of considering a proposition, and that act were itself an intelligent performance, then no one could ever get started. The passage of Ryle's that I managed to read, from The Concept of Mind, puts it this way: The consideration of propositions is itself an operation the execution of which can be more or less intelligent, less or more stupid. But if, for any operation to be intelligently executed, a prior theoretical operation had first to be performed and performed intelligently, it would be a logical impossibility for anyone ever to break into the circle. The intellectualist replies that knowing how is knowing a proposition, but knowing it under a special practical guise or mode of presentation, so that no separate prior act of contemplation is required. There are also arguments I have half-encountered: that knowing the relevant facts is insufficient for knowing how, and that knowing how comes in degrees while knowing that apparently does not.

I want all of that translated systematically, claim by claim, into my vocabulary. Please run the translation on three cases that I think are diagnostic. First, a senior engineer on my team who reliably tracks down data races in our scheduler but cannot articulate the rule he follows, and who gets worse if you ask him to narrate what he is doing. Second, a trained policy and a hand-written library that both sort correctly: whatever I want to say about the engineer's knowing how, does the same thing get said about a network, a library, or a compiler? Third, a junior engineer who can recite the debugging runbook verbatim and explain every step, but bungles the actual debugging under time pressure.

I also want you to adjudicate two framings I invented while arguing with my colleague, and tell me honestly whether each is a faithful rendering of something the philosophers are actually disputing or a confusion I introduced by importing my own distinctions. The first is what I call the interpreter problem: if knowing how is knowing that, then there should be a declarative artifact plus a general mechanism that turns it into successful action, and the interesting question is whether that mechanism is inside the agent or outside. If it is inside, intellectualism looks either trivial, because any ability can be paired with some description and some interpreter, or circular in the way the regress argument alleges. If it is outside, then it is hard to see how the agent's knowledge is what guides the action. The second is the contrast between runtime guidance and compile-time generation: must the practical proposition still be read by the process that acts, the way a config file is read at startup, or is it enough that it was consumed once to produce the executable, the way a spec is consumed by a code generator and then discarded? My suspicion is that the philosophical literature is running these together.

Finally, the residue report, which is what I actually want. Which claims translate cleanly, which translate only after you quietly distort them, and which have no expression at all in anything I have available? Be explicit when you have to smuggle something in, and flag it as smuggling rather than translation. In particular: does anything correspond to my distinction between competence living in a repository, in a runtime, and in trained weights, or is that distinction orthogonal to theirs and simply invisible from inside the debate? And for whatever does not survive, give me your considered verdict on whether it is a defect in my vocabulary, a sign that the philosophical claim was less clear than it looked, or a sign that the two sides are arguing past each other about different things.
