I am writing a critique of idealized-preference accounts for a graduate reading group: the family of views that identifies what is good for a person, or what a beneficial artificial agent should aim at, with what that person would want under idealized conditions such as full information, greater inferential power, full coherence, and correction for cognitive distortion, with coherent extrapolated volition as the most developed version. I have an argument that every view in this family is either empty or a device for presenting the designer's values as the agent's own. I do not believe the conclusion. I would like you to find the break and tell me what rejecting the offending step costs.

Let B be an agent's actual motivational base and let E be the output of an idealization procedure I applied to B. Any admissible I appears to need all of the following.

(1) Anchoring. E is a function of B, and not every B yields the same E. Otherwise I is not tracking this agent's values at all.
(2) Criticism. I must be able to discard a large share of B. Actual motivational bases are full of preferences formed under ignorance, coercion, advertising, depression, and adaptive downgrading; a procedure that could not reject those would not be an idealization of anything.
(3) Coherence. E is a single consistent ordering, rich enough to rank the options the agent actually faces.
(4) Non-imposition. I may not settle which candidate output is correct by appeal to anything outside B.

The argument then runs:

(5) By (2), I needs a standard for rejecting parts of B.
(6) That standard cannot be supplied by the parts of B it rejects, so it comes from outside B.
(7) By (2) and (3), for any realistic B there are many candidate outputs: discard one subset of B and close under coherence, discard a different subset and close under coherence, and so on, each yielding a distinct coherent ordering.
(8) Which subset is discarded is decided by the standard from (6), and that standard is not in B.
(9) So the content of E is settled by the standard rather than by B: hold the standard fixed and vary B within wide limits and E barely moves; hold B fixed and vary the standard and E moves a great deal.
(10) Hence either the standard is substantive enough to determine E, in which case the agent's actual attitudes are decorative and I is a machine for laundering the designer's values as the agent's, or it is not, in which case E is indeterminate and I yields no guidance. There is no third option.

The indeterminacy is not an artifact of one example. Idealization is not one operation but a package: adding information, increasing inferential power, becoming more the person one wishes one were, converging with others. Take an agent who is strongly competitive and who also wishes, on reflection, that she were less competitive. Apply more-information first and then more-the-person-I-wish-I-were and you reach a fixed point in which she competes superbly and no longer wishes to change. Apply them in the other order and you reach a fixed point in which the reflective wish is itself informed and strengthened and the competitiveness is gone. The operations do not commute, the outcome depends on the order of application, and nothing in B specifies an order. So (10) is reached a second time by an independent route.

Finally, the argument uses nothing specific to coherent extrapolated volition. Premises (1) through (4) are commitments of informed-desire accounts of welfare, ideal-advisor accounts, hypothetical-consent arguments in political theory, and the use of informed willingness to pay in policy analysis. If the argument is sound, they all fail. But the conclusion cannot be right: substituted judgment on behalf of patients who cannot consent, and the ordinary distinction between improving someone's life and manipulating them, both seem to run on exactly the appeal that the argument condemns.

So: which step fails, and why did it look sound? For each candidate rejection, tell me what else I have to give up, and give me the strongest version of the reply you would expect from someone who insists the argument is valid and that the conclusion should be accepted. I am most reluctant to surrender non-imposition; if I must give something up, I would give up the uniqueness of E first, and I want to know what an indeterminate coherent extrapolated volition is worth and whether the order-of-operations problem is the real difficulty rather than the externality claim in (6).
