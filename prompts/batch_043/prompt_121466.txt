I convene a second-year course on knowledge and rationality — twenty-four students, one seventy-five-minute session a week, twelve weeks in all. With six sessions left, I told my tutorial that there would be a surprise quiz at the start of one of them. One student replied in writing with the backward-induction argument and concluded, courteously, that I had announced something I cannot deliver. I intend to make good on the announcement, and I would like the attempt itself to be the lesson. So I need an activity, not a lecture.

The setting is a single seventy-five-minute session with those twenty-four students, and the mechanism should also be one I can honestly run across the six remaining weeks. The hard constraint is that the idea has to show up in what happens: no framing lecture, no naming of the paradox beforehand, no slide explaining the elimination argument. The announcement is the only setup. A second constraint is that several of my students already know the argument — one of them produced it unprompted — so the design must not depend on naivety, and I would prefer a design in which the students who reason the elimination through most confidently are the ones the quiz catches out. That is the outcome I want engineered by the rules, not merely hoped for.

I have also decided to replace the usual formulation with a threshold on rational credence: the quiz counts as a surprise on a given session if and only if, at the start of that session, a reasonable student's credence that the quiz is today — conditional on everything available to them — is below two-fifths. Please tell me whether that definition is the right one to run with or whether you would substitute another, and say explicitly what the substitution changes: I want the gap between this credence reading and the knowledge reading (on which the claim is that you will not, and cannot, know which session it is) to be something the class feels rather than something I assert.

What I want from you, concretely:

The rules and materials, and a minute-by-minute timeline. Because a six-week window will not fit in a session, the window must be compressed — eight or so rounds inside the seventy-five minutes, each round opening with sealed bids, the quiz itself a two-minute micro-task on the previous reading. Give me the bidding procedure, how bids are made incentive-compatible without money (bonus points, capped, or tokens), a proper scoring rule with your reasons for choosing it, and whether bidding should be individual, by teams of four, or both, with an aggregate displayed.

The other half of the mechanism is how my day gets selected. I want your recommendation on what I should commit to in advance, what I should keep secret, and why: a sealed envelope, a public randomization from a distribution the class knows, a randomization from a distribution only I know, or a selection made after I see the bids. Address the uncomfortable version of this question directly — whether the sealed envelope is a cheat or is simply what surprise consists in, given that a public randomization from a known distribution seems to be vulnerable to the very same elimination argument applied to the hazard rates.

Then the debrief. It should map particular moments in the session onto particular steps of the argument, and it should extract from the students' own bid sheets the one structural fact about a coherent set of bids over a fixed finite window that makes the whole thing work — tell me what that fact is, how the bid sheets will exhibit it, and how I stage things so that a student says it before I do. Tell me also what the mechanism cannot reproduce: my sense is that it can operationalize the credence reading but not the knowledge reading, and that the self-referential point — that the announcement may be true while being the sort of thing its addressees cannot know — is something I will have to supply in my own words. Say how to supply it without sounding like a capitulation.

Include an honest account of what will go wrong the first time: the student who bids one on the final round, the student who bids zero throughout out of cleverness, the student who refuses to play because the whole thing looks rigged, and the question of whether a surprise quiz carrying real marks is something I may run at all without consent. And tell me what I should do if the window expires with no quiz — whether the possibility of no quiz at all is a bug, a necessary condition of surprise, or the reductio I should have expected.
