My friend Jem is a semanticist who has decided, half for fun and half for a paper she keeps not writing, to tag every sentence she runs into as ANALYTIC, SYNTHETIC, or NEITHER. She's read Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" about four times and thinks he basically won, but she also thinks most people who say he won are cheating, because they keep sneaking something that behaves exactly like analyticity back in under a different name. So she's doing the tagging by some rule she refuses to state, and I've been keeping a list of her verdicts. Its got eight entries so far:

- "Every bachelor is unmarried" — ANALYTIC
- "If it's a vixen, it's a fox" — ANALYTIC
- "Either it rains tomorrow or it doesn't" — ANALYTIC
- "Nothing is red all over and green all over at once" — ANALYTIC
- "A kilometre is a thousand metres" — ANALYTIC, with some remark about it being "just the convention"
- "Copper conducts electricity" — SYNTHETIC
- "The population of Reykjavik is under two million" — SYNTHETIC
- "Every event has a cause" — NEITHER, and she muttered that it's more central to the web than half the things she called analytic, which she said was itself telling

Here's the sentence I want to put in front of her next. I made it up, so it can't be one she's seen in a textbook. Imagine a small field — call it limnacoustics — that studies the low booming noise people hear over big frozen lakes in winter. For two centuries its practitioners called the phenomenon "the boom" and built up a big descriptive apparatus: when it happens, how loud, which lakes, what the folk explanations were. Thirty years ago a group showed that the boom is methane released from the lake bed igniting at the surface. Everyone now accepts this, and the field has been rebuilt around it: the prediction tables, the sensor networks, the lake-ecology work, the historical reanalysis, all of it leans on that identification. Nothing else in limnacoustics is anywhere near as solid, and if it were given up the field would have to start again. But someone standing outside the field could drop it without disturbing anything else they believe.

The sentence is: "The boom is the ignition of methane released from the lake bed."

What I want from you:

1. Which tag does she give it, and how confident should I be? I've got a tenner on SYNTHETIC and I'd like to know if I'm about to lose it.
2. What rule is she most likely running, and what other rules fit all eight of those verdicts just as well? I can think of at least three that survive the list — something about necessity, something about what you can know from an armchair, something about how central a claim is — but I bet you can name a fourth.
3. Take the rules that survive and say what each of them predicts about the boom sentence, and where exactly they come apart. The way I've set the case up, I think at least one of those rules gives a different answer depending on how you spell it out, and I want to know which one and why.
4. I get to slip her one more sentence next week. Which single sentence would do the most work in telling the surviving rules apart from each other, and what would each verdict on it mean?
