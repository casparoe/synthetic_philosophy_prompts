I sit on a small technical panel that has to recommend a single discount-rate schedule for national infrastructure appraisal, where the projects in our portfolio have horizons ranging from about thirty years to about three hundred. Over the last few months I have come to think that the familiar fight about "pure time preference" is largely a pseudo-dispute, and I would like that conclusion checked before it goes into a report.

The decomposition everyone on the panel uses is r = ρ + ηg: the rate at which a unit of future consumption is discounted equals the pure rate of time preference plus the elasticity of the marginal utility of consumption multiplied by the growth rate of per-capita consumption. Take η ≈ 1 and g ≈ 2 per cent a year. Then a member who insists, on grounds of equal treatment of generations, that ρ = 0 is already recommending r ≈ 2 per cent; a member who wants something closer to observed market yields is simply choosing ρ ≈ 3. On this way of looking at it, the whole disagreement is a disagreement about one number, and that number is not really an independent ethical parameter at all, because it can be backed out empirically: observe market yields, measure g, estimate η from how people behave under risk, and set ρ = r − ηg. It also seems to follow that "zero pure time preference" is close to an empty slogan — with positive growth nobody in this framework discounts at zero anyway, and the low-rate camp is recommending a positive rate too.

I have a specific procedure in mind, and I want to know whether the reasoning above survives contact with it. Our benefits are of two kinds. The first is forgone consumption — construction costs, lost output — and for these I plan to convert everything into utility units using a constant-relative-risk-aversion function with the same η, and then discount those utility units at r = ρ + ηg ≈ 2 per cent. The second is lives saved, which I plan to value at current willingness to pay, escalate that value in line with income growth, and discount at the same 2 per cent. One of the projects is a coastal defence whose entire rationale is to avert a low-probability, large collapse in output roughly two centuries from now; I take it that this does not affect the rate, since on my view it is just another benefit stream. Finally, the panel does not agree about ρ. Rather than reopen the argument, I intend to take the arithmetic mean of members' preferred rates — they range from roughly 1 to 5 per cent, so the mean is about 3 per cent — and apply a single flat 3 per cent schedule all the way out to the three-hundred-year horizon.

Is the argument in my second paragraph sound, or is the pure rate of time preference doing work there that I have missed? Where exactly does the procedure in my third paragraph go wrong, if it does — double counting, a category mistake between discounting utility and discounting consumption, something about the willingness-to-pay escalation, or something about that coastal defence project? And is averaging the panel's preferred rates, then holding the result flat over three centuries, the right way to handle disagreement and horizon length, or should the panel be aggregating something other than rates?
