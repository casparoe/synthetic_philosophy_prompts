Here's the paper. I argue three things. First: the standard test for whether a dispute is merely verbal — hold the contested term fixed and see whether a disagreement still survives — can't actually be run from a neutral standpoint, because it needs a shared metalanguage in which to state the residual disagreement, and in the cases people really fight about the contested term is load-bearing in the metalanguage itself. You can't say what the universalist and the nihilist disagree about without using there is, or composite, or arranged, and those are the very words in dispute. So for the disputes that matter, asking whether it's verbal has no neutral answer; it only has an answer relative to which metalanguage you let the combatants share. Second, and this is the part I actually want the job for: once you see that, the residual question isn't the ontological one — what exists? — but an engineering one: which vocabulary should we institutionalize going forward? And that question is not arbitrary, because when a community takes up a vocabulary it inherits only part of the web of commitments that vocabulary arrived with; nobody revises every collateral belief when they switch terms. I call what they end up stuck with residual inferential load, and I claim we can compare candidate vocabularies by the load each one would saddle us with, given the community's actual non-semantic habits of inference. Third, the upshot: a dispute can be fully merely verbal and still matter more than plenty of substantive ones, because the load does the work, not the ontology. So "mere" is doing no work, and we should stop issuing verbal-dispute verdicts as shrugs. My two cases are composition — universalism versus nihilism about the table and the particles arranged tablewise — and a live one from my partner's lab: she's a computational biologist, and her group and a rival group are fighting about whether a cluster of cells that falls out of a single-cell sequencing pipeline is a real cell type or just a convenient description. There's a biomarker program and real money riding on it, and I use it to show the load-comparison actually doing something.
