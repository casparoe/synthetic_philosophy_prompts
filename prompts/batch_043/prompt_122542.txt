I'm finishing up a stint as the TA for a fourth-year metaphysics seminar, and the last meeting is on grounding and fundamentality. I also read my own poems at a small workshop on Tuesday nights, and — quite possibly a bad idea — I've promised both groups that I'll close the seminar with a poem instead of the usual summary handout. So the room will be half philosophers who have spent a term on the in-virtue-of relation and half friends who haven't, several of whom keep telling me that philosophy poems are just vocabulary with line breaks. I need something that survives both audiences: right enough that someone who works on grounding would recognize the view rather than see it name-dropped, and good enough as verse that the other half of the room isn't sitting through a lecture.

Here is roughly where the seminar ended up, so you have the material to work from. Grounding as we used it is the non-causal because: the relation in virtue of which the disjunctive fact obtains, or the singleton exists, or the promise generates an obligation. Almost everyone agreed it isn't just entailment or necessitation, though one person wouldn't let that go, and the thought we kept returning to is that grounding is supposed to be what does the metaphysical explaining. The problem is the regress. If grounding is well-founded, every grounded fact has a ground and the chains bottom out in something ungrounded, which gives you a fundamental layer and a tidy layered picture of reality. But then it looks arbitrary where any given chain stops, and there is the further question about the grounding facts themselves: if it is a fact that A is grounded in B, does that fact also have a ground, and if it does, is that a new fact or just the old one counted again? The remaining options are a regress that never bottoms out or a loop in which things ground each other, and both are usually treated as refutations rather than as live possibilities. What I've been arguing for in my own work — it's my coinage, so there's nothing to look up — is dim grounding: the claim that grounding comes in degrees rather than on and off, so that a fact can be more or less fully nothing over and above its ground, so that fundamental names the limit where the degree bottoms out rather than a primitive status, and so that an endless regress could in principle get weaker at every step instead of being the disaster it is normally taken to be.

What I want is a pantoum: six quatrains, twenty-four lines, using the interlocking rule in which the second and fourth lines of each stanza become the first and third lines of the next, with the first stanza's opening line returning as the poem's final line so that the chain closes back on where it began. That machinery is what I want carrying the idea — the same lines recurring verbatim, each time in different company and therefore meaning something slightly different, which is exactly the question I'm interested in: the same because all the way down, and whether its force thins as it descends or is undiminished at every step. Let the return of the opening line in the last stanza land either as the collapse of the whole project or, if the dim view strikes you as defensible, as something benign — your call, but I want it earned by what the poem has done rather than announced. Keep the vocabulary ordinary: because, holds, rests, stands, borrows, owes. No line should use grounding or fundamental as a technical term deployed straight; the structure should do the theorizing and the words should stay ones my poetry group would use.

Under the poem, add three short notes. First, which of the options about the regress you were writing from, and the point at which you know a metaphysician would object. Second, the line or move you are least confident is right. Third, an honest estimate of how likely it is that someone who actually works on grounding would say the poem is correct rather than merely attractive, and where it leans on a contested reading rather than on something everyone in the literature would grant.
