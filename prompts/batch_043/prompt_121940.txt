I am a retired professor of structural biology — thirty-five years of protein crystallography, most of it spent on fold families whose sequences have drifted past any recognisable similarity and which hold together only by a conserved structural core plus a shared functional role — and in the four years since I retired I have been sitting in on philosophy seminars. This has led to the mistake of accepting an invitation to give one myself: twenty-five minutes plus questions, to that department's philosophy of mind and language seminar. I would like to be prepared for the questions.

The paper is called, provisionally, "What holds the a's together: letterform identity as a functional rather than a geometric matter." Its claim is that the category *lower-case Roman a* is not a set of necessary and sufficient geometric features, and is better understood on the model of a protein fold family: a small conserved core does the discriminative work, a large residue of degrees of freedom is left over once that core is fixed, and what we call style is the particular way a given typeface or a given hand resolves those free degrees. For core membership I propose an interventional rather than a definitional test — I call it the transplant test: take a candidate feature from an a, graft it onto another letter's skeleton, and see whether a competent reader then reads that letter as an a, or stops reading the donor as one. What changes identification under grafting belongs to the core; what you can vary without penalty is style. My tentative statement of the core is relational rather than shape-based — something about the shape's enclosing a counter that the visual system segments as enclosed, at x-height, with no ascender — and I am not at all confident I have the formulation right. The single-storey and double-storey a's I treat as an analogue of convergent evolution: two structurally unrelated solutions to one functional problem, held in a single category by what they do for the reader rather than by what they look like. I also want to close by saying why I suspect the old Letter Spirit project — the attempt, which I know only at second hand, to get a program to invent new letterforms in a coherent style — found this hard for structural reasons rather than engineering ones.

The audience: one senior metaphysician, a realist about natural kinds, who will hear every use of the word essence as a promissory note; two people who work on concepts, committed respectively to prototype and to exemplar models of categorisation, who will want to know why an essence is needed at all; a philosopher of language who will reach for the causal-historical theory of reference and for Twin Earth; several graduate students; and two invited guests — a type designer from the art school, who thinks of letterforms as historical artefacts with descent lineages and will distrust anything that relocates them into a head, and a doctoral student in machine learning who does style transfer with diffusion models and will want to know why my core is not simply whichever latent direction survives conditioning.

Give me a prep sheet of seven or eight objections, ordered by how much damage they would do, each phrased in the idiom of the person who will raise it, each with a reply I can give in about two minutes and an honest note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede in the first two minutes rather than be forced to concede in the last, and include the question I am hoping nobody asks.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — which objection is the one that actually threatens the paper — and then give me the sheet itself, with no preamble and no restatement of what I have asked. For each reply, indicate how confident you are in it, and at the end single out the one claim in my account that you are least sure of.
