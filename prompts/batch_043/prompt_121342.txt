I've been arguing with someone in the comments under a decision-theory blog post — I never studied any of this formally, I got hooked after reading about intransitive preferences somewhere and have been working through it on my own — and the thread basically died when he typed: "money pumps only punish myopia, not inconsistency." He meant it as a conversation-ender. My claim was that an agent with cyclic preferences, who prefers A to B and B to C and C to A and will happily pay a penny each time to swap, is thereby irrational, because you can walk them around the cycle and they end up holding A again minus three cents. His reply was that this only shows the agent was choosing one offer at a time without looking ahead; an agent who works backwards from the last choice and refuses the trades that lead nowhere, or who just picks the best overall plan at the start and sticks to it even when they'd rather switch, never gets pumped at all — so, he says, nothing is wrong with the preferences themselves, only with a particular way of acting on them. I think there's something in that but I also think the slogan is doing several different jobs at once and I can't tell which one he actually needs it to do.

Two cases I came up with while trying to push back, which I can't tell apart: one where no trader ever shows up but the agent still pays, because to avoid the cycle they have to buy a commitment device — say a deposit they forfeit if they switch — so they lose money without anyone exploiting them, which makes me wonder whether being pumpable costs you something even when you're never pumped; and one where the cycle isn't clean and the agent ends up holding C, which they rank below the A they started with, so they've lost money and position. My working hypothesis is that the real fight is hiding in what a pump is supposed to show — that you end up worse off by your own lights versus that somebody else profits off you, or that the possibility of a pump is a defect even if the trader never exists — and that "myopia" is being used to smear together a psychological weakness, a modelling assumption, and a claim about what rationality even applies to (choices one at a time, or plans). It's also not obvious to me wether his point collapses into "irrationality just means being exploitable," which seems to load the whole conclusion in at the start.
