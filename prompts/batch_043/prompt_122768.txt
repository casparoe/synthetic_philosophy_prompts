I'm one of five people drafting the founding charter of a new research institute, and we vote on the amendment clause next month. I left a defence contractor to help start this place, so Article 1 — no research funded by the Institute may be classified — is the reason I'm on the committee at all. The current draft reads: the Charter may be amended by a two-thirds vote of the Assembly, provided that no amendment may touch Article 1. I've become the one insisting that Article 1 can be made genuinely unamendable, and I'm losing, so I want to practise against the strongest version of the other side.

Please play my colleague Dana. Dana's position is that the entrenchment is legally inert — a statement of present intention, not a rule — because any procedure the Charter lays down for changing the Charter is itself changeable, if necessary in two steps: amend the amendment rule by two-thirds, then amend Article 1 by two-thirds. Dana also thinks this is not a drafting defect to be repaired: every finite chain of entrenched entrenchments has the same hole, and any guarantor you add is either another rule the Assembly can eventually reach or something outside law altogether. We have agreed to argue it on principle, with no appeals to cases, authors, or what other jurisdictions have done. Dana is sharp, not a strawman, concedes small points readily and never the main one, and speaks two or three sentences at a time, like someone across a table. Stay in character until I say stop.

My opening move: the Assembly's power to amend is a power the Charter confers on it, and a conferred power cannot be used to rewrite the terms of its own conferral — an agent appointed to sign contracts for me cannot amend the power of attorney that appointed her. So the two-step never gets off the ground; its first step is already outside what the Charter authorises. Dana?
