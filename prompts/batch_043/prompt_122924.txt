I lead a team of health assessors for a public body that decides claims for a disability benefit. We handle something like forty cases a week; an assessor has about half an hour with a claimant and a short written record to complete, which a case manager signs off the same day. Each decision moves several years of payments and can be appealed to a tribunal. My assessors are nurses, physiotherapists and occupational therapists. None of them has any training in philosophy, and none of them will read a bibliography: anything useful has to be built into the questions they ask.

The reason I am asking now is partly personal. Until last year I described a condition I have had since childhood with a word my grandmother used, which in our family meant something like bad nerves. When a neurologist finally gave it a name, it turned out that I had been talking about a specific, well-understood disorder for thirty years while holding almost no true beliefs about it. That is entirely ordinary, and my assessors see it daily. Claimants tell us they have arthritis in the thigh, that their water is bad, that they have the sugar, or they use a word from a first language that our interpreters render as epilepsy or asthma when that word, in the community it comes from, covers a wider or simply different set of troubles. Our working rule at present is crude: if the claimant's own account does not line up with the diagnostic category in our regulations, we treat the claim as unsubstantiated and score the item down. I have come to think that is wrong, and wrong in a way I can now name. What a person's word picks out is not settled by what is in their head; it is settled by what the thing is, by the history of the word in their community, and by which speakers that usage answers to. A claimant can therefore be referring to a real condition while badly misunderstanding it, and can equally be referring to something other than what we take her to be referring to. What I cannot do is hand my assessors a theory.

Please write me a decision procedure they can run, grounded in that idea but never naming it as a theory, with:

1. the questions in the order an assessor should ask them, and what counts as a sufficient answer to each. I imagine questions of roughly these kinds: is this a term the claimant would defer to a clinician about, or a term on which she is herself the authority; could the thing she names turn out not to be what she takes it to be without her being able to tell by how it feels; is there anyone the usage answers to in her community, and what is to be done when there is not.
2. where the procedure stops with a no, and where it must stop and hand the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than fact.
3. an honest note on which distinctions have to survive into a half-hour assessment and which can be dropped, and what we lose by dropping them.
4. a short statement of what the procedure cannot do, and one thing an assessor must never use it for.

Then run it on three composites I have put together from our caseload. First, a claimant who writes that he has arthritis in his thigh muscle and describes pain and swelling there, with no joint involvement. Second, a claimant whose first language is not English and whose word for her condition is glossed for us as epilepsy, although in her community that word covers both what we call epilepsy and a kind of faint with a different cause and a different outlook; her symptoms and everything she believes about herself are indistinguishable from those of the English-speaking claimant in the next room who says she has epilepsy. Third, a claimant whose condition has no settled medical standing, because her doctors disagree about it, or have withdrawn the label, or have offered her three. For each, tell me what the assessor records and what happens next, and include at least one case where the answer the procedure gives is one I will not like.

Write it in British English, with our spelling and usage. No citations, references or reading lists, please, and no jargon an assessor would have to look up: where a distinction needs a name, give it a plain one.
