Two people in my department got into it after a visiting grad student from a country where tipping isnt a thing left nothing on a $40 diner bill, and I somehow ended up as the referee without having a view of my own.

(1) Marta said the amount is almost beside the point: in much of the US, restaurant pay is set up on the assumption that tips fill the gap, so he wasn't just ignoring a custom, he was withholding money from the only person in a position to give it.

(2) Dev said that just relocates the wrongness into bad labor law the visitor didn't create and can't change; one un-tipped table doesn't shrink anyone's paycheck, so at worst he was ungenerous, and being ungenerous isn't the same as wronging someone.

(3) Marta conceded tipping might be a bad example and switched to queueing: where he's from people cluster at the bus stop and board in whatever order, but here there's a strict line, and if he walks to the front he isn't merely rude -- he's taking a slot in a scheme that only works because everyone else stays in it. He'd be exempting himself from a rule whose whole point is that it binds people.

(4) Dev said free-riding needs the practice to be worth maintaining and his compliance to make a difference, and neither is shown: one queue-jumper doesn't collapse a queue, the looser norm back home works fine too, so the local line is one arbitrary equilibrium among several, and a convention nobody is ever permitted to deviate from isn't a convention, it's a law. He added that the visitor has no standing to reshape the convention either -- he can't vote on it -- so demanding obedience while denying him any say is a bit like taxation without representation.

(5) Marta said arbitrariness can't be what dissolves the duty, since conventions like which side of the road you drive on have no better alternative and plainly still bind you; and she added two things: locals can't tell whether a non-complier is ignorant or just defecting, so visible non-compliance degrades the signal even when it's harmless, and the visitor is in a kind of last-period position, taking the coordination benefits and leaving before any costs land. Jaywalking was her third case: in cities where everyone crosses mid-block, the visitor who waits for the light isn't being law-abiding, he's failing to do what drivers there actually predict.

(6) Dev's last move was that a visitor usually can't tell which local conventions are the load-bearing ones (tipping vs. whether you make small talk with the cashier vs. jaywalking), so a general duty to follow local convention is a duty to do something he has no way of identifying -- too demanding to be a duty at all. Defaulting to compliance when unsure is cheap and fine, but that's prudence, not obligation.

Can you go through it move by move and say who's right about what, not just who wins overall? And is there something both of them are missing -- some distinction, or some case, that reframes the whole dispute?
