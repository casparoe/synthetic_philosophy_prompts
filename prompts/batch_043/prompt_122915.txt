I have been working through the torture-versus-dust-specks problem with a small reading group, and I have ended up with an argument for the aggregative side that I can neither refute nor believe. I would be grateful for a diagnosis: which step should be given up, and what does giving it up cost me elsewhere?

(1) The boundaries between persons are not moral joints. That one stream of consciousness contains a stretch of suffering and a different stream of consciousness contains a numerically distinct stretch of suffering is a fact about how the suffering is packaged, not about how bad it is.

(2) So harms aggregate across persons. If an outcome in which one person suffers a harm h is worse than an outcome in which no one does, then an outcome in which N people each suffer h is worse still, and worse the larger N is.

(3) Aggregation is not optional. Whoever denies (2) is committed to holding that the torture of a million people is no worse than the torture of one, and more generally that the number of victims never matters apart from the identity of some particular victim. Nobody believes that.

(4) A dust speck in the eye is a harm of small but positive magnitude. It is not nothing: one would pay something, however little, to be spared it; imperceptibility is not nonexistence; and a sufficient accumulation of such irritations within a single life would be a grave harm indeed.

(5) If a harm has positive magnitude, then for any harm H of finite magnitude there is some finite number n such that n instances of the smaller harm, distributed over n distinct people, amount to something worse than H.

(6) Therefore, for some astronomically large but finite N — ten to the thirtieth will do — the outcome in which N people each endure a momentary irritation is worse than the outcome in which one person is tortured for fifty years.

(7) And so the torture of a single person is the lesser evil next to a policy that inflicts a trivial annoyance on a vast number of people; the prohibition on torture is at best a rule of thumb; and what makes an atrocity an atrocity is only the size of the total of micro-level disutility it leaves behind.

I cannot accept (7). Of the premises, I am most attached to (3): if I must choose, I would rather keep the judgement that a million tortures is worse than one torture than keep anything tidy about aggregation. I am also reluctant to give up (4), since denying it seems to commit me to treating every headache, every itch, and every minor disappointment as morally weightless.

Two escapes I have tried and failed to make work. The first is a threshold view: harms below some level of severity are too small to aggregate across persons, and harms above it aggregate. But severity looks continuous — speck, itch, mild headache, toothache, a day of nausea, a broken wrist, a broken leg, a month in solitary, fifty years of torture — and I cannot see how to site the threshold without arbitrariness, nor how the admission that we cannot locate the line is supposed to answer the original question. The second is to assign sub-threshold harms a magnitude that is positive but infinitesimal relative to any severe harm, so that no finite multiple of them ever reaches it. I cannot tell whether this is a genuine alternative or merely the same problem relocated into the arithmetic. Nor is it helpful to me to be told that the two outcomes are incomparable: I have to choose between them, and an incomparability that leaves me equally free to choose either is just the view I was trying to test.

So which step goes, and why did it look sound? If the difficulty lies in the passage from (2) and (3) to (5) — if the aggregation needed for the million-tortures judgement is in some way weaker than what (5) requires — then I want to know precisely what the weaker principle is, whether it really does deliver the million-tortures judgement on its own, and what it then does to ordinary cost-benefit reasoning, where we routinely weigh a small risk of a severe harm against a certainty of a mild one. If the difficulty is somewhere else, I would like it located. And either way, I would like to be told clearly what I end up unable to say.
