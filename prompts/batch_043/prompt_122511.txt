I convene a small working group on the Dennettian apparatus — real patterns, and the physical, design, and intentional stances — together with a senior colleague who has spent several years assembling what she calls a case list: a long series of systems, each one paired with her verdict on two questions, namely whether adopting the intentional stance toward the system is in order, and whether the pattern thereby described is a real pattern rather than a façon de parler. She has never explained what she takes realness to consist in beyond the rough criterion that a pattern is real when there is a description of the data more efficient than the bit map, and when pressed she has declined to say more. So the verdicts are all the evidence there is.

Her verdicts so far, as she recorded them:

- A mercury-in-glass thermostat: intentional stance not in order; nothing real is picked out by saying it wants the room at twenty degrees.
- A brute-force chess program of the 1990s: in order; saying it thinks its knight is trapped picks out a real pattern.
- A hand-written sorting routine whose source code we have in front of us: not in order; the code is the better description, and there is nothing further for the intentional vocabulary to track.
- The ventilation architecture of a termite mound: the architecture is a real pattern, but a design-stance one; the intentional stance toward the colony is not in order.
- A tropical cyclone: not in order; no real pattern beyond what the physical stance already provides.
- A desert ant's path integration across a featureless plain: in order; the ascriptions are real.
- A hardware random-number generator: not in order; the output is noise, not a pattern.

The system she has agreed to rule on next is one I will call Vesper. Vesper was found by an automated search over a very large space of networks; the search scored candidates only on a coarse behavioral measure across a family of tasks and never looked at, constrained, or described any internal state, and no human being has ever specified what any part of Vesper is for. It has no architecture of internal error signalling or self-correction beyond the external score. Its behavior is predicted far better by attributing beliefs and desires to it than by any physical- or design-stance description we are able to construct. Two further things matter, I think. First, the fit is not unique: several mutually incompatible assignments of contents fit its behavior equally well, and nothing internal to Vesper that anyone has found distinguishes among them. Second, the tasks were rich enough that predicting well required tracking a great deal about the world, so it is not open to us to say that nothing in Vesper's history selected for accuracy. Nothing in my question turns on Vesper's being a neural network of the now familiar kind, though it may as well be.

What I would like from you is the following, and not a survey of the literature, which I can read myself. First, set out the two or three rules — the tests for in-orderness and for realness — that fit all seven of her verdicts, and say which of them you think she is actually running and why the others fit the record just as well. Second, say what each rule predicts about Vesper, on each of the two questions separately, and give your best guess at her verdict with your reasons; if a rule answers the two questions differently, say so. My own inclination is that she will say the stance is in order but the contents indeterminate, though I cannot argue for it. Third, tell me what she commits herself to either way: what principle she adopts if she rules that the stance is in order, and what if she rules that it is not. Fourth, propose the case that would settle the matter. I had meant to propose a market or an insect colony, on the grounds that these are systems toward which people adopt the intentional stance readily even though nothing designed them to have the contents ascribed; but it now seems to me that such a case comes out the same on both readings I have in mind and would settle nothing. If you agree, tell me what would.

End your reply with a single line, with nothing after it, in exactly this form:

\answer{prediction: <label>; rule: <two- or three-word name>}

where <label> is one of: in-order-and-real, in-order-but-merely-instrumental, not-in-order, underdetermined — and where the rule names the test you take her to be running.
