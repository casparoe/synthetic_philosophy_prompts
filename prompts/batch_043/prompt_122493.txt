I am circulating a short position statement on the ontology and epistemology of mathematics to a small reading group, and I would like the set of commitments in it audited before I send it out. I am fairly confident about each thesis taken on its own; what I cannot tell is whether they are jointly stable. Here they are.

(1) Determinacy. Every sentence in the language of set theory has a determinate truth value. The continuum hypothesis is either true or false, and its independence from the standard axioms is a limit on us, not a feature of the subject matter.

(2) Abstractness. Sets, numbers, functions — whatever mathematical objects there are — are abstract in the usual sense: not located in space or time, causally inert, and not the sorts of things we could interact with or be affected by.

(3) Epistemic naturalism. Knowledge requires that the knower stand in some relation to the fact known that a broadly naturalistic picture of us can digest. Causal contact where causal contact makes sense; where it does not, at least reliable tracking of how things stand, with nothing brute, miraculous, or secured by stipulation.

(4) A priority. The warrant for basic mathematical claims is not empirical: it does not derive from sensory observation or from the confirmation of scientific theories. I also hold that mathematics is fallible and revisable all the way down to its axioms, and that mathematicians reasonably rely on considerations that are not deductive. A colleague tells me that this combination is already unstable — that fallibilism about a domain plus reliance on non-deductive considerations adds up to a kind of empiricism about it, which I have just denied. I do not think that follows, but I would rather have it checked than assumed.

(5) Structuralism about identification. There is no fact of the matter as to which set-theoretic objects the natural numbers are, or which construction of the real numbers is the real numbers; what matters is structure, and any system playing the relevant role plays it equally well. I take this to dispose of the familiar worry that because arithmetic can be reduced to set theory in many mutually incompatible ways, our arithmetical beliefs cannot be about anything in particular.

(6) Against indispensability. The fact, if it is one, that our best physical theories quantify over mathematical objects is no reason to believe those objects exist. A paraphrase-friendly or fictionalist treatment of scientific theory is available, and the inference from we cannot state physics without them to they are real strikes me as a bad one.

(7) Extrinsic evidence in set theory. The serious case for new axioms — large cardinal axioms, forcing axioms — is not that they are self-evident, but that they unify, settle questions left independent, and generate a fruitful theory. I want to say that this is evidence of their truth, not merely of their convenience or productivity.

(8) The iterative conception. Our grip on what set theory is about comes from the iterative picture: sets are what you get by iterating the powerset operation along the ordinals. But I concede that the conception, stated that way, leaves the continuum hypothesis open.

One further verdict, which I assert in the draft and which several of the above are meant to underwrite. Suppose two extensions of our best set theory disagree about some sentence S. The two theories are mutually interpretable. Each is well motivated on its own terms; each settles questions the other leaves open; each has consequences the other cannot reach; and nothing available to the practice — proof, simplicity, fruitfulness, unification, consensus among practitioners — breaks the tie. I want to say: even so, one of the two gets S right and the other gets it wrong, and there is a fact of the matter about which.

What I would like from you is an audit, not a survey.

First, go through the commitments and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension and which only look like it. Please discriminate rather than flagging everything; I suspect at least one of the apparent conflicts dissolves on inspection, and I would like to know which one and why.

Second, rank the genuine tensions by severity, in your own order rather than the order I listed them in. For each, say precisely what I would have to give up or qualify in order to remove it, and what that concession would cost me elsewhere in the set.

Third, tell me whether any of (1) through (8) is doing no work in the package — something I could drop without loss and without noticing.

Fourth, identify the single revision that would clean up the most, and say what it would commit me to that I might not want.

Finally, if the tie case is where the package comes apart, say so explicitly, and say which of my commitments it is actually in tension with. I do not trust my own sense of which thesis is bearing the weight there.

Please argue the diagnosis out of the commitments themselves rather than by attaching a label to my position. I know which labels a reader would reach for; what I want to know is which of my theses is doing the damage.
