I'm setting the final for my third-year metaphysics seminar and I want one essay question on dispositions that can't be answered by just reciting the fink / mask / antidote taxonomy. We've done the simple conditional analysis, the standard counterexamples, and the Lewis-style 'retain the causal basis' repair (I don't need citations in the question or the answer — I want to grade argument, not reading).

Here's roughly the shape I have in mind, but please tighten it into a proper exam question. Give them the following case, which I made up and which they won't have seen:

A turbine blade is made of a ceramic in phase α, and it is fragile — it shatters under a hard impact — and it is fragile in virtue of having the α microstructure. The blade also carries a monitoring device that is permanently fitted to it (so it's part of the object, not something external). The device detects an incoming impact a microsecond before contact and reorganises the surface layer of the ceramic into phase β, a different microstructure. Phase β is, quite independently, also a shatter-under-impact structure: blades made entirely of phase-β ceramic shatter under hard impacts just as readily as α ones do. Now the blade is struck hard, and it shatters. Call this the handover case.

The question should push students to do four things: (i) say what cell of the taxonomy the handover case occupies, given that the relevant conditional comes out true and the object does exactly what fragile things do, yet it does not retain the basis it had when we called it fragile; (ii) say whether the retention fix gives the right verdict here, and why the fact that the device is intrinsic matters; (iii) notice that the obvious way to save the fix — letting the retained property be the disjunction of the two microstructures — is a trap, and say why; and (iv) take a stand on what should be repaired, and what that repair costs. I want them to end up at the point where the real issue is how finely dispositions are individuated and whether a 'causal basis' has to be a natural / non-gerrymandered property, rather than at 'counterfactuals are tricky lol'.

Practical constraints: 45 minutes, about 1000 words, 20 marks. Please give me the question as I'd print it, a model answer of around 700 words, and a marking scheme with five must-hit points, the marks each is worth, one line on how candidates typically botch each point, and a short note on what separates an A from a B. Make the model answer actually commit to a view and name its cost — I don't want a model answer that just lists options. Also, if the question as I've sketched it has an obvious loophole (say, a student who responds by disputing that the blade is fragile at the moment of ascription), either close the loophole in the wording or tell me in the scheme how to credit that move.
