I have no academic background in philosophy. I first met the sea-battle problem in a popular book years ago — the one where Aristotle asks whether it is already true, today, that there will be a naval battle tomorrow — and I have been turning it over on my own ever since, mostly by asking what would happen if a government actually tried to run a navy on that doctrine. I would like to commission a satirical piece on that theme.

Please write it as an official document: Bulletin No. 9 of the Admiralty's Office of Future Contingents, in a country where the doctrine is not a thesis defended in a seminar but settled administrative practice — for some statements about the future there is as yet nothing for them to be true or false of, and the Office exists to keep the books accordingly. Around 600–700 words, in numbered sections, plus a short sidebar. The voice should be deadpan civil-service pomposity: forms to be filed in triplicate, jurisdictional squabbles between branches, anodyne reassurances.

A few things I would like the bulletin to contain, because they are where the doctrine starts to creak:

A three-column forecast ledger — TRUE / FALSE / NOT YET EITHER — together with a standing notice that the sentence saying either the engagement will happen or it will not is, the Office concedes, true and even necessarily true, and yet of no operational use whatsoever to anybody.

A ruling by the Office's Marine Assurance Branch, reasoning from an old argument called the Master Argument, that nothing which neither is nor ever will be the case was ever possible — with the consequence that a fleet lost in an engagement that never takes place cannot be indemnified, since its loss was never possible in the first place.

The Office's own invention, which I would like you to develop: applications for a provisional determinacy, a licence allowing a commander to have one specified future statement treated, for planning purposes only, as if it already had a truth value. These licences are non-recourse — the borrowed determinacy is discharged automatically when the day arrives — and the Office presents the fact that no licence has ever defaulted as proof of the system's soundness.

A correspondence column in which a commander writes in to say that, on the Office's own view, whether he wins is already settled one way or the other, so there is no point in his sailing or provisioning or consulting the barometer. The Office's reply should be the real reply available to this position — that some things come about through deliberation and action — but delivered in a way that quietly undercuts it, and it should not, I think, end on a confident note.

Finally, the forecasts themselves: the Office rates engagements as leaning toward occurring or leaning against, and insists that a leaning is not a probability and may not be aggregated, priced, or wagered on, which is of course exactly what the Assurance Branch does with them the same afternoon.

Two constraints matter to me. The jokes have to come from getting the doctrine right: a reader who knows this material should recognise the idle argument and the collapse of possibility into actuality without either ever being named, and should feel the satire is fair to what is being mocked rather than invented out of thin air. And a reader who knows nothing about it should still follow the comedy. So no naming of arguments, no attributions, no citations, no footnotes, and no winking at the audience. Do not resolve the philosophical question; let the absurdity of the administration make the reader feel what the view costs.
