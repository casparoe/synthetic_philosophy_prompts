I am teaching a single eighty-minute session on the Liar in a cross-listed upper-level elective on truth and paradox. Roughly half the room are philosophy majors who have had propositional and first-order logic but no metalogic; the other half are mathematics and computer-science students who have seen the statements of Gödel's first incompleteness theorem and the halting problem and nothing at all of philosophical logic. I do not need the paradox explained to me. What I need is a plan for explaining it, and I want the plan's reasoning about sequence stated explicitly as dependency: what cannot be introduced before what, and what breaks if it is.

My working thought is that the session should present the paradox as a collision among a small set of theses each of which is individually compelling — bivalence, classical consequence, the unrestricted disquotational behaviour of *true*, and the capacity of a language to talk about its own sentences, semantic closure as it is usually called — so that each major approach can then be seen as a decision about which thesis to surrender. But I am not confident about the order in which those four should be made explicit, nor about whether the collision should be derived first and diagnosed afterwards, or whether some diagnosis has to be on the table before the derivation will look like a problem rather than a conjuring trick.

The carrying example is where I am most uncertain, and I want it argued for rather than assumed. Should the whole session hang on *this sentence is false*, on *this sentence is not true*, or on a two-sentence loop? Say what such a sentence must have and must lack if it is to bear the weight: I take it that it must be free of any dependence on contingent fact, so that nobody leaves thinking the difficulty is empirical or epistemic, and free of indexicality, so that it cannot be blamed on the shifting reference of *I* or *now*. Tell me also what the choice of example quietly decides about the space of solutions — my worry is that building on *false* rather than *not true* makes the gap response look more comfortable than it really is.

Then the misconceptions, and the point in the sequence at which each must be headed off, not merely the reply to it. The ones I expect: that the sentence is a mere trick, or is meaningless; that the fix is to ban self-reference; that there is no problem because the sentence simply lacks a truth value; that a gap response and a glut response are the same response; that this is just Gödel's theorem or the halting problem in another vocabulary; that the trouble is vagueness in the predicate *true*; and that distinguishing object language from metalanguage settles it. Some of these I would rather pre-empt before the derivation than correct afterwards, and I suspect the Gödel one has to be handled at the moment self-reference is introduced, or it will permanently distort how the computer-science students hear everything that follows.

Finally, two things. First, a check: one question, or one short written task, whose answer would show me a student has grasped the structure rather than the slogan. My candidate is to hand them a revenge sentence for whichever solution they have just been given and ask what their solution must say about it and whether they can say it in the language they have been given — tell me if there is something better. Second, an explicit list of what I should refuse to explain at this level and say that I am refusing: I have in mind the model-theoretic machinery behind fixed-point approaches, the details of hierarchical constructions, dialetheic logic, the set-theoretic paradoxes, and the question whether revenge can be escaped at all. Tell me which of those I should name aloud as omissions, and how to phrase the refusal so that it reads as a decision about scope rather than as an evasion.
