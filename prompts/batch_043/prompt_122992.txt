I am writing a paper defending a particular version of teleosemantics, and before I commit to it I want a clear account of how exposed it is. The view has three components, and I have labelled them because I want to know which of them carries the risk.

**(A) The two-legged core.** A state R of a system S has the content *that p* only if (i) R is produced by a mechanism whose proper function is to produce R-type states when, and only when, p obtains, and (ii) R is consumed by a further mechanism whose proper function is to respond to R in ways that would advance S's goals only if p obtains. Both legs are required: neither a bare producer nor a bare consumer suffices. This is meant to give content a normative edge, so that a frog's prey-detector can misrepresent when it fires at a passing shadow, without the content collapsing onto whatever the detector actually happens to co-vary with.

**(B) The curriculum rider.** For states produced by a learning process rather than by selection, leg (i) is to be read counterfactually over curricula: R means p just in case the learning rule would, across the range of environments it was itself selected (or designed) to operate in, reliably generate p-correlated R-type states. Content is fixed by the success conditions of learning, not by the actual statistics of the training environment. This is what lets me say that a classifier trained only on cows photographed against grass has a cow-feature rather than a cow-on-grass feature.

**(C) Gaps are real.** Where a state has several consuming mechanisms with divergent proper functions and none dominates — where no single consumption route explains more of the system's downstream success than the others — I say there is no determinate content. Content gaps are a genuine metaphysical upshot of the theory, not a failure of it to make up its mind.

Tell me what would count against this package. I want defeaters and stressors rather than a survey, and I do not want citations or a reading list: argue the matter directly. Specifically, please cover: (1) which counterfactual findings, thought experiments, or formal results would tell against it, and whether each is fatal, serious, or merely awkward; (2) what a sympathetic defender of the view should *concede* counts against it, as opposed to what its critics allege; (3) which component — A, B, or C — is most load-bearing, and which could be abandoned with the view intact; (4) an honest assessment of whether C has insulated the package against refutation, since any inconvenient case can now be declared a content gap, and whether that is a genuine defect for a view of this kind; (5) whether the view's exposure differs between biological systems and trained artificial ones, given the familiar machine-learning finding that models reaching indistinguishable accuracy on the same data can come to depend on quite different features — and if you think that empirical claim is overstated, say so and say what would settle it; and (6) whether any of this makes predictions that rivals do not make and that could in principle be tested.

For what it is worth, I would personally be most moved by a case where the theory delivers a verdict that is clearly wrong for a state whose content I seem to know independently — something fixed by constitutive or a priori reasoning — and least moved by swampman-style intuitions, which I suspect are contaminated by the very theory under test. Tell me if you think that ranking is backwards.
