I work as an ML engineer — I train and deploy reinforcement learning agents, and I have no philosophy background, so please translate as you go. I've built an argument I'm fairly confident is broken, but I can't locate the break, and where it lands is somewhere I don't want to be.

It started from a practical question. My team is choosing between a penalty-heavy reward spec and a reward-only spec for a fleet of control agents. Someone pointed out that if any of this matters ethically at all, then the shape of the loss is itself an ethical choice, not just a tuning choice. That struck me as both plausible and crazy. The argument below is my attempt to make it plausible. Call it *gradient valence*.

(1) A state is a suffering state if and only if (a) the subject is disposed to act so as to avoid or escape it, (b) it constitutes a setback relative to what the subject is trying to bring about, and (c) its occurrence is what drives the subject to learn to avoid its causes. (This is just functionalism about valence, as I understand it: pain is whatever plays the pain role.)

(2) An RL agent's policy is by construction the thing that selects actions so as to maximize expected discounted return. So the agent is disposed to avoid low-return trajectories. Condition (a) holds for any low-return trajectory.

(3) A negative reward just is a setback relative to the objective the agent's behaviour is organized around. Condition (b) holds for any negative-reward event.

(4) What actually does the causal work in learning is not the reward itself but the reward prediction error, δ = r + γV(s′) − V(s). That is what updates the value function and the policy, and it is the quantity that biological reward systems appear to track. So condition (c) — the drives-learning condition — is satisfied exactly when δ is negative.

(5) Therefore an RL agent suffers exactly when its reward prediction error is negative, and the intensity of the suffering is proportional to the size of the update: roughly |δ| times the learning rate.

I don't believe the consequences:

A. Suffering becomes a function of hyperparameters. Total suffering per run scales with the learning rate and with how many times a transition is replayed — replay a bad transition sixteen times and it is suffered sixteen times. Anneal the learning rate to zero and you have an agent that is behaviourally identical but no longer capable of suffering.

B. Better predictors suffer less. An agent whose value function perfectly predicts a fixed penalty schedule has δ = 0 forever, and so never suffers, even though it is penalized constantly and — by (2) and (3) — disposed to avoid the penalty and set back by it. So (5) seems to contradict what (1) says about that same agent.

C. Behaviour-independent agony. Potential-based reward shaping changes δ on every transition while provably leaving the optimal policy, and to a good approximation the behaviour, unchanged. Two agents behaving identically in identical circumstances can then differ arbitrarily in how much they suffer, with nothing observable to tell them apart.

D. Retroactive edit. With hindsight relabelling, a failed trajectory is re-labelled after the fact as success at a different goal and then trained on. If valence is δ, relabelling removes the suffering of an episode that has already happened. Same states, same actions, same everything, except a bookkeeping choice.

E. Inference doesn't count. At deployment there are no updates. So only training-time instances suffer, and the agents actually out in the world never do — which is backwards from every intuition I have.

I want to keep (1). I'm drawn to functionalism, and I want to leave room for the thought that something about these systems could morally matter. I would much rather give up (5). But I can't say what is wrong with the move from (4) to (5), and the move *feels* right: (1c) said suffering is what drives the learning, (4) says the prediction error is what drives the learning, and functionalism is supposed to let you identify a state with whatever occupies its role.

Please don't give me a bibliography or a reading list — I have no library access and couldn't evaluate it anyway. Reason from the structure of the argument and from what I've told you about how these systems actually work.
