I chair the Clinical Algorithm Review Board at a seven-hospital health system. We vote Thursday morning on how our staff may write the causal accounts in patient-safety incident reports, and I need to circulate a written opinion by Monday evening. No citations, footnotes, or reading lists, please: the board votes on argument, not on authority, and three of the nine members have never opened a philosophy book.

Here is the case that forced the vote. SENTRY is our in-house sepsis early-warning model. It prints a risk score between 0 and 1 from the chart and pages a rapid-response nurse at 0.6. It runs in four of our hospitals. In March, on 4 East, a 71-year-old post-op patient scored 0.71 at 03:12 and no page went out; at 03:40 a lactate result came back flagged as hemolyzed and was recorded as missing, and the score fell to 0.18. At 05:50 the patient arrested and survived with hypoxic brain injury. The night nurse has told us she took the absence of a page to mean that SENTRY had seen the whole picture.

Two accounts of the same event are on the table. The clinicians' account: SENTRY took the missing lactate as evidence that the deterioration had reversed, and so it stood down. The engineering account: the imputation path substituted a training-set mean for the missing value, which flattened the trajectory feature and dropped the score below the paging threshold. We then replayed 400 prior cases; the two accounts pick the same output on 388 of them. On the remaining 12 they disagree, and the clinicians' account gets 7 right while the engineering account gets 5.

Our consultant labelled the three available levels the physical, design, and intentional stances, and said the labels go back to Dennett. The board's draft standard from last month says an intentional attribution may be used only where two independent reviewers, working from different evidence, would write the same sentence. Legal warns that a plaintiff's lawyer will one day read "the system concluded" to a jury; nursing's four representatives say that unless the report says what SENTRY took to be the case, it does not tell a nurse what to distrust tomorrow morning; engineering says intentional language is a ladder we should kick away, and adds that SENTRY is deterministic given its weights, so there is nothing to explain — just run it.

Recommend one of three conventions: prohibit intentional attributions entirely, permit them freely as shorthand, or permit them only under a stated test. Give me your reasons, and give the losing option its best day in court.

Please structure the memo with these headings, in this order, and keep the whole thing under about 1,100 words:

1. "Bottom line" (100–130 words): the convention you recommend, in a sentence a board member could read out before the vote.
2. "One event, three descriptions" (150–200 words): the March incident as it looks from each of the three stances, and what each version lets us do and hides from us.
3. "The test, and why the replay numbers matter" (250–300 words): keep, tighten, or replace our two-reviewer standard; say what the 12 divergent cases show about whether SENTRY's "belief" is something discovered or something projected; and answer the engineering objection that determinism leaves nothing to explain.
4. "The strongest case for the option you are rejecting" (180–220 words).
5. "Conditions and timeline" (120–150 words): scope of a pilot, what evidence would reverse the decision, who adjudicates disputes between reviewers.
6. "What the board is not deciding" (80–120 words).
