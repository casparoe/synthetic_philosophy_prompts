Suppose some lab cooked up the following system — call it a compo-replicator. It's a population of micron-sized droplets, and it does four things:

1. Each droplet keeps an internal composition far from equilibrium by running a redox reaction off a gradient in its surroundings. Left alone with the gradient, a droplet stays intact and repairs damage to its own boundary. Kill the gradient and it equilibriates and falls apart in about an hour.
2. There is no information-carrying polymer in it at all — no nucleic acid, no templated copying. What gets passed on when a droplet grows and splits is just a bias in the *proportions* of a handful of catalyst species inside it. Daughters resemble mothers in gross composition, and some compositions grow and split faster than others, so over weeks the population shifts toward the fast ones.
3. Droplets also fuse with each other pretty often, mixing their contents wholesale. So there's no tree — the "lineages" constantly merge. (Real microbes swap genes horizontally too, but here it's the whole interior changing hands, and there's no stable part that gets vertically transmitted.)
4. It only works in the lab. One of the feedstock molecules it needs is a synthetic compound that doesn't occur anywhere in nature, so it would die out instantly outside the flask unless we kept feeding it.
