I'm a novelist, and a scene in my current book has turned into a rabbit hole I never planned on. Setup: my narrator, Iris, is a translator hired to merge two archives that turn out to be about the same man, and she has a running argument with her ex, who teaches philosophy, about whether 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' actually tells you anything. He's a hardline Millian — a name contributes nothing but its bearer, so a true identity just says of one thing that it is itself — while she's a neo-Fregean: names carry modes of presentation, and that's what makes some identities informative and others dead trivial. Right now they just trade assertions at each other and my readers will fall asleep. What I want instead is the document an adversarial collaboration between the two of them would produce: a joint statement both could genuinely sign after a year of good-faith arguing. Not a transcript of a dialogue, and not a verdict on who wins — the signed document.

Concretely I want four things in it. (1) A numbered list of claims both camps can sign, about the data itself — the difference in cognitive significance between 'Hesperus is Hesperus' and 'Hesperus is Phosphorus', substitution failures inside belief reports, and whether the puzzle really needs proper names or generalises to things like 'water is H2O' and 'I am Iris' — phrased tightly enough that they aren't vacuous. (2) A table of the residual disagreements, with the Millian column written the way a Millian would write it and the Fregean column the way a Fregean would, not paraphrases either side could disown. (3) The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them. My guesses, which you should use or beat: whether the cognitive difference lives in the semantic value of the name or in the vehicles/files a thinker uses to track an object; whether the informativeness of an identity is semantic or gets derived pragmatically (test it by embedding the sentence where ordinary implicature is killed off — under negation, inside a question, in the antecedent of a conditional, under 'it's obvious that'); and whether the two sides' talk of 'mode of presentation' and 'vehicle of reference' has quietly collapsed into one and the same claim, in which case part of the dispute is verbal and I want to be told which part and why. (4) For each crux, one concrete case or argument both would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance.

Two cases from my own drafts that I want folded in, since they're what got me hooked. First, the rename: in draft two my protagonist is called Mary, in draft four she's Marianne. A reader who has only read draft four can learn something from 'Mary is Marianne'; a reader who read the drafts in order can't. Is that the same phenomenon as Hesperus and Phosphorus, or is it a different one — about fiction, or about my intentions as the author — wearing the same clothes? Second, the two-dossier case: Iris holds two files on the same man and sincerely says, looking at one and then the other, 'he embezzled the money' and 'he didn't'. Both camps have to say something there, and I want to see whether they end up saying the same thing in different words. Practical notes: start with one headline sentence giving the document's bottom line — the single most important thing the collaboration established about where the disagreement really lives — then go straight into the joint statement, no preamble about Frege and no 'great question'. No bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I'm on a residency with no library and I assume you haven't got one either, so gesture at a view by naming it or naming the person, not the paper. And at the very end, after the document, add a short note on how confident you are that a real Millian and a real neo-Fregean would each actually sign what you wrote, and single out the one line you'd be least surprised to see one of them cross out.
