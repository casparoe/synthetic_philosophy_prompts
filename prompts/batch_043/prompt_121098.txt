I write historical fiction, mostly set in the long nineteenth century, and over some fifteen years of reading in and around abolition, duelling, and the anti-footbinding campaigns, I have drifted into holding a view about how moral revolutions happen that I now lean on the way one leans on a load-bearing assumption. Call it the dishonour thesis: these practices ended when they became dishonourable, and moral argument — argument from a principle to a conclusion about what one may not do — was causally inert in the transition, serving afterwards as rationalisation and as a way of coordinating a code that had already shifted for other reasons. I take the philosophical ancestry of this to be Bernard Williams's chapter on morality as the peculiar institution, in *Ethics and the Limits of Philosophy*, where the thought is that there is no argument the slaveholder was in a position to be rationally compelled by and that describing his outlook as a set of moral beliefs awaiting refutation misdescribes it; and the developed version to be Kwame Anthony Appiah's *The Honor Code: How Moral Revolutions Happen*, where the mechanism is honour — a practice dies when continuing it costs you the regard of people whose regard you need.

I want to know what would count against this, and I would like the defeaters ranked, fatal to merely awkward, with an honest attempt to say what a defender of the thesis should concede rather than what its critics allege. A few considerations I have already turned over, partly so that you can tell me which of them are real and which are evasions I have dressed up as scruples.

First, the one that worries me most: the thesis may be circular as stated. "The practice ended when it became dishonourable" explains nothing unless the honour-status of a practice can be traced independently of the practice's decline. So I would like a list of tracers that do not simply re-describe the outcome — the vocabulary of scandal and of the gentleman in pamphlets and private correspondence, the behaviour of juries in duelling prosecutions, the reprint history of codes of conduct, the age at which girls' feet were first bound by birth cohort, shipping and insurance records, the tone of parliamentary testimony — and an account of what pattern across them would be decisive rather than merely suggestive.

Second, a distinction I suspect damages the thesis more than it appears to: perhaps what changed people was not argument from principle but redescription — testimony, arithmetic, images, the visible facts about the Middle Passage and about the bodies of bound feet. If so, what was inert was not argument as such but normative argument, and I am unsure whether conceding that is a small repair or a collapse into the rival view. Relatedly, the arguments that seem to have worked were internal ones, framed in a vocabulary the audience already accepted — Christian stewardship, or national weakness — and if all effective arguments are internal, then honour may be nothing more than the name of the local internal vocabulary, and my thesis may have quietly become the view it was meant to replace.

Third, two predictions I think the thesis is genuinely committed to, which I would like tested rather than assumed. If honour is the mechanism, then suppressing a practice while leaving the honour structure intact should leave the disposition available for re-expression elsewhere — which is what the psychological literature on cultures of honour in the American South seems to find, in insult physiology, regional patterns of violence, and the shape of contemporary self-defence law. And if honour is the mechanism, revolution should show tipping-point dynamics concentrated among those whose standing is at stake, whereas an argument-driven story predicts diffusion tracking exposure to texts and preaching rather than exposure to peers. In each case: what pattern would I have to give up the thesis over, and what pattern would a defender shrug at?

Fourth, the insulation question. There are only a handful of well-documented cases, they are studied retrospectively, and "honour" is elastic enough to be fitted to whatever local currency of regard was in play. Has the thesis been qualified into something that cannot lose? If it has, say whether that is fatal for a causal claim about singular historical sequences or merely the normal condition of such claims — and tell me what a case would have to look like for the thesis to be straightforwardly falsified: a practice whose honour-status collapsed while the practice continued, or one that ended while remaining honourable to those who abandoned it.

One last thing, which is the reason I am asking as a novelist rather than as a reader of philosophy. My own occupational evidence supports the thesis and I do not trust it. The scenes that move readers are scenes of shame, exposure, and forfeited standing; I have never written a convincing scene in which a character abandons a practice because someone reasoned him out of it, and such scenes read, to me and to my editors, as a mouthpiece reciting a conclusion arrived at elsewhere. This may be because the novel is the natural instrument of an honour mechanism, since it works directly on regard — but that is exactly the sort of self-flattering explanation I should discount, and the alternative is that conversion by argument is simply undramatic rather than rare. So: how much weight should a craft observation of that kind bear, and does the thesis, if it survives your examination, actually license a rule of craft — never show a character being argued out of a moral view — or would adopting that rule be the novelist's version of the same unfalsifiability you have just been asked to diagnose?
