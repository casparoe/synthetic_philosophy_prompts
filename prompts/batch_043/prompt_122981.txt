In the Ant Fugue dialogue in Douglas Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach (1979), the character Aunt Hillary is presented as an ant colony that can think, while her constituent ants are compared to neurones, which are not themselves the subjects of the colony's thoughts. This analogy is meant to make vivid the idea that a mind can emerge from the organised activity of many non-minded parts. Suppose we retain the analogy but attend to one respect in which an ant colony is unlike a brain enclosed in a skull: the colony's membership is porous and its boundary is not anatomically fixed. Ants wander, die, and are replaced; the nest, the pheromone trails, and the cached food stores are parts of the environment rather than parts of the ants themselves. Call this the porous-colony qualification.

I want you to compare two accounts of what, if anything, makes Aunt Hillary's mental states survive the porosity of the colony.

Account A, the neuronal-pattern account, holds that Aunt Hillary's mind is nothing over and above the ongoing causal-informational pattern realised by the ants, together with whatever environmental structures are recruited into that pattern. On this account, what matters for Aunt Hillary's survival is the continuity of the pattern, not the numerical identity of the ants. Aunt Hillary is thus akin to a tune or a whirlpool: a real, emergent entity that depends on lower-level items without being exhausted by any one of them.

Account B, the office-holder account, is less familiar and is introduced here for the purposes of comparison. It holds that Aunt Hillary is best understood not as an instantaneous interaction pattern but as a cluster of socially defined offices: for instance, an office for integrating pheromone reports, an office for arbitrating between conflicting foraging trails, and an office for maintaining the colony's memory of past food sources. These offices are occupied successively by different ants, and they are held together by rules of eligibility, succession, and testimony rather than by any single ant or by a single uninterrupted pattern of neuron-like activity. On this account, Aunt Hillary is more like a corporation or a monarchy than like a brain: the office persists while its occupants change, and the colony's mind is the mind of the office, not of the transient flow of ant traffic.

Compare Account A and Account B. In particular, assess them against the following four cases, which are designed to drive a wedge between them.

1. Gradual replacement. Over several weeks, the ants of the original colony are quietly substituted one by one for ants from a neighbouring colony with the same caste structure and a very similar repertoire of pheromone responses. No ant is killed, and the overall pattern of interaction is preserved throughout; at the end, none of the original ants remain. Does Aunt Hillary survive? Both accounts appear to answer yes, but they do so for different reasons. Explain the difference, and say which account offers the more plausible reason.

2. Fission. The colony undergoes fission into two spatially separated groups. Each group retains the full complement of castes and reconstructs, from its own resources, the same set of offices and a qualitatively identical interaction pattern. Both groups behave as though they remember the same past and both respond to the name Aunt Hillary. Account A appears to allow two continuers, while Account B seems forced either to deny that both are Aunt Hillary or to appeal to a further rule about which office-holder is the legitimate one. Which verdict does each account give, and which is more coherent?

3. Dormant archive. All the ants die, but the nest architecture, the remaining pheromone gradients, and the arrangement of stored seeds preserve a complete structural description of the colony's prior states and a complete disposition to produce the former pattern if new ants are introduced. No ant remains, and no interaction occurs. Is Aunt Hillary's mind preserved in this archive, or has it vanished? Account A, if it identifies the mind with an activity-pattern, must say it has vanished; Account B might say the offices survive in a vacant or archived form. Assess whether the archive case shows that Account B is committed to an implausibly liberal criterion of mentality, or whether it shows that Account A is unable to distinguish a live mind from a perfectly preserved museum exhibit.

4. Lesion and reorganisation. A raid removes the caste responsible for the most complex integrative task. The colony initially fails at that task, but over time other ants are recruited from other castes and the task is relearned, with the final behavioural repertoire qualitatively the same as before but realised by different ants and slightly different pathways. Account A must decide whether this is recovery of the same mind or the death of one Aunt Hillary and the birth of another; Account B must decide whether the office was vacant and then refilled, and whether the intermittent gap breaks the continuity of the office. Which account handles this case more convincingly, and what does the answer reveal about the original analogy between ants and neurones?

Your comparison should not stop at verdicts. State clearly what criterion of personal identity over time each account uses, how each account reinterprets Hofstadter's ant/neurone analogy, and what each implies about whether a human person is most fundamentally a pattern, an office, or an organism. Please confine your answer to the argument; I am not asking for scholarly references, a reading list, or any citations. Which account do you find more defensible overall, and precisely where does it remain vulnerable?
