"A teacher announces on Friday: there will be exactly one exam next week, and you won't be able to work out which day it is on until the morning it happens. The students, being perfect reasoners, argue backwards: it can't be Friday, because if they got to Thursday evening with no exam they'd deduce Friday and so it wouldn't be a surprise; once Friday is out it can't be Thursday either, by the same reasoning; and so on back to Monday. Every day is excluded, so the announcement must be false and the teacher has promised something she can't deliver. The exam then lands on Wednesday and everyone is surprised — which just shows the students' proof went wrong somewhere. Every serious solution in the literature is basically a hunt for that mistake. The standard verdict is that the argument is circular: the students have to keep assuming the announcement is true in order to conclude it's false, and once you notice that, the whole thing collapses and the announcement turns out to be perfectly satisfiable. Practical upshot for a teacher who actually wants to keep the promise: pick the day by a random draw, so that prediction is impossible in principle and what she said really is true. There's not much of a moral here beyond self-reference — announcements that talk about what you can know about them tend to trip over themselves — and in any case if you widen the window, say thirty days instead of five, the problem more or less evaporates, because with plenty of days left the students can never narrow it down to one. It's the same puzzle as the unexpected hanging: swap the exam for the noose and the teacher for the judge, and the prisoner reasons exactly the same way and concludes that he can't be hanged at all."
