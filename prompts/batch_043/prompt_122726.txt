He says civil disobedience is just lawbreaking with better PR and no special moral status. I said no, the injustice of the law is what makes it a distinctive, sometimes-justified kind of breach — and then I wrote down the following, which seems to prove his side. Please act like a political philosopher who's taught this material for years, brainstorm for a second on which steps look safe to you before committing, and tell me honestly which one you'd give up. Also: don't send me to papers, we've got no library access and no reliable internet in the room.

(1) There is at least a natural duty to support and not to undermine institutions that are reasonably just, because they secure goods — peace, coordination, predictable enforcement of rights — that nobody could secure alone, and the cost to me of doing my part is small.
(2) A legal order yields those goods only given general compliance; a system where each person complies only when she independently approves the particular law isn't a legal order at all.
(3) A duty to help secure a good includes a duty to do one's part in producing it, and general compliance is nothing but the sum of individual acts of compliance — so the natural duty includes my complying with the law.
(4) What makes my compliance do any work is not that this particular law is just, but that there is a standing coordinative practice of compliance; so the duty in (3) is content-independent, and it doesn't get weaker when the particular law is unjust — only when the system as a whole stops being reasonably just.
(5) So I have a duty to obey unjust laws too, and civil disobedience always starts out as a breach of duty whose wrongness is not reduced by how unjust the target law is.
(6) And since the wrong is a wrong against the legal order as such, the disobedient owes full submission to the penalty, and how serious the injustice is remains strictly beside the point: on this reasoning, sitting in at a segregated lunch counter is, before any balancing, no less wrongful than quietly dodging a parking fine.
(7) But then the category "civil disobedience" does no work: the injustice of the law is supposed to be what makes it distinctive and sometimes justified, and here it never enters. Worse, the case for disobeying doesn't get stronger as the injustice gets worse. That's absurd.

I'd rather keep (1) and (7) than anything in the middle. So: which step do you reject, why did that step look fine when I wrote it, and what does rejecting it cost me?
