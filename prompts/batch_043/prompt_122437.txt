ok so: you're a professional philosopher — like, fifteen years of refereeing for the big ethics and mind journals, you've read four hundred papers that open with "suppose that…", and you also write an anonymous humour column for a magazine that shall remain nameless. i need next month's column, and take your time with it: before you write anything, do me a short bullet-point brainstorm (i do want to see it, that's fine) on which features of actual intuition-pump practice you're going to exaggerate, and why each one is a real feature of the practice rather than a strawman someone could wince at. then write the thing.

the piece: a parody of a British Standard / ISO specification for intuition pumps. you know, "BS 8801-3:2025 — Intuition pumps. Part 3: Specification for inference-tight cases", or whatever title you like in that register. i want the front matter (title, scope, "normative references" — all of which are undated, withdrawn, or in preparation since 1974), then numbered clauses, then one or two annexes, then a short appendix of correspondence from users. deadpan standards-speak throughout: "shall", "the manufacturer shall declare", "permissible inference leakage". about 800 words, not counting clause numbers.

the jokes have to come from getting the methodology right — i want someone who's actually used thought experiments as evidence to laugh rather than wince, and someone who's never opened a philosophy journal to still follow it. the stuff i want sent up, use at least a few:

- an "inversion clause": a certified pump has to return the same verdict when any incidental detail is inverted (the sex of the bystander, whether the lever is a lever, whether it's a child). compliance is normally achieved by reclassifying whatever detail did move the verdict as constitutive rather than incidental — and there's no independent test for which is which. that's the reductio, so let it sit there without you winking at it.
- a "declaration of load-bearing incidentals" form in an annex, which is, of course, itself a thought experiment.
- some declared ratio — verdict confidence over argumentative work actually done — and a tolerance for leakage.
- a calibration requirement: the pump must be trialled on thirty first-years and on the author's own department, and the author must sign a statement that they would have reached the same verdict had the case come out the other way.
- a hermeticity test: rewrite the case in flat, abstract language; if the intuition evaporates, the pump has failed. (this one should sting, because it's true.)
- an order-of-presentation problem that the standard simply declines to cover.
- a clause requiring dialectical symmetry — the pump shall be deployed against its holder's own view as often as against rivals — with a note that no pump has ever been certified under it.
- a grandfathering schedule that exempts essentially every pump anyone actually uses.
- a closing note: "issued for public comment; comments invited from persons who have independently arrived at the verdict the pump was designed to produce."

the three letters at the end should each be a real complaint about how these things mislead — reversed verdicts depending on presentation order, verdicts flipping when you change whether the side effect turns out good or bad, the fact that the pump only ever seems to work on theories held by people not in the room — and the Committee's replies should be entirely unfazed, answering a different and much narrower question.

rules: don't name any real philosopher, don't attribute anything to anyone real, and don't quote any real text — invent your own authorities (a Cotswold Working Party, Prof A. N. Other of somewhere fictional, whatever). you can allude to famous cases — trolleys, violinists, experience machines, twin earths, china-themed rooms — without saying who dreamt them up. no footnotes, no citations, no reading suggestions. and pls write it in British English throughout: colour, organise, amongst, "whilst" if you can stomach it, prices in pounds.
