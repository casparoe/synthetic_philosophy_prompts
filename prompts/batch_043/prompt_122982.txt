I'm in the final year of an undergraduate seminar on nationalism, collective memory and the modern state, and our closing sessions have been on how old nations really are: the modernist claim associated with Hobsbawm and Anderson, that nations are artifacts of print capitalism, mass schooling, conscription and the invention of tradition, versus the ethno-symbolist reply that modern nationalism works up a pre-modern ethnic material it did not itself create. Two people in the seminar have been arguing past each other about this all term, and instead of another exchange I want the document they should have produced: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between a modernist and an ethno-symbolist.

Two stipulations first, so that the document does not become a dispute about who said what where. First, score nationhood on a three-part scorecard that I have been using in my own notes: (i) a bounded category of membership that non-specialists can locate themselves in, and that is not merely imposed from outside; (ii) a claim to political sovereignty over a territory, advanced in the name of that category; (iii) a shared diachronic story that makes present sacrifices intelligible to members. Each camp should say, in its own words, whether that is the right thing to score, and what it would score instead if not — but the rest of the statement should proceed on the assumption that it is.

Second, treat the following three regularities as the shared and stipulated evidence base. I don't want citations or any further empirical claims beyond them. (a) Infrastructure: wherever we have data, mass shifts in self-ascription follow vernacular print markets, rail networks, schooling and conscription by roughly a generation. (b) The menu: such shifts almost always land on units that already had a name, a remembered battle, a saint's cult or a district of customary law, whereas attempts to build a nation on an administratively novel unit — a colonial province, a planned settlement region — produce attachments that decay quickly once the administrative apparatus withdraws. (c) Reversal: some populations shift twice within two generations, and their grandchildren treat the second identity as immemorial.

The crux I care most about is what to make of (b): whether it shows that modern infrastructure selects from a pre-existing menu, so that the ethnie is doing real explanatory work, or only that it exploits whatever cheap cultural material happened to be at hand, so that continuity of the menu is not continuity of the nation. I also want (c) handled: what should the ethno-symbolist say about a nation whose ancestors were successfully re-described, and is the invention-of-tradition claim self-effacing in the sense that a tradition known to be invented is, two generations on, in any way less genuine? And I'd like a stipulated case both sides will accept as bearing on this: two adjacent districts matched on dialect, confession and customary law before 1850, one of which acquires a rail junction, a vernacular press and a teacher-training college while the other does not. Both camps should record what they predict for those districts in 1900, and what they predict for both after the state that administered them withdraws in 1991.

Finally, a crux about implications rather than origins: does the modernist thesis have any debunking force once it is widely believed? Suppose the constructivist account is taught in schools across some region for thirty years. Do national attachments erode, thicken, or take a second-order form in which people know the construction and affirm it anyway? Each side should write down its prediction in advance.

Format: a numbered section of claims both could genuinely sign — and please flag and remove anything vacuous, saying what you removed and why; then a table of the residual disagreements, each stated in wording its own side would accept, with the crux it turns on and the stipulated regularity or hypothetical case both would count as evidence about it; then a short section on which parts of the dispute are verbal rather than substantive and why; then one closing paragraph per camp on what would actually change its mind.
