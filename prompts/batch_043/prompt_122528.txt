I run a fortnightly rational-choice reading group — mostly second- and third-year maths and CS undergrads. They've all met expected utility and they're fine with backward induction in games, but none of them have done any philosophy. Next week I've got 45 minutes to get them to see what's supposed to be wrong with having cyclic preferences, i.e. what the money pump argument is actually doing. Please don't explain the argument to me; I need a plan for staging it.

What I want is: the order the pieces have to go in and why (what depends on what — in particular whether the cycle itself has to be on the table before anyone mentions money, or whether the pump is what introduces it); the one example that carries it; the misconceptions they'll form and where in the sequence to defuse each; a check question; and what I should openly decline to get into.

On the example: I was going to use either non-transitive dice (A beats B more often than not, B beats C, C beats A, and you'll pay a dollar to swap to the die you'd rather be holding) or the homespun version with three flats and a small admin fee every time you move. Pick one and say what the carrying example has to have and what it has to not have — I think it has to not look like a change of mind, so no getting-bored-of-it or diminishing-returns stories, but tell me if that's the wrong way to put it.

Two things I know will come up and don't have good answers to. One member said last time that if you know the sequence of offers is finite and you know how long it is, you can just backward-induct and refuse the last swap, so the pump only works on people who are suprised by how many turns there are — and if that's right I don't see what's left of the argument. Another pointed out that if the gaps in the cycle are smaller than what it costs to switch, cyclic preferences cost you nothing in practice, so you should just tolerate them. I need to know where in the session to deal with each of those and what conceding them does to the conclusion; I definately don't want the session to end on 'so intransitivity is fine, whatever'. I'm guessing the other misconceptions I should plan for are 'this is just sunk cost again' and 'so you should never change your mind'.

Success at the end looks like someone being able to say what's wrong with cyclic preferences without saying 'you'd end up poorer'. Give me one question that would tell me whether they can.

Give it to me as a timed run-of-show with minutes, then the misconception list keyed to the point in the session where each gets defused. Under 700 words, bullet points fine.
