I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in moral philosophy: 14 students, mostly majors, all of whom have already had a normative theory course. We have a 75-minute session coming up on a topic I have found nearly impossible to teach by discussion alone — the difficulty of telling apart, within morality, a preference an agent genuinely holds from a bias the agent has simply acquired, and the related question of what a collective decision procedure ought to do with the difference.

The students have already read the material on adaptive preferences and on proposals for discounting or cleaning preferences before they are allowed to count in a social choice. On the page every proposed test looks workable. What they do not have, and what I cannot supply by assigning one more paper, is any felt sense of why the distinction is hard to *operate*: why each candidate test — the availability of reasons, endorsement under fuller information, independence from the judgment's causal origin, fit with the agent's own interests — either fails to sort anything or does its sorting by smuggling in a first-order moral verdict under a neutral-sounding description. I want them to run into that themselves, and I want them to propose the tests rather than receive a list of them from me.

So: design an activity I can run in that session.

Constraints on the design.

The distinction, and the vocabulary of preference, bias, laundering and adaptation, must not be named until the debrief at the earliest — ideally not until the last ten minutes. No framing lecture. The problem has to show up in what the students *do* before it is described.

The raw material should be their own moral judgments, not invented cases, and the stakes should be small but real: something the class itself actually decides at the end of the hour, using only the judgments that survive whatever filter they devise. It cannot be anything that touches grades, standing, or anyone's employment, and it must degrade gracefully if two or three students decline to contribute anything personal at all — I will not have a session where non-participation is conspicuous. Assume I can spend about ten minutes preparing in the week before (collecting anonymous cards in advance, for instance), and no more.

The mechanism I keep circling, which you should use or improve on: everyone writes two anonymous judgments in advance — one they would defend in argument, one they suspect they hold only because of where and how they were raised — each with a one-sentence supporting reason. I then pool and shuffle the whole set so provenance is lost, and the group has to sort the pooled cards using a test they invent on the spot. What appeals to me is that the pool is made of their own avowals, so nobody gets to treat the suspect items as belonging to someone else; and I suspect (though I have never run it) that they will not sort better than chance, and that the reasons they generate for a card will feel identical whichever pile it came from. What I lack is the second half: a way to turn the sorting into a real decision, so that they have to actually discount some of the pooled judgments and then notice that the discounted ones cluster — that the test they took to be neutral turned out to have a direction.

A second device I have considered and cannot quite place: hand some cards back with a causal history attached — a story about how that judgment entered their culture, some of the stories invented by me — and see whether anyone abandons the judgment. My guess is that almost nothing gets abandoned regardless of which way the story points, which would show something about how little work the causal history does by itself. But I am not confident this is the right stage, or where it goes, and I would rather you placed it properly or dropped it than included it because I mentioned it.

Deliverable. Please write about 1,500 words in these sections, in this order:

1. "Overview" (~120 words) — what the hour does, and the one thing a student should see by the end that they could not see from the readings.
2. "Materials and advance preparation" (~150 words) — including how I collect and pre-screen the cards without reading myself into a position where I know who wrote what.
3. "The session, minute by minute" (~500 words) — a timeline, the rules and roles, what the students do, and the actual words I should use to prompt each stage.
4. "Debrief" (~350 words) — map three or four specific moments onto the specific reasons each candidate test fails, and say when to name the distinction and what to say in the last five minutes.
5. "Predictable failures" (~250 words) — at least four, each with a handling: the student who announces that everything is a bias so nothing needs sorting, the student who treats the exercise as a chance to diagnose their classmates, the group that colludes on a test designed to protect the majority's commitments, and whatever else you expect to go wrong the first time.
6. "What this cannot do" (~150 words) — the part of the problem the activity structurally cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in lecture or reading.

Please do not include a reading list, bibliography, or citations; I have the literature and can find references myself. What I need is something I could set up and run on Thursday.
