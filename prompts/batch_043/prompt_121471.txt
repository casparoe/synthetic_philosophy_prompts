I've got a first-year seminar on ethical and political philosophy, 16 students, one 80-minute slot, and I want to do a session on Aristotle on money and exchange without talking at them for an hour. Design me a trading game they actually play, in a normal seminar room, that makes them run into three things in order:

(a) that direct exchange between people with different skills and mismatched, partly hidden needs is miserable, and pushes toward some single thing everything can be measured against;
(b) that whatever ends up doing that job works only because everyone treats it as doing it — a stand-in for need rather than something valuable by nature — and that it also has to work as a guarantee that you can get what you need later, at a time when you don't need it yet;
(c) Aristotle's thought that acquisition aimed at nothing but more tokens is the unnatural case, and that breeding money out of money is the worst version.

Give me rules, materials, per-round setup, and a scoring scheme in points only, no real money or prizes. I want the conventionality to land as a surprise rather than as a point I make: build in a moment where I can invalidate the currency mid-game and issue a new one, and let that do the work. Same for (c) — ideally they notice by themselves that once everyone's needs are met the game keeps rewarding more, and that lending tokens for extra tokens feels different from trading. No Aristotle and no Greek terms until afterwards.

Then a debrief: map each phase onto the claims, say what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be honest about which part of Aristotle the game can't stage and I'll just have to say out loud.
