I share a department with a senior colleague who works on real patterns and on the physical, design, and intentional stances, and who is unusually willing to give a crisp verdict on any case you put to her. I have collected six of her verdicts over the past year, and next week I have to rule on a seventh case myself; I want to know what she would say about it, and what rule she is really running.

Her verdicts so far.

1. A thermostat cycling around a set point: yes, there is a real pattern here; the design stance is the right stance to adopt; calling the thermostat a believer is a façon de parler and nothing more.

2. A long stream of digits generated by a short rule no one has yet found, compared with a stream from a hardware noise source: the first contains a real pattern even before anyone finds the rule; the second contains none, even though a flexible enough curve-fitting procedure can always be fitted to it after the fact.

3. A conventional chess program running on a laptop: attributing beliefs and desires to it is legitimate but optional, since the design stance delivers the same predictions at comparable cost, and therefore there is no further question about whether it really believes anything.

4. The familiar giant-lookup-table thought experiment, a system that sustains an hour of conversation purely by retrieving hand-tabulated responses with no internal structure beyond the table: no beliefs, no desires, and no real pattern in the conversation, even though the intentional stance predicts its every move perfectly.

5. A termite mound and the traffic of the colony around it: yes, there is a real pattern there, and it is perfectly in order to describe the colony as trying to hold the nest at a certain temperature; but that does not make the colony a believer.

6. Ascriptions of expectation to the market: useful in practice, and market behavior does exhibit real patterns, but there is no fact of the matter about what the market believes, and the ascription does no work that a lower-level description could not do in principle.

The new case. A system I will call Orchid was produced purely by gradient descent on next-token prediction over a very large corpus and was then deployed as a customer-service agent. No one wrote rules for it, no specification says what it should believe or want, and no one can point to its internal states and say which of them, if any, is its view about the refund policy. Three independent interpretive teams, each with full access to the internals and the trained weights, have produced wildly different but equally predictively adequate readings of the same states: one credits Orchid with beliefs about the policy, one credits it only with tracking statistics of phrasing in the training corpus, one credits it with no content at all. At the same time, its engineers do predict and control it cheaply and reliably from the intentional stance, in ways that no available design-stance or physical-stance model matches. And its behavior is enormously compressible, but only relative to a description that is allowed to presuppose the trained weights and the training distribution; in a bare description language there is no saving at all.

Please answer in exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no conclusion.

1. The two or three rules about when a pattern is real and when the intentional stance is warranted that fit all six of her verdicts equally well, stated sharply enough that they can come apart on some case.
2. What each of those rules predicts about Orchid, both about the reality of the pattern and about whether ascriptions of belief to it are true.
3. Your best guess at her verdict on Orchid, with the single strongest reason for preferring it over the alternatives you named.
4. The one further case that would best separate those rules, together with what each rule predicts about it.
5. The strongest reason to doubt the guess in item 3, and what would make you abandon it.

Where your rules disagree about whether Orchid's intentional ascriptions are straightforwardly true, say so explicitly rather than splitting the difference, and do not settle the question by saying only that it depends on what we mean by belief.
