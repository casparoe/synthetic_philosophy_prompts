I served last year as a juror in a criminal trial, and ever since I have been turning over something about the rules we were handed that I cannot get straight in my head.

The practice, as I ran into it: in the criminal case the judge told us that we could convict only if the prosecution had proved its case beyond a reasonable doubt, and glossed that as being firmly convinced of the defendant's guilt, while adding that this does not mean beyond all possible doubt. When our foreman asked whether the standard corresponds to some percentage, the answer we got back was that it does not, and that no number would be given. In ordinary civil cases, as I understand it, the rule is different: the claimant wins if it is more likely than not that what they allege happened is what happened, and this holds even when the sum in dispute is enormous. But certain civil matters — an allegation of fraud, a petition to end someone's parental rights — get a raised bar, something like clear and convincing evidence, and the reason usually offered is what hangs in the balance. And nobody involved seems to think there is a contradiction in saying that a criminal acquittal and a later civil verdict about the very same event can both be correct, with neither court having got the facts wrong.

Here is what puzzles me. The evidence is what it is — the timestamps, the fingerprints, the witnesses, the gaps. Yet how confident we were told we must be before we may treat the matter as settled seemed to slide around with what was going to be done to the defendant as a result, and with what kind of thing was at stake, liberty and reputation rather than money, even though nothing about the evidence had moved. That looks as though it ought to be a scandal for anyone who thinks that how well supported a claim is has nothing to do with what we intend to do about it. But the law seems entirely untroubled, and I cannot tell whether the law is being philosophically careless or whether I am the one with a wrong picture of what words like proved and known mean.

So my question is: what does this practice — a single notion of proof whose threshold shifts with what is at stake, deliberately left unquantified — actually presuppose? More precisely:

1. Does it presuppose the contested view that whether someone knows a thing can depend on the practical stakes of their being wrong, so that raising the stakes can turn knowledge into something less than knowledge? Or does it presuppose only the much tamer point that how much risk of error we ought to run before acting is a separate question from how confident we are, so that the law is regulating action and saying nothing about knowledge at all? If the tamer point is all it needs, why is the standard phrased as a requirement about the jury's state of mind — firm conviction, an abiding conviction — rather than as an instruction about costs and probabilities?

2. Suppose the stakes really are doing the work. Is the pattern genuinely one of stakes? A suit over a very large sum is decided on the balance of probabilities, while a shoplifting charge needs proof beyond reasonable doubt, and inside criminal law the standard is the same for a trivial offence and for one carrying life imprisonment. If what the practice tracked were simply how much is at stake, these should come apart. So is the practice really presupposing that some interests — liberty, reputation, family — differ in kind from money rather than merely in amount? And if so, is that a claim about knowledge, or a moral claim wearing an epistemic costume?

3. And a reform I have been mulling, which I find at once appealing and repulsive: keep a single threshold of more likely than not for everything, and instead scale the response to the confidence, so that a finding of guilt at a modest probability yields a proportionally reduced sentence rather than an acquittal. My interest in it is narrow. Would adopting it force us to give up something the present practice presupposes, or is the present practice compatible with it and merely choosing a different mechanism? And on each of the candidate accounts of what the practice presupposes, would the reform be an improvement, a disaster, or neither — and does whichever answer you give make you more suspicious of that account?

In answering, please separate what the practice strictly requires from what merely sits comfortably with it, tell me where several theories would license the same practice and I therefore cannot infer anything from it, and say plainly if the pieces of the practice — one threshold across all criminal stakes, a raised bar for some civil matters, no numbers anywhere — are not consistent with one another. I would rather be told the practice is philosophically underdetermined than be handed a tidy story that explains more than it possibly can.
