I write literary fiction, and my fourth novel, *The Chaplet Makers*, comes out in the spring. The piece below is a draft of its afterword; it will also run, in a somewhat different form, as a standalone essay in a magazine of ideas, whose readers will mostly never have heard of the Copycat project. I don't want to write down to them, and I don't want to publish something a cognitive scientist would laugh at.

What I'm asking for is not line-editing. I know how to write a sentence. I'm asking whether the argument holds, and above all whether the small world I invented in the middle of it is a probe or a toy. Assume I can take bluntness.

THE DRAFT

Nothing to Be Last

In the 1980s and 1990s Douglas Hofstadter and his research group built a program, Copycat, to work on analogies of a deliberately impoverished kind. The world is twenty-six letters in a fixed order, finite strings of them, and a handful of relations: successor, predecessor, sameness, grouping, left end, right end. A problem looks like this: abc → abd :: ijk → ?, and the answers people give are interestingly unstable — ijl above all, but ijd is not crazy, and other things can be defended. The point of the poverty is that nothing in an answer can come from what the letters mean. Whatever makes one answer better than another has to come from how the strings get described. Analogy-making becomes something you can watch.

Two things were claimed for the project, and I want to keep them separate. The first is a mechanism: many small processes — the group called them codelets — compete to build partial descriptions of the situation, and the description that wins is the one that, once built, makes the most sense of the whole thing. Hofstadter called the result high-level perception: the claim that answering such a problem is closer to seeing an ambiguous figure one way rather than another than it is to searching and deducing. The second is an observation about what happens to descriptions under that pressure: concepts bend. What begins as replace the last letter by its successor can come out as replace the last letter by its predecessor, or replace the group by the group after it, or put in a d. Hofstadter's name for this is conceptual slippage, and it is the part that travelled.

I think there are two different things inside that one word, and only one of them gets discussed.

Call the first concept slippage. Some item in the description is exchanged for a neighbor: successor for predecessor, a single letter for a run of letters, the right end for the left end. The description keeps its shape and one of its parts is swapped for something nearby.

Call the second anchor slippage. Here nothing is exchanged for a neighbor. What changes is which element of the string the description is fastened to. Consider the answer ijd. The usual way of describing it is as a slippage of the concept the successor of c into the concept the bare letter d. But you can also describe it as a change of anchor: once the description is fastened to the letter d itself rather than to the last position of the target string, the rest follows without any concept having bent. The contest between ijl and ijd is, on this reading, not only a contest about what d means. It is a contest about what the description is about.

My suspicion, and it is the part I trust least, is that the first kind of slippage is downstream of the second. Successor can afford to slip into predecessor, I want to say, only once the anchor has come loose. While the description is pinned to the last letter of a string that also happens to run alphabetically forward, the direction of the alphabet is held in place by the geometry of the string, and there is nothing to flip. Unpin it and the direction is free to go either way.

If that is right, you should be able to see it by taking the pins away. So here is a small world I built while writing my novel, a world with no ends.

My characters make chaplets: closed loops of beads, strung in repeating patterns, no clasp, no beginning. Take the Copycat world and close it. Strings over the same alphabet in the same order, with the same successor, predecessor, sameness and grouping — but read as loops, and counted as the same object under rotation. There is no leftmost bead and no rightmost bead, no first and no last. Whether to identify a loop with its mirror image is a choice I have not made, and I'll come back to it.

What interests me is what closing the loop does to the ways a bead can be singled out. On a line, the third letter of abc is special twice over: it sits at the right end of the string, and it is where the alphabetic run a-b-c stops. In nearly every problem of this kind those two coincide, and you cannot tell which one an answer is following. Close the loop and they come apart. On a chaplet, c is no longer at an end, but it is still the bead after which the run would break. So the loop preserves a way of singling out a bead while deleting the positional one — which, I take it, is why abc → abd :: ijk → ? still wants ijl on a chaplet, though for a different reason than before.

The case I actually care about is the one where the alphabet runs out: abc → abd :: xyz → ?. On a line, z is both the right end and the end of the alphabet, and the problem is well known for the trouble it causes. On a loop, z is no longer the right end, but it is still the end of the alphabet. My prediction, which I have tested on no one, is that the loop version will draw fewer wrap-around answers and more answers that shift the anchor to another bead or flip the direction, because last in the alphabet has lost the support it was getting from last in the string. Whether that is a real prediction about anybody's psychology or only a report of my own intuition is something I cannot settle from a desk.

Two design questions I also cannot settle. First, the mirror: if I identify a loop with its reversal, I delete the direction of the alphabet along with the ends, and I suspect the domain goes dead, turning every problem into a question about shape rather than about order. My instinct is to keep the direction and quotient by rotation only. Second, grouping on a loop: a doubled run — the kk in aabc → aabd :: ijkk → ? — survives rotation but has no position, so the group at the end is unavailable while the group that repeats is not. I don't know whether that makes the chaplet world richer than the linear one or merely vaguer.

Here is why I bothered, and why any of this belongs in a novel about beadworkers.

A metaphor in a sentence needs what the last letter is in abc. Something in the sentence has to be singled out in advance, so that the mapping has somewhere to land. When I revise a metaphor that isn't working, I almost never make it more apt. I change the syntax around it: I move the word the metaphor is about to the end of its clause, or put it in apposition, or repeat it in the next sentence. I am making something last. And I suspect the ordinary complaints about metaphor are complaints about anchoring. A metaphor reads as mixed not because two mappings are inconsistent but because two anchors are competing; a metaphor reads as far-fetched not because the distance is great but because the reader has been left to choose which noun is the c.

That is where the draft stops, and it stops there because I am stuck. I don't know whether anchor is a genuine addition to the machinery or only the program's own notion of salience wearing a different hat, and I cannot produce a case in which the anchor moves while everything the program would call salience stays where it was. If I can't produce one, the middle of this essay is decoration.

WHAT I WANT FROM YOU

1. A one-line verdict at the top — run it, revise it, or rebuild it — and then comments organized by my sections, about a thousand words in total.

2. Whether the chaplet domain works as a probe. Does closing the loop actually separate the two ways of singling out a bead, or does it just relabel the same competition, or make the problems underdetermined in a dull way? If you would change the domain to sharpen it — add a relation, withhold one, quotient by reflection after all, restrict the alphabet — say what and why. I would also like your honest sense of whether my prediction about the xyz case is worth anything, given that I have no way to test it.

3. Where I have the project wrong. I read Hofstadter's 1995 collection and Mitchell's 1993 book years ago and am working from memory of their aims, not from the code. If I've stated a slogan where the actual claim is more careful, or described the architecture loosely in a way that distorts it, correct me.

4. Whether anchor slippage is a real distinction or a redescription. If it collapses into something the study of analogy already has — salience, systematicity, alignability, some notion of what is prominent in a representation — tell me in your own words which one it is and what I would have to change to have a genuine claim. And if the literary half doesn't follow from the first half, say so plainly rather than praising the coda.

5. The strongest objection I haven't answered, phrased the way someone who works on this would phrase it, with a note on whether it kills the essay's thesis or only a paragraph of it.

One constraint: please don't point me to papers, people, or things to read. I'm revising at a residency with no library, and a reading list would sit unused. Argue from the ideas themselves.

HOW I WANT YOU CALIBRATED

For every substantive thing you tell me, mark which kind of thing it is: (a) something nearly everyone who works on analogy, categorization, or the computational modeling of either would grant; (b) a live disagreement among people who actually know that material; (c) your own reconstruction — reasonable, but not something you can attribute to the field. Do the same for the load-bearing claims in my draft: tell me which of my hinges are settled and which are contested. I would much rather learn that the anchor idea is one contested view among several than be told it is the consensus, and I would rather be told it is empty than have it flattered. Where you genuinely can't tell whether something is contested, say that instead of deciding for me.
