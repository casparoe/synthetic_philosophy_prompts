You are a professional philosopher of mathematics whose research is on structuralism and on the two problems that go under Benacerraf's name: the identification problem raised in "What Numbers Could Not Be" (1965) and the epistemological problem raised in "Mathematical Truth" (Journal of Philosophy, 1973). Please think carefully and at length before committing to anything. Begin with a short, unstructured brainstorm in which you list — without yet weighing them — every respect you can think of in which a rule-governed game like chess is and is not like elementary arithmetic, and only then turn to the structured audit I ask for below. I have the standard literature to hand, so please do not spend any of your answer on a bibliography or suggestions for further reading. What I lack is not a map of the positions but a verdict on what this particular comparison can bear.

The situation: in a reading group last week my supervisor reached for what she admitted was stock imagery, and used it to settle both halves of the dilemma at once. Roughly, her line was that numbers are not objects at all but positions in a structure, in the way that the queen in a box of chessmen is not this particular carved piece of wood but a role in a game. Hence it is a pseudo-question which set-theoretic object the number three really is, just as it is a pseudo-question which bit of wood in the box really is the queen; and hence, too, there is no mystery about how creatures like us know arithmetical truths, since we lay down the rules and then work out their consequences, which is exactly the sort of knowledge we are capable of having. She leaned on the analogy rather heavily, and I want to know how much of it is load-bearing.

Please give me the following.

First, the mapping, stated explicitly rather than gestured at: a table of correspondences is fine. What plays the role of the queen, of the wooden piece-token, of the board, of the squares, of the legal moves, of the rulebook, of our stipulating the rules, of the players, of the consequences no one has yet derived, of winning and losing? Mark the cells where arithmetic supplies nothing at all: I expect the trouble to be concentrated where the table goes blank.

Second, the imports. Which features of chess does the comparison quietly carry over into arithmetic, features the target may not have? The candidates I can see are: a board of fixed finite extent given in advance; a rulebook that exists outside the game and can be known by being read; rules that are arbitrary conventions answerable to nothing beyond our interests; a point or telos fixed from outside the game, as checkmate and winning are; and pieces supplied from outside the game bearing properties — white, wooden, chipped — that are irrelevant to play. For each, say whether arithmetic has the corresponding feature and, if not, whether the lack matters for the specific work the analogy is being asked to do here. I want the disanalogies that are merely local separated from the ones that go to the heart of the intended use.

Third, treat the two halves of the dilemma separately, since I suspect the analogy is in better shape for one than for the other.

(a) Identification. On the familiar illustration, two rival reductions of the naturals into set theory — the von Neumann finite ordinals and the Zermelo ones — disagree about questions arithmetic itself never asks, such as whether three is a member of seventeen. Does the chess treatment of such surplus features transfer? In the game we can cleanly separate the role from the matter of the piece because we antecedently have the board and the rules independently of any candidate piece: is having that independently given structure precisely what is at issue in the numbers case, so that the analogy presupposes what it was meant to establish? And does your answer differ depending on whether the structuralism on offer is eliminative, taking structures to be exhibited by systems of objects, or non-eliminative, taking the structure to exist in its own right?

(b) Epistemology. Does the we-stipulated-the-rules story transfer? On a version of structuralism according to which the structure is not made by our stipulations, the epistemic story looks as though it should break off at exactly the point where it was supposed to help. There is a further worry I want addressed: does the analogy so much as reach the hard cases? Chess on a fixed finite board is in principle mechanically decidable — the question whether White has a winning strategy is a finite computation — whereas no first-order axiomatization pins down the natural numbers up to isomorphism, and there are arithmetical truths that no axiomatization we accept settles. So our knowledge, if it is knowledge, outruns both stipulation and proof in a way that the chess case may simply not model.

Fourth, and this is where I trust my own judgment least: I want you to pressure-test a repaired version of the analogy. Suppose we make the chess more like the arithmetic by stipulating that the rules are only partially recorded and are indefinitely extendible, so that no one has or could have the complete rulebook, and that the board has no fixed finite extent. Call it unwritten chess. Once the positions are no longer pinned down by the rules — there being players, as it were, that satisfy everything recorded while lying outside the progression we had in mind — has the identification problem reappeared inside the analogy itself, so that the picture now illustrates the disease rather than the cure? And if the repair works, does the repaired analogy still explain anything, or has it quietly become a picture of arithmetic as a game whose rules we are still discovering, which is not a stipulation at all? If it collapses, tell me what would do better: a natural language whose grammar is fixed while its semantics is not, a score as against its performances, a specification as against its implementations, an algorithm as against its executions. Give me whichever one you think can actually carry the structuralist claim, together with that analogy's own disanalogies stated as plainly as you stated chess's.

Finally, the verdict, with the epistemic hygiene I would want from a referee rather than a textbook. Does the conclusion the analogy was wheeled in to support — unproblematic access to mathematical truth plus no identification problem — survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy doing the work all along, and would the conclusion look very different if the imagery were dropped? Does the answer depend on which version of structuralism is in play, such that the analogy is only honest for one of them? And please be explicit throughout about which of your claims are settled among people who work on this and which are genuinely contested, where you are relying on a contested reading of Benacerraf or of structuralism, and how confident you are in each of (a), (b), and your assessment of the unwritten-chess variant. Do not smooth over live disagreement by writing as though one side had carried the field.
