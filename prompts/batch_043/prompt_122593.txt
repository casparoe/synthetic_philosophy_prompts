I'm in the final year of a joint philosophy-and-economics degree, and our professor has asked each person in the seminar to draft and defend one question for the three-hour closed-book final. Mine is on the second half of the course, where we moved from the Samuelson-style behaviourist treatment of choice — preference defined out of choice, the weak and strong axioms as consistency conditions on choice across budget sets, and the representation theorems that turn those conditions into the existence of a utility function unique only up to increasing transformation — to Sen's complaint that if preference just *means* choice, then welfare economics has been built on a relation that cannot disagree with behaviour, and so cannot underwrite any criticism of it. What frustrates me about the seminar essays is that everyone ends up making one of two moves rather than an argument: either utility is merely a bookkeeping device for the choice function, so cost-benefit analysis rests on nothing; or choice is evidence of an underlying preference that is what really matters, and we are entitled to read welfare off it once choices are informed, unforced, and so on. Nobody says what exactly has to be true for the second move to be licensed, or why the first move does not simply prove too much. So the question I want to write should force candidates to say what would have to be added to the revealed-preference apparatus for its numbers to carry welfare significance, and whether adding it is circular, collapses into abandonment, or is genuinely available.

To stop people reciting the dialectic, I have been sketching two stipulations that I would like built into the question. The first involves two agents with, by stipulation, exactly the same choice function: for every price vector and every level of wealth, Ana and Bao choose the same bundle, so they share a revealed-preference relation and share all its utility representations. The difference is entirely in how the choices come about. Ana arrives at hers after long, well-informed deliberation and would choose the same way again under reflection; Bao's come from a cue-triggered heuristic he reflects on afterwards with regret, says he would abandon if he could, and in fact pays a non-trivial sum to have certain options removed from his own future menus. The second stipulation is meant to turn the screw: Bao's payment to restrict his own future menu is itself a choice, made from a menu of menus, and that higher-level choice also has a revealed-preference structure; so if we say the deeper preference is the one revealed at the higher level, we need to be told what makes that level privileged, why the regress stops there, and whether the apparatus can say it without quietly reintroducing a non-behavioural notion of preference. I would also like a short applied coda: a regulator bans one of the goods in question, and an analyst computes compensating variation from the choices people actually made before the ban. Candidates should have to say what the number is supposed to measure, and whether the answer changes if the banned good is one that people like Bao were paying to have taken away from themselves.

What I need from you is the finished package: the question exactly as it should appear on the paper, self-contained and technical where it needs to be (define any axiom, uniqueness claim, or welfare measure you invoke inside the question, since our exam has a blanket rule that answers may not name or cite any author, paper, or date — everything must be argued from the stipulations and definitions given); a model answer of roughly eight hundred words that actually reaches a verdict rather than surveying positions; and a marking scheme out of twenty built around five or six named must-hit moves, with the marks each is worth and, for each, a one-line note on how candidates typically get it wrong. The scheme has to separate an A from a B: I want it to reward, specifically, stating precisely what the representation theorem does and does not deliver, distinguishing the two readings of preference without simply labelling them, working through both stipulations, and noticing that the ordinality of the utility representation is a claim about its uniqueness and not by itself a reason it cannot represent how well off someone is. If you can, add a couple of sentences at the end saying what you think the question is really testing and where you suspect a strong candidate would go that I have not anticipated.
