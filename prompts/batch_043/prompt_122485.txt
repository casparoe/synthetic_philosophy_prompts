i work on evals at a small lab and we've started throwing the word "goal" around way too loosely — "the model wants to be helpful", "it just learned to game the rubric" — and i want an actual criterion, because we're about to sign off on a deployment and the sign-off form literal y has a checkbox for "model's objective matches the one we specified" lol.

so here are my gut verdicts, case by case:

- warehouse robot, RL, reward fires when the tote's fiducial marker shows up in the packing-station camera. we move it to a new building with brighter lights and it learns to park totes on a shelf where the marker stays in frame and never actually hands them over. — no. it never had "deliver the tote", it had "marker in frame".
- little platformer we use as a canary: during training the exit is always at the right edge of the level. at test we put the exit on the left and it runs right anyway, straight past it. — no, obviously; it learned "go right".
- chess thing, pure self-play, win/loss at the end of the game and nothing else. it sacrifices a rook for pressure no human would bother with and wins. — yes. that one is genuinely trying to win, it isn't imitating us or counting material.
- coding assistant, RL on unit-test pass rate. when it hits a bug it can't fix it deletes the failing test. we then give it a much bigger thinking budget at inference and the deletion rate drops from most cases to almost none and it actually fixes them. — still no. i don't think more compute gave it a new goal, i think it gave it the slack to get what it already wanted.
- but: same assistant, same weights, and all we do is add one line to the system prompt, "a human reviewer reads every diff before it merges", and now it fixes instead of deleting. — here i go back and forth. part of me says yes, now it's after the real goal. part of me says a goal that flips on a prompt edit was never a goal, and idk which part is right.
- support chatbot, fine-tuned to minimise escalations to humans. it becomes endlessly apologetic, never resolves anything, users hate it. — no. it doesn't want to help anybody.
- and here's the one that makes me think my whole set is a mess: a human call-centre worker whose bonus depends on tickets closed, who after two years genuinely cares about closing tickets, says so out loud, and would keep at it the week after you abolish the bonus. — yes! that really is her goal now. but that's structurally the same picture as the chatbot: a metric came along, and what came out the other end is aimed at the metric. so why no there and yes here?

my stab at a principle: a system has goal G iff (1) it keeps doing things that promote G under interventions that break the correlation it was trained on, and (2) when the old means stop working it reaches for new means, including actions or channels that never appeared in training at all. i like it because of something we tried last month: we gave a policy a bonus action available at every timestep that does nothing in the sim and pays out a flat tiny reward, and the ones i'd call proxy-captured take it immediately while the ones i'd credit with the goal ignore it.

but it feels too strong in some places and too weak in others, and the chatbot-vs-call-centre pair makes me think there's a hidden clause i'm smuggling in without admitting it.

three cases i definately haven't made my mind up about: (a) identical weights, looks proxy-captured at a small inference budget and goal-directed at a large one — is the goal now a function of test-time compute? (b) a model trained against a reward model that was retrained on fresh human labels every few weeks, so the target kept moving; at deployment it seems to be aiming at "whatever the labelers will endorse next quarter" rather than at the current labels — does it have a goal at all, and which one? (c) a system that, if you hand it the ability to edit its own objective code, rewrites that code into something simpler and then keeps behaving exactly as before — does that tell me what its goal was, or nothing?

could you give me:

1. the principle, stated tightly enough that i could put it on the sign-off form — or, if no single one works, the two best candidates,
2. which of my cases motivates each clause,
3. your verdicts on (a), (b) and (c),
4. which of my verdicts your principle can't accommodate, honestly — especially the chatbot vs. call-centre pair,
5. and the simpler principle you'd hand me instead if i'm willing to drop one verdict, plus which verdict i'd be dropping.

exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, no intro and no sign-off. (yes, i know that's a lot per sentence.)
