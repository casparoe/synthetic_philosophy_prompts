I am giving a forty-five-minute paper next month at a joint cosmology and philosophy-of-science workshop, with a twenty-minute discussion slot, and I would like a Question and Answer preparation sheet for it. I do not want a defence of the view, still less a rewritten version of the paper; I want the question period anticipated.

The paper's thesis, in summary. The dispute about non-empirical confirmation in string theory and multiverse cosmology has been conducted as though the question were whether such arguments can confirm a *theory*. I argue that the proper object of non-empirical support is never a theory but a *constraint*: what these arguments can underwrite is the claim that the space of serious candidates is narrower than we had supposed, or that some structure is unavoidable given constraints we already accept. I then distinguish two species of argument. Exclusion arguments operate over a space of alternatives that is fixed independently of the theory being supported — fixed, say, by consistency conditions inherited from an empirically established low-energy theory. Saturation arguments operate over a space the theory itself generates: the landscape of string vacua, or the multiverse's realisation of every value of the parameters. My central claim is that saturation arguments are self-licensing and cannot confirm. A theory that first enlarges the hypothesis space and then survives as the only occupant of it has done nothing to raise its own probability, since enlarging the space lowers the prior of every hypothesis within it, including the one doing the enlarging; the very richness that makes a framework hard to falsify cannot be what supports it. I call this the self-licensing problem, and I argue that the notorious measure problem is the same difficulty one level down: leaving the measure free is leaving the space over which any typicality claim is made unfixed, and a multiverse with a free measure has a high likelihood for every possible observation, which makes it confirmation-proof rather than confirmed. I also give an account of why the familiar symmetry objection — that conditioning on our own existence cannot favour the multiverse, since we exist under that hypothesis and under the single-universe hypothesis alike — is right in its conclusion but mislocates the datum, and I offer a positive category, exploratory warrant, for what saturation arguments *can* give: a reason to keep working inside a framework rather than a reason to believe it.

The audience, precisely. About thirty people, in three groups. First, working cosmologists and string theorists, themselves divided between those who treat the landscape plus anthropic selection as the best available account of the cosmological constant and those working on swampland-style constraints, who take the task to be shrinking the landscape rather than populating it. They will be impatient with anything that sounds like philosophy pronouncing their research programme unscientific, and they will want to know what would change my mind: which empirical result, or which piece of mathematics, would move me. Second, philosophers of science, mostly confirmation theorists of a broadly Bayesian temper plus a few who model scientific practice; they will press me to say exactly what a hypothesis space is, what 'fixed independently of the theory' comes to, whether my claim is a claim about confirmation, about priors, or about the pragmatics of research, and whether the self-licensing problem is genuinely distinct from the old complaint that a theory with enough adjustable parameters is unfalsifiable. Third, two senior figures of an older cast who think this whole area abandoned testability decades ago; they will be friendly to my conclusion for reasons I do not share, and I would rather not have my argument mistaken for theirs.
