Hey — I run a pretty informal reading group (mostly grad students from other departments plus two very arguementative undergrads) and we're spending one session on the question of why human extinction seems worse than just a very large number of deaths. You know the sort of thing: the afterlife conjecture idea that a lot of what we care about quietly presupposes that there will be people after us, so that the end of the species is bad in a way that isn't just the badness of everyone dying. We're not reading anything for the session and I don't want any citations, reading suggestions or bibliographies — I want the conceptual map itself, built out in my handout.

What I need is a diagnostic decision tree, not an essay. It starts from one commitment everybody in the room already shares: human extinction next week would be very bad, and worse than the eight billion deaths alone would be. From there, eight branch questions that walk a person to a named view about where the extra badness actually lives.

Constraints:

- No question may ask whether extinction is bad, and no node may contain the name of a position or a philosopher. Every node has to be a concrete case or a yes/no claim someone could answer cold, without knowing what view it leads to.
- Build it from the logical space first, not from a list of names: give me the three or four axes your questions vary (roughly: whether the badness has to be bad for someone, whether it tracks the value of the future that gets lost, whether mere biological continuity of the species helps, whether a successor species helps), lay out which combinations of answers are available, and only then drop the actual views into the cells.
- Seven to nine leaves, each with a short name and one line on its most awkward commitment. It must include: the deflationary nothing-beyond-the-deaths leaf; a person-affecting leaf that cashes the extra badness out in what extinction does to people who actually exist (meaning, projects cut short, time-relative interests); the meaning-presupposes-an-aftermath leaf; at least one impersonal leaf where it's the lost value of the future doing the work; and a leaf where what matters is the cultural or collective project rather than the biological lineage.
- Please use these four cases among the nodes. (1) The sterilizing pandemic: nobody dies early, no pain, civilization runs another sixty years and ends with the last cohort dying of old age. (2) The net-negative future: we know the next ten thousand years would hold far more suffering than happiness, and people still desperately want humanity to continue. (3) Five thousand survivors on an island: the species continues biologically, and that's all. (4) Succession: we die out but a flourishing civilization of uploads or another species inherits our languages, archives and art and builds on them.
- Flag any two leaves your tree can't really separate, and say what further question you'd need to pry them apart.
- Note two or three combinations of answers that nobody actually occupies, with one line each on whether the cell is empty because the view is incoherent, just unattractive, or merely untried.

Plain ASCII diagram is fine, American English please. At the very end, on its own last line, give me \answer{...} with the label of the single branch question that does the most classificatory work — the one whose answer rules out the most leaves.
