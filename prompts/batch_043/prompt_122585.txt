I'm retired — spent most of my working life in freshwater ecology, counting things in trays and arguing about sample sizes — and about a year ago I started going to a philosophy reading group at the library. One of the regulars, I'll call him Pete, has extremely firm opinions about food, and I've been trying to reverse-engineer the rule he's running, because a new case came up last week and I want to know what he'll say about it.

What Pete has committed to so far:

- Per gram of protein, beef is clearly worse than chicken, and he gets there by pointing at land. Cattle tie up something like an order of magnitude or two more land per gram of protein than poultry does; I don't trust the exact number and neither does he. I keep bugging him about it, because on my arithmetic that verdict definately doesn't fall out of just counting animals: a kilo of protein is maybe a fiftieth of a cow that lived a year and a half, versus three or four broilers that lived six weeks. On time-confined, chicken looks worse. So whatever rule he's running has to either load a lot of weight onto land, or treat a cow-day as worth much more than a chicken-day.
- Chicken is worse than lentils, but he won't call lentils clean. Pressed on it he just says "mice," and then refuses to clarify whether he means the mice killed at harvest and by pest control, or the mice that get to exist because there's a field there. I've come to think that ambiguity is the whole ballgame and he knows it.
- Mussels and oysters: basically free, eat as much as you like.
- Eggs from a neighbour's hens that live out their full lives: about as good as food gets.
- Wild-caught fish: he won't give a verdict at all, says it makes his head hurt, changes the subject.

The new case: someone in the group is pushing insect protein — farmed house crickets or black soldier fly larvae, raised in a warehouse on food waste, so the land footprint is basically the warehouse plus a bit of feed. I want to know what Pete says about swapping chicken for that, per gram of protein, and whether he'd change his mind if the insects were raised on dedicated grain instead of waste.

Here's why I can't tell. By my back-of-envelope (don't hold me to it), a kilo of protein is a few chickens but thousands — maybe tens of thousands — of crickets. Insect nervous systems are orders of magnitude smaller than a bird's, nobody agrees whether they feel anything at all, and whatever they feel they don't feel it for long. Meanwhile the land term, which is doing the heavy lifting in his beef verdict, more or less vanishes.

I can think of two or three rules that fit everything he's said so far: one where you add up suffering-time weighted by capacity for suffering, and treat land use as a cost because it kills and displaces wild animals whose lives are worth living; one where wild lives are net negative, so knocking back wild populations counts in farming's favour, and where every plausibly-sentient individual gets counted at roughly its probability of sentience with no big discount for being small; and one where he isn't adding up experiences at all but ranking foods by land, emissions and pressure on ecosystems, using farmed-animal suffering as a rough proxy for badness. The first and third say crickets are great, arguably better than lentils. The second says they're a catastrophe, worse than chicken, because the headcount is enormous and there's no land-use offset to set against it.

So: which does he say, and how confident are you? What other rule fits his verdicts about as well as those do? And what's the single further case I should put to him next month that would seperate the ones that agree on crickets? My own candidates were the same protein raised on dedicated cropland versus on waste, or field-grown lentils versus lentils from a sealed vertical farm with no field and no mice in it at all — but I'd rather hear which one actually cuts, and why. I'm much less interested in whether crickets are yuck than in what he's really counting.
