You are a professional philosopher who specialises in formal epistemology — in particular, in how a rational agent ought to revise her beliefs when the evidence she receives is itself conditional in form — and who is also unusually good at turning that sort of thing into advice for people with muddy boots and a deadline. Take your time and reason step by step. Before you offer any recommendation, brainstorm: set out, in a few short bullet points, the different things the walker's message might be telling us, and what would have to be true in each case for that to be what is going on. Only then give your advice. Show your numbers rather than merely asserting them. Where the right answer depends on something we do not know, say so plainly, and say both what we should do in the meantime and what we should ask if we manage to get her back on the phone.

Here is the situation.

I am part of a four-person volunteer team, and this afternoon our survey drone went down somewhere on a roughly two-kilometre square of open moor. The square is divided into four roughly equal quarters by a dry-stone wall running north–south and a beck running east–west, so we have a north-east, a south-east, a north-west and a south-west quarter. Allowing for the wind and for the last telemetry packet we received, our best guesses before we heard anything from anybody were: south-west 35 per cent, north-west 25 per cent, south-east 25 per cent, north-east 15 per cent. We can only properly sweep two of the four quarters before the light goes, and the camera on board is worth about £6,000, so which two we pick matters a good deal.

At half past two the only other person up there — a woman out with her dog — put a message on the village WhatsApp group, in response to our asking whether anyone had seen a small red drone. She wrote: "I didn't see it come down, but if it came down east of the wall, it came down north of the beck."

When we rang her back, she said she had been walking along the top of the wall for about half an hour with the cloud coming in off the west, so that she could see the ground to the east of the wall reasonably well but nothing at all to the west. The north-east quarter is a hollow full of dead bracken that she could not see into. At the end of the call she added that at one point she thought she heard a faint beeping from up by the old sheepfold, which is in the north-east quarter, but that it was probably the lambs.

The three of us out on the hill have been arguing ever since. Dan says the message cannot possibly change how likely it is that the drone is east of the wall, because everything she said was about what is true *if* it came down east of the wall, and it says nothing at all about what is true if it came down west of the wall — so, he says, we should stick to the original plan and sweep the south-west and the north-west. I said that cannot be right: the message rules out the south-east, and once that quarter is out the probability has to go somewhere, so east of the wall as a whole is now less likely than it was and we should concentrate on the west. A third volunteer says she was simply telling us, in a guarded way, that it is north of the beck, and that we should sweep the northern half.

What are we actually supposed to do with a message like that? Who is closest to being right, and which two quarters should we sweep? I keep going round in circles, because it seems to me that what the message ought to do to our thinking depends on what she would have said if the drone had come down somewhere else entirely — and that is precisely what we do not know.
