hey, this is probably a philosophy question wearing a hoodie, but it's been bugging me for a week.

We have an internal data pipeline called Jefferson (all our services are named after historical figures — long story — Churchill is the alerting service, don't ask). Someone posted in #general asking that we rename it, and a staff engineer replied with basically "I get it, but he was a man of his time, you cant judge him by todays standards," and that ended the thread. I feel like that sentence is doing a LOT of work and I can't say what work, which is why I lost the thread.

So: what are the precise things "he was a man of his time" could be claiming? I have my own crude version, and being a software person I think of it as two: (1) grading on a curve — you judge him against his contemporaries instead of against us; (2) pinning to the current version — you judge him against today's standards, which he couldn't have met because the relevant ideas weren't available to him yet. I'm guessing there are more readings than that, that some are trivial, and that at least one is straightforwardly false.

Three things that make me suspicious, which you should feel free to use or knock down:

- We say "ahead of his time" as praise constantly, and nobody ever answers that with "well, you cant credit him, he was a man of his time." Jefferson is the obvious case: the guy wrote the famous line about all men being created equal while owning people, and depending on which way we're arguing that's either "ahead of his time" or "of his time." If the slogan is a general point about historical distance, why does it only ever get applied to the bad parts? Feels like you don't get to check out only the commits you like.
- There's a real difference between "he wasn't a bad person," "it's not really our business to say," and "we should keep the name on the pipeline." Even if the excuse works on the first one, the argument we were actually having was the third one, and I don't see how the first gets you there.
- I vaguely remember that some people at the time objected to what the Spanish were doing to the native population in the Caribbean — a priest or a friar or something? — so the "literally nobody could have thought otherwise" version seems shaky for Columbus at least. Don't build anything load bearing on that, I'm not sure about it and I can't go looking right now.

Can you lay the readings out as a menu, roughly trivial to radical, and for each one say what exactly it's asserting, whether it's actually plausible, and — this is the part I care about — whether it would support the move my coworker made, i.e. treating the question as settled and the renaming request as out of scope. Please flag especially the readings that are really claims about us (our standing to judge, whether we'd have done the same, what renaming would even accomplish) dressed up as claims about him, because I suspect that's where most of the persuasive force is hiding. And tell me which reading you think someone like my coworker actually means when they type that.

Last thing: say how confident you are in each verdict and call out the single one you're least sure about. I'd much rather get an honest "this bit is me guessing" than a tidy confident essay.
