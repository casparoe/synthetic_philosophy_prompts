I'm a fourth-year PhD student writing a chapter on accuracy-first epistemology, and I have a standing disagreement with my supervisor that I can't locate, because I can't work out which norm she is actually applying. I want to reconstruct the rule she is running and predict her verdict on a case that will come up in my next supervision, rather than to settle who is right.

Her verdicts so far, all about sharp credence functions over finite partitions, all given on the assumption that accuracy is the sole fundamental epistemic good and that pragmatic considerations never enter:

1. Two-cell partition {P, ¬P} with credences (0.7, 0.7): impermissible. She gives the standard dominance argument, and when I re-ran it under the log score instead of the Brier score she gave the same verdict and added that she does not want any of her conclusions to depend on which of the familiar strictly proper rules we use.

2. Presented with a step rule that scores a credence only by whether it clears 0.5 — proper but not strictly proper, and unable to distinguish 0.51 from 0.99 — she conceded that the dominance argument fails there, since (0.9, 0.2) is undominated, but treated that as a reductio of the rule rather than as a permission. A measure that cannot tell those two credences apart, she said, is not tracking accuracy.

3. She will not take a verdict from an improper rule at face value either, on the grounds that an improper rule tells an agent to misrepresent her own credences; and she has said in seminars that arguments which ask what an incoherent agent would *expect* are second-best, because expectation is undefined for incoherent credences. She prefers dominance formulations for exactly that reason, but has also said that if the only argument for a norm presupposed the norm, what we get is a reason not to stray rather than a requirement — I have never got her to say which of those two attitudes wins.

4. She is a permissivist about priors: with no evidence, any probabilistic distribution over the partition is permissible, and she rejects attempts to extract a unique uniform prior from accuracy alone. So she does not require optimality; she allows a whole class of permissible options.

5. She also relaxes sharp probabilism in one direction: an agent with no evidence about a coin's bias may, she has ruled, be in a state properly represented by the set of all distributions with P(H) between 0.3 and 0.7, and not merely by the single function 0.5.

The new case. A manuscript circulating in the department proposes measuring the inaccuracy of a whole credence function not by the sum of its per-cell errors but by its largest per-cell error: the sup-norm distance to the truth vector, the limit of the L^p measures as p goes to infinity. The motivation is epistemic risk aversion — the disvalue of a system of beliefs is the disvalue of its worst mistake, in the way a leximin or maximin aggregator ranks outcomes by their worst coordinate. Over a three-cell partition, two things follow. First, the function (0.5, 0.5, 0.5) has inaccuracy 0.5 in every world and is dominated by nothing: to beat 0.5 in world i you would need c_i above 0.5 and every other cell below 0.5, and the three worlds' requirements cannot be met together. Second, it has the best worst-case inaccuracy of any function, coherent or not, since any coherent function has a smallest cell of at most 1/3 and therefore suffers an error of at least 2/3 at that cell's world; as far as I can tell, (0.5, 0.5, 0.5) is the unique optimum. Note that nothing like this happens with two cells, where (0.5, 0.5) is coherent, so verdicts 1 and 2 do not settle it.

Next week's case: three mutually exclusive hypotheses about which mechanism produced a dataset, no evidence distinguishing them, and an agent who assigns 0.5 to each. Will she call that state rationally permissible, impermissible, or rationally required?

More specifically: which rule fits her track record — probabilism as the fundamental norm with dominance as its epistemic explanation where available; dominance as the operative norm with probabilism as a corollary that holds only given well-behaved measures; a two-stage view on which a candidate measure must first pass a legitimacy filter (strict truth-directedness, or separability, or propriety) and the worst-mistake measure fails it because truthward movements get masked by whichever cell is worst; or an optimality norm rather than mere undominance? Say what each predicts about this case, give me your best guess with the reasons for preferring it, and tell me which further case would discriminate the surviving rules. I have two candidates in mind and would like to know which is more diagnostic: the function (0.55, 0.5, 0.5), which is not strictly dominated but is weakly dominated and not optimal; and the same construction over a hundred cells, where 0.5 to each cell sums to 50.

Two complications I would also like handled. First, I tried to check whether the result is an artefact of the limit. Under L^4 inaccuracy, (0.5, 0.5, 0.5) is dominated — the symmetric function with roughly 0.44 in each cell scores about 0.645 in every world against its 0.658 — but by another incoherent function; nothing coherent I tried dominates it at p = 4 either, the coherent candidates landing around 0.69 to 0.79 in their worst world. So the failure of coherent domination may persist at finite p even where undominance does not. Does that matter for her verdict, or is the p = ∞ case the only one doing work? Second, it has occurred to me that the worst-mistake measure may not be a competitor measure of accuracy at all but accuracy pushed through a risk-averse aggregation, so that adopting it builds in a non-epistemic attitude — the same complaint one would make about reading a concave transform of money as a measure of the value of money. Given her permissivism in verdict 4 and her tolerance of imprecise states in verdict 5, I am not confident she would accept that move, and I would like to know whether she has an easier out: whether she can treat (0.5, 0.5, 0.5) as a legitimate imprecise state misdescribed as a sharp function, and what that would cost her elsewhere.
