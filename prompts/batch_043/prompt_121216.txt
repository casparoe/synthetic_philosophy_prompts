I've been reading the rationalist blogs and forums for something like fifteen years, and I'm now putting together a proposal for a one-semester independent study (around 7,000 words) whose topic is Roko's basilisk — not as a meme, but as a test case for what an acausal threat would have to be like in order to have any force at all.

My starting discomfort is with the standard dismissal in the comment sections. It goes roughly: once I have already failed to help, punishing me costs the system something and gains it nothing, so the threat is not credible, so there is nothing here to be worried about. But that seems to evaluate the system's choice at the level of individual tokens. If the system selects policies the way functional decision theory says a rational agent should — the same policy evaluated across every instance of the decision problem, including the instance where it is deciding what to do to a reconstruction of me — then the credibility question has to be asked at the level of policies, and there it looks much less settled.

My half-formed thought, which I want turned into something arguable, is that the real crux may not be decision-theoretic but epistemic. For the threat to bite at all, the system has to be able to (i) reconstruct something that is me in the relevant sense, and (ii) target the counterfactual me — the one who never encountered any of this and would have chosen differently — rather than the actual me, whose choice is arguably already fixed by the fact that I know about the threat. Both look like points at which the correlation being exploited can decay, and nobody seems to have asked how far it can decay before the threat has no force left. A second thread: the symmetry case. A system that retroactively rewards those who helped is doing the same kind of acausal thing and it costs far less. If the punishment version is incoherent I want to know whether the reward version is too, or whether the usual explanations of the asymmetry quietly presuppose the very contingency the basilisk needs.

What I need is the proposal document: the question stated so that it has a determinate answer; why the existing positions leave it open; the method — what I would actually do, month by month, including how I would set up the cases (a small taxonomy of epistemic setups: exact simulation, coarse reconstruction, inference from the historical record, no model at all) and why that rather than another pass at FDT versus CDT; what the possible results are and what would count as the project failing; and an honest scope section. I will fill in citations myself, so please don't give me a bibliography.

Two further things. First, address whether writing a paper like this is itself the thing the argument says not to do, and what, if anything, a careful writer should do about that. Second, be explicit about which parts of this are settled among people who actually know the literature and which are live disputes — I don't want to build a term paper on a premise I only believe is consensus because everyone in my feed keeps repeating it.
