Hey — I'm a science-and-ideas reporter working on a piece about why smart people stay dug in once they've picked a side politically, and I want to hang the whole thing on Aumann's agreement theorem. The way it was explained to me: if two people are both rational and each knows where the other one has landed, they can't agree to disagree — so any disagreement that survives more than a few minutes of actual talking is proof that somebody is reasoning badly or arguing in bad faith. That's a great hook and I'd like to lean on it pretty hard, but I'd rather find out now than in a correction box that I've misunderstood the thing.

A few things I need pinned down:

1) Is the agreement in the theorem about a single number — my probability for one event versus yours — or does it say their whole pictures of the world have to line up? My current draft implies the second version and my editor definately prefers it.

2) Both of my real-world examples start out with wildly different priors. One of them is a newsletter guy who told me his prior is just: whatever makes the other side look stupid. I'm assuming the theorem still squeezes them together once they've traded enough rounds of back-and-forth, so the different starting points don't matter much? Or is the shared prior doing all the work and I need to restructure the piece around that?

3) Related, and this is the bit I'm least sure I remember right. Someone told me you can swap the shared-prior assumption for a weaker one — the two priors only have to be close in some formal sense, and then you get a ceiling on how far apart their posteriors can stay once those posteriors are out in the open, with the ceiling scaled to how close the priors were to begin with. I took notes on my phone and lost them and I can't find anything online. Does that sound like a real result, and if it is, is the ceiling actually informative in practice or is it one of those bounds that's technically finite and morally useless?

4) Does any of this survive contact with someone who isn't updating at all — a strategist who announces numbers picked to win the argument rather than numbers that came out of evidence? My instinct is that the theorem has nothing to say about that case and I should cut the example, but instinct is what got me into this.

5) I half remember something saying that even when agreement is guaranteed in principle, getting there can take an insane number of rounds, which would sort of be my actual thesis: the theorem is true and irrelevant. Am I garbling that, or is it real?

Is that fair to Aumann, or is it the kind of sentence that gets me a rude email from a decision theorist?
