ok so weird request. I'm in this very informal reading group (four of us, we meet in the back of a wine bar, one guy is a lapsed econometrician who derails everything) and last week we did chapter 25 of The Prince, the one titled something like "How Much Fortune Can Do in Human Affairs and How It May Be Opposed." We ended up fighting about whether virtù and fortuna are actually an explanatory pair or whether they're just a really elegant way of relabeling outcomes after the fact. The lapsed econometrician's line was: any vocabulary in which a win is credited to the agent's virtù and a structurally identical loss is credited to fortuna has zero falsifiable content, and it will survive every possible dataset. I don't fully agree but I can't find the seam in his argument either, and it's been bugging me enough that I want to see it staged instead of argued.

So: write me a ~700 word parody, formatted as a real deliverable memo from an invented Renaissance management consultancy (Bardi & Partners? you pick, but make it sound like a firm that bills in florins and has a "practice area" for statecraft) doing an after-action review for His Excellency the Duke of Serravalle on why the annexation of a neighbouring valley collapsed eighteen months after succeeding. Ground rules, sorry there are a lot: (1) the memo may never use the words luck, chance, or random — every cause has to be routed through the chapter's own vocabulary (virtù, fortuna, occasione, necessity, impetuosity vs caution, the times, the mood of the people); (2) the firm should have invented a metric, call it the Fortune Attribution Index, with a specific number attached to this engagement (say 0.6-something of the outcome variance allocated to fortuna), and the methodology appendix that derives it should quietly be the circular thing my friend is complaining about — successes load onto the client's virtù, failures load onto fortuna, and the index is computed after you already know how it went; (3) include the chapter's flood-control metaphor, but rendered as capital budgeting: pre-emptive dike-and-dam capex approved in a calm quarter, with a number attached, and the memo should be visibly uncomfortable about the fact that the budget item only looks wise or wasteful depending on whether a flood shows up; (4) there should be a short dissenting appendix from a junior analyst who has noticed the symmetry problem, and the partners' rebuttal of him should itself be an instance of the problem rather than an answer to it; (5) the headline recommendation should be the one piece of advice that nobody can follow — adapt your nature to the times — delivered with total confidence and followed by the invoice.

Two last things. If you use the chapter's personification of fortune as a woman who has to be mastered and who favours the young and the reckless, run it through an anachronistic HR-compliance footnote rather than straight into the prose: the joke should be on the author getting flagged, not on the target of the metaphor. And please don't go for the easy stuff — no evil-advisor gags, no "the ends justify the means" bumper sticker, no sneering. The thing being satirized is a conceptual structure: a pair of terms flexible enough to explain everything, packaging itself as hard-headed advice. Someone who knows the chapter well should finish it feeling the critique was fair and a bit uncomfortably accurate, and someone who has never opened the book should still follow the whole thing and laugh at least twice.
