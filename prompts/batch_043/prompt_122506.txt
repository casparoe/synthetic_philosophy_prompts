My housemate and I got into this at like 1am and now I cant tell whether we found something or whether we just said "rational" in two different senses for an hour. I need someone to point a finger at a single step and then tell me what giving it up costs.

Start with Parfit's hitchhiker: a driver will take you to town in exchange for a promise of $100 on arrival; once you're in town paying does nothing for you; the driver can see this coming; so you never get the ride. The usual upshot: you do better if you're the sort of person who'd pay, i.e. it can be rational to be irrational.

Now my variant. Same desert, except every driver in the region shares a permanent public ledger of who paid whom, and they all check it before they stop. Call it the app.

(1) In the original one-shot case, the reason to pay isn't causal — the ride already happened. It's that paying is evidence of being a paying type, and drivers stop for paying types. So the case is supposed to show that rationality sometimes requires acting against your local reasons.

(2) But put the app in and the case changes shape completely: with a public ledger, paying *causes* future drivers to stop for you. Now even a hardline causal decision theorist pays, and there's no irrationality anywhere. So the interest of the original case looks like an artifact of the record being missing.

(3) But hold on. The ledger only pays off if there will be future dealings you care about. On your last ride ever — you're moving away, or you're dying — paying buys nothing, and a driver who knows it's your last ride won't stop. So the unraveling comes back at the horizon, and backwards induction chews up the ledger: at every step, if the next step's benefit is already settled, paying is pointless.

(4) So the ledger doesn't remove the need for the disposition, it just relocates it. What you really need is to be the kind of person who pays even when paying can't help you — a disposition that outruns its own rationale.

(5) And a disposition that outruns its rationale just is an irrational disposition. So we're back to the original moral: rationality requires irrationality. Only now it generalizes. For every ledger-like device — reputation, credit history, the credibility of a threat, promises generally — the same reasoning shows the device only works if you'd honor it in the cases where honoring it does nothing for you.

(6) And these don't compete with each other. The argument for acquiring the promise-keeping disposition is the same argument, run again, for the debt-paying one, the credible-threat one, the tip-the-waiter one. Each is a seperate occasion on which someone earlier decides whether to benefit you on the basis of what they predict you'll do later, and each one's expected gain is computed the same way. So they add.

(7) So: rationality requires you to become as irrational as you possibly can, across the board.

(8) Which is nuts, and also self-sawing — the more irrational you are the worse everyone's predictions get, and note that (6) never notices this, because each disposition is priced holding the others fixed.

Where's the move that isn't licensed? My money is on (6), since the value of any one of these dispositions seems to depend both on how irrational you already are and on how much of the gain the other party can charge you for: a world full of people who reliably pay is a world where everyone bills them. But (6) can't be doing all the work, because with one disposition and a perfect predictor, step (5) already hands you "it's rational to be irrational," and that's already strange enough on its own. So maybe the trouble is up at (4)/(5), or even back at (1). Someone told me the fix is just "come to care about paying; then paying is locally rational and there's no irrationality at all," but that looks like the same trick one level down, since I'd be installing a desire for reasons that will have lapsed by the time it bites.

I'd rather keep (1) and (3) than swallow (7). I dont want to start evaluating whole policies or life plans instead of acts, because then I can't see what stops the guy in town from simply re-optimising.

So: which step do you reject, why did it look fine, and what's the bill for rejecting it? And separately — if you kill (6), do you still owe me a story about how much irrationality is optimal and why the answer isn't zero?
