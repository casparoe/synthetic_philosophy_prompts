QUESTION 4.

(a) Set out Kim's causal exclusion argument against non-reductive physicalism. Your answer should state the causal closure of the physical domain, the claim that mental properties are irreducible to and distinct from physical properties, and the exclusion principle: that no event has more than one distinct sufficient cause at a given time, unless it is a genuine case of causal overdetermination.

(b) Assess this reply: because the mental supervenes on the physical, a mental cause and its physical base are not independent of one another, so a case in which both cause some piece of behaviour is not the kind of case the exclusion principle was meant to rule out, and the exception for genuine overdetermination applies.

(c) What, if the argument works, is left for the non-reductive physicalist?

STUDENT ANSWER.

Kim's exclusion argument is meant to show that non-reductive physicalism is an unstable position. Take a bit of behaviour B — me reaching for a glass of water. Physicalism is committed to the causal closure of the physical domain: every physical event that has a cause has a physical cause, so B already has a perfectly good physical cause P. But I also want to say that my desire for a drink, a mental event M, caused B. And non-reductive physicalism says M is a genuine further property, not reducible to P, because mental properties are multiply realisable — very different physical setups could realise the same desire. Now the exclusion principle: no event can have two distinct sufficient causes at the same time unless it's a genuine case of overdetermination, like two gunmen firing at once. P is sufficient for B on its own, and M is distinct from P, so M's causation of B is excluded. M either collapses into P, which is reduction, or does no causal work at all, which is epiphenomenalism. Those are the only options left, and neither is what the non-reductive physicalist signed up for. That's the instability.

One reply deserves attention. M supervenes on P: no mental difference without a physical difference. So M and P aren't independent of each other in the way two gunmen are. But the exception built into the exclusion principle is there precisely for causes that aren't independent — that's what makes the overdetermination benign rather than merely coincidental — so the principle doesn't exclude M here. The principle was aimed at coincidence cases, and the mental case isn't a coincidence, since the dependence is systematic.

It's worth being clear about what the argument does and doesn't give you. It doesn't establish eliminativism: nothing in it says mental properties don't exist, only that on this picture they don't do causal work. Positively, the argument is best read as an argument for physicalism. Once you grant closure there's no room for extra non-physical causes, so the view that really loses is dualism, which is in exactly the same boat as non-reductive physicalism here. The person who can keep mental causation is the physicalist who gives up non-reduction and identifies M with P, so the argument supports reductive physicalism over both dualism and non-reductive physicalism. The remaining option is to accept epiphenomenalism and say the mind is causally idle, which almost nobody wants.
