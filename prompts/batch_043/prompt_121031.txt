I spent a little over thirty years as an experimental physical chemist, mostly in reaction kinetics, and since retiring I have been reading my way into decision theory. I have arrived at a view about the coherence arguments for expected utility maximization, and I would like it stress-tested rather than confirmed.

As I understand them — and correct me in your opening remarks if I have the geography wrong — the arguments come in two families. The first is pragmatic: an agent whose preferences violate the axioms can be induced to accept a package of bets that loses whatever happens, or to pay repeatedly for revisions of a plan that leave it no better off, and is thus exploitable in a way that does not depend on what it happens to want. The second is representational: an agent whose preferences satisfy the axioms can be described as if it assigned probabilities to states and utilities to outcomes and then maximized the expectation, so the machinery is not an extra posit but a reading off of structure the preferences already had.

My view, which I call the Modest Reading, is that these two families do not jointly deliver what they are advertised as delivering. Consistency, even of a very demanding kind, seems to me to be one thing; a positive norm telling me to maximize is another. The pragmatic arguments look like they need an adversary who knows my structure and is willing to construct a bespoke sequence of offers for me, and the representational arguments look like they deliver an as-if description, not a reason. So I hold that the arguments constrain a well-organized agent without settling that expected utility maximization is what rationality requires of it. Call the rival the Strong Reading.

Two things reinforce this for me, and I would like to know how much weight they bear.

The first is a rule I actually used. In decisions under uncertainty I first discarded every option whose worst outcome fell below a floor — a ruin floor, set by asking what magnitude of setback the lab, the grant, or the department could absorb without ceasing to function — and among the survivors I then picked by expected value. I did not apply a single utility function across the whole range; the floor did the work that a very steeply curved utility is supposed to do, but it did that work by lexicographic exclusion rather than by trade-off. A philosopher friend tells me this cannot be represented as maximizing expected utility for any utility function over any probability assignment, and that I was therefore pumpable. I never was pumped, in forty years of decisions made this way, and I want to know whether that fact is evidence of anything, or merely the unsurprising consequence of nobody having spent their career constructing traps for me.

The second is that the arguments seem to take the agent's probability assignments as given, whereas in scientific practice the numbers are themselves the output of a costly measurement process, and a rule that spends resources sharpening them before acting can look non-maximizing to an observer who treats the unsharpened numbers as the inputs. I suspect this is not really a gap in the theory, but I would like to be told where exactly it gets handled. There is a related point about repeated decisions: a diachronic pump seems to require that my later self still want what my earlier self wanted, and the stability of the agent across time is, it seems to me, part of what is in question rather than something the argument gets to assume.

So: what would count against the Modest Reading, and separately against my two-stage rule? Please do not simply rehearse the considerations that favor the Strong Reading; I want the defeaters — derivations, cases, formal results, empirical findings, or consequences I would find unacceptable — ranked by how much they should move me. Where a defender of the Strong Reading would herself concede that something counts against it, say so, and distinguish that from what only a critic would press. Tell me also whether the Modest Reading has been qualified into a position that risks nothing at all, and whether, for a claim of this kind, that insulation is a defect or merely the normal condition of a thesis about what reasons there are.

What would move me most, I should say, is either a demonstration that the ruin-floor rule is exploitable or self-defeating under conditions that are not adversarial — by a world that is not trying to pump me, merely indifferent — or a derivation showing that my floor either collapses into a utility function after all, so that I was maximizing all along under a strongly risk-averse tail, or else generates a genuine cycle in my preferences. What moves me least are intuitions elicited by cases involving sums of money large enough to be unfamiliar, or by tiny probabilities of enormous gains; I distrust my own responses there, having spent a career watching small samples mislead people.

Please assume I have no library and no internet here, and argue in your own terms rather than directing me to a literature I cannot consult.
