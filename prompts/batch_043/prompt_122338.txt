I sit on a search committee that has to rank sixty-one applicants for a single post, and for two meetings I have mostly taken minutes while two of my colleagues argue. I would be grateful for a point-by-point adjudication of their exchange, since we have to vote on Friday.

We score applicants on two axes, research record and teaching, each out of 100. As it happens, the pool forms an almost perfect staircase: applicant 1 scores (95, 5), applicant 2 (93.5, 6.5), and so on in steps of one and a half points down to applicant 61 at (5, 95) — each applicant a little weaker on research and a little stronger on teaching than the one before. The department's mission statement fixes the trade-off only within a range: a point of research is worth somewhere between 0.8 and 1.25 points of teaching, and no one on the committee is willing to be more precise than that.

Marisol opens. No member of the committee, she says, can honestly rank any adjacent pair: a point and a half on each axis lies inside the noise of the rubric. Yet applicants 1 and 61 are not a pair about which the committee is entitled to shrug. Their files are as unlike as any two in the pool, and depending on where the weight falls, one of them leads by as much as twenty-two points. So the relation we have been asked to apply cannot be transitive: sixty comparisons that are each a non-decision cannot add up to a decision. The demand that we return a strict ranking of all sixty-one is therefore incoherent, and we should instead shortlist a handful and deliberate about those directly.

Peter replies that this overreaches. A measuring instrument with a resolution limit fails to discriminate neighbouring readings while still ordering distant ones, and no one concludes from this that length is intransitive. We should distinguish three relations: detectably stronger than (transitive), no detectable difference (intransitive by design), and the underlying at least as strong as (transitive). The staircase only shows that the second of these is intransitive; it says nothing about betterness itself. The practical upshot is harmless: compute the underlying scores, and the resolution limit simply appears as ties in the output.

Marisol answers that the instrument analogy fails, because this is not one quantity measured imprecisely but two, and the rubric provides no exchange rate between them. That matters, she says, because of what the unfixed weight does to the staircase. Within the stated range each adjacent pair differs by less than half a rubric point in either direction, while the endpoints swing across a range of more than forty points — from applicant 1 ahead by 22.5 to applicant 61 ahead by 18. The imprecision does not stay small as one walks the chain; it is multiplied by sixty. Peter cannot dismiss that as a defect of a coarse instrument, since on his own account the instrument is in good order and it is the weight that is missing.

Peter concedes the missing weight but says it cuts against her. There are not sixty indeterminacies being compounded; there is one unknown number that appears in every link. Fix it anywhere in the stated range and the whole staircase becomes perfectly consistent: the endpoint difference is exactly sixty times the per-link difference, so nothing has been amplified — a single fixed quantity has been multiplied. The endpoints are uncertain only because the committee declined to fix the weight, which was known before anyone looked at the pool. And if the real worry is instead that the committee's per-link judgments are independently shaky, then the errors partly cancel: the spread at the endpoints grows like the square root of sixty rather than sixtyfold, so the endpoint comparison is roughly eight times better in signal-to-noise terms than any single link. On either model, he concludes, the chain is not the source of the trouble.

Marisol accepts the arithmetic but says it was never her claim; her claim concerns the deliverable. On Peter's own showing, the ranking is produced by stipulating a weight the department has declined to adopt, so the output is a decision rather than a measurement — and the staircase is epistemically inert. The fifty-nine applicants in between supply the committee with no evidence whatsoever about the weight, yet their presence is precisely what makes everyone feel licensed to state a verdict about 1 versus 61. The chain manufactures confidence without supplying information.

Peter agrees about the confidence, and draws the opposite lesson: the size of the pool is doing no work in her argument. Had only applicants 1 and 61 applied, the weight would be exactly as unsettled and the verdict exactly as sensitive to it; the staircase is rhetoric. What remains is a question about who gets to stipulate. Stipulating once, at the level of the weight, is the most parsimonious option available, and the mission statement is the department's own document to interpret. Her alternative — an honest partial order with an asterisk on the contested comparisons — will be read as a ranking regardless, and the gaps will in practice be filled by whoever is most willing to speak with spurious precision.

Please work through the six moves in order and tell me who is right about what in each: rate every move from clearly right through to clearly mistaken, with a brief justification for each rating. Then say who is more right overall, and — most importantly — what, if anything, both of them are missing about the structure of the problem.
