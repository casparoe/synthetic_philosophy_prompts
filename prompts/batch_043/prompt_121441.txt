I've got a two-year fellowship staring in October and I want to spend most of it on the standing-to-blame literature — who's permitted to blame, and whether being a hypocrite takes that permission away. But I don't want to write yet another paper on whether the hypocrite's blame is self-undermining; I want to work on whatever is upstream of the rest. The end I care about is practical-ish: that in five or ten years the people who actually write conduct codes — professional bodies, university panels, restorative justice programmes — have something defensible to say about who gets to condemn, rather than vibes about clean hands.

My shortlist, no order: (1) borrowed standing — A wasn't wronged and has no relationship to the wrong but takes up B's grievance; when does that generate standing and when is it just busybodying, and does it matter whether the grievance was adopted or manufactured? (2) the blamer who's never been tempted — she abstains from the domain entirely, so her hands are clean but she has no acquaintance with the temptation; is there a competence or epistemic condition on standing, not merely a clean-hands one? (3) aggregated blamers — a committee condemns, a third of its members would individually lack standing through hypocrisy; does the body inherit the deficit, and what does that depend on? (4) standing drift — how long a reformed wrongdoer's deficit lasts, and whether her own acknowledgement of it shortens the clock.

Which would you prioritise, and what's missing? I want the ordering defended, not listed: tell me the criteria you're using (upstream-ness, doable in two years, neglectedness, how much it would shift real institutional practice), where those criteria trade off against each other, and which of my four are pseudo-problems that dissolve under pressure.
