I've been chewing on a probability puzzle for a few weeks and would like someone to tell me where my reasoning goes wrong — or to confirm it. I have no academic background in philosophy or decision theory (I work in IT and do recreational maths on weekends), so please be patient with the formalities, and feel free to tell me if I am using a term incorrectly. The setup is this: a fair coin is tossed until it lands heads for the first time; if the first heads falls on toss n and n is odd you receive 2^n/n dollars, and if n is even you must pay 2^n/n dollars. So heads on the first toss wins you $2, on the second it costs you $2, on the third you win $8/3 (about $2.67), on the fourth you lose $4, and so on. Multiplying each payoff by its probability of 1/2^n leaves the series 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + …, which I recognise as the alternating harmonic series; summed in that order it equals the natural logarithm of 2, about 0.69, so I take the fair price of a single play to be roughly 69 cents. An article I read online about this game — I believe it was devised by a couple of philosophers some years ago, and I think it goes by the name of a Californian city — insisted that this reasoning is mistaken: because the series is only conditionally convergent, its terms can be reordered to add up to any number you please, including plus or minus infinity, and so the game has no expected value at all. Not zero, not infinite, but undefined.

I have four thoughts about this and cannot tell which, if any, are right. First: isn't the natural order the obviously correct one? The tosses occur in a definite temporal sequence — the two-dollar prize is settled at toss 1, the two-dollar penalty at toss 2, and so on — so summing in the order in which the game is actually played seems to single out ln 2 uniquely, and all this talk of rearrangement strikes me as mere bookkeeping that has nothing to do with the real game. Second: if I am wrong about that and the value is genuinely undefined, then since the sum can be rearranged to yield any figure whatsoever, what is to stop me from declaring the game worth $500 and rationally paying $500 to play it? That sounds absurd, but I cannot see what rules it out once the value is arbitrary. Third: I tried to settle the matter empirically by simulating the game on my laptop, and I also considered the more realistic version where the house has finite funds — say it can pay out at most a million dollars — but my running averages never seemed to settle down, and different numbers of runs gave me anything from a few cents to a few hundred dollars. I cannot tell whether that is just sampling noise I could smooth away with more runs or whether it is telling me something real about the game. Fourth, I invented what I call the Mirror game, identical except that the signs are reversed — you lose on odd tosses and win on even ones — and my intuition is that being offered both games together is worth exactly nothing, since they cancel; yet if neither game has a value, I do not see how the pair acquires one, and that makes me suspect my intuition is doing work it has no right to do.

So my question is: which of these is the correct way to think about the matter? Is the fair price of a single play about 69 cents, is there genuinely no answer, or is the value in some sense up to me? Does the rearrangeability of the series really let me justify any price I like? And if expected-value reasoning falls silent here, does that give me any reason at all to distrust it in the ordinary, bounded decisions where I rely on it constantly — insurance excesses, pension contributions, whether to carry an umbrella? I would be grateful for a clear verdict on each point rather than a survey of what various people have said: if my first thought is wrong, I want to know precisely why the order in which the tosses happen fails to settle the sum; if the answer is that only the finite-bankroll version of the game has a clean value, tell me what that value is and what it does and does not resolve; and if my simulation results are not mere noise, explain what they are showing me.
