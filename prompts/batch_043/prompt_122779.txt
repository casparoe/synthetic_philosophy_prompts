I keep arguing with myself about when it's actually legitimate to tell an adaptationist story about a trait, and the only way I can see to settle it is to write down my gut verdicts on a pile of cases and ask what principle, if any, they add up to.

Heart with four chambers, camera-type eye: adaptation, no hesitation, I'd bet my life on it. Panda's radial sesamoid, the thing everyone calls its thumb: adaptation for stripping bamboo, definitely — but I also want to say it is NOT an adaptation for being a clumsy wrist-bone version of a thumb, and I can't tell whether that second verdict is a real claim or me being pedantic. Human chin: spandrel, byproduct of the face shrinking faster than the jaw, I've never bought any of the stories about it being for speech or chewing or mate choice. Vertebrate blind spot: not an adaptation, it's the price of an inverted retina and a developmental history. Male nipples: obviously a byproduct, next case. Female orgasm: here's the one that bugs me — I really don't want to call it a spandrel, but structurally it looks exactly like the nipple case, and I can't articulate the difference. Fever: I say it's a defense and not just the body malfunctioning, but honestly I can't back that up past it feels designed. Craving sugar and fat: adaptation, absolutely, even though in my actual life it now lowers fitness rather than raising it. Feathers on a bird: I want to say adaptation for flight, but my own rule says they were there before flight and got co-opted, which feels like the wrong answer. Stotting in gazelles: I notice I trust this one much less than the heart case, and I think it's because there are like four or five competing stories and people just keep adding them, but I don't know why that should lower my confidence.

The rule I've been using: a trait is an adaptation for doing X in a lineage only if it was built by selection in that lineage because variants that did X better out-reproduced variants that didn't. Current usefulness isn't enough, and being currently indispensable isn't either. This gets me the nipples and the blind spot and arguably the chin, and it keeps the sugar craving an adaptation, but it gives me the wrong verdict on feathers, it can't say when a co-opted trait crosses over into being an adaptation for its new job, and it completely falls apart on grain — at coarse grain everything is an adaptation and at fine grain nothing is, and I don't know what fixes the size of the thing I'm allowed to ask about.

So here's a case I invented to break the tie, tell me if it's rigged. A burrowing lizard, call it the glasshouse skink: adults have a fused plate on the snout and it sure looks like a shovel. But its non-burrowing sister species have the same plate, only smaller, and developmentally it's just the seam where two scale fields meet; file the plate off and burrowing efficiency doesn't measurably change; males with bigger plates do leave more offspring, but only because females prefer them, and plate size has zero effect on how well they dig; and separately, the plated patch is the one spot on the head that doesn't get abraded, so animals with it get fewer infections. Is the plate an adaptation, and if the answer is adaptation for X but not for Y, what exactly is the X?

What I'd like: a principle stated precisely enough that I could apply it cold, with the case that motivates each clause, its verdict on the skink, its verdict on a couple of cases I haven't listed (the human taste for narrative storytelling, which I go back and forth on, and whatever case you think is most likely to break it), and an honest statement of which of my verdicts it can't accommodate. If two of them can't live together, tell me which one to drop and what it costs me. And please don't give me a reading list or tell me who to cite — I want the shape of the argument, not sources.
