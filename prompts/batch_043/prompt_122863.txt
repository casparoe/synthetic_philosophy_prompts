I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I go to a fortnightly reading group where a handful of us work through popular books on mind and language. Last week one of the group — who did a philosophy minor and is treated as our resident expert — walked us through the Twin Earth argument, and I wrote up her summary afterwards from my notes. The wording is mine, but I think I've kept the content:

> Putnam asks us to imagine a planet superficially identical to Earth, except that the clear, drinkable liquid in the lakes and rivers isn't H2O but a different compound, XYZ, which looks, tastes and behaves exactly the same. Before the rise of chemistry nobody on either planet could tell the difference, so Oscar and Twin Oscar are, molecule for molecule and belief for belief, in exactly the same psychological state. Yet Oscar's word "water" picks out H2O and Twin Oscar's picks out XYZ. So two speakers can share every mental state there is and still mean different things — meanings just ain't in the head. What fixes reference instead is the causal-historical chain running from a speaker's utterance back to the stuff itself, which is why Putnam says we should let the experts do the defining (the division of linguistic labour), and why the stereotype in your head — clear, drinkable, wet — only picks out the right extension so long as it happens to be true of the real stuff. The upshot, and this is what the book goes on to emphasise, is that Descartes was wrong to think the mind is transparent to itself: since what I mean depends on environmental facts I may not know, I can be ignorant of, or mistaken about, what my own words and thoughts are about, and no amount of introspection can settle the question. The book then makes the same point with a case about a patient who insists he has arthritis in his thigh: on this view the doctor isn't really correcting him, because the patient just means something slightly different by "arthritis", so there's no fact of the matter about which of them is right.

Two things are bothering me and I can't tell which of them is the real problem. The claims about introspection and about being wrong about your own thoughts feel stronger than anything a careful philosopher would extract from a thought experiment about a word for a liquid — but I can't locate the point at which the argument stops delivering them, and I don't want to dismiss the summary just because the conclusion sounds odd to an engineer. Separately, I tried to test whether I'd understood the mechanism by rebuilding the case with something from my own work, and I got a verdict I don't know how to interpret. Imagine two teams at different companies, with identical tooling, identical runbooks and indistinguishable observable evidence, both using the phrase "flaky test" and both quarantining a test after two unexplained failures. On Team A's build farm, the quarantined tests fail intermittently because of genuinely nondeterministic scheduling interleavings; on Team B's, they fail because of a slowly drifting hardware clock in one rack, which nobody has found and which nobody has the budget to hunt for. Both teams picked the term up from the same well-known blog post, so the usage has a single historical origin. Does "flaky test" refer to different things on the two teams, in the way "water" refers to different things on the two planets — or do the criteria the speakers actually apply do all the work here, so that both teams mean the same thing and Team B is simply mistaken about the cause, rather as pre-1750 speakers were mistaken about water? Does the shared causal origin settle the matter if reference really is a historical chain? Does it make any difference that the XYZ difference was discoverable in principle by doing chemistry, whereas Team B's clock drift is discoverable only by an investigation nobody will fund — and if the case does work, how does it behave if you gradually dial the clock drift down towards zero, so that there is no sharp point at which the hidden cause changes?
