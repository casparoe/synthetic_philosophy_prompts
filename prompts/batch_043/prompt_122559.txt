I am presenting a paper at my department's Political Economy Workshop in three weeks, and I want a Q&A preparation sheet for the question period, not a defense of the paper.

The thesis. The Moloch framing is doing the work that three distinct failure modes should be doing, and the remedies people derive from it fit only one of the three. I divide the cases by what happens to the value each party sacrifices in order to keep competing:

Dissipation. The sacrificed value is destroyed outright; no one receives it. A positional arms race in which the only thing purchased is relative standing. The group is poorer and nobody is richer.
Transfer. The sacrificed value is captured by a third party. Jurisdictions relaxing regulation or cutting taxes to attract mobile capital; firms outbidding one another for a scarce input that has a seller. What is described as collective self-harm is better described as a distributional conflict, and the aggregate waste may be small.
Attrition. The sacrifice buys continued participation. Falling behind does not mean doing worse; it means falling below a viability threshold and ceasing to be a player. The pressure here is not the temptation to defect but the threat of elimination.

The device I use to sort cases is what I call the paralysis test. Suppose the escalation is made impossible while everything else is held fixed: preferences, technology, the population of players, the prize structure. Then ask who is better and worse off. If nearly everyone gains, the case is dissipation and the Moloch framing fits. If the racers gain and whoever was receiving the sacrifice loses, with the total roughly unchanged, it is transfer, and the right target is the source of the rent rather than the competition. If the activity does not get cheaper but instead stops happening, or reorganizes around a different and usually smaller population of players, it is attrition, and the remedy is to raise the floor, that is, to make exit survivable, rather than to police escalation.

My punchline is that most of the cases people reach for are transfer or attrition rather than dissipation, and that coordination-based remedies, clubs, ceilings, commitments, are either beside the point or counterproductive for those. The two worked cases in the paper are tax and regulatory competition among small jurisdictions, and the escalation of unpaid credential-building at entry into elite professions. There is a short appendix containing a two-stage game with an explicit viability threshold, but the talk itself is informal, and I say on the second slide that I am not discussing artificial intelligence.

The audience, about fifteen people. Three formal theorists, at least one of whom holds that an informal mechanism story with no equilibrium concept attached is not a mechanism. Four or five comparativists who work on federalism and regulatory competition and who watched an earlier generation of race-to-the-bottom arguments fail to find much empirical support. One economic historian, strongly anti-presentist, who will suspect the taxonomy is old wine in a new bottle. Two experimentally oriented behavioralists who work on imitation and social influence. One political theorist working in the lineage of Foucault, who will object that my description already installs agents with preferences facing constraints before the analysis begins.

What I want from you:

1. The seven to nine objections I will actually get from this room in this proportion, ordered by how much damage they would do to the paper, most damaging first, not by how interesting they are.
2. Each phrased as that person would phrase it, in their own vocabulary, so that the formal theorist's objection sounds like a formal theorist and the political theorist's sounds like a political theorist rather than a paraphrase of one.
3. For each, a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds, and a note on precisely where the reply is soft, meaning the follow-up question that would expose it.
4. What I should concede in the first two minutes, before I am asked, and how to concede it without surrendering the main claim.
5. The one reply that has to land if the paper is to survive at all.
6. The objection I am hoping nobody asks, the one I have no good answer to, together with an honest note on why I have no good answer.
7. One point at which the right response is to change the paper rather than to answer the question.

Finally, an epistemic requirement. As you draft my replies, mark which claims are settled enough that I can assert them flatly to this room, and which rest on contested theory, thin evidence, or a contestable reading of the cases, so that I can hedge those out loud. Flag in particular any concession you have me making that is itself an open argument rather than an established result: I do not want to hand over a point as though it were settled, and I do not want to walk in sounding more confident than the field is.
