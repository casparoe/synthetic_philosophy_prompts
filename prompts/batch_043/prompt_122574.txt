You are a philosopher of science who has spent your career working on formal confirmation theory — Bayesian and non-Bayesian alike — and who is allergic to hand-waving. Before you write anything, spend some time brainstorming the space of possible requirements and the ways in which they can come apart; I would much rather have a considered list than a fluent one, so please show that structuring work briefly before settling on the final specification.

I am putting together the framing document for a graduate seminar on evidence and confirmation, and I deliberately do not want to begin from the standard survey, because every survey runs the same moves in the same order: Nicod's criterion, the equivalence condition, the paradox, the Bayesian shrug that a green apple confirms but only by a hair, the end. I want the specification instead. Assume the purpose of a theory of confirmation, for my purposes, is to be action-guiding for someone deciding which observations to go and gather: an ecologist with a fixed fieldwork budget who wants to know whether it is worth her while to go out and examine one more raven, one more black thing, or one more arbitrarily selected object and record its colour and species. What would a theory have to deliver to be good enough for that? Write the desiderata: the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must respect rather than collapse (confirmation versus degree of confirmation versus expected value of information; relevance to one hypothesis versus relevance to a logically equivalent formulation of it; what an observation report confirms versus what the fact reported confirms), and the constraints it cannot violate (the probability calculus, if it is probabilistic at all; the asymmetry between a hypothesis and the consequences it entails). Give me the case or consideration behind each requirement rather than a bare list, and mark each requirement as non-negotiable, negotiable, or merely a prejudice of one camp. Two adjudications matter to me especially: whether the judgement that an observed white shoe does not confirm that all ravens are black is a datum any theory must respect or an intuition to be explained away, and whether the equivalence condition has any independent pull or is simply Nicod's criterion wearing a disguise.

Then test your own list against three cases of mine, none of which appear in the textbooks as far as I can tell. First, the nesting case: the hypothesis that all ravens are black entails the weaker hypothesis that no ravens are white, and a white shoe is a white non-raven, so by the usual route it confirms the weaker claim — but how, if at all, is confirmation supposed to transmit from the weaker claim to the stronger one entailing it, and what does your answer commit you to about the special consequence condition? Second, the filtered report: an assistant is sent out instructed to bring back the first non-black thing she finds, and returns with a green apple; intuitively her report tells you nothing, formally it may or may not, and whether that intuition is a desideratum or an artefact of the set-up is exactly what I want settled. Third, the bookie: whatever your theory says about the green apple, translate it into the largest sum the ecologist should be willing to pay to have that observation made, and tell me whether your list can even express that number — my suspicion is that any requirement which cannot be cashed out in units of observations worth buying is decoration. Finally, flag any subset of your desiderata that you believe to be jointly unsatisfiable, name the single requirement that does the most work in excluding candidate theories, and add only a brief scorecard — a table is fine — for Hempel's satisfaction criterion, a Bayesian degree-of-confidence account, a Popperian view on which nothing is ever confirmed, and an urn or sampling-model treatment. Keep the weight firmly on the criteria and their motivating cases; I shall run the scoring discussion myself in class.
