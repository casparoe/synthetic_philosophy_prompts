My opening move. Take the usual centipede game: at each node the mover may stop play and take the larger of two piles, leaving the smaller to the opponent, or pass, in which case both piles grow and the opponent faces the same choice, so that the further play goes the more there is for both, while at every node the taker does better than the one taken from. I grant you everything you could want: both players are expected-utility maximizers, this is common knowledge at the outset, and there are no ties. My objection is that your argument cannot even be carried out. Each step of the induction — at node n, take, because your opponent would take at node n+1 — needs the counterfactual that rationality and its common knowledge still hold at node n. But by your own conclusion a rational opponent would have taken at node n-1; so if I actually find myself at node n, I have decisive evidence that common knowledge of rationality has already broken down, and hence no reason to trust the prediction about node n+1 on which the step depends. The reasoning looks self-undermining: the only nodes at which its intermediate steps describe a real choice are nodes whose occurrence refutes its premise. So tell me what the argument is supposed to establish — guidance for a player who somehow finds himself at node 5, a proof that node 5 will never be reached, or neither — and if it is the second, explain why a chain of reasoning whose steps concern situations its own conclusion rules out should settle what I do at node 1.
