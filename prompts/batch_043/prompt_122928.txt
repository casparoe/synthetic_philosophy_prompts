I am preparing a short set of teaching notes on supertasks and want to get the dialectic right before I commit to a line of argument in them. My understanding of Thomson's lamp is as follows: the switch is toggled at 1/2, 3/4, 7/8, ... of a minute before noon, so that infinitely many switchings are compressed into a finite interval; because the durations sum to one minute, the entire supertask is finished by noon; but because the sequence of states — on, off, on, off, ... — has no limit, nothing in the description fixes the lamp's state at noon, and it was concluded that a supertask cannot be completed. The standard reply, as I understand it, is that the story is merely incomplete rather than contradictory: the set-up is silent about the state at noon, and any consistent way of filling in the physics is admissible.

I would like to go further than that, and I want to know whether the further step is sound. It seems to me that the indeterminacy is an artifact of giving the lamp a two-valued state space. Suppose instead that the device is a dimmer-controlled lamp whose brightness may be any real number in the unit interval, and that the nth task is to set the brightness to 1 − 2^(−n). Now the states themselves form a convergent sequence, the state at noon is determined — full brightness — and the analogue of Thomson's difficulty does not so much as arise. And since the state of a real lamp is a continuous quantity governed by continuous dynamics, this is, I take it, not a mere toy: it shows that the celebrated indeterminacy is an artifact of an idealized on/off switch rather than a feature of supertasks as such, so that the extensive literature about there being no determinate final state rests on an idealization nature does not permit. On that view the only genuinely open question about supertasks is whether time and matter are infinitely divisible, which is an empirical question — and one we could in principle settle by running a fast controller through a large finite number of switchings, say forty, and checking whether the resulting end-state matches the continuum prediction.

Is this argument defensible, and if not, where exactly does it fail? In particular, does the convergent version genuinely escape the difficulty or does it merely relocate it; am I entitled to infer anything about real physical systems from it; and could the finite-approximation test I describe discriminate between the competing views at all, or does it test something else entirely?
