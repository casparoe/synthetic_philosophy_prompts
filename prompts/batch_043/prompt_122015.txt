I'm in a graduate seminar on formal epistemology and need to hand in a proposal for the term paper (4000 words, one semester, no empirical component) before I start writing. My topic is the presumption of independence in heuristic arguments, and my half-formed idea is that the literature treats it as a single principle when it should be split along two axes. First, a justification axis: an independence default can come from a symmetry or maximum-entropy thought (independence is what you get when you impose no structure beyond the marginals) or from a causal or no-common-cause thought (nothing connects these variables), and I suspect these license quite different inferences even though they are used interchangeably. Second, an algebra axis: independence of A from B is provably incompatible with independence of A from A-and-B, and independence at a coarse partition typically becomes dependence when the partition is refined, so any default needs to say which Boolean combinations it ranges over. If that split is right, then the interesting cases are not the heuristic arguments that fail because an independence assumption was false, but the ones that get the right answer anyway — where errors cancel, or where an arithmetic correction factor quietly repairs an assumption that was never justified — and the unit of assessment should be whether a given independence assumption is self-correcting under aggregation or whether small dependencies compound.
