The question, worth 25 marks, was as follows.

**Truth — practice paper. Answer all three parts.**

Deflationary, or minimalist, theories of truth hold that the truth predicate does not stand for a substantive property, and that the whole theory of truth consists in the instances of the equivalence schema: the proposition that p is true if and only if p.

(a) If the word "true" does not describe anything, what is it for? State the expressive (or "logical") function that deflationists standardly appeal to, and illustrate it with a case in which the truth predicate does something that could not have been achieved by simply asserting, one at a time, each of the sentences one is prepared to endorse. *(8 marks)*

(b) The "generality problem". The instances of the schema are infinite in number, so no one can accept or state them one by one; and yet the deflationist wants to say something general — for instance, that those instances are all there is to truth — which looks like the very sort of substantive claim she denies. Explain how a minimalist (Horwich is the usual example) responds to this, and why critics take the response to be inadequate. *(9 marks)*

(c) A further case. An archive holds a very large number of statements in a language that no one can now read. The archivist has no translations and cannot produce a single one of the relevant instances, but she says: at least half of the statements in the archive are true. Does the deflationist have the resources to say what the archivist is doing and what she is committing herself to? And is this a difficulty of the same kind as the one in part (b), or a different one? *(8 marks)*

The answer I have to mark reads as follows.

---

Deflationism is best seen as a refinement of the redundancy theory of Ramsey and Ayer: to call a proposition true is not to ascribe to it some further property, over and above whatever the proposition itself ascribes. The theory is therefore given entirely by the equivalence schema — the proposition that p is true if and only if p. Once one has all the instances, the proposition that snow is white is true iff snow is white and so on, one has said everything about truth that can be said, and there is no hidden nature of truth left over for philosophers to uncover.

(a) None of this makes the word "true" useless. Deflationists distinguish what a word describes from what it does. Even if "true" describes nothing, it has an expressive or logical function: it permits a kind of ascent, to a standpoint from which we talk about our own sentences rather than about the world. My example: in saying "it is true that snow is white" I commit myself to the claim that snow is white without asserting it in my own voice, and that is something I could not do without the truth predicate. This is what Quine had in mind by semantic ascent. It is also why the word cannot be eliminated: without it we would have to assert everything flat out, and we should lose the capacity to stand back from a claim and endorse it from outside.

(b) The generality problem is that the deflationist cannot say what all and only the true propositions have in common. The instances are infinite in number, so she cannot list them; and since she denies that truth is a property, she has no other way of picking out the propositions her theory is about either. So her "theory" is really an infinite list that nobody can state. Horwich's reply is that a theory need not be a finite list: the instances are generated by one finite schema, together with the claim that they exhaust the facts about truth, and that is a perfectly general statement of the position. Critics reply that this exhaustion claim is itself a general claim about the nature of truth, of just the kind the deflationist wanted to avoid, so that the view collapses back into the sort of theory it was meant to replace.

(c) The archivist's case is not a new difficulty; it is the same difficulty as in (b), and it is answered by the same move. What the archivist is doing is making a blind ascription — she endorses statements she cannot produce — and what she wants to say is a vast disjunction of conjunctions of instances, which the truth predicate lets her say in one sentence instead of a million. So the case actually shows that the generality problem is no real difficulty at all: the deflationist can use "true" itself to generalize over the instances, and therefore needs no general account of what truth is. And since the archivist can make her claim without any translation, the deflationist needs none either; meaning and translation are simply irrelevant to truth, which is exactly what a deflationist should say.
