The background, such as it is: the standard debate asks whether taking someone's word for a moral claim ("factory farming is wrong", "you shouldn't have sent that email") can give you the thing that's worth having here — knowledge, or moral understanding, or whatever it turns out to be — with the pessimistic side saying there's something off about pure moral deference even when the source is reliable and better informed than you. I got into it via an argument with a friend who is vegetarian and who admited he basically took the conclusion from a philosopher friend and never worked through the arguments himself. I found myself thinking he'd done something wrong, but I couldn't say what, and my own attempts kept sliding into "he doesn't really understand", which didn't feel like the right diagnosis either — it's not that he's confused about the reasons, it's more that he wouldn't notice or respond to the thing himself. So my half-formed idea is that both sides have picked the wrong object. They argue about understanding-why, i.e. grasping the reasons, but what moral deference seems to actually threaten is something closer to a perceptual or skill-like capacity: noticing the relevant features for yourself, being moved by them, being able to go on without being told. Call it responsiveness, or first-hand moral acknowledgement — I don't care about the label — and treat it as closer to know-how than to knowledge-that. On that reformulation the usual asymmetry (deferring to a physicist is fine, deferring to a moral expert is somehow not) should get a different explanation than the ones in circulation, and the "paradox" feeling — moral testimony seems both perfectly fine and somehow problematic — should change shape instead of having to be either dissolved or just accepted.
