After four decades in experimental physics I have started reading philosophy seriously in retirement, and the free will dispute looks unlike anything in my old field: centuries of argument, no new empirical input that I can identify, no contested calculation, and apparently no convergence. In physics a standstill like that usually means the question was ill-posed, or that the two parties were measuring different quantities under one name. I want to know whether that is what is going on here, and I would rather have a diagnosis of the disagreement than a verdict on it.

What I think I see is this. The compatibilist says the agent could have done otherwise because, had his reasons or his character been slightly different, he would have acted differently; the incompatibilist replies that, given the actual past and the laws, no other action was ever open. I cannot hear these as contradicting each other. Whether a system has alternatives depends on the grain at which its states are described: at fine grain the trajectory is unique, at coarse grain many trajectories are compatible with the same macro-description. So my hypothesis is that could have done otherwise is a predicate with a suppressed granularity argument, and that each side is quietly reading in the grain that makes its own conclusion fall out. Call it the resolution hypothesis.

Two things make me doubt this tidy story. First, the disagreement seems to track the valence of the cases: incompatibilists lean hardest on blame, while compatibilists often seem content to say that a praised agent need not have been able to do otherwise — as though the two sides were theorising different halves of the responsibility relation. Second, even when I stipulate a grain, the parties still part company: the incompatibilist insists that at any grain the agent's having the reasons he has is itself something that happened to him, which sounds less like a claim about alternatives than about what would count as the agent's own contribution.

So: is the standstill verbal, a hidden parameter of granularity, or is it a substantive clash about what responsibility requires — alternative possibilities, sourcehood, or something else — that survives once granularity is fixed? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress? My own candidate is an experiment: present ordinary subjects with a single scenario narrated at two different temporal resolutions and see whether their verdicts about whether the agent could have done otherwise track the resolution rather than anything about the case. Would that settle anything, or only relocate the dispute?
