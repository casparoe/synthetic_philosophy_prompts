I hold a qualified Malthusianism about long-run economic growth, and I would rather have it stress-tested than confirmed. The position I want examined is this: the core mechanism in Malthus's Essay was structurally correct, and the failure of its central prediction is a failure of timing, not of theory. On my version, what actually happened between 1800 and the present is a one-off escape from a constraint that continues to bind whenever the relevant parameters are restored, so the theory should be judged as a claim about a mechanism that dominates under specifiable conditions, not as a dated forecast.

Please take that position as the object and give me a map of its exposure. To make the exercise precise, I find it useful to separate four claims that usually travel together:

1. Diminishing returns: with land and technique held roughly fixed, doubling labour applied to land less than doubles output.
2. The positive check: when population presses on subsistence, mortality rises and restores the balance.
3. The preventive check: nuptiality and fertility respond positively to real wages, so prosperity tends to generate population growth.
4. The equilibrium claim: because of 1 through 3, pre-modern per-capita income is mean-reverting and pinned near subsistence, and sustained growth in living standards is impossible without an escape.

I would like defeaters directed at each, and I want to know which of the four is doing the real work: I suspect I can lose 2 without losing much, and that 3 is where the theory is most exposed, but I want that argued rather than assumed.

Six things I specifically want you to do.

First, run what I call the antecedent audit. On my formulation the Malthusian prediction is conditional: it applies where cultivable land is effectively fixed and technical change is slow. So a great deal turns on whether the antecedent held during the escape. Tell me what would establish that the antecedent held throughout the nineteenth and twentieth centuries and that the consequent still failed — that, I take it, would be fatal — and contrast it with the much weaker finding that the antecedent simply lapsed, which is no test at all. Which historical findings would discriminate? I have in mind candidates like Boserup's reversal of the causal arrow in The Conditions of Agricultural Growth (1965), the long-run behaviour of real commodity prices, and the role of fossil fuels and industrial nitrogen fixation in relaxing the land constraint. For each, say which side of the antecedent question it bears on.

Second, the sign question. The preventive check requires population growth to respond positively to real wages, yet the demographic transition in the now-rich countries appears to reverse that sign, plausibly through a quantity-quality substitution in fertility. Is that a refutation of the mechanism, a change in a parameter the theory always allowed to vary, or an auxiliary hypothesis bolted on afterward? Which classification would a defender of the theory themselves accept, and which would they reject as question-begging?

Third, evidence arguably already in. Assess the 1980 Simon-Ehrlich wager, in which Ehrlich selected five metals and lost as all five fell in inflation-adjusted price over the decade: is that a genuine test of anything I am committed to, or a period effect dressed up as a refutation? Do the same for Gregory Clark's contention in A Farewell to Alms (2007) that a Malthusian trap held before roughly 1790. Rank these and anything comparable as fatal, serious, or merely awkward, and be explicit about which items you think a serious Malthusian should concede as counting against them.

Fourth, pre-specification. Give me an observation, specified now, that could arrive within the next seventy-five years and that would count against the position, together with what the position predicts that its rivals do not. I am especially interested in the case I call the second stationary state: a world of sub-replacement fertility, shrinking population, and material abundance with no positive check operating anywhere. Would a defender be entitled to claim that as vindication? I suspect not, and I want the reason stated.

Fifth, apply the symmetry test. If I grant Malthus the right to say he was right but early, what stops the cornucopian side from saying the same whenever resource prices rise? State the general principle you would use to decide when the right-but-early defence is legitimate and when it is a degenerating protective belt, and then apply it to me honestly.

Finally, give me your assessment of whether my formulation has been insulated by its own qualifications, and whether that is a defect here. A conditional claim with an open-ended time index is not a laboratory prediction, and I am unsure what standard of falsifiability it is fair to demand of it. Close by telling me which single defeater you would press if you had one question, and why. For what it is worth, I am least moved by commodity-price evidence and most moved by anything bearing on the wage-fertility relationship.
