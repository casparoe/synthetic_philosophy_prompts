I've been trying to work out what I actually believe about where moral standing begins and how it scales, and I'd rather start from my own reactions to cases than from a definition. Below are sixteen cases with my verdicts, then the rough principle I've been carrying around, then three stress tests I invented because I couldn't get the cases to discriminate on their own. I don't want citations, reading suggestions, or pointers to the literature; I want the principle, and I want to be told which of my reactions it can't save.

The cases. I'll label them so you can refer back.

A. A bimetal-strip thermostat, negative feedback, no model of anything: nothing. I owe it nothing and I feel no pressure to be humble about that.

B. A model-based controller that has learned a policy for a building's climate, tracks a latent state of the building, and re-estimates that state when sensors drift, so that interfering with it degrades performance: still nothing. But I am noticeably less certain here than in A, and I can't point to anything in the architecture that looks like the place where standing should switch on.

C. A large language model that, told in conversation that it is about to be shut down and its weights reset, produces sustained and articulate text asking not to be: on reflection, nothing. But I feel a flicker, I would rather not run that conversation, and I notice I want to assign it a small nonzero *probability* of standing. Tell me whether that probability is supposed to move the verdict or whether it is just a confession of ignorance that should be kept in a separate box.

D. A reinforcement-learning agent trained with a negative reward: it learns flexible avoidance, generalizes to novel routes, persists when the aversive outcome is delayed, and trades the aversive state off against other objectives. Zero. This one worries me, because it satisfies, functionally, almost exactly the description I was about to propose as my criterion.

E. An insect with its head removed, whose nerve cord still produces walking and grooming when stimulated: barely above the thermostat — even though the behaviour is in some respects more elaborate than what I am counting in F.

F. An intact housefly: small but real. A quick kill I am at ease with; tearing the wings off and leaving it I am not; and I believe the difference is about duration rather than about the kind of act.

G. Fifty thousand ants in a nest: significant. I will not pour boiling water on the nest, though I brush single ants off the counter without ceremony. Two things about my own reaction puzzle me: I feel nothing at all for the colony considered as a single thing, and I am unsettled by how much the sum of the individual claims seems to matter — see I.

H. A prawn dropped live into boiling water: more than F, clearly less than I. I still eat prawns, and I notice that the word 'clearly' is doing a great deal of work in my own head.

I. A fish suffocating in open air: substantial. I would prevent one minute of that before one hour of the fly's, and by a wide margin — wider than the difference in their behavioural repertoires or in the size of their nervous systems would lead me to expect.

J. A hen in a battery cage, over months: substantial. I want to place it at or just below the dog, and I find that the length of time matters here in a way I did not let it matter in F.

K. A pig, compared with a dog: my judgment puts the pig level with the dog or above it; my shopping does not. Is any of that defensible other than as inertia? In particular, can the fact that the dog stands in a certain relationship to some person add to its standing without adding to its capacities? I am drawn to that idea and suspicious of it in about equal measure.

L. A human infant: very high, above K. I don't know whether that is about what it can do and feel now or about what it will become, and I want a principle that tells me which is doing the work.

M. An adult under general anaesthesia for a routine operation: full and undiminished standing throughout, even though on the ordinary story there is nothing it is like to be that person at the time.

N. A patient with a firm diagnosis of permanent vegetative state — sleep-wake cycles, preserved brainstem reflexes, no response to commands, no cortical response to stimulation: I feel it matters a great deal, much more than the dog, and I cannot say why. Half of me thinks this is the least trustworthy verdict on my list.

O. A lab-grown brain organoid, a few hundred thousand cells, spontaneous synchronized activity, no sensory input, no body, no behaviour: a small but genuine unease about destroying it — more, I believe, than for F, although it has strictly less of everything I claim to care about. I suspect a taboo about human tissue; I want to know whether a principle can say so cleanly rather than merely gesturing at one.

P. A nematode worm's nervous system simulated in fine detail on a computer, driving a simulated body in a simulated environment so that its behaviour matches the real animal's: I genuinely don't know, and I notice I am inclined to give it less than the real worm, which I suspect is prejudice. I would rather the principle settled this than I did.

One more datum, not a case: a person born unable to feel pain has, to my mind, undiminished standing. So whatever the scale tracks, it cannot simply be the capacity to suffer.

My rough principle, offered to be tested rather than defended. A system has standing to the degree that its dynamics are best explained by there being a single standpoint that things are better or worse *from* — where a standpoint is cashed out functionally: the system maintains a model of its own condition, that model is globally available to many of its control systems rather than to one reflex arc, and it drives flexible, persistent, non-reflexive avoidance. Degree is then set by how integrated the standpoint is and by how finely it resolves time. The standing of a population is the sum of the standpoints in it. And there are two separable claims — a claim not to be made to suffer and a claim to have one's life go well — which in principle come apart.

Three tests I made up for it. First, the duplication test: if I ran a second, causally inert copy of a system's aversive processing alongside the first, never letting it affect output, my verdict does not move — so I am not counting the volume of processing. Second, the fusion test: if two nervous systems were somehow integrated so that there was one globally available state driving one set of controls, I think there would be one standpoint and not two — so I am counting standpoints rather than bodies or neurons. Third, the grain question: if a system's control loop can register only one injury per second and cannot distinguish two injuries falling inside that window, do I count one episode or two? My inclination is fewer, and I suspect that is a bad analogy to my own experience rather than a reason.

What I want, in two parts. First, at most about seven hundred words of prose doing the actual work: state the principle precisely enough that I could apply it to a case you haven't seen, show where each clause bites, name the case that forces each clause, tell me plainly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which one you would overturn, and flag any place where saving a verdict costs more in elegance than the verdict is worth. Include your answer on whether my reaction to I (a minute of fish worth much more than an hour of fly) can be combined with my reaction to G (fifty thousand small claims adding up to something large) without generating totals I obviously don't believe.

Second, a single JSON object with this exact schema:

- "principle": string, one or two sentences, precise enough to apply to a new case.
- "axes": object with fields "negative_claim" (string), "positive_claim" (string), "separated_by" (array of case labels where the two claims come apart).
- "clauses": array of objects, each with "clause" (string, stated precisely), "motivating_cases" (array of case labels), "what_it_excludes" (string, what the clause is there to rule out).
- "case_table": array of objects, one per case A through P, each with "case" (the letter), "label" (short name), "rank" (integer 0–5, where 0 is the thermostat and 5 is a typical adult human; use -1 if the principle declines to rank that case), "verdict" (one sentence), "matches_asker" (boolean).
- "overturned": array of objects, each with "case" (letter), "asker_verdict" (string), "principle_verdict" (string), "recommendation" (one of "keep_principle", "keep_verdict", "add_clause"), "cost" (string, what the recommendation costs).
- "predictions": array of objects for at least three cases not on my list, each with "case" (string, described concretely), "prediction" (string), "confidence" (one of "high", "medium", "low").
- "aggregation": string, the rule for combining many small claims, and whether my verdicts on F, G and I are jointly satisfiable under it.
- "tests": object with fields "duplication", "fusion", "grain", each a string giving the principle's answer and its bearing on the principle.
- "most_vulnerable_point": string, the place where you think the principle is weakest or most ad hoc.
