I'm putting together a graduate seminar on rational choice for the spring, and I want to use, as a foil in the second week, a survey chapter on alternatives to expected utility theory that a colleague sent me. Before I hand its discussion of Buchak's risk-weighted expected utility (REU) to students as something to pick apart, I need to know how much of it a defender of that theory would actually grant. I don't need citations or a reading list — I can supply those myself — and I don't need a survey of the literature. What I want is an audit of a specific paragraph, plus an audit of a diagnostic exercise of my own that I have started to distrust.

Let me first fix the formalism, so that we are talking about the same object; please correct me if I state it wrong. Outcomes of a gamble are ranked from worst to best, x₁ through xₙ, with utilities u(xᵢ). The risk function r maps [0,1] onto [0,1], is strictly increasing, and fixes 0 and 1. The value of a gamble is u(x₁) plus, for each step up the ranking, the utility increment at that step weighted by r applied to the probability of getting at least that-good an outcome: REU = u(x₁) + Σᵢ₌₂..ₙ r(P(outcome ⊒ xᵢ))·[u(xᵢ) − u(xᵢ₋₁)]. Expected utility is the special case where r is the identity. Risk aversion, on this picture, is not the concavity of u but the shape of r — a person who underweights the probability of rising above the worst outcome is risk-averse even if her utility for money is perfectly linear — and the two components are supposed to be separable: one is a fact about how much she cares about increments of goods, the other a fact about how much she cares about what happens in better states as opposed to worse ones.

Here is what the chapter says, in its entirety on the subject:

"Buchak's theory is best understood as a redescription of the familiar point that a risk-averse agent has a concave utility function for money. Instead of bending the curve over outcomes, she bends it over probabilities, replacing each probability p with a weight r(p), and the two manoeuvres do the same work: the theory's so-called risk attitude is just diminishing marginal utility in another notation. What is genuinely new is that the weights attach to the cumulative probability of an outcome's being at least as good as a given one, so that the weight an outcome receives depends on where it sits in the ranking of the gamble — and this is precisely where the theory breaks down. Once weights depend on rank, the theory must violate dominance: an agent may prefer one gamble to another that is at least as good in every state and better in some. That is not a risk attitude; it is a failure of instrumental rationality. Nor is the machinery new. Reweighting cumulative probabilities is exactly what prospect theory's weighting function does, so risk-weighted expected utility inherits prospect theory's liabilities, including its violations of stochastic dominance and its inability to be stated without a reference point and loss aversion. Finally, because the risk function can be tuned to fit whatever risk attitudes an agent happens to have, no pattern of choice could refute the theory, which makes it unfalsifiable rather than false. The upshot is that risk-weighted expected utility is not a genuine rival to expected utility theory: it is expected utility theory plus a parameter that does no work except to license the violation of dominance."

Take that paragraph apart clause by clause: which clauses would a proponent of the theory accept without argument, which are exaggerations of something true, which are outright errors, and — this is the part I care about most — does the chapter's final verdict follow from whatever survives the corrections? I have three specific hunches, which I would like either confirmed or dismantled. First, I believe the dominance charge is simply false as stated, and that the chapter has fused two things the theory keeps apart: what REU gives up is independence, plus something like sequential consistency in dynamic choice, while it retains stochastic dominance. Second, I am much less sure about the prospect theory comparison — there is an obvious family resemblance, since both are rank-dependent, but the chapter seems to me to be importing features of prospect theory that REU does not have, and possibly attributing to REU a difficulty that in prospect theory arises only for certain parameter values. Third, the closing charge of unfalsifiability strikes me as too strong in the word but not empty in substance: my understanding is that the representation is unique, with u fixed up to positive affine transformation and r fixed given that normalization, so that a single pair has to fit the agent's whole preference ordering at once — but I am unsure how much comfort that really gives, given that r is not observable independently of u.

That last worry is connected to the second thing I want checked, which is my own construction. In the seminar I had planned a short elicitation exercise meant to demonstrate to students that the shape of u and the shape of r are different components: fix an outcome pair, say nothing and two hundred dollars, elicit a certainty equivalent for each gamble that pays the larger amount with probability p and nothing otherwise, normalize so that u of nothing is 0 and u of two hundred is 1, and then look at the resulting curve — expected utility predicts that the utility of the certainty equivalent is p, whereas REU predicts that it is r(p), so with r(p) = p², for instance, the two theories diverge visibly by the time p reaches one half. I have come to think the exercise as stated is worse than useless: the students will be plotting certainty equivalents in dollars, whose curvature in p is the composition of r with the inverse of u, and I cannot see how to get u on the interval independently of the very data I am using to estimate r. Is that the right diagnosis, is there a repair that isolates the two components cleanly, or is the honest moral precisely the joint-uniqueness point, so that the exercise should be dropped and replaced with something that shows the in-principle identifiability of the pair?

If there is a defensible reading of the sentence "the risk function does no work" — a reading on which it is fair, and one a proponent might even concede — I would like to hear it, since I would rather teach the strongest version of the objection than the one my colleague happened to write down.
