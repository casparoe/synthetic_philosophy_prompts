Hi — bit of an odd ask. I'm on a small ad-hoc committee that has to spend a one-off pot of money (about six person-years of postdoc time plus two workshops, spread over three years) on conceptual work around the therapy/enhancement distinction, and I've been landed with drafting the call for proposals. The money comes with strings: the publicly funded health system behind it is in the middle of rewriting its coverage rule, and the working draft says, roughly, that the system pays for interventions that restore or maintain normal functioning and doesn't pay for anything that goes beyond that. So the agenda isn't really "think interesting thoughts about human enhancement" — it's "which conceptual questions would most change how that sentence gets written, if somebody actually answered them". One person on the committee, a health economist, says definately that the whole distinction is just a leaky proxy for cost-effectiveness and we should scrap it and fund QALY work instead. I half agree and half don't, so feel free to take that head on.

These are the questions that keep coming up in our meetings, more or less as I'd put them — I don't think the list is right and I suspect at least one item on it is a dud. (1) The shifting-baseline problem: on any account of "normal functioning" with statistical content, once an intervention is taken by nearly everyone the untreated become the statistical outliers, so the distinction looks like it inverts or collapses under wide uptake. Is that a genuine problem for a coverage rule, or a curiosity? (2) The flattening problem: a lot of plausible near-future interventions don't raise your peak capacity at all, they slow decline or compress variance — holding muscle mass into your eighties, cutting required sleep with no performance cost, smoothing out the attentional dips across a working day. Is keeping a trajectory steady therapy or enhancement, and does the answer depend on whether the comparison runs within one life over time or across a population at a time? (3) The positional problem: for traits whose value is partly competitive, one person's gain is another person's loss, and enhancement starts looking like an arms race rather than a benefit. Should a public rule track that, and is the externality doing all the real work here? (4) The means-sensitivity problem: the same functional outcome reached by a pill, by schooling, by designing buildings differently, or by embryo selection. Does the verdict change with the route, and should the rule be means-sensitive at all? (5) The many-users problem: clinicians, insurers, regulators, parents and disability-rights campaigners all invoke "therapy versus enhancement" for quite different purposes, and maybe there's no single distinction underneath, or maybe some of those uses are just confused.

What I want back is an agenda, not a survey. Pick the four or five questions you think should get the money, in order, and defend the ordering: for each, what it's upstream of (which other questions its answer would settle, reframe, or render pointless), how tractable it is in roughly three years with philosophical tools alone versus needing empirical input we can't fund, how much it would genuinely shift the wording of a coverage rule as opposed to just being interesting, and what kind of result over the next couple of years would move it up or down the list. I'd also like you to name the one or two questions on my list — or ones I haven't thought of — that you think are overrated: the ones that sound deep but dissolve under pressure, or that wouldn't change anyone's mind about the rule. And say why. No literature review, no reading list, no citations please; I've read the survey articles and I can find papers myself, I want your own reasoning. In British English, and under about 1200 words if you can manage it.
