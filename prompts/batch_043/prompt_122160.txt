Set up: let S be a slur and N its neutral counterpart, same extension — everyone who is S is N and vice versa. Im using letters because I dont want to type an actual slur into a prompt. The argument is meant to show S and N differ in *semantic* content, and then it just keeps rolling somewhere I really dont want to be.

(1) The offense survives embedding. Put S inside a negation, a yes/no question, the antecedent of a conditional, or an indirect report ("she said that...") and it stays offensive. Conversational implicatures dont do that — they're the paradigm cancellable thing, and this isn't: "X is not a S, and I'm not suggesting anything bad about anyone, I mean it literally" still lands as offensive.

(2) The standard diagnostic: content that projects out of embeddings and resists cancellation is conventionally attached to the expression, not worked out on the fly from what this speaker meant on this occasion. That's just the test for conventional vs. conversational content.

(3) Conventional content is a fact about the form–content pairing in a language: the lexicon hands you the content when you produce the form. So whether it's there has nothing to do with the speaker's intentions, their politics, whether they "mean it," or how anyone takes it.

(4) So the offensiveness is a property of the form S. It's triggered whenever the form gets tokened.

(5) But you can't mention a word without tokening it. Quote marks, "the word ___", a whiteboard in a philology class, a dictionary headword, my own exam question — all of them put a token of the form into the world.

(6) So mentioning S produces exactly the same offensive content as using it. "I was only mentioning it" can't be a defence: by (3) intentions are irrelevant and by (2) the content isn't cancellable.

(7) Same for reclamation. If a community starts using S without offense, they aren't using the same word benignly — on this picture they've got a new lexeme that happens to sound the same. Accidental homonymy, not a change of use.

(8) And that's where I get stuck, because: (a) obviously a dictionary entry isnt doing what the guy on the bus is doing; (b) if (7) is right, then when he says "it's just a word" and she says "it's a slur" they aren't disagreeing, they're just using two different words with the same spelling — but there really is a disagreement there, that's basically the whole point of the fights about these words; (c) my tutor muttered something like "offense is a perlocutionary effect" and I waved it away and now I can't remember why I was allowed to.
