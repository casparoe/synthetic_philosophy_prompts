You are a philosopher of measurement who has spent a career working alongside auditors, statisticians and programme evaluators — someone equally at home with the literature on Goodhart's law, surrogate measures and specification gaming, and with the unglamorous business of deciding whether a number can safely have money attached to it. Take your time and think carefully before you write. Begin with a short unpublished brainstorming pass in which you (a) list the distinct ways an indicator can stop tracking the thing it was chosen to track, (b) sort those ways by whether they operate through the measured party changing what it does, changing what it records, or changing which cases enter the count, and (c) mark which of them a small review team could in principle detect before the money is committed. Then write the deliverable. Please write it in British English; the rest of this brief is in American English, but the document itself will be handed to my team in the UK.

The role: I lead the performance and payment team at a regional public body that commissions employment-support and skills services for long-term unemployed adults from roughly forty providers. An increasing share of the fee is tied to outcomes — 25% this contract year, and the board has signalled 45% next year. Each metric is fixed about eighteen months before the contract year it governs, by a team of four analysts, none of whom has formal training in causal inference, econometrics or the philosophy of measurement. Each one has to be signed off by our finance director and then by a board subcommittee, and we have about two working days of analyst time per metric, including the write-up. We set or revise around a dozen metrics a year; each one locks in payment for a year covering some 20,000 participants and £40m of public spend. When a metric is badly specified we do not merely get a distorted number: we have paid out on outcomes that did not exist, and we have spent a seven-figure sum on disputed payments and remediation after providers worked to the measure rather than to the purpose.

What I need is a written procedure my team can actually run — an ordered set of tests, with the hard cases built in — first on a candidate metric before we commit money to it, and again whenever we are tempted to raise a target or tighten a definition mid-year. Please build it on the best available thinking about why optimizing a proxy degrades it, translated into questions an analyst can answer in a few hours from material we actually hold: prior-year distributions, contract management records, provider audit files, and two or three interviews.

Requirements on the procedure:

1. Ordered steps, each giving the question, what counts as a sufficient answer, which of our documents would answer it, and a stop rule — when to kill the metric outright, when to send it back for redesign, when to cap the payment attached to it, and when to escalate because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts.

2. It must distinguish, and handle differently: the proxy merely being weakly correlated with what we care about; the correlation degrading specifically in the top of the range, which is exactly where payment bites; the proxy being a symptom rather than a lever, so that moving it does not move the outcome; providers reshaping their behaviour to the number in ways that make the underlying situation worse; degradation of the record rather than of the service; and reshaping of the counted population by selection, exclusion or reclassification, so that the average improves while nobody is better off.

3. It must say what to do when no number is to be had: no baseline distribution, no untreated or prior-year comparison group, or a construct such as meaningful and sustained work whose definition we do not agree on internally.

4. It must be honest about which steps are judgements dressed up as procedure, and name who should make each of them.

5. It must settle a disagreement inside my team. Several of our analysts argue that some of our metrics are different in kind, because hitting the number is partly constitutive of the good rather than merely evidence of it — an emergency-department waiting-time target, or being recorded as in work on the last day of the quarter. On that basis they say the usual warning about measures becoming targets simply does not apply. I do not think that is right, but I also do not think the standard proxy-versus-target story covers these cases cleanly. Give me your view on whether the constitutive/evidential distinction is real, where in the procedure it belongs, and what it should change about how we treat such a metric.

6. It must say what to do about the anti-gaming checks themselves: once we monitor, say, the share of participants who cannot be contacted for follow-up, or the rate of re-registration, that monitor becomes a number someone is judged on too.

7. Include a short section on which version of the several claims that travel under this name you are operationalizing, and what your procedure therefore does not protect us against. I would rather know the blind spots than be told the procedure is general.

Then apply the procedure to three candidate metrics, a few hundred words each, showing the step at which each one stops, survives, or gets sent back: (a) payment on the share of participants recorded as in paid work for at least thirteen of the twenty-six weeks after leaving the programme; (b) payment on ninety per cent of initial assessments being completed within five working days of referral; (c) payment on improvement in a participant-reported wellbeing and confidence score collected by providers themselves at exit. I want at least one of these to come out in a way we will not like — tell me plainly if that means killing or de-weighting a measure we have already built the payment model and the public business case around, and give me the one-page argument I would need to put to the finance director.

Finally, an epistemic section. Mark each step with the confidence you would attach to it, and tell me what would change your mind: what argument, or what finding in our own data, would lead you to a different verdict on a metric, and where this procedure is most likely to misfire — the kinds of metric it will be too harsh on, and the kinds it will pass while leaving us exposed. Also note where your translation of the theory into steps leans on a contested reading of what the underlying claim actually says, or on an assumption about how providers respond that we should test rather than trust.

Do not give me a bibliography, further reading or citations; if a step depends on something you take to be established, say what it is in a sentence and move on. Around 1,800 words plus a table or two, no padding. I would rather have a document that survives contact with my finance director than a tidy one.
