I record a long-form interview series for the science-and-ideas section of a national newspaper: forty-five minutes with a single guest, edited, audio only, aimed at listeners who are curious but have no background in the subject and who cannot see anything. My next guest, whom I will call Dr. V., is a perceptual psychologist who also designs typefaces. Her new book defends a theory of what makes every 'a' an 'a', and I want questions that press the theory rather than invite her to restate it. As I understand her position, she holds the following:

(1) What makes every 'a' an 'a' is not a shared shape but a shared contrastive role: a letter is a category defined by its place in the system of distinctions a script uses to keep words apart, much as a phoneme is defined by its place in a phonological system rather than by a particular sound.

(2) Shape belongs to a separate layer — style, or what she calls the spirit of the letterform — and any variation that does no contrastive work is merely stylistic.

(3) Identity therefore goes by the system, not by the mark: Latin A and Greek capital alpha are different letters though they are drawn alike, while the capital A and the lowercase a in the word Madagascar are the same letter twice though they are drawn nothing alike. Shape is neither necessary nor sufficient.

(4) What anchors a given mark to one system rather than another is the practice of the people who produced and read it: a mark counts as a token of Latin a rather than of its identically drawn Cyrillic counterpart in virtue of which writing practice it belongs to.

(5) Letters are normative kinds: a child's wobbly, malformed a is still an a, indeed a bad one, whereas a water stain shaped exactly like an a is not a letter at all, because nothing can be correct or incorrect about it.

Please draft ten to twelve questions, ordered so that they get harder, and for each one give: the question as I should actually say it aloud; one line on what it is testing; the answer you expect from someone holding (1)–(5); and a follow-up that takes that answer seriously and makes the next move expensive. Constraints: no question whose best answer is a restatement of (1)–(5); no biographical or how-did-you-get-into-this openers, since the first question should already be substantive; nothing that depends on showing the audience an image, on naming a particular typeface or designer, or on a listener having read anything; and no references, titles, or names of other writers anywhere in the set.

I want the set to cover at least these five pressures:

- The circle in (1): contrastive roles are defined over the distinctions a script uses to mark meaning-differences, but those distinctions are marked by letters. Ask what breaks the circle. If the expected answer is the inventory of recurring glyph shapes, press the point that shape is then doing the foundational work after all.
- The anchoring problem: (4) and (5) seem to pull apart on a forger who types the Cyrillic character into a Latin word, or on a machine-generated glyph interpolated by a font model with no writer behind it. Get her to commit to which one governs, and then ask about a script no one can read, or one used by exactly one person, where she may have to accept that there is no fact of the matter.
- Whether she has any account of letterform identity at all, as opposed to letter identity: what makes a hand-painted sign-writer's a and the a of a high-contrast nineteenth-century display face the same letterform when they differ in every measurable respect; and whether a continuous interpolation from an a to a u has a determinate crossing point, or whether the spirit of a letter turns out to be a statistical fact about a population of readers.
- Holism about design: the a of a typeface is shaped to sit with the b, the g and the s, so ask whether the identity of the letterform depends on the whole alphabet, and what that implies for a capitals-only display face or a face with a reduced inventory.
- A closing question that lets her say where the theory is unfinished or cannot yet answer, rather than where she is confident.

Finally, if any of the five pressures above misfires — if the theory already dissolves it trivially, or if I have misdescribed what follows from her own commitments — say so in a line and give me the harder version of that question instead.
