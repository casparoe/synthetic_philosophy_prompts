**Question (25 minutes).** Explain the Frege–Geach problem for metaethical expressivism. In your answer: (a) say why the problem arises specifically from moral sentences occurring in unasserted positions, such as the antecedent of a conditional; (b) distinguish the problem from the neighbouring objection that expressivism cannot make sense of moral disagreement; (c) set out one expressivist solution and explain what that solution has to deliver — in particular, what it must say about the occurrence of a moral sentence inside a negation, and about the validity of an argument like: if stealing is wrong, then getting one's little brother to steal is wrong; stealing is wrong; so getting one's little brother to steal is wrong. The rubric says a first-class answer states the problem precisely, keeps it separate from neighbouring objections, and offers a response that would work for arbitrarily deeply embedded occurrences rather than for a single sample argument.

**Student's answer.** Expressivism holds that moral sentences do not describe moral facts and do not express beliefs; they express non-cognitive attitudes such as approval and disapproval, and the meaning of a moral sentence is given by the attitude it expresses rather than by truth conditions. The Frege–Geach problem starts from the observation that a moral sentence can occur where it is not asserted — in the antecedent of a conditional, inside a negation, or as a disjunct — and in those positions the speaker expresses no attitude toward the subject matter: I can say that if stealing is wrong then getting my little brother to steal is wrong without expressing any disapproval of stealing. But for the modus ponens argument to be valid, the sentence that stealing is wrong must mean the same in both premises. If its meaning is just whatever attitude it expresses, and it expresses no attitude in the antecedent, there is nothing left to secure the validity, and the argument looks like an equivocation.

This is the same difficulty in another form as the familiar worry about disagreement. If a moral sentence only reports the speaker's own attitude, then when you deny what I assert we are not really disagreeing: we are each accurately reporting different attitudes, and there is nothing for our dispute to be about. Both problems show that expressivism cannot give moral sentences a stable content independent of the speaker's psychological state, which is why the view is often said to collapse into a kind of subjectivism.

The best-known solution is Gibbard's. On this view the state of mind expressed by a moral sentence is the acceptance of a norm, and complex sentences express higher-order attitudes: the conditional expresses one attitude directed at a combination of attitudes, namely disapproval of the pair consisting of favouring stealing while not disapproving of getting one's brother to steal. Since this is a single attitude with a structured object, the same attitude can be assigned to the antecedent occurrence, and validity is explained: anyone who held the attitude expressed by the major premise and the attitude expressed by the minor premise would be unable, as a psychological matter, to withhold the attitude expressed by the conclusion. Validity, for this sort of view, just is the psychological impossibility of combining those attitudes, so expressivism is committed to a psychologistic account of logic. The same machinery also handles negation: saying that stealing is not wrong expresses disapproval of disapproval of stealing.
