I'm in the second year of a PhD programme, and for about a year now I've been keeping a private list of the verdicts that a philosopher I work with — I'll call her H. — has given, in conversation and in the margins of other people's papers, on the question whether an agent in some described situation has a reason to act. Next week she has agreed to rule on one further case, and I would like to arrive able to predict what she will say; more than that, I would like to arrive holding one additional case capable of separating the rules she might be running, since the verdicts I already have seem to me to fit more than one.

Her verdicts so far are these. She says that a woman who falsely believes her train leaves at six has no reason to be at the station at five, though she does have a reason to be there before nine. She says that a man who has never vividly imagined what an untreated condition will do to him, and who currently wants nothing to do with doctors, does have a reason to have it looked at. She says that a man who is about to drink what he takes to be gin but which is in fact petrol has no reason to drink it. She says that a woman in a deep depression, whose only surviving concern is not to be a burden to her sister, does have a reason to accept treatment. She says that a man whose sole project is arranging pebbles on a beach, who is fully informed, free of any pathology, and whose project would survive any amount of reflection, has no reason to take up a career or to cultivate a friendship. She says that an agent who could be moved to φ by two routes, only one of which is sound, and who would in fact take the unsound one, still has a reason to φ. She says that an agent whose motive would dissolve the moment she made the reasoning explicit to herself — the kind of motive that survives only when it is not looked at directly — nevertheless has a reason. She says that a householder with no evidence whatsoever that his wiring is dangerous does have a reason to have it checked. And when I once pressed her on whether the depressed woman has a reason that she cannot act on, she said I was running two questions together and declined to answer.

The case she has agreed to rule on is this. Arno has no concern for anyone else and no disposition whatever to be moved by the suffering of others, but he is not defective in any of the ways that usually do the work in these examples: he is factually informed, he makes no error of inference, his imagination is unimpaired, his projects are mutually coherent, and he is not suicidal. What he does have is a purely formal second-order concern — he wants his motives and intentions to be ones that survive reflection, and to be able to avow them to himself without contradiction. He is in a position to inflict severe and permanent suffering on a stranger he will never meet again, at no cost to himself and with no risk of discovery. Does he have a reason not to?

What I want from you is this. Tell me which two or three rules fit the entire track record above — and state each rule in a form I could apply to a fresh case, rather than naming a school or an author — then say what each predicts about Arno, which one you would bet on and why, and how much of your confidence rests on the verdicts themselves rather than on which rule you independently find most plausible. Along the way, say whether you think H.'s refusal to separate having a reason from being able to be moved by it is itself evidence about her view, or merely caution. Finally, tell me which single further case I should put to her in order to divide the surviving rules. My own candidate is the following, which you should feel free to discard if it doesn't do the work: Elin returns a lost wallet because of an ingrained habit of scrupulosity that she would abandon if she ever asked herself whether scrupulosity is worth having, though she never asks, and there is nothing factually mistaken or incoherent in her motives — so that there is a route to her action from her actual motives, but not, apparently, from the motives of a fully rational version of her.

One request about form. Please answer in continuous prose — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — written the way you would write an email to a colleague rather than the way you would write a handout; somewhere around eight hundred words.
