I install and repair lifts for a living, so this is not my field at all, but me and my brother got into it again at his kid's birthday barbeque on Sunday and I've been chewing on it since. He's doing one of those part-time degrees and kept saying the thing about separating the art from the artist like that closes the discussion, and when I started actually testing myself on examples I found I don't have a consistent line at all. So I wrote down my gut reactions and I want to know whether there is one principle behind them, or whether some of them just have to go.

Here's my list. (1) A detective novel from the 1930s by a writer who, decades later, we found out was apparently vicious to his wife and kids. Nothing in the book reflects that. I think it's a great book, I recommend it to people, I feel no guilt. (2) A sci-fi film I adored as a teenager; the director was later convicted of assaulting a young actor on the set. I can't watch it now. Not I refuse to, I mean the actual enjoyment is gone, it feels like the film itself has gone bad on me. But that's structurally the same as (1) — bad thing done by the maker, nothing about it in the work — so either I'm being inconsistent or there's a difference I'm not seeing, and I want to know which. (3) A song with a gorgeous melody whose lyrics celebrate a real massacre from the point of view of the men carrying it out. Can't listen, and — this is the part I'm surest about — I think it's a worse song, not just a song I avoid. (4) A novel written in the first person by a narrator who is a raging bigot, where the book is obviously holding him up for ridicule. I think it's superb and the bigotry is a large part of why. (5) A stand-up special that is genuinely the funniest thing I've heard in years, except a lot of the laughs land on a group of people who get hurt by that sort of thing in real life. I feel uneasy and I honestly can't tell whether my unease is this is worse comedy or this is fine comedy that it's wrong of me to enjoy. (6) A horror film where the entire point is that you're meant to enjoy watching people get killed. Completely fine, zero guilt. Which doesn't sit well with (5) — both are me taking pleasure in something cruel, and I judge them differently. (7) The one I keep going back and forth on: a memoir by a man who did something genuinely monstrous and is plainly not sorry, where the writing is extraordinary and part of why it's extraordinary is that you can feel he really isn't sorry — the beauty seems to depend on the actual badness rather than just sit next to it. (I have the same wobble about a cathedral built with forced labour, where what's beautiful about it is partly the sheer scale that only forced labour could have paid for.) With (7) I want to say great book, terrible man, don't give him money, but I can't tell if the first bit is me lying to myself. (8) A painting in a museum that turns out to have been looted. I feel wierd standing in front of it, but the weirdness is about who it belongs to, not about the painting — it seems like a different complaint entirely, and I'd like any principle I adopt to explain why.

My brother's attempt at a rule was something like: if the bad thing is in the work, it makes the work worse; if it's only about the person, it changes whether you should pay for it or recommend it, but not how good it is. Test that against my eight and repair it where it breaks, and be straight with me about which of my verdicts it can't save. Two things I especially want: what your best principle says about (7), and whether (1) versus (2) is really just me being inconsistent or whether you can find a principled difference — and if you can't, tell me which verdict you'd overturn. Also give me a verdict on a case I haven't listed: an instrumental cover of the song from (3), same melody, new lyrics about something completely innocuous, recorded by musicians who had nothing to do with the original. And tell me what one extra case I should settle my gut on, because it would decide between the two candidate principles you think are strongest.
