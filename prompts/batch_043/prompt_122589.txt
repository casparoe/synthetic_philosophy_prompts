I sit on a committee that is reviewing the undergraduate curriculum of my university's history department, and the more closely I look at what the department already does, the less able I am to say what it thinks history is for. I would like a philosophical reconstruction of what our practices presuppose -- not, to begin with, a defence of them.

The practices, as concretely as I can describe them.

First, the course that satisfies the general-education requirement for non-specialists is organised around a short list of named figures, founding moments, and masterworks; the catalogue copy promises that students will encounter greatness and be enlarged by it. The department also funds an annual named lecture in memory of one of its own founding scholars, at which a senior historian is invited to speak about a predecessor's achievement rather than about his errors.

Second, the research economy of the same department runs on the opposite scale. Doctoral training, hiring, and promotion reward the archival monograph, and the question put to it is whether it establishes something new from primary sources on a subject that perhaps a dozen people in the world care about. In graduate seminars the grand narrative, the exemplary episode, and anything written for a general reader are treated as signs of intellectual laxity.

Third, a large and growing share of our teaching is unmasking in orientation: courses whose organising claim is that canons, founding narratives, and revered texts are instruments of power, and that the historian's job is to expose them as such. These are among the courses students are most often required to take.

Fourth, two conventions about time, which I describe carefully because I do not know how standard they are. No course on any event less than fifty years old may count toward the major, on the stated ground that the recent past is politics and only the distant past is history. And the university may name nothing -- a building, a lectureship, a prize -- for anyone who has been dead fewer than twenty-five years; when an existing name becomes controversial, standing policy is to annotate the plaque rather than remove it.

The puzzle. Followed consistently, each of the first three strands seems to destroy the others. If the canon is an instrument of power, the general-education course is indoctrination. If the only serious history is the archival monograph, the canon is arbitrary and the unmasking course is popularisation with a political slant. If history's business is to hold up examples of greatness, the specialist's devotion to the small fact is a hobby and the unmasking course is vandalism. Yet the department funds all three, requires students to take all three, and offers no account of how they hang together. The conventions about time deepen the puzzle: they say that a subject must be safely past before it can be treated historically, and that criticism, once trained on a name, is to be recorded beside that name rather than acted upon.

What I want from you:

1. Reconstruct the implicit theory. Under what single view of what history is for do all five features make sense together? A colleague has told me that Nietzsche's early essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life -- the second of the Untimely Meditations, 1874 -- is the text in which precisely this problem is posed, in terms of a history that looks for greatness and example, a history that reverently preserves the past for its own sake, and a history that puts the past on trial, together with the claim that each becomes destructive when it dominates, that each needs the others, and that each needs a limit. Use that framework if it is genuinely useful; do not assume that supplying the three labels answers anything.

2. Separate strict presupposition from good fit. Does the arrangement really presuppose that history is answerable to the present's capacity to act -- that the standard is life, in Nietzsche's sense -- or is that merely one story that can be told about it? Would a view on which history's only obligation is to get the past right, or one on which its point is to produce citizens able to criticise their own society, or one on which its point is to sustain a community's sense of continuity, license exactly the same five features? If more than one theory licenses the same practice, say which, and say what further fact about the department -- what it would do in some case it has not yet had to face -- would discriminate between them.

3. Say where the practice is internally inconsistent rather than merely in tension, if it is. In particular: does the annotate-don't-remove policy presuppose that the critical attitude can be switched off once it has done its work? Does the fifty-year rule presuppose that a community needs a protected zone of unexamined present, and can that be reconciled with requiring every student to take the unmasking course?

4. Tell me what would have to change under each of the three rival views named in (2): which features would go, which could stay, and what new feature each view would force on us.

5. Finally, is there a reading on which the department is not confused but simply offering different justifications to different audiences -- greatness to applicants and donors, rigour to hiring committees, critique to the faculty meeting -- and if so, is that incoherence, or is it, on the Nietzschean view, exactly the right arrangement?

I would like roughly nine hundred words. Please argue for the reconstruction you take to be most defensible rather than surveying the alternatives, and say plainly where you think the practice is underdetermined.
