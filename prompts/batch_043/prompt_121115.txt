I've been asked to write a short nonfiction afterword for a novel I'm finishing — the book is set roughly eighty years from now, during the rollout of the first heritable recalibration of the human hedonic set point — and the afterword is supposed to say, in about fifteen hundred words, what I actually think about abolitionism (the project of ending suffering by rewriting the biology that produces it) rather than what my characters think. My difficulty is that whenever I set my position out as a list it reads as though it ought to be fine, and then someone presses one line and I can't reconstruct why it seemed fine. Three separate readers have confidently told me that three different commitments are the load-bearing mistake, and I no longer trust my own sense of which. So here is the set, more or less as I would publish it.

(1) Tranquilism about value: the only thing that is intrinsically bad is suffering, understood as the phenomenally aversive quality of experience — the sting as distinct from the signal — and there is no such thing as an experience's being intrinsically good; what is good is only the absence or relief of the bad. It follows that there is no duty to create happiness, only to abolish and prevent suffering. (2) The stakes are set by the size of the future: what makes abolitionism urgent for me is not the suffering of the next hundred years but the suffering that a technologically mature civilization could contain across astronomically many generations of minds, so ensuring that the recalibration takes hold and sticks is among the highest priorities available to us, and a large future full of flourishing minds is vastly better than a small one or an empty one. (3) Suffering is functionally replaceable: nothing irreplaceable is done by the aversive quality itself. Every job we might credit it with — injury avoidance, learning from error, moral attention, the discipline of long projects — could in principle be done by states that are fully informative, urgent and motivating but not unpleasant; you keep the nociception and the salience and drop the sting. (4) Motivational hedonism: at bottom we are only ever moved by the prospect of reducing or avoiding experiential discomfort, and a desire just is a disposition to act that involves some present or anticipated aversive tension; there is no being moved by a reason one does not feel as unease. (5) Implementation should be constitutional rather than pharmacological: the right target is the developmental program that fixes the hedonic set point, edited so as to be heritable and species-wide, rather than a lifetime of medication that has to be re-administered, re-funded and politically re-won every decade. (6) No hedonic lock-in: the settlement has to stay revisable, both because the people most affected cannot consent to a change in their own affective constitution and because I don't trust any generation, including this one, to have got the calibration right first time; I want the old architecture preserved in restorable form — an archive, as I think of it, of the unrecalibrated genome maintained alongside an inducible switch — so that abolition remains a standing decision later people could in principle reverse rather than a one-way door. (7) And a personal one, which may be only occupational: I want there to still be novels. I want readers who can be gripped, frightened, grieved and left unsettled, and I want to go on wanting that for myself; I don't take this as a defence of suffering — I'm not claiming that agony ennobles anyone — but I would rather not sign up to a programme whose success consists in nobody minding anything. (8) One case verdict I'm attached to: a friend who says she would refuse the recalibration for herself, on the grounds that she would rather keep her griefs than be gently rewritten, should be left alone — and not merely as a matter of political expediency, since I think there is something in her refusal I would have to respect, though I can't say what, given (1).

What I want is an audit, not an introduction to the literature. Go through the eight and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and how badly, ranking the tensions by severity rather than flagging all of them as equally damning; for each real tension, say exactly what would have to be given up and what that concession would cost me elsewhere in the set; and tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I'd also like you to separate out anything that only looks inconsistent — I suspect at least one item is defensible as stated and that I've been treating it as a problem because it sounds bad at a dinner party, but I can't tell which; if something dissolves once a distinction I've left implicit is made explicit, show me the distinction. Please don't start from which commitment you assume I'd most like to keep: I've gone back and forth on that for a year, and I'd rather see whether the set itself tells me.
