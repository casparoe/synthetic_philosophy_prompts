I am seventeen years old, and next term I have to give the opening essay for my school philosophy journal's issue on the mind. The theme I have been handed is whether consciousness is a user illusion, and I have already read enough of the standard expositions to guess their shape in advance: the desktop metaphor, the denial that there is any inner place where it all comes together, the reassurance that this is not a denial that we are conscious. I would therefore be grateful if you would write something more exacting than a survey. Please write an essay of roughly 1,500 words that takes up a narrower question than the usual one and defends an answer to it.

The narrower question is this. Suppose we grant, for the sake of the essay, the user-illusion picture of consciousness: our apparent acquaintance with our own experiences is a simplified, user-facing summary produced by neural machinery we never meet, and it is a mistake to read that summary as a faithful report of what is really going on underneath. Does that picture dissolve the puzzle of consciousness, or does it merely relocate it? I want a verdict on that, not a map of the terrain.

There are three things in particular I would like the essay to work through, because they are what actually bother me and I have not seen them handled.

First, I think a distinction is being run together. An interface can hide, or it can misrepresent. The icon on my laptop for a document hides the magnetic and electrical story of how the file is stored, but it does not assert anything false about it; by contrast, a progress indicator announcing that thirty seconds remain lies when the real estimate is twenty minutes. Call these an omission-interface and a misrepresentation-interface. Which sort of thing is consciousness supposed to be on the user-illusion view? If it is only an omission-interface, then I cannot see what the view denies. Saying that my experience of a red patch leaves out the story about opponent-processing, wavelengths and reflectance seems perfectly compatible with there being something it is like to see red, in the full-blooded sense the realist wants. The view would then be close to contentless, however provocative it sounds. If, on the other hand, it is a misrepresentation-interface, then the view owes us an account of what exactly the interface asserts, and of how a merely physical system can assert anything at all, which begins to sound like the original puzzle returning in a different coat. So the view looks to me either trivial or incomplete. I would like the essay either to grasp one horn of that dilemma and show that it is less damaging than I have made it, or to show that the dilemma is badly posed, or to take the third route I cannot quite see, and argue that the view is not trivial because what it denies is not the existence of qualitative states but something else in the neighbourhood, and then to say precisely what.

Second, here is a case I invented, because I could not find a real one that isolates the point. Patient M is fitted with a neuromodulatory implant for intractable pain. After the implant, her responses to noxious stimulation are unchanged in every way we can measure independently of what she says: heart rate, cortisol, the withdrawal reflexes, the speed with which she learns to avoid the stimulation, and the slow changes in her life that psychologists associate with lasting distress all look exactly as they did before. What has changed is what she reports and apparently believes. She says, consistently and without hesitation, that the stimulation is intense but not unpleasant; she describes it the way you might describe a loud noise you have no feelings about. She is not confabulating in any ordinary sense, she is not confused, she passes every test of comprehension we set her, and she is genuinely surprised by her own avoidance behaviour. On the user-illusion view, is there a fact of the matter about whether M suffers? If suffering is what shows up on the interface, or just is the interface, then M does not suffer, and the operation ceases to look like pain management and starts to look like a cure for suffering itself, which sounds like a wonderful thing. But if suffering is something the interface can get wrong, then the view seems to have conceded something very close to the realism it was supposed to replace. Please decide which way to go, and say whether the fact that a theory forces a clean verdict in a case like this is a recommendation of the theory or a sign that it is too simple. Then tell me what follows for practice: if M's say-so is the only evidence we will ever have, does the user-illusion view give us any remaining reason to treat a patient's sincere self-report as the gold standard in pain medicine and in law, or does it quietly undermine it?

Third, the metaphor seems to me to need a consumer, and I do not see where one is supposed to come from. An interface is an interface for somebody. On the user-illusion story the audience cannot be a small person inside the head, since that is precisely the inner theatre the view exists to demolish. But if there is no audience at all, I do not understand what the interface is for, or in what sense it is an illusion rather than merely one stage of processing among others. The related worry: suppose we build a machine with a self-model that regularly outputs something equivalent to I am now in a conscious state. If nothing is on the receiving end of that output, is the machine under an illusion? Please say how the view deals with the missing consumer, and whether removing the consumer turns the view into a different claim altogether, perhaps the claim that consciousness is a kind of self-description rather than an illusion in any interesting sense.

Two further constraints. The essay will be read aloud to a panel of two teachers and some older students, so it should be fully intelligible to a careful seventeen-year-old, defining any term of art before using it, and it should not condescend. And I have no access to a university library or to journals, so please argue from distinctions and cases rather than from citations; I do not need a reading list, and I would rather have one example worked through thoroughly than five names mentioned.

Finally, and this is the part I care most about: the essay must finish by choosing. A qualified or conditional verdict is entirely acceptable, as is an answer of the form the view is right about X and wrong about Y. What I do not want is a conclusion of the sort that says the question is deep, both camps have insights, and the matter awaits further empirical work. Please also make sure the essay gives the strongest objection a defender of the reality of conscious experience would press against whatever conclusion you reach, and answers it, rather than leaving it as a caveat in the final paragraph.
