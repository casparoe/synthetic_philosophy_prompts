I'm a high school senior doing an independent study project on the foundations of quantum mechanics, and I've run into something that I think is more of a conceptual problem than a physics one. I've been working through the standard spin-1/2 singlet setup: a pair of particles flies apart to two distant labs, each detector can be set to one of three angles 120° apart in the same plane, and quantum mechanics says that when the two settings coincide the results always disagree, while when they differ the results agree three quarters of the time. My teacher had me check by hand that no shared script — a list each particle carries away from the source saying what to output for each of the three possible detector angles — can push the agreement rate above two thirds at 120° while still reproducing the perfect disagreement at equal settings; after enumerating the eight possible scripts for one side and working out what the other side would be forced to be, I convinced myself that's right. What I can't get past is why this is supposed to be an argument against locality, since to me it just looks like an argument that the particles must be carrying something richer than a script.

So I built a model, and I'd like to know what's wrong with it, or whether it is already a known thing that just has a name I haven't learned. Call it the ledger model: instead of a script of instructions, each particle carries away from the source a complete ledger of the whole experimental run, including which two angles the experimenters are going to end up choosing — the choice of angles and the contents of the ledger both trace back to the same event in the shared past, so nothing has to travel from one wing to the other at measurement time, and the model is, as far as I can tell, perfectly local. It reproduces the quantum numbers exactly, it violates no Bell inequality, and the correlations still can't be used to send a message. When I described it to my teacher he said something like that's just the free-will loophole and changed the subject, which felt like a way of dismissing me rather than answering me, because as far as I can tell the inequality simply assumes that the settings are independent of the particles, my model denies that assumption, and so the theorem never touches it.

Three things I would really like straightened out, roughly in order of how much they're bugging me. First: is there a precise sense in which the ledger model is cheating, and can the amount of cheating be measured — is there some threshold for how strongly the settings and the hidden variables have to be correlated before a local model can simulate the singlet correlations, or is the correlation required essentially total, so that the model has to be fine-tuned to the point of absurdity? Second: if the ledger really is local, why isn't it treated as the answer to the problem, given that the popular story is that Bell forces us to give up locality — is the objection to it a scientific one, or is it ultimately that it makes the experimenters' choices look less free, and should I be worried that this just relocates the mystery into whatever set up the ledger in the first place? A friend of mine proposed a variant where, instead of the ledger being written at the source, each particle sends its answer backwards in time to the moment of preparation; I can't tell whether that is the same model with the arrow of time reversed, whether it escapes the objection somehow, or whether it is simply a third thing with its own problems. Third: every summary I find says Bell's theorem proved that nature is nonlocal and that hidden variables are impossible, but the ledger looks to me like a local hidden variable model, so at least one of those claims has to be wrong, and I'd like to know which one and in exactly what sense. I'm presenting this to our school science seminar in two weeks, and I'd much rather find out now whether the whole thing collapses under one objection I haven't thought of.
