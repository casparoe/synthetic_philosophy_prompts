hey — hoping you can help with something for tommorow. I run a tiny reading group, four of us plus a retired philosopher who mostly turns up to argue, we meet in the back room of a pub, and we've spent the last four weeks on the question of when a conscious experience actually happens. It's the final session and instead of a summary I want to give everyone a poem: I'm going to risograph it as a folded sheet with the two halves facing each other, and two of us will read it in alternating voices, so it has to be short — under ninety seconds aloud, no footnotes, and it should still be a good poem for someone who has never heard of the debate.

The case we kept circling is colour phi. Show someone a green dot in one spot, then a red dot a few degrees away maybe 150ms later, and they report a single dot that travels across the gap and changes colour partway along its path — except they report the change as happening before the second dot has been shown, which shouldn't be possible, because at that point nothing red has happened yet. So: either (the Orwell reading) the subject really did experience the green, and then afterwards the memory of that experience gets quietly rewritten to include the change, the past edited the way records get edited in Nineteen Eighty-Four; or (the Stalin reading) nothing reaches consciousness until the red arrives, and the whole travelling, colour-changing dot is composed then and presented as if it had been there from the start, with the green never having been experienced at all — verdict settled first, trial staged afterwards for the record. The two stories agree about every event in the brain and differ only about which side of the line between perception and memory the editing falls on, which is why half the room says there's no fact of the matter and the other half says that's a dodge. (Two things my philosopher friend will wince at, so please avoid them: the subject does not first see a stationary green dot and then a stationary red one and infer motion, they report seeing one moving thing; and "it's all an illusion / the brain fills in the gap" is not a third option, it's a shrug.)

Here's what I want the poem to do rather than say. My idea — tell me if it's unworkable — is that the poem is printed twice, the second version with a handful of words altered, so that the reader has to decide whether they're looking at a memory being revised after the fact or at the only version there ever was, the earlier one having never been experienced at all; the page looks the same either way, and that sameness is the argument, so the poem must not contain any line that names the two readings or announces that they're indistinguishable. I'd also like the colour word to change mid-line and before the line ends, at a point upstream of where the second thing is given, and for the very last line to be one that survives both readings unchanged — the line that would be the same whether it was remembered or composed late. Two more things: don't let the moving thing be a dot on a lab screen, find me some concrete non-laboratory image (something at a fairground, or a sparkler dragged through the dark, whatever) that carries the timing; and leave room, somewhere near the end, for a claim I raised and nobody liked — that experiences don't come with a timestamp, that the "when" is something added afterwards, so the question of whether the change came before the red rests on a mistake. I don't want it argued for, just made room for: maybe one line you can't place in the sequence, that could have come from anywhere. Someone who knows the literature should be able to tell which lines belong to which reading and why the ending can't settle it.
