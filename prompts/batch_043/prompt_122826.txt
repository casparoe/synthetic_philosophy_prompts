hey — I need you to think through something with me in the philosophy of social kinds. Money, specifically, not race or gender for once.

I think I have the standard moves down, but I keep suspecting that "money is socially constructed" is quietly doing three or four different jobs at once, and I want to test that by forcing it onto a real case: Nixon closing the gold window in august 1971.

Facts as I have them — tell me if any are wrong, but please don't build anything on correcting me: under Bretton Woods the dollar was pegged at 35 dollars to an ounce of gold, and it was foreign central banks (not ordinary people) that could actually swap dollars for gold; the US had been bleeding gold for years, spending on Vietnam and on domestic programmes, and France under de Gaulle was gleefully converting dollars; in august 1971 Nixon suspended convertibility, threw in a 90-day wage and price freeze and some kind of surcharge on imports (I forget the number); the Smithsonian agreement at the end of that year reset the peg to something like 38 an ounce, and by 1973 the whole thing had gone floating and gold's price was just whatever the market said.

What I want:

1. A disciplined mapping. On a constructivist story of money, what was the dollar before august 1971 and what was it after? What actually changed — the kind, the convention doing the anchoring, the attitudes that constitute it, or nothing metaphysical at all? Be explicit about which of the versions of the view you're using: the status-function / constitutive-rules one, the "money is a coordination equilibrium and there were always several" one, the chartalist "the state's tax demand plus legal-tender rules does the work" one, and Menger's emergence-out-of-barter story. For each: what does it predict about this episode, and where is it simply silent?

2. Three questions I think get run together, and I want them kept apart: the genesis question (what launches a monetary kind at all), the maintenance question (what keeps it being money on an ordinary tuesday in 1976), and the identity question (is the post-1971 dollar the same currency as the pre-1971 dollar? and if yes, in virtue of what — continuity of name, someone's say-so, users' attitudes, the fact that the coins in my pocket still say "legal tender for all debts public and private"?). For each of the three, what does 1971 actually show, and where does the framework run out?

3. The bit I definately can't get straight: constructed by whom? Every version of the slogan says something like "we collectively accept it" — but here one man and a handful of officials changed a rule, and nobody asked the rest of us, and the thing kept working. Does the constructivist have to admit that only some restricted class of agents' attitudes are constitutive (states, central banks, whoever posts prices, whoever accepts the stuff in trade)? And if so, does "socially constructed" survive that restriction, or does it quietly collapse into "imposed by whoever happens to have the power"? Related: does construction require actual uptake by everyone, or is "enough people, in the right positions" enough — and if it's the latter, what fixes the threshold, and is the threshold itself a social fact in the same sense?

4. Where the lens breaks, and what the case does back to the theory. Two things nag me. (i) If the dollar was already a social kind before 1971 — gold convertibility was itself a convention, and a convention only central banks could even exercise — then 1971 isn't the metaphysical drama it looks like. So is this episode really evidence for constructivism, or is it neutral between constructivism and any view on which the dollar was always just a liability of a state? (ii) The obvious contrast: Weimar, Zimbabwe, and a dozen others went fiat and the money went to zero. If "socially constructed" is supposed to tell us what money is, why doesn't it tell us why some fiat monies hold their value and others don't? Is that just out of scope — the theory is about the kind, not about the value — and is that answer a defence or a retreat?

5. One last thing, which may be me just pattern-matching: I keep wanting to apply the looping-effects idea from the human-kinds literature — you classify people into a kind, they change in response, the kind stops fitting — to money. Gresham's law looks like that. So does the way that once people start quoting prices in gold, or indexing contracts, or pricing oil in a unit the issuer doesn't control, the unit of account starts to drift away from the thing the state says it is. Is that a real structural analogy or am I seeing faces in clouds? If it's real, what does it predict about 1971 that the plain constructivist story misses — and does it suggest the state was less in charge of the kind than the theory likes to think?

Please: no literature survey, and don't just reel off Searle / Haslanger / Menger / Knapp at me as if the names were arguments — use them where they earn their keep, and where the framework genuinely cannot answer one of my questions, say so and say what kind of further fact or distinction would let it. Somewhere around 900 words.
