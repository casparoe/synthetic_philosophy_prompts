So this is a stupid way to get dragged into philosophy, but here we are. My brother-in-law is on a county endangered-species review panel, and because I have a reputation for being "good with words" he asked me to help with the bit of their report that has to say what a species even is. Two people on the panel mailing list have been going at each other for about a year:

- a pluralist: there is no single correct species category; the interbreeding concept, the diagnosability/phylogenetic concept and the ecological concept are all legitimate, and which one you use depends on what question you're asking;
- a monist of the general-lineage sort: there is exactly one species category, segments of metapopulation lineages, and the other concepts are only operational criteria for detecting those, and they can mislead.

They've finally agreed to do an adversarial collaboration and I volunteered to produce the document, so I need it drafted properly. The population they actually have to rule on is a mess: five populations of a flightless ground beetle along one ridge, call them A through E. Neighbours interbreed freely where they meet; A and E don't interbreed at all where the ridge loops back on itself; mitochondrial DNA puts A+B+C in one clade and D+E in another, cutting right across the interbreeding pattern; and C carries a chromosomal inversion that keeps it looking distinct even where it swaps genes with B. Every concept gives a different count. I've also been pushing a move of my own on them, which I call the swap test: take a delimitation produced under one concept, hand it to somebody working under a different one, ask them to predict an unmeasured trait of one population from the traits of the others in the group, and score how often the prediction survives. I think both of them should have to state in advance what they expect that to show.

What I want: a numbered section of claims both of them can sign (about what the concepts actually do, about what a species category is supposed to deliver for conservation law, about the beetle case itself), then a table of the remaining disagreements where each row gives the pluralist's version and the monist's version in wording each side would accept for itself, then the cruxes, and for each crux a case or piece of evidence both accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down before the fact. Use the beetle case and the swap test wherever they genuinely bite. My guesses at cruxes, which you can overrule: whether there is a fact of the matter about species boundaries independent of our investigative interests, and whether a criterion that tracks lineage separation is automatically the better one. One of them also keeps insisting that species are individuals rather than classes and I can't tell if that's part of this fight or a separate one — sort that out for me. And if any of the arguing was only about the word "species", say which part and why.

Please make it something both of them could actually put their names to, keep it tight rather than exhaustive, and write in American English. End with a single last line that reads "Summary:" followed by one sentence.
