Hi there. I'm a novelist and I need you to ghostwrite a document for a book I'm stuck on, and I'd rather you wrote it straight than dramatized it — the dramatization is literally my job.

The setup: two characters who can't stand each other get locked in a room by a funding committee and told to produce one joint statement on the reliability of introspection. One of them — call her the acquaintance side — holds that introspection is a genuine and somewhat special source of knowledge about your own conscious life: when she sits down and reports what a feeling is actually like, she takes herself to be reading something off rather than guessing, and she thinks reports like that carry a default authority that needs specific reasons to be overturned. The other — call him the construction side — holds that what we call introspection is just the ordinary human capacity for making sense of a mind, the same inferential, narrative, memory-and-cue-using machinery we deploy on other people, only pointed inward, with a richer private dataset and a much stronger motive to come out with a coherent story. On his view the special authority is an illusion manufactured by how fluent and unhesitating the reports feel.

What I need is the document they actually produce. Not a dialogue, not a verdict on who is right — the text both of them could put their names to.

The shape I want:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims. Make them load-bearing, not handshake mush. "Both sides agree introspection is sometimes wrong" is useless to me; give me claims with teeth that neither of them would be embarrassed to sign. I'd like at least these covered: the transparency-style point about belief, i.e. that you very often settle what you believe by looking outward at the world rather than inward at a mental state, and exactly what that does and doesn't establish; whether the live disagreement is mostly about phenomenal character rather than about attitudes, motives and causes; and whether introspective error is better modelled as random noise or as systematic distortion, since those have completely different consequences for how much any single report is worth.

2. A disagreements table. One row per surviving dispute: the dispute stated in neutral terms; then the formulation each side would accept as a fair statement of its own position; then your verdict on whether that row is a genuine disagreement or a fight about words, with the reason. I suspect at least one row is verbal and I want it named as such.

3. A cruxes section: the sub-questions where settling them would actually move somebody. For each one, the concrete case or argument both camps would accept as bearing on it, and each camp's prediction written down in advance. No "we'd have to see the data" gestures — I need both of them committed.

4. A short section on what each side would count as evidence against itself. If one of them can't name anything, say so, and tell me what that implies about whether the dispute is empirical at all.

The cases. Please use mine rather than real experiments, and please don't cite anything at all — no studies, no author names, no paper titles, no dates. I'd only have to strip them out, and I don't want to accidentally end up reproducing somebody's footnote.

- The cold-read test. A writer who has just been through something writes a single page describing as precisely as she can what the experience is actually like — not the events, the feel. The page goes out unattributed to two groups: people who have recently been through the same kind of thing, and people who haven't. Both are asked whether the page records something they recognise or something merely plausible. Assume, for the sake of the case, that the groups come apart sharply: the experienced readers say yes, that's exactly it, how did you know; the others say it reads like competent invention. Both my characters agree that this result bears on their dispute. They do not agree about what it shows. Have each of them predict the result in advance and say why.
- The transcription lag. Someone keeps a running spoken record of what they are feeling, in the moment, for a week, and at the end of each day also writes a page about that day. Assume the two records disagree systematically: the same-day writeups are tidier, more causally connected, more story-shaped, and they confidently cite motives the running record never mentioned at the time. Which way does this cut, and how hard?
- The expertise question. My acquaintance character has spent twenty years writing about her own inner life in close detail. The construction character's reply is that twenty years of practice at producing fluent self-descriptions is precisely what you would expect to manufacture a feeling of certainty rather than accuracy. Is there any way to seperate getting better at looking from getting better at storytelling? If there isn't, say so, and say whose problem that is.
- The seam. There are moments where something is certainly going on in you and introspection returns nothing: the instant of falling asleep, the gap between settling on a word and the word arriving, the way a room can change without your having seen it change. Both sides agree these happen; they disagree about what they prove. Make that disagreement exact rather than atmospheric.

Two pieces of scaffolding I've found useful while drafting and that you should feel free to use. One is a gradient: first-person authority is strong at the top, over whether anything is going on at all, and thins out as you move down through what it is like, how intense it is, what caused it, and why you did it — so the argument is about where the fall-off begins and how steep it is, not about whether there is a gradient. The other is the familiarity trap: going back to the same feeling again and again makes it feel better known, and that could be either genuine refinement of access or fluency being misread as accuracy. I don't know which, and the book sort of turns on it, so don't paper over it.

Tone: this gets read aloud in a room by two people who resent each other, so let the agreed claims be prose somebody could actually say out loud — but I do want the numbered section as a numbered section and the table as a table.

Oh, and one last thing: if you think my setup stacks the deck, that any of the four cases is rigged for one side, add a short note at the end saying which one and what a fairer version would look like. I'd rather find out now than halfway through a rewrite. It's a wierd request, I know, but I've been going back and forth on this chapters for about two years and I gues I finally want someone to just write the thing.
