Hi — I write novels, and the one I'm working on right now keeps forcing me to work out what I actually think about everyday conventions, because half my scenes turn on a character either honouring or flouting one. I want someone to audit my position before I put a version of it in a character's mouth and get taken apart at a festival panel.

Roughly where I've landed:

(1) Queueing-type conventions are genuine moral obligations, not just etiquette. Pushing into a queue is *wrong*, not merely rude, because these are mutually beneficial coordination schemes and it's unfair to take the benefit while skipping the cost.

(2) A visitor has to follow the local version specifically, not just any old version — "any consistent queue will do" is not good enough. Locals set the terms; I'm a guest.

(3) Jaywalking is fine. Crossing against the light on an empty street isn't wrong at all, and I don't owe anybody anything for it. Traffic laws bind only when they're actually doing coordination work and someone is affected.

(4) Tipping in the US is obligatory, and I think it's a different kind of thing from queueing: it isn't coordination, it's compensation for a wage structure that exploits servers. So I tip properly even in cities where the local tipping convention is weak or mean.

(5) In places where tipping isn't the norm, I don't tip, and I dont think I do anything wrong — even though hospitality workers there are often badly paid too.

(6) I won't follow a convention I think is morally bad (norms that rank people by caste, gender, age, whatever). Being a guest doesn't require that of me.

(7) I also follow the local convention when it literally makes no difference to anybody — standing on the right of a completely empty escalator, in a country where that's the done thing. I'd feel like a jerk not to, even though nobody's affected and nobody's watching.

Two scenes from the novel I keep testing these against. In the city it's set in, people hold a place in a queue by leaving an object in it — a shoe, a bag — and the locals insist a visitor has to honour the object the same as a person. And in that same city everybody crosses wherever they like, the drivers expect it, and it's against the law.

What I want: go through the seven and tell me which pairs are really in tension and how badly, ordered by severity, with what would have to give in each case and what the cheapest repair would be. Tell me too which of the tensions only *look* like tensions and dissolve when you think about them properly — I've got a feeling at least one of these is fine as it stands, and I'd rather not throw away a commitment I don't have to. Please don't just flag all seven as problematic; I want to know which one I actually have to drop.

And be clear about which bits of what you say are settled and which are genuinely contested among people who work on this sort of thing — don't present a contested view as if it were the consensus, say which claims you'd only give 60/40, and flag wether anything in your answer is more guess than argument.
