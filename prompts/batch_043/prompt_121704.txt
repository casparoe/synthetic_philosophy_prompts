I'm writing a paper on indicative conditionals and I want to know what my own credences commit me to. Background: I accept that the equation Cr(if A, B) = P(B|A) cannot hold in full generality — the triviality results show, as I understand them, that no proposition expressed by an if-sentence can have its probability track the corresponding conditional probability across the board, given only modest assumptions about how conditionals compose with the things around them. But I also have definite credences in conditionals whose consequents are themselves conditionals, so I cannot take the comfortable way out and deny that conditionals are the kind of thing that embeds. I need a rule that delivers the numbers I actually give. Here are eight cases with my verdicts; all probabilities are mine and are meant to be taken as given.

(i) A fair coin and a fair die, independent. If the coin lands heads, the die comes up six. Verdict: 1/6.

(ii) If the barometer has dropped sharply in the last hour, a storm is on its way. P(storm) = 0.1; P(storm | drop) = 0.8; if I imagine reaching over and forcing the needle down, my credence in a storm stays at 0.1. Verdict: 0.8. I do not move to 0.1, even though I know the needle exerts no causal influence on the weather.

(iii) A particular patient, already known to be ill. If she takes the drug, she will recover. P(recover | takes) = 0.6, P(recover | does not take) = 0.7 — the drug is mostly given to the worst cases — but I am confident it is efficacious, and under an intervention my credence in recovery is 0.9. Verdict: about 0.9. I do not say 0.6. Case (ii) shows this is not a blanket preference for conditional probability: here the supposition behaves like something I set, there like something I learn.

(iv) If she carries the variant, she will develop the disease. P = 0.3. The variant is not something anyone can set, and I have no view about whether it is causal. Verdict: 0.3, and I feel none of the pull I felt in (iii).

(v) If the storm comes through, the barometer will drop. Both readings agree. Verdict: 0.9.

(vi) If it rains tomorrow, the sun will rise the day after. Verdict: credence near 1, but I would never say it. I am not sure whether a theory of my credences should be expected to explain this gap, or whether it shows I should be theorizing about assertability instead.

(vii) Three candidates: X and Y from party P, Z from party Q. P(X) = 0.5, P(Y) = 0.2, P(Z) = 0.3, so P(a P candidate wins) = 0.7. My credence in if a P candidate wins, then if X does not win, Y wins is about 1, since among P candidates there is no third option. My credence in if X does not win, Y wins, considered on its own, is 0.4. So I violate modus ponens in credence: high credence in the antecedent, near-certain credence in the conditional, and only 0.4 in the consequent. Note that 0.4 is exactly P(Y | not-X).

(viii) The one I go back and forth on. If I take the supplement, my joint pain will improve. P(improve | takes) = 0.6, but people who take supplements are health-conscious, and I am genuinely unsure whether the supplement does anything at all. Imagining that I read a study in which I take it, I answer 0.6. Imagining that I decide to start tomorrow, I answer something lower — or I find I have no number.

What I have been toying with is a single shift rule. For each antecedent A there is a map taking every world I regard as live to the world or worlds I would count as the closest A-worlds (I have seen this called imaging, but the label does not matter to me), and Cr(if A, B) is the share of my credal mass that lands in B once the shift is applied. Nesting is handled by composing shifts, failures of import-export and of modus ponens fall out for free, I get a verdict when P(A) = 0, and the triviality arguments never get a grip, since I am not assuming the equation anywhere. The problem is the numbers. I worked out that the shift rule agrees with the equation exactly when the mass flowing into each A-cell is proportional to that cell's prior probability; call this balance. Balance is exactly what fails in (iii), where the intervention moves all the sick worlds onto sick-and-treated worlds, and exactly what seems to hold in (i), (iv), (v) and (vii) — in (vii) a closest-world rule would give me at least P(Y) = 0.2 for the inner conditional plus whatever the mass on X-worlds contributes, which lands on 0.4 only if that mass is distributed proportionally. But balance now looks suspiciously like a relabelling of the verdicts I want, and I cannot see how to say what makes a supposition balanced or unbalanced without already knowing which answer I am after.

So, concretely, I would like:

1. A principle, or two or three candidate principles, stated precisely enough that I can compute verdicts from them, with the case that motivates each clause and the case each clause is designed to survive.

2. A test and, if possible, a repair of the shift rule. Can balance be cashed out non-circularly — in terms of causal structure, of what gets held fixed under the supposition, of the question a conditional is addressed to, of whether the antecedent is the sort of thing I could come to know or only bring about? Or does the rule have to split into a learn-rule and a set-rule, and if it splits, is that a semantics for conditionals or just a description of my own sensitivity to framing?

3. Which of my verdicts has to go. I suspect the (ii)/(iii) pair and the inner verdict in (vii) cannot all stay, and I would rather be told which is least defensible than have all three accommodated by a principle複雜 enough to fit anything.

4. Predictions for two cases I have not settled. First, the coin is already flipped and I have seen that it landed heads: if it had landed tails, I would have lost ten pounds. I have a definite number — about 0.5 — although P(A) = 0 and the equation says nothing; I know this one is strictly subjunctive, but I would like one rule for both moods, and I want to know whether that desire is itself doing theoretical work. Second, disjunctive antecedents: what should I expect to say about if X or Y wins, the market will fall, given what I say about the two singular conditionals, and does your principle make me accept or reject the inference from the disjunctive conditional to each of them?

5. Since I have verdicts about embedded conditionals, I cannot retreat to the claim that conditionals do not express propositions. So: does the account you favour actually escape the triviality results, or is there a version of the collapse that reaches it anyway — and what in particular do I give up by abandoning the equation, given whatever made it attractive in the first place?

I would rather have two or three precisely stated candidates with their costs marked than one confident answer. Where a candidate forces me to overturn a verdict, say which and why that verdict is the weak one.
