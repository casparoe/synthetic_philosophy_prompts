I convene a fortnightly reading group on decision theory for about fourteen advanced undergraduates, mostly philosophers and mathematicians with no economics background, and we have one ninety-minute session left in the term. They have already worked through Newcomb's problem and the familiar standoff between causal and evidential decision theory, and I want to use the last meeting on updateless decision theory: the thought that what gets evaluated is an entire policy, a mapping from the observations one might receive to the actions one would then take, scored from the standpoint one occupied before any of those observations arrived, so that learning something new does not by itself reopen the question of what to do. I would like them to walk out with the sense that the live disagreement is about which standpoint a policy is scored from and not about whether evidence should be used, and I want that to come from what they do in the room rather than from anything I say. So I am after an activity with real but inexpensive stakes: course points, chocolate, the standing of a small wager among themselves, nothing that requires actual money. Last year I staged Newcomb's problem by playing the predictor from a written script, and the entire debrief was spent arguing about whether my predictions could possibly have been accurate rather than about the decision rule itself, so I would like to avoid leaning on a predictor whose reliability becomes the topic.

I have a sketch and a worry, and the worry is the reason I am writing. The case I have in mind is the one where a very probable branch pays out if the agent is the sort that pays in a very improbable branch, and the agent then finds itself in the improbable branch, where paying now is a pure loss. My worry is that I cannot see how to stage it. If I let them learn which branch they are in before they choose, every reason to pay evaporates: the sensible student declines, which is the updateful answer, and all they learn is that the updateless recommendation looks like a sucker's move. If I hide the branch from them, there is no update for them to be updateless about, and the whole thing collapses into ordinary expected-utility reasoning behind a veil, which they will find obvious and unilluminating. I have tried patching this by asking each of them for the same decision twice, in two rounds separated by a filler task, and only afterwards revealing which answer was executed and which was used to fix the payout in the branch they were not in; but that seems to me to work only if both answers are given under exactly the same information, which means the device selecting the branch must be resolved before both rounds, and then I am back where I began. So give me the best staging you can devise: the ground rules, whatever has to be prepared in advance, the payoff scheme, and how the ninety minutes should be divided, with the incentives arranged so that the conflict shows up in what the students actually choose and forgo.

Three further requests. First, a debrief that maps particular moments in the session onto particular features of the view, including the point that a policy is a function of the evidence so the updateless agent is not ignoring anything, and including the awkward question of where the initial standpoint is supposed to come from for an agent that was never behind any veil at all. Second, someone will certainly object that staging the case by means of a prediction or a coupled partner makes the connection between the live choice and the other branch merely evidential or statistical, when what the view requires is a logical or counterfactual one; I want that met honestly rather than papered over, together with a plain statement of what the activity cannot reproduce and what I will therefore have to supply in my own words. Third, I would like a short closing phase of about ten minutes in which the chancy device is replaced by a mathematical proposition nobody in the room can settle in the time available, so that which branch one is in becomes a matter of logic rather than chance, and the group has to say whether the case still goes through and what changes if it does. Tell me also what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and how to handle it. Please write the whole thing as continuous prose in one piece, with no headings, bullet points, numbered lists, bold text or tables, the way you would set it out in a long email to a colleague, and please do not add references or suggested reading, since I have the literature to hand.
