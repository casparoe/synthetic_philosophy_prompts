hey — I'm on this tiny advisory panel for a mid-sized city (around 900k, pretty mixed: a big port, a university, lots of recent immigration, old neighbourhoods that quietly hate the new condo towers) and we're supposed to say something useful about how strict this city's rules should be. Not *which* rules — that's a different fight — but how much weight the whole system should put on people actually following whatever the rule happens to be, and how much slack, how much punishment for deviating. People keep reaching for the tight culture / loose culture vocabulary for this (tight = strong norms, low tolerance of deviance, heavy sanctions; loose = weaker norms, more tolerance, more slack), and every time we try to convert that into a recommendation we realise we have no idea what we're even asking a theory of this to deliver.

So: write me the desiderata. What does a normative theory of "how much rule-following a society should demand" have to get right, given that its job is to hand a body like ours a defensible recommendation about one real, fairly diverse city, one policy area at a time, on a two-year review cycle. For each requirement, the case or consideration behind it, and whether it's non-negotiable or just one camp's bugbear.

Things I keep tripping over, in case they help — tell me which of these are genuine requirements and which are prejudice dressed up as rigour:

- Tightness doesn't seem to be one number. The same city can be ferocious about queue-jumping and totally relaxed about what you believe or who you sleep with. Is domain-separability a requirement, and if so does it kill the whole idea of scoring a culture on a single tightness axis?
- The ratchet: the machinery that enforces tightness — monitoring, mutual reporting, willingness to punish — is costly to build and, as far as I can tell, harder to dismantle, so a city can end up stuck tighter than it would choose if it were choosing fresh. Does the theory owe us guidance about where we *are*, not just where we'd aim from scratch, and is "path-dependent" a feature a theory is allowed to have?
- Threat laundering. The story we keep hearing is that tightness is a response to real threat (density, disease, scarcity, invasion), but surely it's *perceived* threat that does the work, and the people who benefit from tightness are the ones with the loudest megaphone. A rule that says "match tightness to threat" might just be handing a propaganda shop the dial. Is resistance to that manipulation a requirement — and can anything satisfy it?
- The pluralism tax: tightness needs agreement on *content*, so in a diverse city tightening raises the stakes of whose norms win. Maybe looseness is partly a technology for living with people you don't share norms with. Is "sustainable under disagreement about content" a requirement?
- Whose tightness? "The city" isn't an agent. The person who eats the fine and the person who enjoys the orderly queue are different people, and so is the minority whose norm it never was. Does the theory have to name whose costs count, and does that requirement exclude most of the answers people usually give?
- It should be able to say "this city should be tighter" without implying "therefore any particular rule is good" — tightness isn't correctness.

Also: I suspect some of our desiderata are secretly empirical claims we can't settle from an armchair (does tightness actually buy safety and coordination, or is it just what threatened places do?). Flag any that are like that. And flag any subset you think can't be jointly satisfied — that's the part I most want, because if our list is impossible that's the real finding. Tell me which single requirement knocks out the most of the usual answers.

Short scorecard at the end if you want, couple of lines each for the families of answer you think are the real contenders (something like threat-functionalist/consequentialist vs. autonomy-first vs. coordination-and-equilibrium-selection — regroup them if I've divided the space wrong). No bibliographies and no "go read the literature": our librarian left and we can't get papers, and I don't care who said it first. Use hypotheticals rather than real countries where you can, and if you do lean on a real place, say you're not sure of the facts. Under about a thousand words.
