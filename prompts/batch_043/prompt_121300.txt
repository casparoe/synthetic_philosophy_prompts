I work in control theory — state-space methods, optimal and robust control, reachability and invariance — and that vocabulary is what I actually think in. Please restate in it, as faithfully as you can, two claims from the philosophical literature on artificial agents: the orthogonality thesis, that an agent's degree of capability or intelligence imposes almost no constraint on the content of its final goals, so that nearly any level of capability can be combined with nearly any final goal; and the instrumental convergence thesis, that agents with widely different final goals will still tend to pursue certain instrumental aims — self-preservation, goal-content integrity, resource acquisition, cognitive enhancement, technological perfection — because those help with almost any final goal.

Do the translation item by item. My own first pass has *power* as the size or shape of the reachable set the controller can still steer the state into, self-preservation and resistance to being switched off as remaining in the region from which the terminal target is still attainable, and reward tampering as corrupting the measurement channel so the error signal looks small. Improve on that, and flag anything I have smuggled in.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually want. Cover at least these:

1. Orthogonality can be read modally, as a claim about the space of possible designs, or empirically, as a claim about what to expect of systems we actually build. Can my vocabulary separate the two, and is the modal reading close to trivial in it, given that a cost functional is a free parameter of the design?
2. Instrumental convergence says what *most* goals lead to, but that needs a measure over goals. What becomes of the claim in a vocabulary whose only natural distributions are over plants, disturbances, and tasks?
3. How much convergence survives when the problem is feasibility — keep the state inside this invariant set — rather than optimality, and when the horizon is short or the discounting heavy?
4. A colleague argues that the formal power-seeking results locate the explanation in the structure of the environment rather than in the agent's capability. If that is right, do the two theses even compose into the familiar conclusion that a more capable agent with arbitrary goals will seek power, and which thesis is carrying the weight?

Finally, say what cannot be expressed in my vocabulary at all. I suspect the main casualty is the distinction between a system's own final goal and the objective it was specified or trained on, since control theory takes the cost as exogenous and has no place for intentionality. Tell me whether that residue is a blind spot of my framework or a sign that the philosophers' notion of a final goal is thinner than it looks.
