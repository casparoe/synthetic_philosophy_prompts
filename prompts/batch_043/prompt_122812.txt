I'm running a one-off session on voting and collective choice for about 18 undergrads, mostly philosophy and PPE with a couple of econ majors who've seen a bit of social choice but not much. 80 minutes, one room, no laptops. I want them to feel Gibbard–Satterthwaite in their bones rather than have me state it, so the theorem shouldn't be named until the last ten minutes and its name shouldn't appear on any handout.

Here's roughly where I've got to, and where I need help.

The choice they're making: what to do with the last two sessions of term. Four options, and I want them arranged so that people's preferences plausibly sit on a single axis (how formal/technical vs. how discursive the sessions are — a proofs workshop, a guest speaker, a student-led debate, a trip to a local archive). That axis matters later, but they shouldn't be told it exists.

My plan is a few rounds with the same private preferences and a payoff in points they care a bit about (counts toward a quiz exemption, no actual money, nothing that will get me in trouble with the department). Round one: plurality, with me acting as a polling firm that releases a slightly noisy poll before ballots go in. Round two: same preferences, different rule (runoff or Borda), so the winner changes and someone notices that voting sincerely cost them. Round three: open season — announce the rule, let them think, collect ballots, and compare what happened to what sincere voting would have given.

Then the part I'm most unsure about. I want to split them into teams of three and have each team invent a voting rule that can't be gamed, with the other teams paid in points for breaking it with a falsified ballot. My hope is that they converge on the same three or four escape hatches on their own, and that the debrief is mostly me pointing at what they already built. I'd like your version of that phase: what the rules of the design contest should be so that it doesn't collapse into everyone proposing ranked choice, how to score rule-invention vs. rule-breaking so both sides actually try, and what to do when a team proposes something genuinely strategy-proof that I haven't anticipated.

Two extra things I can't work out. First: I'm tempted to let abstention be a ballot option in round three, and I can't tell whether that's a second, interesting kind of manipulation or just noise that will muddy the main point — tell me whether to include it and where. Second: someone always raises the thing where you'd do better staying home than voting at all, and I never handle it well; if it's a different result from the one I'm teaching, say so explicitly and tell me how to stop it swallowing the session.

Give me: the full runnable thing — rules, ballot and scoring materials, the payoff scheme, a timeline with minute marks, and how the poll's noise level should be set (I don't want it so precise that everyone can compute an optimal ballot, or so vague that nobody strategizes). Then a debrief that maps specific moments onto the specific assumptions doing the work: deterministic, at least three outcomes actually in the running, no dictator, and no restriction on what preferences people can have. I also want the debrief to keep this clearly separate from Arrow's result, which half of them will have heard of and will try to merge with this one.

Be honest about: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (collusion, the econ major blurting the answer in minute five, someone voting sincerely on principle, ties, someone who's already read about this spoiling the design phase, groups too small for anyone to have real information about others); and which bit of the idea the game simply cannot deliver, so I have to say it out loud myself. My instinct is that it can't show that every reasonable rule is manipulable, only that a handful are, and that it can't convey that the result is about what's possible given information rather than about how often real people strategically vote — but tell me if I'm wrong about what's missing.
