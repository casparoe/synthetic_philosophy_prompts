I work as a backend dev at a small company — no philosophy background, this is just stuff I've been reading about on my own — and we inherited this nightmare of a nightly cron job that nobody understands. It was written about four years ago by a guy who left, it generates a report nobody has opened in years, and deleting it would make my on-call life a lot easier. Every time I bring it up my teammate shuts the conversation down with "don't tear down a fence until you know why it was put up" (the thing people online call Chesterton's fence). What drives me crazy is that I think I can honestly say two things at once: first, we actually could dig up why it was originally put up, from the git history and the guy's old design notes; second, that original reason is basically dead, but we're now fairly sure the job is load-bearing for a completely different reason — some other service reads a table it happens to write as a side effect, which nobody ever designed on purpose. So on my read of the slogan its own test is nearly satisfied, and my teammate still thinks it says "leave it alone forever."

So please take that one sentence apart for me. What are the different precise claims it could be making, from the completely trivial to the actually radical? The ones I've half-formed on my own are something like: a procedural "go gather information before you act" thing, which nobody would disagree with; a claim about original intent, i.e. that things get made for reasons and you should recover those reasons; a stronger claim about invisible current function, where the fence is holding up something now that it was never designed to hold up, so even knowing the original reason isn't enough; a Burkean-sounding thing where institutions contain accumulated trial-and-error knowledge that no individual can reconstruct in an afternoon, maybe not even articulate; a cost-asymmetry point, that wrongly removing something is irreversible and spreads its damage around while keeping it is cheap and reversible; and a flat presumption against any change, where the status quo wins by default. Some of those feel like the same idea at different strengths and some feel like totally different ideas wearing the same sentence. Where does each one land — platitude, sensible and substantive, speculative, or just false — and what does each one commit me to if I accept it?

Three more specific things I'd want on the menu, because they're what my actual argument keeps turning on. (1) Does "know why it was put up" mean the historical reason someone had at the time, or does it mean I need a working model of what the thing is holding up right now? Those come apart badly in my case, since the historical reason is knowable and worthless while the current one is what matters and is partly unknowable. (2) Does the principle get weaker when the fence is young and was put up by someone no wiser than me — or does it not care at all how the fence got there? My teammate talks as if age itself is evidence the thing earned its place, but I can't tell whether that's inside the slogan or a separate idea people bolt onto it. (3) Does the reverse version follow from the same logic — "don't put up a fence until you know what it will break"? If both hold, it kind of looks like the real principle is just "don't touch complex systems you don't fully understand," which would tell me to do nothing ever, including nothing about the cron job, and that can't be right. Finally, and this is the part I actually want to bring back to work: for each reading, tell me whether it would really support my teammate's conclusion that I have to leave the job running, and if the honest answer is that most of them only buy him "do some investigation first," say what a reasonable stopping point would look like — how would I know when I've understood it enough? A list I can argue from would be perfect.
