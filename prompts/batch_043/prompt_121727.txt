My friend and I spent maybe two hours last night arguing about moral compromise, and I went to bed sure I'd won and woke up not sure at all. Her line is that you don't split the difference with injustice. Mine is that most of collective life is people with different values finding a deal that leaves everyone better off than no deal, and that the gains come from intensity: I let you have the thing you care about twice as much as I do, you let me have mine, and refusing the trade is just losing on purpose. But when I test that against actual cases my gut goes off in different directions:

- I'm vegetarian and I cook a roast for my dad at Christmas. Obviously fine, not even really a compromise.
- Budget fight: I want $40M for after-school programs, the other bloc wants $0 and the money for police overtime. We settle on $18M, which I genuinely think isn't enough. Fine, I take it.
- Sex ed: we wanted comprehensive, they wanted abstinence-only, the deal teaches both. I say fine; she says the curriculum now says something false.
- Logroll: I vote for your library, you vote for the stadium I think is a boondoggle. This feels worse to me than the budget split even though it's kind of the same shape, and I can't say why.
- Sentencing commission: I think the right sentence is 20 years, my colleague thinks 5, we agree on 12 to get a unanimous recommendation. Feels bad, like haggling over what someone deserves — but structurally that's the budget case again.
- Coalition: a group whose flyers I think are racist offers to canvass for our side. Taking the canvassing: fine. Putting my name on the flyer: not fine, even though it changes nothing about the election outcome.
- Hiring: I think A is clearly the best candidate, everyone else thinks B, and the deal everyone can live with is to hire C, who nobody thinks is best. I go back and forth on this one all day.

My own attempt: I've started calling the bad kind "enrollment" — the deal doesn't just let the other side have some of what they want, it makes me a co-signer on their position — and there's a separate thing, call it the "ratchet," where a compromise resets the baseline so next time you're defending a weaker version of your own view. So: trade is fine over how much of a good each side gets and over who decides; it's not fine when it enrolls me in a claim I think is false or when what's traded is a judgment about what a person deserves.

Is there one principle that gets all of that, including why the library/stadium deal feels different from the budget one? Test mine and repair it, tell me which of my verdicts you'd throw out and why, and say what it predicts for the hiring case. Also tell me what would change your mind and where your reasoning is most likely to fail — my worry is that I'm just dressing up "this particular deal makes me feel icky" as a moral distinction.
