I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and for a couple of years now I have been bumping into a distinction I cannot name, partly because I do not know what term to look under.

Three situations that feel to me like the same thing.

First. My father had dementia. My mother would say, about the man in front of her, "that is not him any more" — and then, a month later, when some old mannerism surfaced for a few seconds, "there he is." What she was tracking was plainly not the body, which was continuous throughout; it was not memory, since by then he could not remember much of anything; and it was not his current behaviour taken on its own, which was the same in both cases. It seemed to be something like whether a certain story could still be got going about the person in front of her. When it could, he was there; when it could not, he was not.

Second. I re-described a period of my own life: a job I left, which I had told as a principled stand, I now tell as an elaborate way of running away. Nothing about the past changed. But the re-description changed what I did next, and not merely how I felt about it. Meanwhile I have changed my politics, my tastes and my habits beyond recognition over fifteen years, and none of that registers, to me or to anyone who knows me, as becoming a different person — apparently because nobody ever folded those changes into a story. So changes count, or do not count, depending on whether they get narrated, which seems backwards if the story is only a description.

Third. A colleague was in a coma for three weeks. Throughout, his debts, his promises and his job were treated as still attached to something, and everyone involved — family, employer, bank — behaved as though there were a single thing those attachments hung from, even though during that period nothing was being narrated by anybody. Afterwards people said he was "still in there." I want to know what the "he" and the "in there" are supposed to be.

Roughly, then: a locus we treat as what a human being *is*, which has its own persistence conditions, which we locate at a person without locating it anywhere in space, which survives wholesale changes in memory, character and behaviour so long as a story can be told, and which seems to weaken or fail when no story can be told by anyone, including by other people on the person's behalf.

Is there an established concept for this, and if so, which is the nearest one and how does my description differ from it? I have seen the phrase "narrative identity" used loosely and it does not seem to be quite it, since that seems to be about the story a person tells of themselves, and my cases seem to work — or fail — whether or not the person is doing the telling. I also want to know what this thing is *not*: it is not a soul, not psychological continuity of the memory-and-character sort, not a legal person, and not just a set of traits. And I would like the boundary cases adjudicated, particularly this one: if two incompatible but competently maintained stories are told about the same life — by the person and by their family, say, a bit like two long-lived branches of a repository — do we have two of these things, one with priority, or a single one in an inconsistent state, and what would settle it?

I am not after a label to quote. What I want is an account with edges: what conditions a thing of this kind has to meet to persist, what makes it the same one over time, when it stops existing, and whether it can be got wrong about the person it belongs to. If nothing established fits, please say so plainly and give me a definition and contrast cases instead.
