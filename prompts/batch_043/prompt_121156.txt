hey, need a hand with something for a reading group I run — six or seven philosophy undergrads, one paper a fortnight, very informal. We did Taurek last week (Should the Numbers Count, the five-on-one-side-of-the-sinking-ship-or-one-on-the-other case) and one of my housemates, who's training as a paramedic, basically rolled his eyes at the whole discussion. His line was: look, this isn't a puzzle, we do this every day. Triage is a solved problem, triage counts people, and anyone who thinks you should toss a coin to decide who gets the ambulance would last about a week in the job. He was clearly using it as a knock-down: if your theory can't be operationalised by an actual first responder, the theory is broken, and the numbers obviously count.

I want to take the analogy seriously instead of just saying philosophy isn't medicine, so: audit it. Give me the correspondence table first — who plays the rescuer, who are the six, what corresponds to the scarce resource, the clock, the protocol, the fact that you'll meet more patients next week. Then where it breaks. The ones I can see but can't rank: in Taurek's case there's no scarcity of means (either group is saveable and saving the five costs nobody anything extra), the harm is all-or-nothing death for everyone involved, you know all the relevant facts, and there's no queue of other people you'll be responsible for afterwards — triage has every one of those. So is what triage counts actually a headcount, or is it counting something Taurek's set-up doesn't even contain? He also mentioned the four tag colours — walking wounded, can wait, needs help now, and the ones they write off — and said that in a mass-casualty incident you're supposed to leave the black tags, and that military triage sometimes treats the less badly hurt people first to get them back on the line. If even roughly right, that looks like the thing being maximised isn't lives at all, and I can't tell whether that helps Taurek or sinks him.

Two more things I got stuck on and want your honest read of, because I think they're where the real work is. First, the coin flip. Taurek likes a coin flip, and a coin gives *everyone* a 50% survival chance — but there are other ways to randomise: draw one of the six names and save the group that person is in (the five each get 5/6, the lone one 1/6), or draw one name and save only that person (1/6 each, four lives just thrown away). Those three procedures are doing really different things to the lone guy, and I don't know which one "no one has a stronger claim" is meant to recommend, or whether the choice between them *is* the actual disagreement rather than the numbers question. Second: what if you face the same five-vs-one choice every weekend for a year, one group at a time, nobody ever knowing about the others? Does the coin-flipper owe an answer for the hundred-odd extra deaths, and does the triage analogy get any stronger once the cases are serial rather than one-off?

So: the mapping, then the disanalogies sorted into ones that matter for his conclusion and ones that are just noise, then whether "the numbers obviously count, and Taurek is unemployable" survives once the disanalogies are on the table. And if triage is the wrong analogy for what's at issue in Taurek, tell me what a better one would be.
