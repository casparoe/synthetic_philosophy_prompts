I'm in the second year of a joint politics and philosophy degree, doing a module on propaganda, truth and democracy, and we spent a couple of seminars on Arendt's essays on truth and lying in politics. I've come away basically convinced by something I've started calling the fragility thesis, and I want it stress-tested rather than confirmed. The thesis, as I hold it: the distinctive damage of organised political lying isn't that it produces false beliefs, and isn't even that it makes people cynical, but that it erodes the shared factual world — the background of taken-for-granted happenings you need in order to orient yourself at all and to disagree with anyone about anything — and that this erosion is specially hard to repair because factual truth, unlike rational or mathematical truth, has nothing compelling about it: it's just what happened, it could always have been otherwise, and that contingency is precisely what gives the liar (the professional image-maker, the regime that rewrites the record) a freedom the truth-teller doesn't have, since the truth-teller is stuck repeating a contingent fact while the liar can invent something tidier. I'd also hold the mechanism claim: that the liar's need for coherence pushes towards whole-systems-of-lies rather than isolated ones, which is why the damage is structural and not just a pile of errors.

My seminar tutor says the whole thing is unfalsifiable, and my housemate who does psychology says it's just motivated reasoning plus a poor epistemology, so I want the strongest version of what would move me. Please give me a map of the thesis's exposure: what would count against it, ranked from fatal down to merely awkward, and — importantly — separated out by which moving part it attacks. I care about the difference between (a) the contingency/liar's-freedom claim, (b) the structural-coherence mechanism, and (c) the end-state claim about the loss of a shared world, and I'd like to know which of those actually carries the thesis. On (c) I've been using three end-states of my own to sharpen it: substitution, where the lie is simply believed; saturation, where people believe nothing and every claim is dismissible as spin; and compartmentalisation, where people still track the facts perfectly well but treat them as factional badges in tribal scorekeeping. Which of those does Arendt's own mechanism predict, what observable difference would tell them apart, and would finding one of them rather than the others count against her or just relocate the problem? I also want two candidate defeaters taken seriously rather than waved off: first, the archival argument — that ubiquitous cheap recording and cross-checking now impose consistency costs on the liar that she didn't face, so the asymmetry she describes may have shrunk or flipped; second, something like an observer problem, where if the shared factual world is really dissolving, it's unclear what standing the theorist has to detect that, and the thesis threatens to saw off the branch it sits on.

What would a sympathetic Arendtian themselves concede as counting against it — not just what a critic would say — as against what they'd rightly dismiss as a change in tempo rather than in kind? Where does the thesis make a prediction its rivals don't (the bias-and-motivated-reasoning story, the indifference-to-truth story, plain old institutional-decay stories), and would a finding that vindicated the rivals actually be incompatible with it? Be honest about the insulation question: is the fragility thesis the sort of thing where any surviving fact-tracking gets explained away as the process not having gone far enough, and if it is insulated by that move, is that a defect here or just what a claim about a gradual structural process looks like? Don't give me a reading list or footnotes — I can find those myself — argue it straight, and if you think I'm wrong about which part is load-bearing, say so.
