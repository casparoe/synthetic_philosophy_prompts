I'm in a small reading group on early modern philosophy of mathematics — four of us, mostly grad students — and we got completely stuck on Hobbes last week, and I've been chewing on it since. Here's the knot as I understand it, so tell me where I've got him wrong: Hobbes is as thoroughgoing a nominalist and empiricist as you like — everything in the understanding starts in sense, universals are just names applied to many particulars, there are no abstract objects — and yet he also thinks geometry is pretty much the one body of knowledge that is genuinely demonstrative, and he wants to export its method into natural philosophy and then into politics. The usual story about how he pulls that off is that geometrical definitions are generative and therefore causal: you define a figure by the motion that produces it, and because the figure is something we ourselves make, we know its cause, and a demonstration that proceeds from such definitions is knowledge in the strict sense — whereas in physics we only ever see effects and have to conjecture backwards to causes.

But me and one of the others made up a little test that makes me think that story is hiding something. Call it the generation-swap test: if the certainty really comes from our producing the object, then handing someone a wrong account of how the figure is generated ought to wreck their grip on the proof; if what's really doing the work is the names and the rules for shuffling them, then a wrong generation plus the right names should be completly harmless. So picture a student who has learned every definition, can recite and re-derive the theorem about a triangle's angles summing to two right angles, but who, when you ask them to produce the figure, joins three dots freehand with a wobbly ruler and describes a circle as what you get by tracing around the bottom of a cup. Do they know the theorem on Hobbes's own terms? Does the wobble matter? Push one step further: no drawn line is perfectly straight and no physical circle really satisfies its definition, so if the construction has to be something we actually do, geometry seems to inherit all the messiness of bodies; and if the construction is something we do only in the understanding, it starts to look like we've quietly climbed back into names and motions of the tongue, at which point nothing separates a genuine demonstration from Hobbes squaring the circle.

What I want is the machinery, not a reading list — I've got the primary texts to hand and don't need any citations. Walk me through what each moving part is doing for him: sense, the mental image or phantasm, the name, the definition as cause, the construction, the demonstration. Which one is actually carrying the weight of necessity, and where is the honest answer that he's equivocating or just doesn't have a stable line? And if you can, say what any of this does to his rhetoric about his civil science proceeding geometrically — is that a real methodological claim, or mostly a boast that he got to keep even after the geometry went wrong on him?
