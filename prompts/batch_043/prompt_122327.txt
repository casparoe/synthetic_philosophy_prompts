I want a two-way case analysis in the philosophy of physics: a methodological principle applied to a real historical episode, and the episode then used to test the principle. The area is the relation between information that is in principle inaccessible and the identification of a theory's ontology. The episode is Einstein's 'hole' argument, invented late in 1913 while he was still defending the non-generally-covariant 'Entwurf' equations with Grossmann, and the route from there back to general covariance and the field equations he presented in November 1915.

The principle I want applied — call it the inaccessibility norm — comes in three readings I want kept apart. (a) No-observer inaccessibility: no observer constructible within the theory could ever be in a position to ascertain the quantity's value. (b) No-difference inaccessibility: the quantity's value makes no difference to anything any such observer could observe. (c) No-determination inaccessibility: the laws together with all accessible data fail to fix the value. The norm says, in some version, that a quantity meeting the relevant condition is not to be counted as part of the theory's ontology, and in its strongest version that questions about its value are not genuine questions.

The setup, as I understand it — correct me only if something here is outright false, and otherwise don't restate it. Take a region of spacetime, the 'hole', containing no matter fields. Because the field equations are generally covariant, if one assignment of the metric to the manifold's points is a solution, so is any smooth redistribution of it that is the identity outside the hole and differs inside. The two assignments agree on every point-coincidence and therefore on everything any observer could in principle register, yet disagree on which point inside the hole carries which metric value. Einstein took this to show that generally covariant equations cannot determine the metric in the hole, and so are inadmissible.

What I want, under the four headings below and in under 950 words total:

1. The mapping. Which of (a), (b), (c) does the hole argument actually establish, and are they separable here? By the lights of each reading, what should Einstein have concluded in 1913 — and would the norm, applied consistently, also have indicted things he wanted to keep: the manifold substantivalism he had effectively inherited from earlier theories, the 'Entwurf' theory's own restricted covariance, or the very idea that spacetime points are individuals with determinate identity? Say what counts as the inaccessible quantity, exactly, and who the observer is supposed to be, since the argument is silent until those are fixed.

2. What the norm cannot explain about the episode. It should not be forced to speak where it is silent. In particular: why Einstein read the argument as a refutation of general covariance rather than of substantivalism; why it took two more years to return to generally covariant equations, given that the considerations which actually moved him were about the Newtonian limit, conservation, and accounting for the Mercury perihelion advance; and the standard objection that general covariance is not itself a physical constraint, since almost any spacetime theory can be rewritten in generally covariant form — so that if the argument retains any force, it must turn on something other than covariance, and the 'inaccessibility' framing may be downstream of a prior verdict about which differences are physical.

3. What the episode does to the norm: confirmation, anomaly, or a case the norm was never designed for? Specifically, is the norm an epistemic principle at all, or a disguised thesis about the individuality of spacetime points? And does it under-determine the moral: does it favor the standard anti-substantivalist conclusion, or does it equally support sophisticated substantivalism and relationism, so that it licenses only the thin claim that one should not multiply undetectable differences?

4. A transfer test. Apply exactly the same three readings to two contemporary cases where the inaccessibility is physical rather than mathematical: the interior of a black hole for observers who stay outside, and whatever lies beyond our cosmological horizon. Do the three readings come apart there in ways they do not in the hole argument — for instance, where each quantity is accessible to some observer but no single observer can access all of them, so that a fourth notion, joint inaccessibility, seems to be needed? Say whether the norm yields the conclusions its friends would want in those cases, and what that tells us about the moral you drew in (3).

Close with a verdict of no more than three sentences: should someone who accepts the inaccessibility norm accept the standard anti-substantivalist moral of the hole argument, and does the episode give anyone who rejects that moral a reason to restrict the norm? No citations or literature survey, please; I know the secondary literature and will supply references myself.
