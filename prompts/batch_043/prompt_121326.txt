I'm in the last year of my philosophy degree, taking a seminar on radical translation and its critics — three weeks on Quine's field linguist and the business about 'gavagai', a week on Davidson's reply — and I'm writing my term paper on whether indeterminacy is a metaphysical thesis or only an epistemological one. I've been working through cases with a friend in the department, writing down my gut reaction on each before I let myself think about theory, and what's come out looks to me like it may not be a pattern at all. I'm not after who said what — I have the reading list and can chase footnotes myself — I want to know whether there is a principle behind my reactions, and what it commits me to.

Here are the verdicts. (a) The standard case: one manual translates the native word as 'rabbit', another as 'undetached rabbit part', another as 'rabbit stage', another as the universal rabbithood. My reaction: there is no fact of the matter which is right — though 'rabbithood' strikes me as a worse manual than the other three in a way I can't explain without simply helping myself to my own ontology. (b) The same set-up and the same behavioural evidence, except that we have implanted electrodes and found a neural detector that fires for whole-rabbit shapes and drives the utterance, with no corresponding detector for parts or stages, and the natives' internal state under a rabbit-presentation and under a rabbit-part-presentation differ as much as they do for any two stimulations you like. My reaction: now it is determinate — the word means rabbit. (c) Two manuals that agree about everything the natives could ever encounter and differ only about what their general terms pick out on the far side of a mountain range that no member of the community will ever cross and no linguist will ever survey. My reaction: there is a fact of the matter here — the stuff over the range either is the same kind of stuff or it isn't — even though nothing could ever settle it; and I notice this sits badly with (a), where rabbits, rabbit-stages and undetached parts are all right there in front of the natives and I still said there was nothing to be right about. (d) Two manuals that differ only about what the natives would say under a stimulation that can be produced, but that destroys their capacity for language the instant it is produced, so the disposition is never manifested and never could be. My reaction: I go back and forth. Some days I say there is a fact, because they have the disposition; other days I say that a disposition no possible evidence could reveal is not the sort of thing a translation manual can be answerable to. (e) A language whose last speaker died two centuries ago, leaving a corpus of a few dozen inscriptions and no bilingual text; two manuals fit the corpus. My reaction: there was a fact about what those speakers meant and it is now irretrievably unknown — which feels different in kind from (a), not merely a different degree of ignorance. (f) Two manuals that fit all possible stimulation-and-assent evidence, one of which makes the natives right about nearly everything in their immediate surroundings while rendering their logic strange (their negation-like particle turns out not to be truth-functional), the other of which preserves their logic and makes them massively wrong about their immediate surroundings. My reaction: the first manual is the right one — but I want to say 'right' in a choose-this-one-and-get-on-with-the-fieldwork sense, and I can't tell whether that is a different thing from being true or the same thing said more honestly.

My own attempt at a principle, which I'm fairly sure is broken: two manuals are genuine rivals, one of which is correct, just in case some possible piece of evidence would discriminate them; where they fit all possible evidence equally there is nothing to be correct about; and where discriminating evidence did or could exist but is unavailable to us, the fact remains and we are merely ignorant of it. That gets (a), (b) and (e) about right, I think, and it gets (c) and possibly (d) wrong — and I can't see how to patch it. The obvious repair, adding a clause that lets facts about what the environment contains settle things even when no evidence could reach them, seems to wreck (a), since that environment contains rabbit-stages and undetached parts as well as rabbits.

So: is there a single principle that delivers all of this, or a small set of candidates? For each candidate I'd like it stated precisely enough that I could apply it myself, with each clause tied to the case that forces it. I'd like its verdicts on three cases I haven't listed: two manuals that fit everything except a single assent-disposition out of millions; two manuals that are discriminable only by an experiment no ethics board would ever approve; and my own case, where I ask whether my word 'rabbit' means rabbit or rabbit-stage and every manual seems to fit me as well as it fits the natives. And I'd like an honest account of which of my verdicts a candidate cannot accommodate, and which you would overturn and why — including the sub-verdict in (a) that 'rabbithood' is somehow worse than the others, and the wobble in (f) about what kind of 'right' I meant.
