Hi — I'm a journalist, I cover science and ideas (mostly magazine, occasionally radio), and effective altruism has been one of my beats for about five years. I've got a shot at a six-month fellowship — part time, I'd still be filing — and I need to turn in a two-page proposal in about ten days. I've got a half-formed idea and I need someone to hammer it into something a panel would actually fund.

The idea, roughly: anyone who writes about this stuff knows the three standard complaints. (1) The institutional or structural critique: EA treats the world as a pile of discrete, tractable giving opportunities and basically takes the background economic and political structure as fixed, so it's blind to systemic change. (2) The earning-to-give problem: the case for taking a high-earning job to fund donations rests on a replaceability claim — if I don't take the job, someone else will, and they'll give less — and the critics say that reasoning takes the labor market as given and can't see the job itself as part of the causal structure you'd want to change. (3) Measurability bias: you fund what you can count, so the whole portfolio skews toward interventions with short, quantifiable, attributable effects and away from anything diffuse, long-run, or structural.

What nobody seems to ask is whether the three compose. My suspicion is they don't — that (1) and (3) are in tension. The structural critique says go fund diffuse systemic work, but picking among systemic bets requires some kind of expected-value comparison, which is exactly the quantifying move that (3) says is corrupted. So the critic who presses both owes us either an account of why the unmeasurable work is better that doesn't smuggle measurement back in, or a reason to stop ranking at all. Maybe that's wrong and they fit together fine. Either way, that's the question I want funded.

Two tools I want to bring to it, which I havent seen put this way and which I think are my actual contribution:

- A universalization test. Some pieces of EA advice have premises that survive everyone acting on them and some don't. Buy a bednet: if everyone did it, the reasoning behind it doesn't wobble much. Take the highest-earning job you can and give the money away: if a lot of people acted on that, the salary gradient, the counterfactual occupant of the job, and maybe the size of the problem your donating against would all shift — which is to say the argument's premises aren't stable under their own adoption. I want to know whether that difference tracks anything real, whether it lines up with the measurable/systemic divide, and whether it's a genuine objection or just an elaborate restatement of a familiar collective-action point. I genuinely don't know, and I'd rather the proposal admit that than bluff.

- Endogenous measurability. Measurability isn't a fixed feature of an intervention; it responds to being selected for. Once something gets funded because its effects are countable, the organizations delivering it get better at producing counts, and the number drifts away from the thing you actually cared about. So "measurable" is partly an artifact of the funding regime. That makes the measurability critique stronger in one way — the bias is self-reinforcing — and weaker in another, since it turns into a generic selection-pressure story rather than something special about EA. I want that tension worked out rather than resolved by assertion.

Method is where I need the most help, because I don't want to propose a literature review and I can't do original empirical work at half time for six months. My instinct is three worked cases, picked to sit at different points on the measurable/systemic axis — something like a direct-delivery intervention with good short-run numbers, a policy or institutional reform campaign, and something long-horizon and diffuse like funding a research field or movement infrastructure — and to run each through the same battery: does the structural critique land here, does replaceability generalize here, what does the universalization test say, is the measurability endogenous. Then ask whether the battery returns the same verdict across cases or just re-describes each one in new vocabulary.
