I sit on the governance committee of a housing cooperative with roughly 2,400 adult members. We are rewriting our rules, and a subgroup has proposed doing away with board elections altogether: every six months, twelve members would be drawn at random from the full membership, each serving a single one-year term and ineligible to be drawn again for five years. No one may put themselves forward. The draw is from everyone.

One of our longest-serving board members objects, and I cannot locate what is right or wrong in his objection. He says elections are the only thing that makes the board answer to us, and that a lottery would seat people who never had to persuade anyone of anything. Several of us pointed out that turnout at our last three board elections was under ten percent, so the authorization he is appealing to is thin. He replied that even a small electorate is a standing threat, and that a board no one can vote out will simply behave differently from one that can. That last claim feels like the part doing the real work for him, but I cannot say what makes it true or false.

The subgroup's answer is a mechanism they call a re-draw. If two hundred members sign within thirty days of a board decision, the decision is set aside and a freshly drawn twelve decide that question again, with no member of the first group eligible. They present this as accountability directed at the decision rather than at the person. It sounds reasonable, and yet it seems to me to put back precisely what the lottery was meant to take out: the second decision will be made under pressure from whoever can organize two hundred signatures, and the people with the strongest stake in a given decision are the people with the strongest reason to organize. So I want to know whether the re-draw really answers his objection, or whether it quietly converts the board back into something that has to be fought over.

A second disagreement, possibly separate. To stop the body from being unrepresentative, the draw would be stratified: a fixed number of seats for members from each of our four income tiers, and a fixed number for each building size. Another member says that once you are choosing the categories you have a quota council rather than a lottery, and that you may as well let each category pick its own person by election. My sense is that someone drawn to fill a seat reserved for an income tier has not been asked to speak for anyone and is answerable to no one in that tier, which is different from being elected by them. But I cannot tell whether that difference matters, or whether his point is precisely that it will not survive contact with how the body actually behaves.

So, concretely: is the objection about elections really an objection about accountability, or about something else, such as competence, or the fact that choosing to stand is itself information about who wants the job and why? Does the re-draw answer it, or does it reintroduce the dynamic the lottery was supposed to remove, and is there a version of it that would not? Does stratification make the body a representative body in disguise, and would that be a problem? I am less interested in whether selection by lot works in general than in what exactly is supposed to be lost when a vote is replaced by a draw, and whether what is lost can be supplied by something bolted onto the draw.
