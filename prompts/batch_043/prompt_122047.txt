I am writing the after-dinner speech for the tenth anniversary of the refugee sponsorship partnership I helped to found in the town where I live, and I would like you to draft it. The setting matters to what the speech can and cannot do, so let me set it out.

The room is a hotel function room with about ninety people in it. Half are volunteers and their families, plus six or seven of the sponsored households, some of whom have been here under two years and whose English is still limited. Also present are the mayor; the county council's cabinet member for communities, who three months ago voted to cap the number of households the county will agree to sponsor; a diarist from the local paper; and a good many of my former colleagues and graduate students from the philosophy faculty, who will know precisely which arguments I am skirting.

The difficulty is this. I did my doctorate under Michael Dummett. Several people in the room have since looked him up, and the diarist has been ringing me about it. I do not want to defend him and I do not want to disown him; I want to say something true. Here is the position as I understand it, and you should take my summary as given rather than as something to correct or supplement. In his book on immigration and refugees he argued that a state is entitled to control its borders and to set a number, and he grounded this in a notion of absorption capacity: a country can take in only so many people in a given period if newcomers are to be integrated rather than left as a permanent separate population, and exceeding that rate harms incomers and hosts alike. But he also held that most actual hostility to immigration in this country is racially motivated, and that anyone who would restrict immigration owes an account of why the objection would apply equally to incomers of any race, religion or origin. And he held that the claim of a refugee is categorically different from, and far stronger than, the claim of someone who simply wants to come.

Four things about the partnership bear on the speech. First, we have always drawn the households we sponsor by lot from the list our partner agency refers to us; we have never selected for likely fit, and we publish each year the number of households we could not take. Second, my own argument against the capacity reasoning, which I want to get across to people who have never met it, is that capacity is not something a town finds but something a town makes: it is a function of how many befrienders, tenancies and language hours we organise, and a community that under-invests and then pleads low capacity is pleading its own stinginess. The speech needs to say how this room could tell the difference between being genuinely at capacity and merely being unwilling. Third, I have seen it suggested that Dummett softened his view late in life; I do not know whether that is right, and I do not want the speech to rest on it either way. Fourth, the cap was voted for on stated grounds of pressure on school places and of the character of the town, and I think those grounds fail his own test.

So the constraints. I will not use absorption capacity as a fig leaf for the cap: if the idea appears at all, it must appear as a constraint on what we can do well rather than as a warrant for refusing, and the gap between the two must be visible without my calling the cabinet member a racist or turning a celebration into a council meeting. I will not say that the sponsored families have enriched us, or that we are the lucky ones: their claim is one of need, allocated by lot, not a favour returned, and gratitude of that kind makes admission a transaction. I will not say that what the town owes is unlimited, because I do not believe that and neither did he; nor will I say that the households we have taken discharge it. I will not imply that the cap does not really matter because we will always find a way round it. I will not describe anyone's journey, invoke providence, or use a bereaved family in the room as an exhibit: several of those present are not religious and at least one lost family members on the route. And I will not close the tension with a formula such as the head says limits and the heart says welcome; the speech has to locate the conflict and then take a side, in terms a hostile reader could quote on Saturday without being able to make me sound like either a nationalist or a sentimentalist.

Practical requirements. Eight to ten minutes spoken. Plain words and short sentences, comprehensible to someone listening in a second language and to a fourteen-year-old at the back. No jargon, no footnotes, no philosopher's name besides his. My former colleagues should be able to tell which objection I am making; the volunteers should not have to. It should end with one piece of concrete business: asking people to sign up as second-year befrienders, since we lose most volunteers after the first year and that is exactly where the integration we talk about comes apart. The ask has to follow from the argument rather than be bolted on at the end.

Please draft the speech, and beneath it add three or four lines noting which sentences you think are most likely to be lifted out of context and what they would look like if they were.
