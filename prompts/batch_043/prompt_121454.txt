I've been reading people -- deflationists, I think they're called -- who say that calling a claim true adds nothing over and above just making the claim, and I want to test that against my own reactions, which don't line up as neatly as I'd like.

(1) Someone says the trains are cancelled; I say "that's true." Thin: I've only repeated them, with a bit of emphasis.
(2) "Everything in the new safety manual is true," said by a colleague who has read only chapter 3. Thin: a compressed way of endorsing many claims she could in principle go through one at a time.
(3) "Everything the inspector testified to was true." Here it seems to me that "true" earns its keep: I can't recite or even paraphrase most of what she said, and I take myself to be saying her words matched how things were.
(4) "The claims in the undeciphered star catalog are true." The same feeling, only stronger: there is nothing here I could unpack even in principle, and yet I'm still saying something about the world.
(5) "It's true that you should return the wallet." Thin: it just restates the "should," and no relation of matching seems to be in play.
(6) "There are truths about the night of the fire that no one will ever establish." Substantive, and it matters to me: the world has a determinate way of being that outruns anything we can check.
(7) "My belief that there was a gas leak was true, and that's why we got out in time." Substantive -- truth looks like it is doing explanatory work -- but it is exactly the sort of case I can unpack, so it sits badly with (1), (2) and (5).

My own stab at a principle: "true" does real work, ascribing a genuine property, exactly when the claim is not one I could produce and endorse myself; in every other use it is a device for echoing, compressing, or underlining. That gets (1) through (6) right and mispredicts (7). Is there a principle that gets all seven, or a repaired version of mine that costs me less than I fear? Please state it precisely, say which case motivates each clause, and be honest about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. Two predictions I'd also like: what it says about (8) "some of what my grandmother told me about the old country was true, and that's why I wasn't shocked when I first went there" -- which I go back and forth on, since she said it in a language I half speak and it seems to explain something -- and about a community whose word for true can be applied only to claims that have already been publicly checked, so that nothing untested can be called true. Are they talking about the same thing I am? Please answer in American English.
