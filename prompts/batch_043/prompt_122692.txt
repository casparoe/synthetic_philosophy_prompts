Something I keep going in circles on, and I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. The evidential vs causal vs functional decision theory argument has been going for roughly fifty years, and the newest turn in it has reproduced the stalemate in miniature: the move that's supposed to rescue evidential decision theory says that in a small world your choice tells you almost nothing about what anyone else does, but in a very large world there are probably lots of agents similar enough to you that your cooperating is strong evidence that they cooperate too, and if the world is big enough that evidential payoff dwarfs the little gain from defecting — so the evidential agent cooperates in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma and gets the twin/copy results that functional decision theory was supposed to have to itself. The pushback I keep running into is that the correlation does all the work and is never earned: either it comes from a common cause you can't influence, in which case treating it as a reason to act is just the tickle defence dressed up, or it's a bare statistical fact about how many agents happen to be running your policy, in which case you aren't cooperating with anyone, you're reading off a population average and calling it agency.

I've started splitting the setups into signal-driven ones, where the evidential link is a real common cause — shared environment, upbringing, culture — and population-driven ones, where there's no causal connection at all and the evidence comes entirely from population size plus your prior over which policies are common. My hypothesis is that people are arguing past each other between those two kinds of case, and that underneath it the fight isn't about decision theory at all: it's a disagreement about which cases count as legitimate data — are setups with near-perfect predictors fair tests of a theory, or pathological ones — sitting on top of a bedrock, unargued-for view about whether a reason to act has to be causal. But its not clear to me that it's that tidy. So: is this a verbal dispute, a clash of standards of evidence, two questions under one heading, or is there a genuine formal or empirical question hiding in it — about what correlation structures and priors are realistic, or whether the theories provably converge on some large class of cases? And whatever you think it is, tell me honestly what would count as progress: a theorem, a criterion for admissible test cases, an experiment about actual cooperation in big anonymous populations, a reframing of the question, or nothing, because the impasse is where it should be.
