I work in infrastructure at a small AI lab and sit in on an alignment reading group. I have no formal philosophy background, so please make the machinery explicit rather than relying on the names of positions I am probably mangling.

Last week the group got onto acausal trade: the case of two agents who can never exchange a signal — because they sit in causally disconnected regions of spacetime, or in different branches, or one is a simulation running inside the other's model of the world — each deciding whether to pay a real cost to benefit the other, on the grounds that the two decisions are correlated not by causation but by something like logical or algorithmic dependence: both agents are, in the relevant sense, computing the same decision procedure, so if I cooperate, a sufficiently similar counterpart cooperates too.

Our most theory-minded member said the whole topic is much less exotic than it sounds, because it is really just program equilibrium. His picture: two players submit programs to a referee instead of choosing actions directly. Each program is handed the other program's source code as input and must output cooperate or defect. The referee runs both once, together, with no communication between them. Because a program can inspect the other's source, it can implement something in the neighbourhood of cooperate if and only if I can verify that the other program cooperates with a program with my source; there are fixed points in which both programs cooperate; and so the prisoner's dilemma is dissolved without any causal influence running between the players. He drew two conclusions. The first is deflationary: the acausal in acausal trade is doing no work, what we have is an ordinary mechanism-design result with a self-referential trick in it, and any decision rule that handles the tournament correctly is simply whatever engineering works — no contested theory of rational choice required. The second is practical and aimed at my team: if we want a future system to be able to trade with us acausally, or to be able to predict that we will cooperate with it, then what matters is not adopting some disputed decision theory but publishing a legible, machine-checkable description of our agent's decision procedure — open weights, a formal specification of the policy, a signed commitment that the deployed artifact hashes to the published spec, that kind of work.

A second member pushed the opposite way. In the tournament, he said, the correlation between the two outputs is thoroughly causal: a referee executes both programs, each program literally reads the other's text, the dependence has an ordinary common cause, and both players are guaranteed to exist, to face the same payoff matrix, and to be written in a notation the other can parse. In real acausal trade — us and a civilization we will never see, or us and a counterpart in another branch — none of that is on offer. So the analogy quietly imports precisely the channel that was supposed to be absent, and it supports neither conclusion.

I find both stories plausible, and I have a budget question hanging on this, so I would like an actual audit rather than a verdict.

Please give me: the mapping stated explicitly, as a table of correspondences if that is clearest — what plays the role of the referee, of the submitted program, of the source inspection, of the payoff matrix, of simultaneity, of the self-referential trick by which each program reasons about itself as seen from outside, and of the fixed point; then a separation of the disanalogies that are load-bearable for his two conclusions from the ones that are noise. In particular I want to know what features of the tournament the analogy smuggles into the target: a trusted executor, a shared syntax, guaranteed existence of the counterparty, symmetry of predictive power, full transparency, common authorship or common code.

I also want three complications addressed, because I suspect they are where the argument actually lives.

First, a variant I invented and cannot evaluate: a one-way tournament. A publishes its source and B can read it; B's source is sealed, and A receives only a signed commitment — a hash of B's program plus a machine-checkable proof that B cooperates with any program that cooperates with it, produced without revealing the source. Does cooperation still get off the ground when legibility runs in one direction only? If it does not, does that show the tournament needs a common cause that genuine acausal trade lacks? And what would that imply about the case I actually care about, where a far more capable system can predict us and we cannot predict it?

Second, existence uncertainty. In the tournament both players certainly exist and certainly face the same game. In the acausal case, whether there is any counterparty facing the corresponding decision is itself unknown, and my paying the cost may be correlated with nothing at all, or with a counterparty that takes the benefit and gives nothing back. Is this a distinct problem, or the same problem in a different costume, and does the tournament picture have any way of representing it?

Third, the practical conclusion on its own terms. Suppose the deflationary story about mechanism design is right, or suppose it is wrong. Either way, is investing in verifiable, published decision-procedure specifications actually justified by acausal-trade considerations, or does that work stand on entirely different and more ordinary grounds — conventional commitment, reputation, contracting with a party that can simulate our source — which would mean the analogy is only incidentally related to why the work is worth funding? I would rather be told that the conclusion is right for the wrong reasons than have it defended where it does not hold.

Finally, if the tournament is the wrong picture, tell me what a better one would be: another engineering object — a published interface contract that a foreign client may or may not implement, a mutually referential treaty, a compiler that inlines its callers — that carries the real structure better. I do not need citations or further reading; I need the argument, and I need to know which parts of my colleague's case are just naming.
