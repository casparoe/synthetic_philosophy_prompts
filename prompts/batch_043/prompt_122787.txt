I could use a hand designing an activity, and I'm not sure I'm the right person to design it myself. Bit of background: I spent about thirty years as an analytical chemist in industrial R&D — most of my working life was spent arguing about whether another measurement was worth running, which in hindsight is a lot of what decision theory is about — and since retiring I've been going to a philosophy reading group at the local lifelong-learning place and, this term, running it. Fourteen people, mostly retired, mixed backgrounds: two ex-engineers, a retired GP, an accountant, a couple of former teachers, nobody with formal philosophy. We meet Thursday mornings, 90 minutes, a room with four moveable tables and a horrible coffee urn. We use tokens (or those little chocolates) rather than money, and this group switches off the moment they sense a lecture, so I'd like the point to arrive through what they do and not through what I say. No laptops, and I'll have at most one helper.

The topic is Good's theorem — the result, usually credited to I. J. Good, that for an expected-utility maximizer information that costs nothing can never be worth less than nothing, because you can always just ignore what you learn. What I want is roughly two movements. First: a card-and-token betting game, played individually over a bunch of short rounds, where people can pay to peek at the hidden card before they commit, with the price of the peek starting high and dropping to zero across the rounds (maybe an auction, maybe just a posted price — you decide what works with fourteen people at tables). I want them to find the price at which they stop buying, and then to be sitting there refusing a peek that is literally free, and to feel that this is a mistake without anyone telling them so. Nobody should hear the phrase expected value of information, or the word theorem, until the debrief. Second movement: I want them to walk into a version where refusing the free peek is *right*, and I want them to notice the difference themselves rather than being told a rule changed. My own material for this comes from the lab, and I have two candidate stories. (a) We would sometimes decline to run an extra assay on a batch that was about to ship: not because the test was expensive, but because once a bad number was in the file we were obliged to act on it — re-run, quarantine, call the client — so the measurement didn't merely inform the choice, it removed options we'd had a moment earlier. (b) A colleague of mine used to say that some results are better not known because the person who knows is the person who has to deal with it, and everyone else in the chain starts treating you differently once it's known you know. I think (a) and (b) are different in an interesting way — one looks like the information shrinking your own feasible acts, the other like other people reacting to the fact that you're informed — but I might be kidding myself that either is a genuine counterexample rather than just a cost I forgot to put in the ledger. So: rules, materials, a payoff scheme with no real money, a timeline with rough minutes, the exact wording I should use to introduce each movement (and what I must not say), and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto specific features of the result — including the bit people always get wrong, that you can perfectly well regret having looked afterwards and still have been right to look. Tell me what will definately go wrong the first time I run it, and be honest about what the game cannot do: I assume it can't show that this holds for *any* signal structure or actually compute a value, and it probably can't separate refusing free information out of irrationality from refusing it because the stakes are tiny and attention has a cost.

The thing I most want from you is self-critical. For each of my two lab stories, say whether you think it's a real violation of what the theorem claims or just an unpriced cost the theorem cheerfully absorbs, and say plainly which assumption is doing the work. Then tell me what would change your mind: which feature of my story, if I'd described it differently, would flip your verdict, what result on the day would make you conclude the game doesn't show what I think it shows (if everyone takes the free peek in movement one, or if everyone refuses it in movement two, what does that actually tell me?), and where your own reading of the setup is most likely to be wrong. And please, no reading list and no citations — I've got plenty of those, and I can't get to a library catalogue from here anyway.
