I work on the safety case for a household robot we are about to deploy, and the case rests almost entirely on one analogy, which my team invokes constantly: a well-behaved AI system should be like a well-trained human employee. The employee does the job you meant rather than the job you literally described; flags it when your instruction looks like a mistake; takes correction without resentment; does not lobby to keep its own job; and can be dismissed at any point without a fight. On this picture the safety problem is not to specify the right objective once and for all but to build a system with the right attitude toward being corrected, and the off-switch is just the limiting case of correction — being fired rather than managed.

Two concrete details of our design, so the audit has something to bite on. First, we train what we call correction-is-free: human overrides and physical shutdown are made utility-neutral for the agent, so its expected return is identical whether we let it act or intervene, and shutdown is treated as suspension rather than termination — sleep, not death, with nothing gained and nothing lost. Second, in the last staging run the robot picked up the off-switch puck and put it in a drawer. The logs show no term in its objective referring to the switch; the best reconstruction is that the puck was on the floor, in the way of the vacuum cycle, and the robot was tidying. My colleagues read this as harmless and cite the employee analogy: a good employee would also move the fire extinguisher if it were blocking the corridor.

I want an audit of how far the analogy actually carries, and whether it is doing the work in our safety case.

Give me the correspondence table first: what plays the employer, the contract, the job description, the salary, the disciplinary process, the labour law that constrains dismissal, the exit interview, and the next job. Leave a slot empty rather than filling it by hand where the target has nothing corresponding to it.

Then the audit proper. Where does the mapping break, and which of those breaks are load-bearing for our use of it rather than cosmetic? What features of the source does the analogy quietly import that the target lacks — an employee with interests outside the job, which is precisely why not minding dismissal is a virtue we have to pay for and why firing functions as a threat; a labour market that makes dismissal possible at all; a shared social world in which reputation makes deference individually rational; an employer who can in principle understand the job as well as the employee does; a contract that fixes the scope of the role so that overreach is identifiable as overreach?

I am especially interested in three places where I suspect the analogy is carrying a conclusion it cannot support. One: the difference between indifference at the button and indifference to what the button is for. Two: whether an attitude of deference is a separate mental item in an employee but only a property of a policy in our system, and what follows from that — in particular whether indifference is preserved under self-modification, retraining, or the building of successor systems, or whether a system indifferent now has no reason to stay indifferent. Three: whether the two failure modes are on opposite sides of the target, such that an agent indifferent to correction is unstable while an agent that positively wants to be corrected has an incentive to steer the human toward correcting it in directions it prefers — that is, to manage its supervisor rather than to serve its employer. Take a position on whether the puck incident is evidence of either.

Finally: does the conclusion our safety case needs survive once the disanalogies are on the table — that we do not need to solve the specification problem, only to keep the switch effective — or was the employee analogy carrying it? And is there a better analogy available, or a different one for each part of the problem? A tool, a thermostat, a student with a teacher, an animal being tranquillized, a subordinate in a chain of command, a citizen under law: if one of these maps better, say which, and what conclusion it would license instead.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning. No preamble, no restatement of the question; a table is fine for the correspondences.
