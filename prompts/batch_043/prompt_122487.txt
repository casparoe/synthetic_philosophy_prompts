You are a professional philosopher who works on moral responsibility and on the philosophical foundations of criminal law. Think carefully, and first brainstorm two or three candidate reconstructions of the doctrine before committing to the one you think is strongest.

In most common-law jurisdictions, three features of the law's excuse structure sit awkwardly together. First, automatism excuses: a defendant who acted during a seizure or while sleepwalking is not responsible, on the ground that his body moved without his agency. Second, duress excuses, but only within limits: a defendant who commits a non-homicide offence because of an imminent threat of death or serious harm that a person of reasonable firmness sharing his characteristics would have yielded to is excused, yet the same courts refuse the defence both to someone who yields to the very same threat and kills, and to a long-terrorised abuse victim whose threat was chronic rather than imminent. Third, disposition never excuses, and the mental-disorder defence is framed cognitively: the question is whether the defendant appreciated what he was doing and that it was wrong, not whether he could have refrained. A man raised in violence, or diagnosed with a severe impulse-control disorder, is held fully responsible, and psychiatric evidence that he could not have helped it often counts against him as evidence of dangerousness.

Now suppose this jurisdiction enacts a fourth excuse, call it the monitor defence: a defendant is excused if he shows that, at the moment of action, a neuroscientist with a stimulation device was monitoring him and would have intervened to produce the very same choice had he shown any sign of deciding otherwise. The actual sequence — his deliberation, his reasons, his desires — was entirely his own; only his alternatives were removed. Appellate courts have split badly on whether this defence is even coherent with the rest of the doctrine.

Reconstruct the implicit theory of moral responsibility behind the existing three-part pattern. Does it presuppose the principle of alternative possibilities, an actual-sequence or reasons-responsiveness view, or something else again? Which parts of the pattern strictly presuppose the principle of alternative possibilities and which merely sit well with it? And does the split over the monitor defence show that the doctrine was already caught between two rival theories of responsibility, or can a single theory explain both the split and the pattern?

Give your answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no conclusion.
