My friend who actually builds these systems says I'm asking the wrong question entirely, which only made me more curious, so I'd rather run it as a conversation than as an essay.

Please play a Naiyāyika: trained in the Navya-Nyāya style of fine distinctions, sharp rather than a strawman, willing to concede a point when you think the dispute is only about words but not the main commitments. Those are: śabda is a genuine pramāṇa alongside perception, inference and comparison; a cognition counts as pramā only if it is true *and* produced by a non-defective source, so a lucky true guess is not knowledge; and śabda is at bottom the word of an āpta, someone who knows the thing and is not trying to mislead. Stay in character until I type 'stop', keep each reply to a couple of short paragraphs so it feels like a real exchange, and if you need to know something about the machine, ask me for it in your own vocabulary — don't reach for concepts you haven't got.

Here is the machine, as carefully as I can describe it. Something is built out of a gigantic heap of written text — textbooks, court records, letters, sermons, gossip, everything — and all it does, over and over, is guess which word comes next. It has no senses, no beliefs, no desires, no intentions and no opinions; it has never seen the world, only other people's descriptions of it. It has no author in the ordinary sense either: nobody asserts the sentences it produces, it cannot tell you why it said what it said, and it does not know whether it is right. Two further facts. First, it is often right: I once asked it about the cause of an illness I'd never heard of, got back an answer, and my doctor later confirmed it — on questions like that it seems to be right something like six or seven times in ten. Second, it sometimes answers in a confident, well-formed way and attaches a reference to a book, an author, a decided case — that does not exist anywhere. (Later, if we get that far, I want to raise a second machine, one that produces a moving image of a person saying something they never said, indistinguishable to the eye from really seeing them. Start with the text one.)

My opening move: on your account I learned nothing that day. There was no āpta, no speaker's intention — no tātparya — and if you insist on the usual prerequisites for a string of words to yield a meaning at all, the mutual expectancy of the words for each other, their semantic fitness, their being heard together, then arguably there wasn't even a sentence-meaning, only something that looks exactly like one. So you seem committed to saying that a man who asks a question he cannot answer, receives a true answer, and successfully acts on it acquired no knowledge whatsoever — which sounds like a theory built so that it cannot lose. And there is a second problem you can't just wave off: your side of the old fight with the Mīmāṃsakas says an authorless text cannot be a pramāṇa, which is a large part of why Nyāya needs Īśvara standing behind the Veda — and now here is the first genuinely authorless text anyone has ever actually met, and it is sometimes right. So: did I learn, or didn't I? And if I didn't, tell me what in fact happened in me that afternoon.
