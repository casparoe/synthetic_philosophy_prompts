I'm applying for a twelve-month postdoctoral fellowship and need a research proposal of roughly 1,200 words, aimed at a generalist philosophy panel but with a political-theory section on the committee. Please don't supply any references or a literature review — I'll insert citations myself, and I don't want invented ones.

Here is the half-formed idea I need turned into something fundable. When people accuse political opponents of arguing in bad faith, the accusation is almost always read as a claim about sincerity: the opponent does not really believe what she is saying. But I think the accusation is doing several quite different jobs, and that the failure to separate them is a large part of why discussions of polarization, echo chambers, and political distrust go in circles. Provisionally, I want to distinguish:

1. Insincerity proper: asserting what you do not believe, for advantage.
2. Etiological failure: you believe it, and you cite evidence for it, but the belief's actual causal history runs through identity-protective or audience-rewarding mechanisms rather than through that evidence.
3. Asymmetric standards: the evidentiary bar rises only for conclusions that are inconvenient for you, and drops for convenient ones.
4. Misdirected aim: sincere belief, honestly applied standards, but the activity itself is competitive rather than inquisitive — the point is to win, and inquiry is incidental.

My suspicion is that most real-world bad-faith attributions are, on closer inspection, allegations of 3 or 4 being reported in the vocabulary of 1, and that this misdescription has consequences: the remedies people reach for (call out the hypocrisy, expose the other side to better information, insist on sincerity) are aimed at 1, which may be the rarest of the four.

Please develop this into a proposal with the following components.

First, a precise statement of the central question. I want it stated so that it has a determinate answer rather than being a topic. My current attempt is something like: is arguing in bad faith a single graded normative property, or a family of distinct failures with different diagnostics and different remedies — and, if the latter, which of them do echo chambers actually produce? Improve on that if you can, and make explicit what would count as an answer.

Second, why the obvious rival framings leave the question open. Two in particular. (a) The symmetry worry: because both sides accuse each other symmetrically, and each explains the other's accusations as symptoms of the other's bad faith, it is tempting to infer that no such attribution is epistemically respectable — an argument structurally parallel to the symmetry argument about peer disagreement. I think that inference is too quick, since symmetric attribution is perfectly compatible with the four failures being distributed asymmetrically; but I need the point made properly, and I need to say what kind of evidence could in principle break the symmetry. I'd like to use prior statements made under the opposite partisan valence as a kind of revealed baseline, and I want you to pressure-test whether that move can ever be non-question-begging.

Third, the method, as four work packages with rough timing across the twelve months: the taxonomy and its adequacy conditions (the categories must have different observable signatures and different remedies, or they aren't doing any work); the epistemology of attribution, including the symmetry argument and the baseline idea; a treatment of echo chambers that distinguishes their informational mechanism (participants lack corrective evidence) from their incentive mechanism (participants are performing for an audience that rewards certain conclusions), and argues that these call for different institutional responses — I'm attracted to the idea of valence-blind procedures, where standards are fixed before it is known who benefits, and I want you to develop that and to say what its limits are; and finally the normative upshot.
