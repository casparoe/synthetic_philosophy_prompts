I am writing a paper on Carnap’s views on politics and planning. At a workshop on the use of Carnapian framework choice in municipal policy, I raised an objection to a proposed planning tool. The tool, which I will call the Aggregate Wellbeing Framework, represents citizens solely as locations of expected wellbeing and ranks policies by total expected wellbeing. I objected that this is not a neutral piece of conceptual engineering: by building in a maximising aggregative structure, it seems to settle, by stipulation, a substantive moral question about how to weigh one person’s relief against another’s.

A respondent replied with a one-liner often used to express Carnap’s principle of tolerance: logic has no moral requirements. She went on to say that, because adopting a linguistic or conceptual framework is a practical rather than a theoretical decision, my objection confused two levels. Choosing the Aggregate Wellbeing Framework, she said, is like choosing classical rather than intuitionistic logic: it is to be judged by convenience, fruitfulness and the degree of agreement it commands, not by moral rightness. Therefore, my moral criticism of the framework was, in her view, simply misplaced.

I do not think the one-liner settles the matter, but I cannot articulate why. Can you lay out, as a menu, the different precise claims that the one-liner might be making? I want to separate the trivial readings from the substantive, defensible, contentious and false ones. Please include at least the following lines of thought, and add others if they are needed: (i) no particular logic or framework is morally obligatory to accept; (ii) no framework is morally forbidden, so any framework may be adopted; (iii) framework choice is conventional, and moral predicates do not apply to conventions; (iv) practical does not mean non-moral, because a practical decision about which framework to adopt can still be a moral or political decision; (v) moral claims can be made inside an adopted framework, but the framework itself is prior to moral assessment; (vi) questions about adopting a framework are external rather than internal, and external questions are not answerable by moral reasoning; (vii) the moral implications of a planning framework are only internal to that framework, so the framework cannot be morally criticised from outside; (viii) a strong reading on which planners have no moral obligations in choosing indicators is false; (ix) the analogy with logic breaks down because a planning framework such as the Aggregate Wellbeing Framework is not a neutral syntax but a device for distributing scarce public resources.

To make the comparison vivid, suppose the alternative is a Capability-Sufficientarian Framework: it represents citizens as bearers of a right to a sufficient level of thermal comfort, and it forbids trading shortfalls in that sufficiency for aggregate gains elsewhere. For each reading, say whether it really supports the claim that the choice between the Aggregate Wellbeing Framework and the Capability-Sufficientarian Framework is no more a moral matter than the choice between classical and intuitionistic logic.
