I'm a philosophy PhD student (mind and language, but I've been sitting in on the ML lab's reading group for about a year) and I've been drafted to fill a 40-minute slot on the frame problem at their Thursday meeting in a couple of weeks. Audience is maybe fifteen people: mostly first- and second-year ML PhD students, two or three philosophy people like me, nobody will have read anything in advance, there's a whiteboard and I'd rathre not use slides. Don't write the lecture for me — I want the plan. What has to be in place before what, and why that order: what actually depends on what, as opposed to what just makes a nice narrative. My own half-baked way in is a three-way split, and I'd like you to tell me if it's wrong before I build a lecture on it: (1) a specification/formalization problem — writing down what an action changes without writing out roughly M×N sentences about what it doesn't change, which I take to be more or less handled by action schemas and successor-state axioms in the situation calculus; (2) an inference problem — even given a compact description, the agent has to avoid re-deriving or re-checking the whole rest of what it believes to see what survived the action, which is the non-monotonic/circumscription-ish territory; and (3) a relevance problem — which of the indefinitely many things the agent knows bear on what it's doing at all, which I don't think (2) touches and which I think is the bit that makes philosophers reach for Dreyfus and Fodor. If that carve-up is itself a contested way of drawing the lines, say so.

The bit I keep going back and forth on is the example. Every version I have in my notes is the robot-and-the-bomb-in-the-room setup, and I think for this crowd it's actively misleading: the bomb smuggles in stakes, and the story keeps sliding into "how could it have known the wagon wouldn't scratch the floor" (which I take to be the qualification problem) and "pulling the wagon out also moves the battery" (ramification) — neither of which is the thing I'm trying to get them to see. So give me one example to carry the whole forty minutes and be explicit about what it has to have and what it has to lack. My constraints: statable in two sentences, it has to make the "what stays the same?" question bite without leaning on risk or on knock-on causal effects, and it has to survive being re-described in a learned-world-model / next-token-prediction setting, because the second I say "state" someone will say "we don't do symbolic states any more and we're doing fine." Relatedly: where in the sequence do I head off "isn't this just computational complexity / combinatorial explosion" (I don't think it is, but I'm not sure I can say why in one move), where do I head off "deep learning dissolved this," and where do I head off the outcome I'm most worried about — that they walk out thinking the frame problem is just a fancy name for "cognition is hard when you have limited time," at which point it stops being a distinctive problem at all.

Also give me one question to end on that would show me whether they actually got it rather than just being able to repeat a slogan, and tell me what I should openly refuse to get into at this level. Two more things. First: I may have the shooting case wrong — I want to use the one where minimizing changes leaves you with a model in which the gun mysteriously becomes unloaded during the wait and the turkey survives; if I've mangled it, correct me, and tell me whether it even belongs in forty minutes or whether it'll eat the whole slot in the formalism. Second: please seperate, clearly, what I can present as settled among people who actually work on this from what's genuinely contested — I really don't want to stand up and imply, as a matter of record, that Dreyfus or Fodor showed symbolic AI was hopeless, or that circumscription is "the solution," and I'd like to know which of my three labels would get me argued with in the room.
