Hi — I write the public-facing label copy for a small archaeology museum, 60 to 80 words a pop. Over the next few months we're doing three rooms back to back: one on Troy and the late Bronze Age Aegean, one on King Arthur and post-Roman Britain, one on the Exodus and Egypt. Each room needs maybe 25 labels, and basically every one of them forces the same decision: how strongly am I allowed to commit to a claim about something that might be history, might be legend, or might be a story that later grew around a real place, a real ruin, or a real name. My problem isn't ignorance of the material, it's that I have no consistent way of setting the level of commitment, so right now it's my mood on the day, then our curator (great guy, zero theory background, just asks "are we sure?") signs off, then the director reads it and wants to know why the Troy room says "probably" while the Arthur room says "in the story".

So I want a procedure. Something I can run in about ten minutes per label, in a fixed order, with a stopping rule.

The bit I keep tripping over is that three things get collapsed into one and I need the procedure to hold them apart: (a) the thing happened; (b) something happened and people kept talking about it, more or less accurately, for generations before anyone wrote it down; (c) somebody composed a story, and the story then got attached to a real hill, a real destruction layer, a real name in an inscription. Some specific traps the procedure has to handle rather than hand-wave:

- The anchoring trap: a story is set at a site we've actually dug, and there's a destruction layer roughly in the right century, or the hero's name shows up in an inscription more or less contemporary with the events. I suspect that tells me something about what the storytellers needed, not necessarily about the event, but I can't say when.
- Silence: no Egyptian record of the Israelites, no contemporary source naming Arthur. When is an absence allowed to count and when is it just an absence?
- Awkward or embarrassing details: the move where you say the tellers would never have invented this. And the countermove, that a tradition can manufacture awkwardness precisely to feel authentic. Also doublets — two variant versions of what looks like one episode — do they count for or against?
- Independent attestation: two sources saying the same thing, and the question of when they're actually independent.
- Majority expert consensus against something a large chunk of our visitors are dead certain is true.
- The religious case vs the secular case: museum policy is we never say a treasured story is false, but we also can't write labels implying historians back it when they don't. So somewhere in here there's a line between what evidence can warrant and what the story means to people, and I need the procedure to stop at that line and hand it to a human.

Deliverable: a small ladder of commitment levels, maybe four to six rungs, with canned phrasings for each rung in plain visitor English; the tests, in order, that sort a claim onto a rung; where the procedure just stops with no; and where it has to kick the decision to a person because it's a values question. Be explicit about which distinctions survive being simplified for someone with no theory and which you're dropping and why.

Then run it on three of my actual drafts. Don't spare my feelings on the first one, I really like that line and I'll defend it if it deserves defending:

1. Troy: "The Trojan War was real — the Greeks really did sail against this city and burn it, just as Homer says."
2. Arthur: "Behind the knights and the round table there was probably a real Roman-British war leader, though we'll never know his name." The local tourism people love this one.
3. Exodus: a label where the board wants nothing that "denies anyone's faith", and my current draft opens "there is no evidence that any of this happened".

For each: show the tests actually firing, give me the resulting label in the house voice, and tell me which rung it lands on and which single test decided it.

Finally, be honest about where this thing will misfire: what kind of claim it systematically over-hedges or under-hedges, whether it does anything useful when every source we have is literary, and what I should do on the days when my curator and I disagree about a rung. No bibliography, no citations, I don't care who originated which criterion — I need to be able to run it on a Tuesday afternoon.
