I'm a journalist who mostly covers science and ideas, and I'm noodling on a retro piece about the Sokal hoax more than a quarter-century later. I was arguing with some friends — one did a philosophy minor, another is a physics guy — about whether the whole thing is really a story about expertise and peer review, or whether its more of a media story about how academic disputes turn into public spectacle. That got me wondering about using Søren Kierkegaard's extremely grumpy nineteenth-century critique of the press and the public as the lens. I don't need a bibliography or citations; just work with the facts as I give them. Recap: in 1996 Alan Sokal, a physicist, submitted a deliberately bogus article to Social Text, a cultural studies journal, for a special issue on the science wars; the piece was stuffed with fashionable jargon and flattering conclusions, claimed that quantum gravity was basically a social and linguistic construct, and was published without outside expert review; a few weeks after it appeared, Sokal revealed in Lingua Franca that it was all a hoax, and it blew up into a huge public fight about science, the humanities, scholarly standards, and whether Sokal had acted ethically. The Kierkegaard background you'll need: after he was on the recieving end of a Copenhagen satirical paper's attacks (the episode usually called the Corsair affair), he wrote a book in 1846 called Two Ages, and there and elsewhere he attacks the way the newspaper press conjures up something he calls the public — an abstract, leveling, irresponsible crowd in which actual individuals get dissolved into chatter, everyone has an opinion, nobody is accountable, and real ethical or religious seriousness gets lost. I'm especially interested in the way the press for him doesn't just report on a public but helps manufacture it.

So: analyze the Sokal affair through Kierkegaard's critique of the press and the public. I know the standard story is about science wars, peer review, and postmodernism; what I want is a disciplined mapping. Who are the Kierkegaardian actors here? Is the relevant public the readers of Social Text, the academic left, the physics community, the readers of Lingua Franca, or the later broader audience that argued about it? What is the press doing — is Social Text itself an instance of the leveling press, is the hoax an attack on it, or is Lingua Franca and the wider coverage the real press mechanism? Where do Sokal and the Social Text editors stand as single individuals versus functionaries of an abstraction, and what would it even mean on Kierkegaard's terms for one of them to act as an individual rather than as part of the crowd? What are their options and responsibilities, and what does the revelation do to the public it created? Then flip it and ask what the episode does to the theory: does it confirm Kierkegaard's fear that the press manufactures an irresponsible public and turns everything into spectacle — note that Sokal's hoax maybe fed the spectacle even while exposing it — or is it an anomaly, because here the hoax actually revealed something true about scholarly standards, and Kierkegaard's religious-existential framework just doesn't have a vocabulary for peer review, expertise, or institutional epistemology? Be honest about where the Kierkegaardian lens is silent or strains, and tell me whether a different frame, maybe one about gatekeeping or epistemic trust or media incentives, does better on those parts. I'd much rather you say Kierkegaard has nothing to say about X than force the theory to speak.
