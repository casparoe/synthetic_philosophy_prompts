I am trying to work out what I actually think about the KK principle — the thesis that if you know that p then you know that you know that p — by trusting my reactions to cases rather than by trying to settle it from general considerations about safety, margins for error, or the idea that any condition you are in must be distinguishable from nearby conditions in which you are not. Here is where I land, and I would like someone to tell me what principle, if any, these verdicts add up to.

1. Vera sorts day-old chicks by sex for a living, at roughly ninety-four percent accuracy, and cannot say how she does it; when asked whether a given chick is female she reports that she just sees it. Verdict: she knows, and she does not know that she knows.

2. A radiologist reads a scan, can point to the spiculated margin and the architectural distortion, and says she is certain. Verdict: she knows, and she knows that she knows.

3. A student can write out every step of a proof he was made to memorize but has no grasp of why the steps hang together, and says of course he knows the theorem. Verdict: he does not know the theorem, and he does not know that he does not — his confidence is worth nothing as a guide.

4. Marta holds one ticket in a fair million-ticket lottery, drawn tomorrow. Verdict: she knows her ticket will lose, but she does not know that she knows it.

5. Now stipulate a calibration readout: a device that, without telling you whether you were right on any particular occasion, truthfully reports your own hit rate on a task over a specified population of cases. Vera is shown a truthful readout of her ninety-four percent rate on this task under this lighting. Her first-order performance has not changed at all, but I now want to say that she knows that she knows. This is the pair that worries me: Marta and readout-Vera both have an unimpeachable statistical ground for their confidence, yet I want to give second-order knowledge to one and withhold it from the other, and I cannot say cleanly what the difference is meant to be tracking.

6. Same Vera, but the readout comes from a laboratory she has no way of auditing, and a colleague she half-trusts has told her the laboratory overstates accuracy by ten points. On this one I oscillate: some days I say she knows that she knows, some days I say the regress has not bottomed out anywhere and she does not.

7. The radiologist of case 2 is told by a senior colleague she has no reason to distrust that the film is normal. Verdict: she no longer knows, and she does know that she no longer knows — so ignorance can be luminous even when knowledge sometimes is not.

My tentative principle is that you know that you know p just in case you have grounds for thinking your belief in p was formed reliably that are evidentially independent of the grounds on which you believe p. I suspect this is close but not right, and I would like it tested against the cases and repaired. Please do not give me a tour of the literature; argue from the cases themselves.

Commit to a position: give me one principle, defended, rather than a menu of candidates with their trade-offs, and say plainly which of my verdicts has to go and why.

Please answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no concluding summary:

1. The principle, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case you have not seen.
2. Its verdicts on my seven cases, including whichever of my verdicts it overturns, together with the reason for overturning it.
3. The clause that does the work in the Marta and readout-Vera pair, and why that clause is not simply a label for my wanting different answers there.
4. A verdict on case 6, plus one new case of your own construction on which the principle yields a result you yourself find uncomfortable.
5. The residual cost: what the principle cannot explain or must deny, and how much confidence you would put in it, stated as a definite claim rather than as a range of options.
