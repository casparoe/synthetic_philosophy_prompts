I am putting together a proposal for a term paper in a decision theory seminar — roughly 6,000 words, one semester — and I want the proposal itself to do framing work rather than to survey positions. The topic is the Death in Damascus case, in some version like this: a predictor of near-perfect reliability has already gone to whichever of two cities you will be in tomorrow; you know this, you know roughly how the prediction was made, and you must choose between staying and going. The two cities differ in their non-fatal payoffs, so a theory that treats your choice as making no difference to where the predictor already is will send you to the nicer one — while the agent who can predict her own choice, or who knows her own decision procedure well enough to see what it will deliver, will regard that recommendation as something she knows to be bad for her.

My half-formed idea is that the standard debate treats as a single package at least four features that can be varied independently:

1. how reliable the predictor is, and what its reliability is keyed to — perfect prediction, merely very high reliability, or reliability only about agents of your general type;
2. how much self-knowledge the agent has: whether she can predict her own choice, or her own decision procedure's output, as opposed to merely knowing the predictor's track record;
3. whether the agent has available some way of settling the question other than deciding — a coin, a delegate, a policy adopted in advance, a pill that makes her choice opaque to herself;
4. the value structure: whether death is an infinite or merely very large disvalue, and whether the non-fatal goods are asymmetric enough to matter.

The hunch I want turned into a question is that most of what feels paradoxical here is generated by (2) rather than by anything about the correct decision rule, and that if (2) is removed the case collapses into something comparatively dull — whereas treatments that build self-transparency into the set-up make the instability look like a defect of decision rules when it may instead be a fact about what an agent can stably intend when she knows her own procedures. I hold this hunch weakly and I want it stated in the proposal as a hypothesis that could turn out false.

Please write the proposal. I would like it to contain the following.

A precise statement of the question, considerably narrower than how should one decide in Death in Damascus, and phrased so that it admits a determinate answer — ideally something of the form: the distinctive instability of the case depends on X and not on Y, because holding X fixed while varying Y does not remove it, and removing X while holding Y fixed does.

An account of why the obvious candidate answers leave that question open: that the causal-style recommendation is simply correct and the agent's self-knowledge is just bad news she must live with; that evidential or ratificationist approaches dissolve the case by refusing to treat the choice as something to be evaluated against a fixed prediction; that any view on which your choice is evidence about where the predictor already is has the instability written into it from the start. Argue against them, or say what is right in them, without invoking the literature by name.

A method section: what I would actually do across a semester. Which variants of the case I would construct to isolate each of the four features; what I would count as a test of which feature is doing the work; and how I would argue from the behavior of the variants to a conclusion, rather than simply recording which variants feel paradoxical. I am especially unsure how to make the second step non-question-begging, given that intuitions about constructed cases are exactly what is in dispute.

What a positive result would look like; what would count as the project having failed; and which of those two outcomes I should brace for.

An honest scope assessment: where a 6,000-word paper would have to cut, which threads are most likely to expand past what a semester allows, and what minimal version of the project would still be worth writing.

Do not give me a bibliography, a literature review, or citations — I will add those myself, and I want the proposal to rest on the arguments rather than on who has said what.

Finally, one standing request. As you write, keep separate what is genuinely uncontroversial in this area from what is contested among people who work on it: tell me where my hunch runs against a strong current view, where it merely restates something most people already accept in different words, and where the field simply has no settled opinion. Do not smooth contested claims into consensus in order to make the proposal tidier than the subject matter is.
