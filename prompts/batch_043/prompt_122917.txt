I teach an upper-level seminar on probability and induction, and I am writing this year's final paper. Last night I spent three hours arguing with an old friend who builds pricing models for a reinsurance company, and I want to turn that argument into one of the questions.

His position, in summary: the reference class problem is a pseudoproblem that only looks hard because philosophers imagine small data sets. In his world you have millions of records, hundreds of candidate ways of sorting cases, and a cross-validation score. You sort candidates by out-of-sample performance, pick the winning partition, and read the probability for the individual case off the winning cell. If two partitions tie, he says, nothing of consequence hangs on which you use, since by hypothesis they are predictively equivalent. So either there is a fact of the matter about the right class, or there is no fact of the matter and it does not matter.

My position: the tie is exactly where the problem lives. Two partitions can be equally fine-grained, fit the observed frequencies equally well, predict out of sample equally well, and still assign the same individual materially different probabilities of the same outcome; and no further data of the same kind will break the tie, because the data-generating setup is silent about which partition carves the world at its joints. His own tie-breaker is itself a choice among partitions: out-of-sample success is measured by splitting cases into training and test sets, and there are indefinitely many such splits. He replied that this just pushes everything into model selection, which is an engineering problem, not a philosophical one. I think that is the sentence the exam should target.

Please take the role of a professional philosopher of science who has also spent years as an external examiner, and think carefully before you write. Begin with a short brainstorming section (three to five bullet points) on what would make this question diagnostic: what a student who has merely memorised the label would write, where the question could collapse into vague generalities, and where it could become so baroque that nobody can engage it in forty-five minutes. Then give me the deliverables.

Constraints on the question and everything that follows.

1. It is one of three questions in a three-hour paper, so forty-five minutes, worth 20 marks, target answer about 900 to 1000 words.
2. It must be fully self-contained: it defines the reference class problem in its own terms and defines every invented term. A student with no library access and no internet access must be able to answer it, and nothing in the question, the model answer, or the marking scheme should require naming, citing, or attributing any author, paper, or historical episode.
3. Build it around one concrete invented case with explicit numbers. The case should have these features: two partitions of the same population are equally fine-grained; both fit the observed data equally well; both predict out of sample equally well on every split anyone has tried; they yield probabilities for the same outcome for the same individual that differ enough to flip a decision at a stated threshold (something like 0.2 against 0.6, with the threshold set at 0.4); and the partition formed by crossing them is too sparse to yield a usable frequency. Present the two positions in the dispute under neutral labels of your own devising.
4. The crux should be sharper than which class should we use. I want students to confront at least these three things: why the instruction to use all the evidence or the most specific class does not dissolve the problem (maximal specification drives frequencies toward zero or one, so any non-trivial single-case probability requires discarding information, which means the problem is one of relevance, not of scarcity of data); whether a robustness or invariance requirement across all admissible partitions can be satisfied without collapsing into triviality, and what would justify weighting the admissible partitions equally rather than in some other way; and whether the dispute between the two positions is substantive or merely verbal, given that they agree on every observable prediction.
5. Include one further twist that a student cannot have read anywhere, so that the question cannot be answered by recall. Two candidates I would accept, if you can make one of them work better: consider the class of individuals for whom admissible partitions disagree, and ask whether taking that class as a new reference class, or averaging over it, is principled or merely relocates the problem one level up; or suppose the choice of partition must be made before a deadline, and that whichever partition you choose will be revisited later when more data arrive, so that the probability for the same event on the same evidence changes over time, and ask what this does to the idea that there was a correct single-case probability all along.

Deliverables, in this order: your brief brainstorming; the exam question as it will appear on the paper; a model answer of roughly 900 to 1000 words written in the voice of a strong student; and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points with marks that sum to 20, each with a one-line note on how candidates typically get it wrong. Finish with a short paragraph stating what separates an A answer from a B answer, in terms of the specific moves each must contain, and one paragraph listing the warning signs that a candidate is reciting rather than arguing.
