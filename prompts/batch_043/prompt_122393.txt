hi! so in my philosophy club we've done the trolley problem about a hundred times, and I read about this MIT thing called the Moral Machine — an online thing where millions of people clicked through self-driving-car versions of trolley dilemmas (spare the old lady or the baby, the dog or the business guy, one person or five) and it ended up being talked about like it was real data on what the public thinks self-driving cars should do. I want you to analyse that whole episode through the idea of intuition pumps (Dennett's term I think) — thought experiments built so your gut hands down the verdict for you.

I made up a rough test for when a pump is fair to lean on: (a) the feature that flips your judgement in the made-up case has to be the same feature actually doing the work in the real case, and (b) the made-up case isn't allowed to quietly delete a feature that does half the work in real life. In real driving you basically never get a clean one-versus-five — it's tiny risks spread over millions of miles, and the actual engineering choices are things like how late to brake or how far back to sit from the car in front.

So: use my test, or a better one if you've got one, and tell me what the trolley/Moral Machine stuff genuinely explained about the self-driving car debate and what it completely missed or actively distorted. Then flip it round — what does the episode tell us about intuition pumps as a tool, especially once a pump stops being something you argue with in a seminar and turns into a million-person click survey that people then treat as "public opinion" to feed into policy? (I vaguely remember some official ethics report saying you shouldn't programme those trade-offs at all, but I might be misremembering, so don't just take my word for it.) And be honest about where this whole lens just shuts up and has nothing useful to say. Please answer in British English, cheers.
