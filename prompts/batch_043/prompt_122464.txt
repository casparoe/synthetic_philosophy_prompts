i keep reading interpretability papers that say things like "the model represents deceptiveness" or "direction 12,847 in the residual stream codes for the Golden Gate Bridge", and I genuinely can't tell what would make those claims true or false. before I read any more of it I want the spec.

what I need it for: an audit. someone hands me a trained system and a claim that it represents X, and I have to decide whether "it represents X" is the kind of sentence I'm willing to write into a safety case for deployment. so I don't need a theory good enough for a philosophy of mind journal; I need one that tells me what I'd have to establish, and how sure I'd have to be, before a deployment decision rests on it.

so write me the requirements list: what any adequate account of "neural network N represents feature F" has to deliver to be usable for that, with the case behind each requirement. the ones I keep tripping over, in no order, and please add the ones I'm missing:

- it has to not be trivial. every system covaries with something in its environment; a tree ring carries information about rainfall and we don't rush to say it represents rainfall. what does a network have to be doing over and above carrying information?
- it has to rule out gerrymandered features — if I can always find some direction or some disjunctive region of activation space that fits any labelling of my dataset I like, then "represents F" is doing no work.
- it has to say something about the basis problem. there's no privileged basis in a residual stream, so "the feature is a direction" seems to be a fact about coordinates rather than about the network. but people reply that sparsity, or the training dynamics, or the data geometry picks out a basis, and I can't tell if that's an answer or a shrug.
- it has to handle superposition and polysemanticity: one unit, many features; more features than dimensions; and the related granularity mess where widening a dictionary splits one feature into five and I can't tell whether the network has five features or one or whether that question has an answer at all.
- it has to distinguish the network using a feature from a probe reading a feature off it, and it has to survive the awkward cases: you knock out the circuit that supposedly implements F and the model keeps behaving fine because other components compensate; or the feature is causally load-bearing in one context and inert in another.
- it has to give me a standard of misrepresentation. if a representation can be wrong, what makes it wrong for a network — the training distribution, the objective, the designer's intentions, the distal cause that historically shaped the weights? a vision system confidently calls a graffitied stop sign something else; is that a misrepresentation, or just an input the network never represented in the first place?
- it has to handle identity over time and across systems. features seem to rotate and drift across checkpoints and across fine-tuning; models trained on different data seem to converge on similar ones, and people now train a single dictionary across two different models and get shared features. when is it the same feature in two places?
- and it has to handle second-order cases: a network that has a feature for "my F-detector fired", or that reports in natural language that it's thinking about F. is that a representation of F, a representation of its own representing, or neither?

for each one say whether it's non-negotiable or just a preference of some camp, and be honest if a subset of them can't be satisfied together — that's the part I most want, if the list turns out to be inconsistent. also tell me which single requirement knocks out the most popular accounts people actually lean on, and why.

two other things. I keep saying "I don't care about the metaphysics, I just want to know whether the feature is really in there" — if that sentence is incoherent, say so. and my own hunch is that the basis thing already settles the whole debate: no privileged basis, so there are no facts about what a network represents, only features-relative-to-an-interpreter, and the rest is bookkeeping. wasnt sure whether that's a real argument or a non sequitur, so push back on it if it is. skip a long scorecard of specific methods (probes, dictionaries, circuit analysis) unless scoring one of them actually changes what belongs on the list.
