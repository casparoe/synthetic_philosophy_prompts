You are a professional philosopher who has published on the theory of normative reasons and referees regularly for the main metaethics journals. Please treat what follows as a referee report on a position rather than as a request for an introduction: I know the internalism/externalism literature well enough that a summary of it would be wasted words, and I would rather you spend them on the structure of what I have signed up for.

I am drafting the framing chapter of a dissertation on reasons, and a friend who read an early version said something I cannot shake: that my view is a pile of verdicts each of which looks defensible and which cannot all be true together. I cannot locate the fault line myself, so I want an audit, not a defence. Here is where I have landed, roughly in the order in which I acquired the commitments. (1) Reasons are facts: what reasons an agent has is settled by how the world is, not by her beliefs or desires, and a reason is not a mental state of hers. (2) The reachability constraint: a consideration is a reason for an agent only if there is a sound deliberative route from her actual motivational set to her being moved by it. (3) I reject the instrumental principle: sound deliberation need not proceed by showing that acting would promote something the agent already wants; deliberation can be genuinely substantive, and I do not want my internalism to collapse into Humeanism. (4) I accept what I call eclipse immunity: if an agent is in a psychological condition that blocks every currently available route — severe depression, exhaustion, ideological capture — considerations can still be reasons for her, and we can truly say so to her. So the apathetic patient who wants nothing and can now reach nothing still has a reason to take the medication. (5) But I also accept set-respect: the route in (2) must start from the agent's actual motivational set, not from a set I would prefer her to have. So for the contented exploiter — fully informed, internally coherent, procedurally rational, simply lacking any other-regarding concern — I want to say that it is not true that he has a reason to stop, even though I condemn him and would happily say so to his face. (6) I want agents to be fallible about their own reasons: a sincere, informed, reflectively endorsed judgement that one has no reason to act can still be false. (7) And I want the account to earn its keep: claims about reasons should be capable of doing real deliberative and critical work, and I am suspicious of normative facts that could never surface in either activity.

What I want from you: go through these seven and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look that way, ranked by severity, and for each real tension say exactly what would have to be given up and what that costs me elsewhere in the set. I expect that at least one apparent tension dissolves on inspection; if so, identify it and state the distinction that dissolves it, since I would rather you discriminate than flag everything. I also suspect that everything turns on how idealized the sound-deliberation counterfactual in (2) is permitted to be: the more idealized it is, the more easily it delivers (4), but the more it seems to threaten (5) and to hollow out (6). Test that suspicion rather than assuming it, and work out, before you write, how each of (1), (2) and (3) bears on each of the two cases taken separately — I suspect the patient and the exploiter either come apart for a principled reason or collapse into the same verdict, and I cannot tell which. I have no settled preference about which commitment to sacrifice; if one is clearly the cheapest to lose, say so and say what losing it costs.

Keep the whole response under 800 words. Lead with a single sentence giving how many of the tensions are genuine and between which commitments the deepest fault line runs; save your verdict about which commitment I should actually abandon for the final sentence. A compact ranked table is welcome if it saves prose.
