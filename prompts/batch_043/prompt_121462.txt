I have to teach the non-identity problem in a single seventy-five-minute session, and what I want from you is a plan for the explanation, not an explanation of the problem.

The setting: a guest session in a master's seminar on population and public-health ethics. About twenty students, nearly all from epidemiology or health-policy backgrounds, each with at most one previous applied-ethics module. They read fast, they are comfortable with counterfactuals in their own work, and they are likely to file anything that sounds like possible-persons talk under "metaphysics" and disengage. There is a downstream use for the session: in two weeks they submit a short policy brief arguing for or against a preconception health programme (say, lead abatement plus folate supplementation targeted at people who intend to conceive within the year), and I want them to leave able to tell which of their own arguments survive the non-identity argument and which quietly presuppose an identifiable individual who is made worse off.

My candidate carrying case, which I would like you to vet: a fertility clinic switches to a cheaper batch of embryo culture medium. The cheaper medium slightly raises the rate of a lifelong but mild condition — a manageable endocrine disorder needing daily medication — in children born from embryos cultured in it. Because the medium also affects which embryos develop to the transfer stage, the child any given couple takes home will be a different child depending on the medium used. The clinic knows all this and chooses purely on cost. Same number of children either way, each life plainly worth living, no one made worse off relative to their own alternative. The attraction of the case for me is that it has one dial: I can stipulate that the medium affects the condition but *not* which embryo is transferred, which turns the very same story into an ordinary harming case. I want to know whether one case with a switch beats two separate cases for this audience, what exactly the case must have and must lack to do the work, and whether I should close off the escape hatch by which a student says the *parents* are the ones wronged, or leave it open and use it later.

The points I need you to plan around:

1. Dependency structure. What must be in place before the puzzle can even be stated as a puzzle, and in what order? In particular, must the idea that a life can be well worth living while still going badly for its holder be secured *before* I introduce the claim that a different individual would exist otherwise, or can it stay implicit until a student tries to solve the case by comparison? Say which, and say specifically what breaks if I run them in the wrong order.

2. Misconceptions, and where each belongs in the sequence. The ones I predict: that this is a problem about personal identity over time, of the ship-of-Theseus sort; that the gametes were already there so it is the same person; that the upshot is "no one is harmed, so anything goes"; that the upshot is the much stronger claim that we have no duties to future generations whatsoever; that the fix is simply to compare this child's life to the better life they could have had, without noticing that the comparison has no subject; and that the whole thing is a symptom of consequentialism, or equally of rights talk, and dissolves if you pick the other one. Tell me which of these I should pre-empt before they arise, which I should let surface and correct live because the correction does useful work, and at what minute each should be headed off.

3. How much of the solution space to open, given the time, and where: the impersonal comparison, the threshold or non-comparative-harm move, rights to an adequate or open future, and the manoeuvre where the argument is shown to prove too much by being run against a deliberately malicious variant of the same case. Or should the session end without a solution — and if so, how do I keep that from landing as a refusal to answer?

4. My own terminology. I have been using *identity-entangled* for an effect such that, had the act not been performed, the individual who bears the effect would not have existed, so that the individual cannot be held fixed across the counterfactual; and *identity-neutral* for the opposite. Should I hand these labels to the students, and before the case or only after they have tried and failed to name a plaintiff? And do the labels themselves mislead, for instance by making the puzzle look like a problem about causal dependence rather than about the unavailability of a comparison?

5. The check for understanding. My candidate is to have each student take a paragraph of their own draft brief and run what I call a plaintiff check: name the individual who is worse off, and say what would have happened to that very individual otherwise; if no one can be named, mark the argument as identity-entangled and say whether it retains any force. Is this the right test, and what could a student who has not really understood still manage to say in response to it?

6. What to leave out, and say out loud that I am leaving out. I am inclined to refuse the debate over whether an individual's origins are essential to them, the modal metaphysics of merely possible people, the asymmetry in population ethics, and any taxonomy of solution-types. Tell me which of these are unsafe to omit at this level and which refusals are honest.

Please give me the answer as a session plan with rough timings, not as an exposition of the problem.
