I'm teaching the last session of a unit on moral psychology, on the problem of telling a moral conviction apart from a preference or a prejudice that has learned to speak in moral terms. I'd like to close with a pantoum: four quatrains, the second and fourth lines of each becoming the first and third lines of the next, and the closing line returning the poem's opening line.

The reason I want this particular form is that its device *is* the problem. A conviction and a disguised bias share their symptoms: both feel unchosen, both feel as though they bind everyone, both arrive as "that's wrong" rather than as "I don't care for that." So we test for them indirectly:

- the substitution test: hold the conduct fixed, swap who is doing it, and see whether the objection travels with the conduct or with the people;
- the off-switch test: ask what descriptive fact, if it turned out false, would end the objection. A judgment that can name none — or that survives the falsity of every fact it does name, as in the dumbfounding cases where condemnation persists after each of its stated reasons has been defused — looks like a taste wearing the grammar of duty;
- the inheritance test: ask whether you would want someone raised without your history to acquire it, and whether you would be willing to hand it over together with the real reason you have it.

Write the poem so that at least two of its recurring lines can be read both ways: as principled insistence where they first appear, and as distaste, boundary-guarding, or tribal loyalty when they return. The shift should come only from the lines around them or from a change in punctuation, never from a change of wording. Give the speaker something concrete to object to that the speaker themselves concedes hurts no one — a courtyard gathering that runs late, a shop's hours, a marriage, a plan to build — so that the reader can run the substitution test silently while reading.

The final stanza should bring the opening line back, but it should not adjudicate. I want that third hearing to land as a verdict nobody can force: the reader should reach the end not knowing whether what they have just heard is a moral judgment or a preference its holder would be embarrassed to name, and should feel, from the shape of the poem, why the ordinary tests cannot get an answer out of the speaker.
