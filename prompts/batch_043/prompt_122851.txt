You are a professional philosopher with research-level command of formal epistemology, decision theory, and the early twentieth-century debates about probability, risk and uncertainty. Please work carefully and in the following order: first spend some time quietly brainstorming candidate concepts — drawing on economics and statistics and psychology as well as philosophy — then eliminate the ones that don't fit, saying in a line or two why each was rejected, and only then settle on your answer. I have no library access and cannot chase anything up, so please give me no reading lists, bibliographies or citations: I want the conceptual work done in your own words, not pointers to where it might be found.

I am looking for the established name, if there is one, for something I keep noticing — and more than the name, I want the carving: what exactly the phenomenon consists in, what it is not, which nearby notions it gets confused with, and where its edges are. A bare label would not help me; I have a use for the distinction and I need to know how far it extends.

Three instances. Two clinicians, working independently, both put the probability that a patient's symptoms are caused by condition X at sixty per cent. One has seen several hundred such patients and worked through the same panel of tests many times; the other has seen five, has read two case reports, and is going largely on a resemblance to a case she once saw. Both are competent, both say sixty per cent. The first acts on it without fuss. The second is uneasy: at identical numbers, identical stakes and identical payoffs, she prefers the less invasive option even though on her own figures the invasive one is better for the patient. Second instance: an investor sizes two positions identically on the numbers — same probability of gain, same upside, same downside — but stakes less on the one resting on a single conversation with a supplier than on the one resting on twenty years of comparable data, and this is not the stake-size difference you get from risk aversion, since the payoff distributions are the same. Third instance: a committee is told by two advisers that there is a seventy per cent chance a structure will fail within the decade; one adviser has a thousand inspection records, the other a model and three inspections; the committee treats the two seventy per cents very differently.

What makes this hard, for me, is that the difference survives every explanation I have tried. Stipulate that no further evidence can arrive before the decision is made, that neither agent will ever learn whether she was right, and that no one is watching: the difference in willingness to act persists. So it does not look like the value of information, or reputation, or options on future learning. It is also not that the agents have different probabilities — they agree — and it is not that they cannot state a probability.

There is a second thing I associate with it, and I am not sure whether it is the same thing or a companion. The thin sixty per cent is fragile: one further test result moves it to thirty, while the same result moves the substantial sixty per cent to fifty-five. Equal probabilities respond differently to identical news.

Some things I half-remember that may or may not be the same animal: a case about two urns, one of known composition and one of unknown, where people prefer to bet on the known one even though the odds come out the same; cases where people refuse to give a number at all and offer a range instead, or say only *somewhere above half*; and a well-known finding in psychology to the effect that confidence tracks how coherent a story the evidence supports rather than how much evidence there is. If any of these is what I am describing, I would like to be told plainly, and told in what respects the fit is exact and in what respects loose.

Boundary cases I cannot settle on my own. Two agents with the same probability and the same bulk of evidence, where one's evidence is varied — independent sources, different methods, some of it pointing the other way — and the other's is bulky but redundant, all traceable to a single measurement or a shared assumption: my instinct is that the second is in the same position as the thin-evidence clinician, but is that the same dimension or a second one? Symmetrically, evidence that is plentiful but pulls both ways: does that count as a substantial basis or not? And is this property even the sort of thing that admits of more and less in general, or only in some cases?

Finally, the question I most want answered rather than ducked: is the difference I am describing rationally decision-relevant at all, or is it, on a standard Bayesian view of credence and expected utility, mere noise — a feeling a well-trained agent should be rid of? If there is a serious tradition holding that it matters for action and not merely for the description of belief, say what it would have to claim.

If the phenomenon has an established name, give it, define it, and mark its borders against each of its nearest neighbours. If it fits nothing established, or fits several things only partly, say so, and coin a term with a definition, a statement of what it excludes, and a note on whether the two components I describe — the reluctance to act at equal probability and the fragility under new evidence — are one thing or two.
