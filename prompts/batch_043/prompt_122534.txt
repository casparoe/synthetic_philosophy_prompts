I'm putting the final section together for a first-year aesthetics reading packet — the seminar has just finished the unit on the paradox of tragedy, so we've been through the Humean conversion story, make-believe accounts of our emotions toward fiction, and the meta-response family that locates the payoff in our awareness of our own capacity for pity — and I'd rather end with something that makes the students feel how strange these explanations are than with another critique. So I need a piece of sustained satire, and I want the joke to be a real feature of the theories rather than a costume put on them. What I have in mind: a policy white paper issued by an invented government body, something like an Office of Sentimental Yield or a Bureau of Affective Efficiency, mandated to maximise the net aesthetic pleasure the population derives from tragedy while minimising the expenditure of actual suffering that currently goes into producing it. The paper's reasoning should be a straight-faced working-out of what those theories share: that the enjoyed state is not quite the suffering itself but a passion of the same affective material transformed by artistic form, or the appreciative registration of one's own responsiveness, and that on either account the machinery is largely indifferent to whether the object is real — indeed real bereavement is an inefficient input, since it resists formal closure, cannot be replayed, carries beliefs that overwhelm the frame, and is ruinously expensive per unit of pleasure. So the Bureau proposes to replace unmanaged grief with state-commissioned tragedies: licensed mourning slots, deaths pre-composed in consultation with a Bureau dramatist, fees scaled to conversion yield, and a modest schedule of indemnities for citizens who insist on real funerals. I'd like a departmental rivalry running through it — a Conversion Desk and a Meta-Response Directorate fighting over the same budget on the genuine theoretical disagreement about whether eloquence or self-regarding appreciation of one's own pity is the more efficient lever, each proposing a different reform — and I'd like one unflappable annex, cast as a cost table or a note on implementation, dealing with the inconvenient empirical finding that the public persists in grief over actual losses and treats this as though it mattered, which the Bureau classifies as narrative resistance or a market failure to be corrected, never as a reason to stop. If you can find room for it, a line item for a Fictionality Margin that the efficiency targets are steadily eroding would be a nice touch, since that quiet self-undermining is most of what I want students to notice: the more successfully the Bureau optimises, the more it destroys the conditions that made the pleasure possible in the first place.

A few constraints, please. Keep it around 700 to 800 words, laid out in numbered sections with the deadpan register of actual civil-service prose; no winking, no nudging, no moment where the document admits what it is doing, and no named or identifiable living philosophers — the satire should target the shape of the arguments, not their authors. It shouldn't lean on any quotation, citation, or reading list, and it should be funny and legible to a first-year who has read only the one unit while still being recognisable as fair, rather than a strawman, to someone who knows the literature well.
