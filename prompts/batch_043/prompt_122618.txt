I'm in the final year of a philosophy degree, taking a one-semester seminar on intergenerational justice and climate policy. The main assessment is a 6,000-word paper due at the end of term, but first I have to get a two-page proposal signed off by my supervisor, and I'd like you to draft that proposal for me.

Here is my half-formed idea, which I can't yet turn into a question with a determinate answer. The literature seems to treat "our duties to future generations" as a single problem, but I suspect it is two problems that keep being run together. On the one hand there are what I'm calling *delivery duties*: duties to hand over some determinate basket of goods, options, or welfare levels to whoever ends up existing. On the other hand there are *custodial duties*: duties about how we conduct a practice or institution we currently occupy — keeping it intact, passing it on in a condition that its next occupants can themselves take up — which are owed not to any particular future individual but in virtue of the role we now play. My conjecture is that the familiar non-identity objection gets its grip only against delivery duties: if our energy choices determine which future people exist, no one is made worse off by our choosing the high-emissions path, and a duty to deliver welfare to particular people has nothing to latch onto. Custodial duties, if there are any, look immune to that objection, because they were never duties to benefit identified individuals. But they seem to have their own defect, which I'd like to make into the real test of the paper: if custodial duties aren't anchored to anyone's welfare, they may be dischargeable by cheap symbolic acts — declaring a principle, launching a fund that never binds anyone, preserving a name — and then they constrain nothing about emissions at all. Call this the empty-custodianship objection. So the project, as I vaguely see it, is whether a custodial account can be stated strongly enough to survive the empty-custodianship objection while staying clearly distinct from the delivery account whose problems motivated it.

To sharpen the question I've invented a case I want used as the central test. Call it the successor-values case. Suppose we can predict with reasonable confidence that, as a partly foreseen consequence of the economic and educational choices we make now, the generation living eighty years from now will care much less than we do about the natural world — wilderness, species, a stable climate — and much more about goods we now regard as trivial. Assume also that the shift is not a decline in their welfare by their own lights; they are, by their own values, doing very well. Does what we owe that generation require providing what they will actually want, or preserving what they will later wish we had preserved? And does it make a difference that we ourselves caused the shift in their values? I think this case discriminates: a delivery view should say something like "give them what improves their lives by their own lights," while a custodial view should say something like "preserve what a custodian of this practice must preserve," and those come apart here. I also want a stress case where the chain has an end — a population that predictably will have no successors within a few generations — to see what the custodial account says when there is no one to hand anything on to.

Please turn this into a proposal with the following parts, under clear headings and roughly two pages in total.

Give each one or two sentences on where it runs out, not a survey.

4. A week-by-week plan for the remaining twelve weeks, including what I'd actually read or write at each stage and where I'd expect to get stuck.

I take the three live risks to be that the custodial account quietly collapses back into a delivery account, that it survives only by becoming merely expressive, and that the successor-values case turns out not to discriminate between the two accounts after all — but say if you see others.
7. A short closing note on scope: what is most likely to make this too big for a 6,000-word seminar paper, and which part you would cut first.
