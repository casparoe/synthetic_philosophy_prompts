I am finishing a literature-review chapter on whether maximal moral goodness is a desirable ideal, and I have become convinced that the debate that began with Susan Wolf's 1982 paper in the Journal of Philosophy has stalled in a way no one in it seems willing to name. The moves have been repeated for forty years. One side says that a life wholly organized around moral demands would be drained of the non-moral excellences — the aesthetic, the personal, the playful — that help make a life worth having, and that we therefore have reason not to want to be, or to want those we love to be, moral saints. The other side replies either that this only shows how disordered ordinary attachment is, or that the unattractive figure has been misdescribed: build the ideal out of love rather than duty, or out of perfected virtue rather than out of maximizing, and the saint is warm, witty and whole. Everyone has read everyone. Nobody moves. My supervisor's view is that it is simply a standoff between value pluralism and the supremacy of morality, but that strikes me as too quick, since both sides grant that there are non-moral values and both grant that morality matters a great deal.

I do not want a verdict on who is right. I want a diagnosis of what kind of standoff this is, argued from what each side actually commits itself to, not a survey of positions.

The hypothesis I have been working with is that at least three different questions are being asked under one heading, and that no argument can settle the matter while they remain conflated: a question about ideals, that is, about what is properly admired or held up; a question about an agent's all-things-considered reasons for action; and a question about self-cultivation, that is, about what it is sensible to aim at in forming a character, given how agents like us in fact go wrong. Two complications make me doubt that this captures everything.

First, the crowding-out claim. Read modally — moral perfection is by its nature incompatible with the non-moral excellences — it is strong but hard to argue for; read as a contingent psychological generalization about creatures like us, it is plausible but turns the whole debate into an empirical one. Consider the felicitous coincidence case: someone whose deepest non-moral loves, say a scientific or artistic vocation, are also, as it happens, the maximally morally productive things she could be doing, so that nothing has to be sacrificed. Is she a moral saint who retains the non-moral goods? Does her possibility refute the modal claim, or is she simply not a case anyone was ever arguing about?

Second, the operative conception of maximal moral goodness. Understood as maximization of expected moral value, the resulting character looks one way; understood as the perfection of the virtues, where the virtues plausibly include warmth, humour and a proper concern with one's own projects, it looks quite another. Some of the disagreement may be two different imagined persons under a single name.

So: is this a verbal dispute, a set of distinct questions, a clash of bedrock intuitions about the shape of a good life that neither side can argue for, or a substantive disagreement resting on a hidden empirical premise? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a distinction, a reformulation, evidence about the lives of people who actually live under extreme moral demands, or nothing at all? If you think the question is ill-posed, say how, and say what it costs the rest of the literature on moral demandingness.
