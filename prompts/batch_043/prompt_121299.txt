Question 4 (1,200 words maximum). In a recent essay Daniel Dennett argues that the most serious near-term danger from AI is not superintelligent agency but counterfeit people: systems that can pass, indefinitely and undetectably, as conversational partners, while having nothing much of the understanding, agency and history that make a human being a person. Set out that argument as sympathetically as you can, and then do the following. (a) State the objection that Dennett's own position appears to generate: if, as he has long argued, whether something is an intentional system — and, on his account, whether it is a person — is a matter of whether the intentional stance earns its keep in predicting it, then a system that passes as a person just is one, and the word counterfeit marks no real distinction. Say how strong you take this objection to be. (b) Say what, on the best reconstruction you can give, is supposed to distinguish a genuine from a counterfeit person, and whether that distinction is supposed to be visible from the intentional stance alone or only from some other stance. (c) Say what is supposed to be lost if counterfeit people become cheap and plentiful, and explain why Dennett treats this as a threat to a practice or institution rather than as a mere aggregate of individual deceptions. (d) Give one reason to think the distinction survives, and one reason to think it does not.

Submitted answer. Dennett's starting point is the intentional stance: treating a complicated system as if it had beliefs and desires is a prediction strategy, adopted when the physical and design stances are computationally too expensive, and it earns its keep by generating predictions we could not otherwise get. Ascriptions of belief are not discoveries of an inner glow; they are claims about patterns in a system's behaviour that any competent observer could in principle detect. But this does not make them arbitrary. Dennett is explicit that he is a realist of a mild sort about real patterns — a pattern is real if it is genuinely there to be exploited, not merely projected by us. So the objection in (a) is not fatal, though it is not trivial either: what makes something a person, for Dennett, cannot simply be that it fools a user, because fooling a user is a fact about the user rather than about the system.

The distinction between a real and a counterfeit person comes, I think, from his contrast between competence and comprehension. Natural selection produces competence without comprehension, and so, on his account, does much contemporary machine learning: the system can produce the right output without there being anything it understands about why that output is right. A large language model is trained on the recorded output of millions of comprehending human beings, so its apparent understanding is borrowed rather than earned; it is, one might say, a cuckoo chick in a nest built by comprehending hosts. None of this shows up within a single conversation, which is exactly what makes the counterfeit dangerous, and which is why the answer to (b) is negative: the distinction is not visible from the intentional stance at all, only from the design stance, when we look at how the system came to have its capacities.

Notice that this is really Searle's Chinese Room argument restated in Dennett's vocabulary. Syntax is not semantics, and no system that merely manipulates symbols according to rules could ever have genuine intentionality. The room produces Chinese it does not understand; the model produces English it does not understand. The point is the same, and Dennett's contribution is the observation that the systems have now become good enough to fool us in practice.

That matters because, as (c) asks, what is lost is not a series of individual deceptions. Money is the right analogy: a counterfeit bill does not merely cheat one shopkeeper, it degrades an institution that depends on nearly all bills being genuine. Likewise, the practices of testimony, promising, advice, praise and blame presuppose that the entity you are addressing can be held to account — can recognise you as an agent too, and can answer for what it said. Dennett's conditions of personhood (rationality, the applicability of the intentional stance, reciprocity, the capacity for verbal communication, and a kind of self-consciousness) are all relational in this way. Fill the world with entities that can take the role of an interlocutor but cannot occupy it, and the role itself becomes worthless: we can no longer assume, of a stranger's words, that a somebody stands behind them. This is why he thinks the response has to be institutional — requirements of authentication and disclosure for machine-generated text — rather than a matter of individual vigilance.

As for (d): the distinction survives because comprehension is a real property of real mechanisms, and mechanisms can be inspected. The brain is a syntactic engine that becomes a semantic one by being hooked up to the world in the right way, and from the design stance you can tell whether a system has that hook-up or only a statistical shadow of it. It does not survive because once a system's behaviour is indistinguishable from a person's across every probe we can devise, there is, on Dennett's own third-personal methodology, nothing left for the word comprehension to pick out. Heterophenomenology admits no facts about a subject's mental life beyond what can be established from the subject's reports and public behaviour; so either the difference shows up in what the system can be brought to say and do, or it is not a difference Dennett is entitled to. I think he is, in the end, trying to have it both ways, and that the line between counterfeit and genuine holds only for as long as the systems are not yet good enough to make us doubt it.
