A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about whether the shift from foraging to farming was a disaster for the people who lived through it, and we finished exactly where we began, which is what I want to ask about rather than the original question. What troubles me is how much we agreed on. We both accept that early farmers were, on most skeletal measures, shorter and sicker than the foragers around them; that farming eventually supported far more people per square kilometre; that sedentism brought infectious disease, storage, and the possibility of accumulated property; and that the surviving ethnography of twentieth-century foragers is a biased sample, drawn disproportionately from people already pushed onto marginal land. We even accept the point bioarchaeologists make when they warn that skeletons are a selected sample of the dead, so that neither of us can read health straight off a burial. None of that was in dispute for a moment. And yet every fact either of us produced seemed to slide off what the other was actually claiming.

His position was roughly the Diamond one: farming made individual lives worse, in diet, disease, workload, and hierarchy, and it spread because it out-reproduces its rivals, not because anyone was better off under it. Mine was closer to Sahlins: foraging was a genuinely affluent way of life, in the sense of wants satisfied with little effort, and the transition looks like a decline in the texture of an ordinary life that no one chose and that everyone was, over centuries, drawn into. As we got more precise we found a third, separable claim wedged between us, which came in through the argument that inequality does not in fact arrive with the fields: some of the earliest farming communities were strikingly unranked, and some sedentary foragers were not egalitarian at all.

My own diagnosis, which I would rather have tested than confirmed, is that this is not primarily an empirical dispute at all. It is a dispute about which counterfactual comparison is the relevant one, whether the first farmers should be measured against their own forager ancestors, against foragers living at the same time elsewhere, or against a world in which the transition never happened, crossed with a dispute about the currency of evaluation, whether we care about average welfare per life, total welfare, or the sheer number of lives eventually lived at a given standard, which is where I suspect something like the repugnant conclusion is quietly doing the work. Underneath both lies a prior point of my friend's that I think may be genuinely damaging to the whole framing: if in most regions the transition took a millennium or more, proceeded in fits and starts, and was driven by demographic packing and climate rather than by anyone's decision, then calling it a mistake may be a category error, and the honest description might be a ratchet or an escape-proof coordination trap rather than a choice anyone could have made differently.

So I want your diagnosis of the stalemate, not a verdict on the Neolithic. Is my shape for it right, or is there a real empirical question still doing decisive work underneath: about forager workloads, about whether the health decline was universal or regional, about violence and child mortality before farming? Is my friend's ratchet point a dissolution of the original question or only a restatement of it in more flattering vocabulary? And given whatever diagnosis you favour, what would count as progress here, a distinction we are failing to draw, a measurement that would genuinely settle something, or a change of question? Please do not give me a reading list or any citations, since I can look those up myself, and please write in continuous prose, without headings, bullets, bold, or tables, the way you would write a considered letter to someone who has been going round on this for years.
