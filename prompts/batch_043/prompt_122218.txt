im a novelist — third book, out in the spring — and i need a poem off you, but it has to be doing more work than just being atmospheric. quick set-up: it's set on a made-up island off the west coast that i'm calling Sgeir Mhòr, and the narrator finds the notebook of a woman called Ailsa MacKinnon, who lived there most of her life and spent about forty years making a card index of every single object on the island — shingle, fenceposts, her own wellingtons, the gulls, everything, one card each, in a ledger she kept in the bothy. there are nine ravens. and there's one crate, sealed by her uncle in 1963 with a note saying not to open it till the ledger's finished; by the time we meet her the ledger is finished, so whatever's in the crate is literally the last unrecorded object on the island. (it's a pebble, grey granite — but dont say so if it spoils it.) what Ailsa is chewing over in the last pages of the notebook is the sentence "all ravens are black". she's worked out that "nothing non-black is a raven" is just the same claim in different clothes, and she's worked out — this is the bit i want the poem to land — that the pebble is a non-black thing that isn't a raven, so it speaks for the hypothesis, and that its also a non-white thing that isn't a raven, so by exactly the same move it speaks for "all ravens are white" as well. and here's my own small twist on it, which i think is right but tell me if its not: the usual shrug — aye, technically, but the nudge from one non-black object is infinitesimal — only works because the world is enormous and heaving with non-black stuff. on a small island with a finished ledger it isn't infinitesimal at all; that crate doesn't nudge the hypothesis, it settles it. i want the poem to sit with that rather than wriggle out of it.

form: a pantoum, six quatrains, roughly four stresses to a line, loose rhyme if you can manage it but sense first. the rule, in case it helps: each stanza's second and fourth lines come back as the next stanza's first and third, and in the traditional close the final line is the poem's opening line. i like it here because the repetition is never quite a repetition — the same line returns with new neighbours and so it means something slightly different, which is exactly what's happening to the pebble. before you write, please act as though you're both a working poet and a philosopher of science: brainstorm for a short paragraph on which two or three lines ought to be the repeating ones and what the rotation does to them, sanity-check the logic of that double confirmation yourself before you commit it to a line, then give me the finished poem plus a couple of sentences on what you changed in revision. keep it concrete — oilskins, index cards, paraffin, the shingle, the crate, the nine birds — and don't let it become a puzzle-poem with a punchline or a sly little lecture. no footnotes, don't use the word "paradox", and ideally somebody who's never heard of confirmation theory could hear it read at a launch and just enjoy it, while somebody who has can point at the exact couplet where the equivalence and the double confirmation happen. end it with her hand on the crate, or with the crate already open, i don't mind which — but leave it unresolved whether what she's feeling is confirmation or just relief.
