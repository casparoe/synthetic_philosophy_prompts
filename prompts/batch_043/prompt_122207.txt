For the final session of a reading group on forecasting and judgement, I would like a sestina — six six-line stanzas followed by a three-line tornada — spoken by someone four years into a project they had estimated would take two, trying to decide whether to go on. Take the six end-words to be plan, class, average, mine, exception and defer, and let them rotate: the formal conceit is that re-sorting one's own case into a wider reference class recycles the same vocabulary with its meanings shifted, so each word should be doing visibly different grammatical and emotional work every time it returns. Give each stanza one live feature of the inside view / outside view dispute — the causal story that makes the inside view feel compelling; the fact that choosing the reference class is itself a judgement, so that deference to a base rate can become a place to hide rather than a form of modesty; the standing advice not to adjust the base rate for what one knows about the specifics; the regress involved in needing an inside view of one's own reliability in order to know whether to defer at all; and what is left when the case has no decent reference class. Let the tornada gather all six words into three lines and land on the point that the choice of reference class cannot itself be handed over to a reference class. It should be accurate enough that someone who knows the literature can say which of those moves is being made in each stanza, and it should end with the tension open — neither a shrug towards the numbers nor a blessing on the speaker's own judgement.
