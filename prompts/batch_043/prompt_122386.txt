I work as a software engineer on the software an organ procurement organization uses to coordinate donation, and I have gotten pulled into the definition-of-death literature sideways, through the data model. My schemas encode two separate pathways for determining death, one neurological and one circulatory, and the clinical staff treat them as two routes to the same state. Reading around, I find that people have been arguing about whether they really are two routes to one state for about fifty years, and as far as I can tell nobody has moved.

The positions, as I understand them. One side says the brain is the integrator of the organism: when it is destroyed the body is no longer a self-regulating whole but a collection of tissues that happen to be supported by machines, and the circulatory criterion is only a roundabout way of detecting the same underlying event, since stopping circulation is what stops the brain. Critics reply that this is an empirical claim and a false one: there are documented cases of patients declared dead by neurological criteria whose bodies were then maintained for months or even years, with wound healing, temperature regulation and, in one case, the gestation of a fetus. They conclude that the neurological standard is a convenient legal fiction adopted to make organ retrieval possible. A third group says the whole organism-level framing is wrong and the death that matters is the death of the person, the irreversible loss of the capacity for consciousness. A fourth says there is nothing to discover here at all: death is a status we assign, and the only real question is when we are permitted to treat someone as dead.

Three things make me suspect the dispute is not what it looks like. First, in practice we apply the circulatory pathway only after a decision has been made not to attempt resuscitation, so the criterion seems to track a decision rather than a biological event. Second, the literature keeps sliding between two different words, irreversible and permanent, which are not the same thing and which do the same amount of work in the arguments. Third, from where I sit the integrator claim looks like a systems claim of a kind I deal with every day, and there are two very different things it could mean: that the organism is a system with a single coordinating component whose failure is the failure of the system, or that the organism is the pattern of coordination itself, which happens in ordinary circumstances to depend on the brain. Those give opposite verdicts on the long-maintained bodies.

Here is the case I use to test myself, which I have not seen anywhere. Suppose a patient's brain is intact and their body below the neck is failing, and we can keep the brain oxygenated and metabolically supported by an external circuit indefinitely; we already support individual organs this way, so nothing new is required in principle. Now suppose we replace the rest of the organism, organ by organ, with mechanical substitutes, never interrupting cerebral function. At the end there is no heart, no lungs, no circulation in the ordinary sense, and there is a conscious, functioning brain.

My hypothesis is that this is not one debate but three questions under a single heading, one biological, one conceptual about what sort of thing death is a property of, and one practical about what we may do and when, and that each camp thinks it has answered all three. I want a diagnosis, not a verdict. Diagnose the impasse: is it verbal, is it a real disagreement about which cases count as data, is it bedrock disagreement about what the concept is for, is a hidden empirical question carrying the weight, and where does my hypothetical case, or something like it, put pressure on each position. I can tell you what each side argues; tell me what kind of disagreement they are having and, given that, what would actually count as progress, or else argue that no progress should be expected and why. Please do not give me a survey of the positions or a reading list. I have no journal access from where I am writing this, so I need the reasoning rather than the references.
