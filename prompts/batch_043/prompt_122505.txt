So my friend Priya and I got properly into it last night after a screening of Kurosawa's Rashomon (1950) — two hours, a lot of talking over each other, no resolution — and I want a third opinion because I think I'm right and she thinks I'm missing the point of the film. Context: I'm prepping a discussion section built around it for a first-year seminar on how we know anything about the distant past. The seminar does the usual moves: Herodotus passing along variants he doesn't vouch for, Thucydides admitting he can't recall the exact words of the speeches and reconstructing what the occasion called for, the general problem of working with sources that are late, interested, and contradict each other. I want the film to be a worked example of that, not just a mood.

Priya's read: four accounts, mutually incompatible, no authoritative version supplied anywhere, and the framing story at the gate implies everyone is lying to themselves as much as to each other — so it's a relativist parable, and teaching it as source criticism is teaching that source criticism is futile.

My read: it's practically a textbook case of source criticism operating, and the fact that it looks like a relativist parable is exactly the trap. Heres roughly what I argued.

One: the accounts don't actually disagree about the brute facts. A man is dead, in the grove, his wife was there, a bandit was there, there's a horse and a jewelled dagger. What they disagree about is cause and blame — a fair duel, a killing after she taunts them into it, a suicide. That split matters, because it's the split historians actually live inside: you can sometimes nail the what while the why stays contested, and that doesn't make the what unknowable.

Two: the woodcutter gives two accounts, the official one at the trial where he found the body and saw nothing, and the one he tells at the gate where he watched the whole fight. He has a reason to lie in the first one, since he took the dagger. So the film hands you a witness whose later story contradicts his earlier sworn one, and hands you the interest that explains the lie. That's not relativism, that's work.

Three: Priya's answering move is that everyone in the film has an interest, so interest-criticism only ever tells you about the tellers, never about the event. I said that was too fast and made up a rule to fight her with: call it the stubborn detail rule — where two accounts contradict each other about the main event but converge on some incidental detail that no teller has a stake in asserting, that detail is a decent bet to be historical. I'm honestly not sure the film gives me many such details, and I'm not sure the rule survives the husband's testimony at all, which arrives through a medium and can't be cross-examined by anybody, not even the court.

Four, and this is where the argument actually fell apart: the film ends by catching the woodcutter in his lie and then handing him an abandoned baby, and the priest gets his faith in people back. That looks like the film switching the question from can we know what happened to can you be the kind of person who tells the truth. Priya says that supports her. I think it's the film adding something the method talk leaves out, and I can't say exactly what.

What I want from you: read the film through the source-criticism lens and tell me where the reading earns its keep and where I'm forcing it. Specifically — is the stubborn detail rule any good as I've stated it, and does Rashomon support it or break it? How should a source critic treat the medium: is that a source at all, or a dramatisation of what you're stuck doing when the interested party is dead? Is the woodcutter's gate account more credible because it's told in a plainer, less theatrical style, and is trusting plainness just a style prejudice dressed up as a method? Which two or three scenes would you actually put in front of nineteen-year-olds, and what would you want them to notice? And please be straight with me if the film resists me: if it's really a film about self-deception rather than about evidence, say so, and say what I'd be getting wrong by teaching it my way.
