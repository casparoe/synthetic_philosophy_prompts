I am writing a problem set for a graduate seminar on decision theory, and what I want from you is a diagnostic decision tree, not a survey of the literature. The starting commitment is this: the Pasadena game, in which a fair coin is tossed until it lands heads and you receive (−1)^{n−1}·2^n/n if the first heads falls on toss n, has no expected value, since the expectation is the alternating harmonic series, whose positive and negative parts both diverge and which can be rearranged to sum to any real number. Take that as agreed. The question my students have to answer is what, if anything, a decision rule should say about options of this kind, and the tree should take them from the starting commitment to a position.

Build the tree out of the logical space rather than out of a list of doctrines: begin with the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, and only then locate the actual positions among those combinations. I want eight to ten branch nodes and six to eight leaves. Every node has to be phrased as a question someone could answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and it should turn on a concrete case or claim about which the positions genuinely diverge, not on a request for a label.

Five cases must appear as nodes. First: would you pay ninety cents to replace your ticket with one that pays one dollar more in every state? Second: would you pay a positive fee to exchange a standard ticket for one whose payoffs are the same multiset but permuted across toss numbers, and then a further positive fee to exchange it back? Third: a package of one thousand independent Pasadena games, all to be resolved, against a sure payment of six hundred and ninety dollars, which is just under a thousand times what the series sums to in its natural order; say what becomes of any long-run-average defence once the mean is undefined. Fourth: two options driven by independent tossing processes, each of them a Pasadena game, so that the expectation of the difference is a double series that is not absolutely convergent and whose value therefore depends on how the joint states are ordered; what does your rule say about their ranking, and does that verdict move when the order of summation moves? Fifth: the same gamble where the toss number is not shown to you and the states come as an unordered countable partition with no distinguished first, second and third cell, so that the natural order is unavailable; does your verdict survive losing it?

The leaves must include, described structurally rather than by author: silence, on which there is no value and no verdict; ranking by statewise or extended dominance; ranking by the expectation of the difference; assignment of a value by truncation or by a chosen summation method; the bounded-utility dissolution; and some form of permissivism. Split or merge leaves if the logical space demands it. Attach a conventional name to a leaf only where you are confident of it, and mark such names plainly as recollection that may be wrong: I care about the structural claims rather than the attributions, and nothing here will be cited. Then identify at least three combinations of answers the tree allows but that nobody occupies, saying of each whether that is because it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored, and flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate instead of pretending they differ.

Write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way you would write a long email: walk me through the tree node by node in sentences, numbering the questions if that helps and writing the leaves out as prose, with no headings, no bullet points, no boldface and no tables.
