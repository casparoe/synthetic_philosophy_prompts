Bit of context so this doesn't sound like an exam question: my mate Dev is building a startup (I do some backend work for it on the side, in Go, so I inevitably think about all this in terms of state, versions and rolling deploys) that does what he calls living wills 2.0. You record a values snapshot while you're well; the app then keeps computing a drift score, meaning how far now-you has moved from then-you, based on your messages, voice notes, photos, whatever you've hooked up; and there's a chatbot trained on everything you've ever written that can answer, once the written directive runs out, what past-you would probably have said about a decision nobody wrote down. He also wants optional clauses: a sunset clause where the directive dies automatically once the drift score passes a threshold, and a reverse-Ulysses clause where you state in advance that if you spend more than thirty days in a row demanding to be released from the directive, you get released. The plan is for it to end up as a decision aid a clinician or GP can use at the bedside in ten minutes with messy records. I've got no philosophy background beyond a few Radio 4 things, and we had a proper row about it in the pub, so what I want is the spec, not the theory.

So: write me the desiderata that a theory of the earlier self's authority over the later self has to meet to be good enough for that job. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement — I want the motivating case attached to every item, not a list of slogans. The cases I want covered at minimum: my gran, who has vascular dementia, wrote years ago that she wanted to be kept at home and made to do her exercises, and who is now cheerful, watches daytime TV, refuses the exercises, and does seem genuinely happier; Dev's cousin, who is bipolar, pre-committed in a psychiatric directive to being medicated and held during episodes, and who during an episode insists he has never been better and is furious about it; someone whose values really did change in a way they'd defend — a religious conversion, coming out, a political radicalisation — and who now revokes; someone who wrote the directive in a bad patch, depressed or right after a diagnosis or under family pressure, and whose later self repudiates it for that reason; the adaptation worry, where a disabled person reports high wellbeing and refuses treatment the earlier self demanded, and we can't tell growth from settling; the mundane case of the 1 a.m. version of me cancelling the 6 a.m. commitment, which a good theory should scale down to, or explain why it doesn't scale; and the simulator, i.e. whether an answer from the chatbot counts for anything, what happens when the written directive and the simulated past-you disagree, and what happens when the simulated past-you is asked something the real earlier self never considered and confidently answers something absurd. Also: we usually don't know whether the current refusal is competent, and the competence call is itself contested — anosognosia, or a family insisting that a change in someone's values is a symptom — so say what the theory owes us there.

Then tell me which of the requirements are genuinely non-negotiable and which are just preferences of one camp, and be honest about where the list breaks: I specifically want the pairs of desiderata you suspect can't be jointly satisfied (ten-minute bedside decidability versus really tracking deep value change; never overriding a competent current refusal versus making pre-commitment work at all; and so on), and which single requirement does the most to knock out the popular candidate views — roughly, strict current sovereignty, the precedent-autonomy line, psychological-continuity accounts, endorsement or deep-values accounts, and Dev's simulator idea. No reading lists or citations please, I'm not going to chase them up, and if you think one of Dev's features is conceptually confused rather than just risky, say so. Format-wise: "The desiderata" (numbered, each with its case, ~700 words), then "Hard constraints vs. camp preferences" (~200), then "Where the list breaks" (~250, this is the bit I care most about), then a short "Scorecard" (~150, only if you can do it without hedging to death), then "What I'd tell Dev" (~120, bluntly, which features survive).
