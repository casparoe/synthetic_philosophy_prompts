I co-teach a cross-listed upper-level seminar, Agency, Choice and Machine Learning — fourteen students, half from computer science and half from philosophy, ninety minutes, one whiteboard, a projector, no laptops in the room and no robot to borrow. Next week's session is on inverse reinforcement learning and the thought that we might read human preferences off human behaviour, and I want the students to run into the central limitation rather than be told it. Last year I did this as a lecture with a handout and it went badly: everyone agreed in the abstract that behaviour underdetermines what an agent wants, everyone then blamed noise, partial observability or the agent's own imperfect rationality, and nobody felt the underdetermination as a structural fact that more data cannot fix. So I would like a runnable activity — rules, roles or teams, materials, a payoff or scoring scheme — in which the point shows up in what happens in the room. What they should come away seeing is the difference between two kinds of ambiguity, the kind that dissolves when you get more behaviour and the kind that no amount of behaviour, in any environment you rebuild, can dissolve; and then, because the second kind turns out not to be a behavioural ambiguity at all, what the question we actually wanted answered was really asking.

Let me fix the setting so no one can appeal to ignorance, and state the fact the session has to land. Hand out a small grid world — please design it: ten to fourteen squares, deterministic moves, one absorbing goal square, at least two distinct routes from start to goal, at least one square every route passes through, at least one square the agent is never observed in, and somewhere the agent could visibly dawdle or double back. Give teams the agent's complete behaviour, an arrow in every square saying where it goes next, noiseless, with the transition rules and the discount printed on the handout (say, a point tomorrow is worth 0.9 points today, and discounting continues after arrival at the goal). Ask each team to write down what the agent wants as one number per square. The fact they must collide with is this: take any assignment of a number Φ to the squares and change the reward of a move from s to s′ by adding 0.9·Φ(s′) − Φ(s); the total discounted score of every route from a given starting square then shifts by exactly the same amount, so the ranking of routes is untouched and no choice changes — and this holds in the world as given and in any world you rebuild it into by adding or closing paths, and it survives even if you stop assuming perfect optimality and let the agent be a soft, noisy chooser that favours higher-scoring options without always taking the best. (I have a vague memory that the reinforcement-learning literature names this; use the name in your reply to me if you like, but the students will not know it and the session must not depend on it. If your design uses a fixed stopping time instead of discounting after arrival, tell me whether the invariance survives that change.) Your job is to make fourteen people rediscover this by proposing numbers and finding that they disagree while all of them reproduce the arrows: the rival hypotheses must be ones real students would actually volunteer — she wants to get to the café versus she likes the riverside stretch and finds leaving it costly — not numbers gerrymandered to look different, and the disagreement must be verified against the data rather than asserted, so build in a check in which each team confirms its numbers generate the arrows. Then two beats I want engineered by the rules and the scoreboard rather than announced by me. First, a scored prediction round in which the world is altered — a short cut opens, a path closes — and every team's numbers predict the same choice, so the scoreboard ties and the room discovers that a rebuilt world will not separate them either. Second, a divergence beat where the numbers finally do come apart, and note that it cannot be another behavioural prediction, since the first beat has just shown those all agree: it has to be a question of a different kind, such as which square it would be better for her to be placed in, where to put the single bench we can afford, or what she thinks of the square she has never visited.

Please give me the map and the behavioural data as I would photocopy them, the wording of the handout, the team structure and scoring scheme, a timeline with minute marks, the rival hypotheses you expect students to produce with their actual numbers, and a debrief that maps each moment onto a piece of the idea — including the distinction between preference as choice-disposition, preference as what is good for someone, and preference as what someone would choose among options they have never faced, and the point that picking one member of the equivalence class by a simplicity rule is a stipulation and not an inference, especially since which member looks simplest depends on how you write the numbers down. Be honest about what the activity cannot stage and I will have to supply in discussion: the proof itself, the fact that real humans are not optimal and are only partly observed, and the fact that the state space is itself something the modeller chose. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — the team that produces gratuitous numbers nobody would defend, the team that insists we just pick the simplest, the student who blames noise, the one who sees the trick in the first five minutes — and how to handle each. Do not give me a reading list or citations; I have those. And tell me, finally, where the activity risks making the lesson look like a sceptical parlour trick rather than the thing that should worry someone building a system that infers what we want from what we do.
