I hold a chair in philosophy and work chiefly on the ontology of the historical and social sciences. Before moving into philosophy I was trained in applied mathematics and spent several years modelling regime shifts in ecological systems, so I am fluent — notationally as well as intuitively — in that vocabulary: state spaces and trajectories, alternative stable states and their basins, bifurcations and tipping points, critical slowing down and other early-warning signals, timescale separation and quasi-steady states, resilience as distinct from stability, hysteresis, and the problems of partial observability and of choosing one's observables.

I am writing a paper on the concept of collapse, and I want a translation exercise performed on the main readings of the familiar archaeological cases — the eastern Mediterranean at the end of the Late Bronze Age, the Classic Maya polities of the southern lowlands, Rapa Nui. The readings I want translated are these four:

(1) The breakdown reading: a comparatively rapid, substantial and enduring loss of sociopolitical complexity — administrative hierarchy, literacy, monumental construction, specialised craft production, long-distance exchange — accompanied by real costs in population and welfare.

(2) The transformation reading: what looks like an ending from the vantage point of palaces, archives and elites is better described as reorganisation. Largely the same people, often in the same places, continue under a different and in some respects less extractive political form; the entity that ceased to exist was the one the record — and the recorders — were tracking.

(3) The complexity-and-marginal-returns reading: complexity is an investment whose returns diminish, and disintegration is what occurs once the cost of maintaining differentiation exceeds what the differentiation purchases.

(4) The processual reading: collapse is not an event but a process distributed over generations and across regions, so that dating it, or saying whether it happened at all, depends on the temporal and spatial window one selects.

Please restate each of these as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary of dynamical systems, and tell me explicitly what each must stipulate in order to be stated that way: which quantities are the state variables, what counts as an observable and what is merely a proxy, over what timescale the transition is to be measured, and what identifies the system as the same system on both sides of the transition. Where you have had to supply something that the original claim did not contain, say so rather than presenting the result as a neutral translation.

Then give me the residue report, forced by three cases of my own construction:

(a) Prosperous simplification. Administrative hierarchy, literacy, monumental building and long-distance exchange all disappear within three generations, while settlement and skeletal evidence indicate stable or growing population, improved nutrition and reduced violence. In state-space terms this is a large excursion to a new attractor. Tell me what, if anything, in your vocabulary settles whether this is a collapse, and what the honest answer is if nothing does.

(b) Instrument capture. Our proxies for complexity are calibrated on the institutions of the prior regime; when those institutions vanish, the proxies saturate near their floor, so the measured magnitude of the shift is partly an artefact of what we can still see — the silence of the record is generated by the same event it is used to measure. Restate this as an observability problem, and say how much of the appearance of abruptness and depth it can absorb.

(c) Fragmentation without extinction. The polity divides into smaller units that persist, some of them recognisably continuous in population, language and material culture. Whether the larger system "ended" or "continued in another form" seems to require identity conditions for a social entity over time. Say whether that question has any expression in the vocabulary at all.

Finally, and this is what I actually want to know: sort the residue into what fails to survive because the target vocabulary is impoverished — it has no place for agents, for value, for what the change meant to those who lived it, for the identity of a corporate body across time — and what fails to survive because the original claims were less clear than they looked. Is the upshot that "collapse" is a single phenomenon my former colleagues simply lack the resources to describe, or that it has always been a term doing several jobs at once — description, periodisation, and evaluation — so that part of the archaeological dispute is a dispute about which job it ought to do? If I were to defend the second conclusion in print, what is the strongest objection a modeller would make, and how should I answer it?
