Throwaway context: about two months ago I got seconded off my normal job onto a small team helping a transitional justice commission here draft its charging framework for the old regime. Not a lawyer, by the way — I'm the person who reads things and writes memos. The fight on the team is basically: do we prosecute under the penal code that was on the books while the crimes were happening (which mostly covers ordinary murders, tortures, disappearances, but not the systemic part, and not the people who gave the orders from a desk), or do we write new offenses now and apply them to conduct from before. Half the room keeps saying "Nuremberg did exactly that, so we're fine," and the other half says "Nuremberg was victors' justice and a show trial," and I've lost patience with both moves.

So here's what I actually want. Take the Nuremberg and Tokyo tribunals and analyze them through the lens of the legality principle — nullum crimen, nulla poena sine lege, no crime or punishment without pre-existing law. What I want from the lens is a careful mapping, not a verdict.

Some things I need you to keep separate, because I think everyone on my team is collapsing them:

1. Retroactive creation of a new crime (crimes against peace, crimes against humanity).
2. Retroactive removal of a defense that existed in the defendants' own military law — the superior orders point.
3. Retroactive innovation in who gets to try whom at all, i.e. an international court over the heads of a sovereign state, and over heads of state.

I want to know which of those three the legality principle actually bites on, and how hard, because it seems to me (2) might be a different problem from (1) and people lump them together.

Also: one of my colleagues sketched a ladder that we've started using, and I want you to use it rather than your own taxonomy, because I need to see where it breaks. Rung 1: an existing crime, retroactively redefined or re-labeled. Rung 2: a new crime whose underlying conduct was already criminal in the defendant's own domestic system (murder, theft, rape are crimes everywhere) but now charged as something bigger. Rung 3: a new crime covering conduct that was widely condemned morally and politically at the time but not codified as an international offense. Rung 4: a new crime covering conduct nobody had clearly condemned as criminal when it happened. Place the main Nuremberg and Tokyo charge categories on those rungs and tell me if the ladder is doing any real work or if it just restates the conclusion.

And I want a second test run alongside it: the swap test. Would we accept the same doctrine if the other side had won and were prosecuting us? Concretely: the Allies were not going to charge their own area bombing of cities or the atomic bombings as crimes against humanity, and Tokyo left the Emperor alone. Say what the swap test shows — does it show the doctrine is corrupt, or does it show something narrower and more interesting, like that any tribunal is going to be asymmetric in who it can reach and that the legality principle was never designed to fix that?

Then the other direction: what do these two tribunals show about the legality principle itself? Is this a case it confirms (it's a fair-notice principle and the defendants did have notice, in some sense, that what they were doing was monstrous), an anomaly it has to swallow (the one time everyone agrees we should break it), or a case it never anticipated — i.e. evidence that the principle is really about constraining officials and giving citizens a stable planning environment, and simply has no application to people who made the law themselves? (Someone on the team keeps invoking that German postwar writer who said that extremely unjust statutes aren't really law; I want your view on whether that's a way out or a way of changing the subject.)

Please don't give me "the crimes were so heinous that legality had to yield" and don't give me "victors' justice, end of story." I've read both. And where the principle is genuinely silent, say so rather than making it speak. Maybe 900 to 1200 words of analysis.
