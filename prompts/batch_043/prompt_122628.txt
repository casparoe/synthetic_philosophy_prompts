I'm a novelist, and I'm about two-thirds of the way through a book set almost entirely in the lobby, corridors, and linen cupboards of a pensione that has one room for every positive integer. Two characters argue about the infinite across several chapters: the night porter, a retired schoolteacher who has worked out for himself that he does not believe in any completed infinity, and a young guest who has just finished her first term of a mathematics degree and is drunk on it. I want the porter to be wrong in an interesting way rather than in a stupid way — wrong the way a clever autodidact is wrong — and so I have been reading the source he keeps quoting at her, the First Day of Galileo's *Two New Sciences*, in the old Crew and de Salvio translation. I did a little philosophy as an undergraduate, long enough ago that I am embarrassed to say how long, and I retain roughly one idea: that Cantor's reply to all this is to define "same size" as "admits a one-to-one pairing." What I have never understood is how much of the old puzzle that definition actually *settles*, as opposed to merely re-describing it. I'd be grateful for help with the following passage, which is the one my chapter hinges on:

> But if I inquire how many roots there are, it cannot be denied that there are as many as the numbers because every number is the root of some square. This being granted, we must say that there are as many squares as there are numbers because they are just as numerous as their roots, and all the numbers are roots. Yet at the outset we said that there are many more numbers than squares, since the larger portion of them are not squares. Not only so, but the proportionate number of squares diminishes as we pass to larger numbers, Thus up to 100 we have 10 squares, that is, the squares constitute 1/10 part of all the numbers; up to 10000, we find only 1/100 part to be squares; and up to a million only 1/1000 part; on the other hand in an infinite number, if one could conceive of such a thing, he would be forced to admit that there are as many squares as there are numbers taken all together.

> So far as I see we can only infer that the totality of all numbers is infinite, that the number of squares is infinite, and that the number of their roots is infinite; neither is the number of squares less than the totality of all the numbers, nor the latter greater than the former; and finally the attributes "equal", "greater", and "less", are not applicable to infinite, but only to finite, quantities.

My own reading — and I suspect it is the reading my porter would like — is that Galileo noticed that the squares and all the numbers are *literally equal in number*, glimpsed the pairing criterion two and a half centuries before Cantor, and then lost his nerve and concluded instead that infinities cannot be compared at all. I also have the young guest reply that this is a mere historical curiosity, since Cantor later proved that some infinities really are bigger than others, so Galileo's conclusion is simply false. Neither of these can be quite right, and I would like to know what Salviati is actually doing in this passage: is the business about the squares thinning out as you go up (one in ten, one in a hundred, one in a thousand) meant as a *rival* way of measuring size that he then discards, or only as a rhetorical way of sharpening the paradox? And what is he gesturing at with the aside about an infinite number being something he can "only conceive"? Is he denying that a completed infinite multitude makes sense, or only denying that we can compare them?

The part where I have most departed from the texts — and where I need the most help — is the third chapter, where I give the pensione a by-law I invented, which I call the Hundred-Door Rule: a guest may be asked to move at most once a night, and never to a room more than a hundred doors from the one they are in. My porter then claims that under this rule the pensione is full in a sense Hilbert's Hotel is not: you cannot make room for an infinite party of arrivals, and it seems to me, fiddling with it on the back of an envelope, that in fact no more than a hundred-odd rooms can ever be freed at once, because the guests can only shuffle locally and cannot open up gaps faster than they close them. I have two worries. First, is the arithmetic actually right, or have I sneakily assumed something I shouldn't? Second, and more important for the book: does this show anything at all about *size*, or only about what a manager can accomplish in a night? A beta reader told me my rule is just smuggling in extra structure — distance along an infinite corridor — and then blaming cardinality for not respecting it, which struck me as unfair but possibly correct. If it is correct, what is the right vocabulary for the thing my porter is groping toward, and would a genuinely finitist character even want it? Relatedly, when I try to explain the setup to my writing group I hear myself saying things like "an infinite hotel is one that can absorb a new guest even when every room is occupied, and that is what makes it infinite," and then someone muttered that this definition and the ordinary one come apart in the absence of some version of the axiom of choice; I would love to know whether that is true, and, if it is, whether it is the sort of thing I can gesture at in a single sentence without embarrassing myself in print.
