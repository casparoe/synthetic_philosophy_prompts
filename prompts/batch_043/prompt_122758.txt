I'm in a seminar on formal epistemology, and after two weeks on the lottery and preface paradoxes I keep noticing that criminal trials seem to run on the opposite intuition. I'd like to know what that practice implicitly presupposes.

As I understand it: the jury is instructed to break the offence into its elements and to convict only if it is sure of each one, but it then returns a single verdict on the charge as a whole and is never asked the further question whether, taking everything together, it is sure the defendant did what the charge alleges. When a conviction is attacked on appeal as unsupported by the evidence, the court asks whether a rational jury could have found each essential element to that standard rather than whether it could have found the charge as a whole. My professor also mentioned that the jury has to agree on each element but need not agree on the route to it: some jurors can reach the verdict by one theory of the killing and others by a different one, and it still stands. Yet the same courts refuse to convict on bare statistical evidence, even where only two people could possibly have done it and nothing distinguishes them.

So what epistemology of proof does this structure presuppose? Is it committed to treating a charge as a single hypothesis confirmed holistically rather than as a conjunction of independent findings, so that the lottery worry never gets a grip — and if so, is that a strict presupposition of the practice or just one theory that fits it well? Could an atomistic, threshold-on-each-conjunct view explain the same structure instead, perhaps by treating the aggregation question as one the procedure deliberately declines to ask? Does the refusal of bare statistics come from the same commitment or a separate one, and are the two consistent? Where is the practice underdetermined: if a jury is 0.9 confident of each of four independent elements, is convicting a straightforward upshot of the rules as they stand, a misapplication of them, or something the rules are simply silent on? And would anything actually change if the element-wise questions were replaced either by one holistic question about the charge as a whole or by a requirement that the jury certify the conjunction explicitly — or would only our ability to justify the verdicts change, and not the verdicts themselves?
