Some background, because it probably explains the shape of the view. I'm a retired physical chemist, thirty-odd years of reaction kinetics and lasers, and since retiring I've been working though some philosophy on my own with no one to correct me, so I may well be reinventing things badly.

Here's where I've landed. I hit the old sea battle example — a sea battle tomorrow, that whole business — and my instinct as a scientist is that the only honest reading of "there is no fact of the matter yet" is this: a proposition about a specific future event has no truth value now iff the complete physical state of the world right now, together with the laws of nature, does not entail which way it goes. Call it the entailment criterion. Two consequences I'm happy to bite on. First, whether the future is open is an empirical question about the physics of our world, not a conceptual one: if the world turned out deterministic, then every tomorrow-proposition already has a truth value, the intuition that the future is open is really just an intuition about unpredictability, and what people have been reacting to is chaos and coarse-graining. If the laws are irreducibly chancy, then some tomorrow-propositions are genuinely gappy and that's the entire phenomenon. Second, openness comes out as local and patchy rather than a global feature of the future: this event tomorrow is settled, that one isn't, depending on what the dynamics actually does.

I also want to bolt on a second, home-made clause, because I don't like what the bare criterion does to ordinary practice: the clause says that gappy propositions are exactly the ones where the right thing to reason with is the objective chance, and that nothing in our actual practices — assertion, planning, betting, promising, conditional reasoning — needs the gaps filled in. Our practices are bookkeeping on a branching structure and they'd work exactly the same whether or not the branches carry truth values.
