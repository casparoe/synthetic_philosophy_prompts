I'm in eleventh grade and I run a small math-and-philosophy club at my school: about sixteen members aged 16–18, very mixed in confidence with numbers (a few competition-math people, several who actively dislike being asked to calculate), and I have one 75-minute session with no projector and nothing beyond ordinary classroom supplies — printed circles, string, rulers, scissors, tape, pens, and possibly a phone for a random-number app.

I want an activity that makes Bertrand's paradox something they run into rather than hear about. My rough plan: split into three or four groups, give each a different physical randomizer for producing a chord of a printed circle — for instance two spins of a pointer to pick endpoints on the circumference; one spin to pick a radius plus a bead slid uniformly along it with the chord drawn perpendicular through it; something that scatters points inside the disk so they can be used as midpoints — and have each group tally by hand how often the chord comes out longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle, pooling results so the estimates converge near 1/3, 1/2 and 1/4 and the groups then discover they disagree. Then a second phase in which the randomizer is hidden: one team is privately assigned a procedure and everyone else must buy or sell, at a price they name, a bet on whether the next chord will be longer than that side, settled in chips. I'd like the underdetermination to show up as a risk of losing something rather than as a curiosity, and I'd also like a third move where they can bet on — or pay chips to ask a yes/no question about — which procedure is in play, so that whatever they were doing with chords gets done with procedures instead and they can see for themselves whether that solves the problem or only relocates it.

Please design the whole thing to be runnable: exact rules, what each group receives, how many trials and how the tallies get pooled, the pricing and payoff scheme in chips (no real money, and it shouldn't just reward whoever is fastest at arithmetic), timings, and what I say — and don't say — at each stage, since I don't want the phrase principle of indifference, or any distinction between random and arbitrary, to be named before the debrief. Then give me the debrief: which moments I should point back to and what each is meant to show; what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I'm most worried about the two students who already know the paradox, about sample sizes too small for the frequencies to separate, and about the group holding the hidden randomizer leaking or faking it); and honestly, which part of the problem the activity cannot stage. My worry is that because everything in the game is settled by collecting more data, it may teach the opposite lesson — that this is just a sloppily specified experiment — when the interesting version is supposed to be about cases where there's no repeatable setup to gather frequencies from at all. Tell me how to handle that in the last ten minutes, and what to say if someone asks why we don't simply answer that there is no answer.
