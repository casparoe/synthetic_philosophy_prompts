I chair the assessment-accommodations committee at a mid-sized university, and I want to replace the improvisation we currently do with something we can actually run. We sit fortnightly and dispose of thirty to forty requests a term, fifteen minutes a case, and our formal question is narrow: may this student use this external resource during an invigilated closed-book exam in this paper. The people in the room are two rotating faculty members, a disability-services adviser, and a student representative; none of them has philosophy training, the decision has to be written down in language a student can appeal against, and the department that owns the exam has to be able to live with it. A colleague in the philosophy department suggested, after watching us argue about whether a laptop is different in kind from a set of notes, that the question we are really asking is whether the resource is, for this student, part of the mind that sits the exam or merely something that mind uses — and pointed me at the extended-mind literature and its central examples and objections: the man with the notebook, the parity principle, the complaint that mere causal coupling is not constitution, and the worry that on a loose reading of the view every gadget I rely on becomes part of me. I found the literature suggestive and unusable in equal measure. I do not want a summary of it; I want it turned into a procedure.

What I need is an ordered set of questions my committee can put to a request, together with, for each question, what would count as a sufficient answer and what evidence a student can realistically be asked to produce for it — a clinician's letter, a short demonstration at the start of term, a usage log, a statement from a tutor. Build in the conditions that seem to matter and say how each is to be tested in fifteen minutes: whether the resource is reliably and immediately available to the student under exam-hall conditions rather than in principle; whether the student consults it automatically and trusts what it returns rather than checking it against something else each time; whether the information in it is the student's own in the relevant sense, or was generated by a tutor, a classmate, or software; whether the student endorses what it gives them or would disown it if challenged. Include explicit stop rules, distinguishing the failures that end the request there from the ones that merely count against it, and mark the points at which the question stops being one of fact about the student and becomes one about what the examination is supposed to be measuring, at which stage the procedure must hand the decision to the department that set the paper — and tell me how to frame that referral so the department does not simply answer by restating its own no-aids rule. I also need the conditions stated so they cannot be satisfied by arrangements made in the week before the exam. Please make sure the procedure handles a shared resource whose content was produced jointly or by someone else, a resource that performs operations the student cannot check or reproduce, a resource the student depends on but which is only intermittently available, and a student who could still do the work internally but for whom the external route is much less exhausting.

Then run it, at length, on three cases in front of us now. First: a student with a degenerative visual condition who for six years has read and written everything through a screen reader and a refreshable braille display, and who wants the same setup for a three-hour papers exam. Second: a student requesting a single printed sheet of formulae and decision rules, compiled by their tutor, for a statistics exam whose rubric partly rewards knowing which test to apply to which design. Third: a student who wants to dictate answers into a phone and have a grammar tool restructure the sentences, having used that setup daily for two years, in a subject where written expression is itself a graded component. At least one of these, I suspect, will come out differently from how my committee would vote today, and I would rather be told so than flattered: give me the verdict the procedure yields in each case, tell me which of our reflexive approvals it refuses and which of our reflexive refusals it grants, and for whichever result we will resist, argue whether that is a defect in the procedure or a defect in our practice. Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot do: where it must yield to our legal duties and to the department's authority over its own assessment, where it depends on a student's self-report that may be unreliable or strategic, and where no amount of procedure will settle whether unaided recall ought to be part of what a degree certifies.
