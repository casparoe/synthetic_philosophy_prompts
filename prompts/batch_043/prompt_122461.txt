hey — I'm a PhD student trying to finish a chapter on whether (and how) numbers can do any work inside a contractualist framework, and I've got one bit I keep going in circles on. I'd love a gut-level take from outside my own head. Please skip citations, reading lists, footnotes, all that — I have the literature, what I don't have is the conceptual shape of the thing.

Setup as I see it. The individual-based restriction says you compare candidate principles by asking, for each person taken separately, how that principle treats them relative to the alternatives, instead of pooling everyone's costs and benefits into one sum. That's what's meant to block the familiar utilitarian result where a million headaches outweigh one shattered leg. But then plain rescue cases push back hard, and the late-ish Scanlonian fix is to let numbers back in as roughly a tie-breaker among claims of comparable strength, plus something like lexical priority for the stronger individual claim when strengths differ.

Two things I can't get straight.

(1) The creep problem. Suppose I grant that a large enough number of only slightly weaker claims can outweigh a single somewhat stronger one. I take it I have to grant that, because otherwise the view seems committed to ignoring arbitrarily many people whose claims are just barely weaker, which is nuts. But once cross-strength trade-offs are allowed at all, why doesn't the obvious chaining argument go through: ten claims at strength 0.9 outweigh one at strength 1; ten at 0.81 outweigh one at 0.9; ten at 0.73 outweigh one at 0.81; and a few hundred steps later a horde of mosquito bites outweighs a death. The escapes I can see are (a) a hard cut-off — numbers count only inside bands of "roughly equal" strength and never across bands, which looks arbitrary and just relocates the arbitrarily-many-people problem to the band edge; or (b) a "saturation" rule where the first k complainers count and everyone after the k-th adds nothing, which does block the chain but seems like pure stipulation; or (c) swallow the creep and give up the intuitive core of the whole view. Is there a principled option (d)? I keep reaching for something like diminishing marginal weight for additional people — the millionth headache matters less than the tenth — but I can't see what in the reasonable-rejectability machinery would generate a scaled quantity like that, since rejectability looks like an ordering or at best a yes/no relative to a comparison class, not a magnitude you can discount.

(2) A case I made up that seems to jam the machinery: overlapping groups. Standard version: you can rescue A, B, C or rescue D, E, F, G, and the four-person side wins. Now take: you can rescue P, Q, R or rescue P, S, T, U. P is saved either way, so P has no complaint against either principle. If you only compare people whose fate actually differs, you get a straight three-against-three tie; if you count everyone in the affected group, you get four against three. Both ways of setting up the comparison feel available, and the answer flips with your choice. So what is the individual-based comparison even over — the people who fare differently under the principles, or everyone? And note that if it's the former, you can turn any rescue case into a tie by piling enough shared people onto both sides, which is transparently gaming it, but I can't say in contractualist terms why that's illegitimate rather than just stipulating against it.

What I'd find useful: (i) where the creep argument in (1) overreaches, and what the strongest thing a defender woudl actually say in reply; (ii) whether the overlapping-groups case is a real problem or just a relevent-notational artifact, and if it's real, what it shows; (iii) whether you think (1) and (2) are secretly the same problem wearing different clothes. Please don't just walk me back through the standard dialectic — I know it — I want to know what's wrong with my argument, or whether something in it is actually on to something.
