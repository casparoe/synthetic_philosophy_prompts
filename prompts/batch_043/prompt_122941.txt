I co-edit a small bilingual poetry series — facing-page editions, about four titles a year — and the three of us are currently rewriting our guidelines for translators. The sticking point is form. The draft we have says a translator should reproduce the source's formal constraints wherever English permits, and one of my co-editors, who used to sing lieder professionally, wants that sentence cut. His argument is an analogy, and he returns to it every time we meet: a poem is a score, a translation is a performance. A singer transposes Schubert down a semitone when the written key sits badly in her voice; she stretches a tempo, she breathes in a different place, she changes the color of a phrase; and no one thinks she has betrayed the piece, because what a performance answers to is the music, not the key signature printed on the page. Insisting on a key that ruins the voice would be a worse performance, not a more faithful one. English, on his view, is simply a different instrument with a narrower range and a different mechanism for making sound, and he wants the guidelines to license renderings that keep image, cadence, and tone while dropping rhyme, meter, and the alternation of masculine and feminine line-endings. Hofstadter's *Le Ton beau de Marot: In Praise of the Music of Language* (1997) is the best-known statement of the opposite view — that the music is not ornament but the thing itself — and I would rather our guidelines not settle that dispute by a wave at an analogy.

Our standing test case is a stanza I wrote myself for the purpose, so that the constraint set would be unambiguous rather than something we argue about:

J'ai vu passer plus d'une année,
J'attends, du matin jusqu'au soir,
Je suis la chambre condamnée
Où se consume mon espoir;
Je ne suis plus qu'une figure
Sans voix, sans nom et sans couleur;
Il ne reste qu'une blessure
De tout un hiver de douleur.

Eight octosyllabic lines, rhymed ABABCDCD, with the classical French alternation of feminine endings (lines 1, 3, 5, 7 — année, condamnée, figure, blessure, all with a mute final -e) and masculine ones (2, 4, 6, 8 — soir, espoir, couleur, douleur). I have also been pushing a three-way distinction on the board, which nobody has yet found useful: constraint inheritance, where the translation takes on the source's actual constraints (octosyllables, ABABCDCD, feminine/masculine alternation mapped onto English in whatever way works); constraint transposition, where the translation accepts a formal burden of comparable weight but a different shape — a strict syllable count and a fixed stanza with a refrain, say, with rhyme replaced by systematic internal echo, or the feminine/masculine alternation mapped onto alternating stressed and unstressed line-endings; and formal release, where the translator works for image, cadence, and tone and accepts free verse or prose. My suspicion is that the musical analogy, taken seriously, cannot underwrite the third of these, because a transposition preserves the intervals and changes only the absolute pitches, which is transposition in my sense and not release.

What I need from you is an audit. Give me the correspondence table explicitly: what plays the role of the score, the key, the instrument, the singer's range, the tempo, the ornamentation, the audience, and whatever else the analogy needs in order to run. Then tell me where the mapping breaks, and separate the disanalogies that matter for the work he is making the analogy do from the ones that are merely decorative — in particular I want your view on three pressure points: that a score is notation rather than meaning, whereas a poem's words are both at once; that in music the notes just are the form, so that playing the notes is fidelity to form rather than a substitute for it; and that the audience of a performance normally knows the piece and can compare, while the reader of a facing-page edition often cannot, so the translation becomes the only original that reader will ever have. Say plainly whether his conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and which of my three categories the analogy genuinely supports, if it supports any — including the possibility that it supports inheritance rather than release, which would make it an argument against him. Finally, I have been toying with a different analogy, the contrafactum: writing new words to a melody that already exists, where the new text has to satisfy the tune's meter, accent pattern, and phrase structure, as hymn writers do. Tell me whether that is a better analogy for translation under formal constraint than the score-and-performance one, what it would predict about which constraints must be respected and which may be traded away, and what it would miss. Close with a single paragraph of bottom line addressed to my two co-editors: which of the three policies our guidelines should adopt, and whether the score-and-performance analogy can honestly be cited in support of that policy in a document translators will read as binding.
