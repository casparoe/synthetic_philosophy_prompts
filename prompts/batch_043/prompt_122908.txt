I convene a graduate reading group on the metaphysics of levels and implementation, and our next session is on the careenium setup: a large lump of the fictional material careenium, inside which arrangements of simmballs — blobs of simm — can be read as a system of symbols with content, sitting on top of a substrate that is doing whatever physics careenium does. The question the scenario exists to sharpen is who shoves whom: when something happens in there, does the symbol level do any of the shoving, or does only the substrate ever shove anything, with the symbol level merely a way of talking about patterns the substrate makes?

I do not want another tour of the well-known positions. I want to know which of the diagnostic tests people actually appeal to in this dispute carry real weight, and which of them quietly presuppose the answer. Below are eight such tests, stated as neutrally as I can manage.

1. The substitution test. One and the same symbol-level transition — some simmball sentence token producing another — occurs twice, realized on the two occasions by careenium configurations with nothing describable in common at the microphysical level. The same downstream substrate trajectory follows both times.
2. The interpretation-sensitive perturbation test. An intervention that is negligible by any microphysical measure — tiny in energy, unremarkable in the substrate vocabulary — scrambles the coherence of the symbol-level reading, and the system's subsequent behavior then diverges wildly from the unperturbed run.
3. The compression test. A symbol-level model forecasts future careenium states better than any substrate-level model that can actually be written down and run, at equal cost in bits and computation.
4. The homegrown-symbol test. Configurations arise that carry content nobody designed, assigned, or intended; symbols that grew rather than were installed. These reliably steer what the substrate does next.
5. The empty-symbol test. A symbol to which nothing corresponds — a hallucinated or invalid token, the simmball analogue of a term with no referent — nonetheless brings about large downstream physical effects.
6. The bottom-dropping-out test. Every candidate for the role of the substrate is itself an abstraction: the microstructural description of careenium is a high-level pattern over something deeper, and so on down. Whether the phrase the substrate does all the work picks out any determinate level is therefore contested.
7. The impossible-intervention test. Hold the substrate fixed and vary only the symbol. There is no way to run this: varying the symbol just is a substrate variation. Whether the exclusivist is entitled to read that as a win is the point at issue.
8. The vocabulary-deletion test. Strike every symbol-level term from the best available explanation of what the careenium does, and ask whether what remains supports the same counterfactuals at comparable length. If it does not, the symbol level is doing explanatory work nothing else does.

Please give me:

1. A tier list of the eight, in three or four tiers, with the top tier holding the tests with the most genuine bite.
2. A number for each test: its share of a total of 1.0 of dialectical credit, so that the eight numbers sum to one. Say in one sentence why each lands where it does.
3. An identification of at least one test that is question-begging, or that answers a different question than the one we began with, together with a note on which way it begs.
4. A closing paragraph on what would change your mind: name the test or consideration that, if it came out differently — or if I had stated it differently — would reorder your tiers, and say where your ranking is most likely to be an artifact of my framing of the tests rather than a verdict on the dispute itself. If the overall shape of your ranking betrays a prior commitment you hold, say so explicitly.

Aim for under 800 words, and assume I already know the standard arguments for and against the autonomy of the symbol level, so spend your words on the ranking and its defense.
