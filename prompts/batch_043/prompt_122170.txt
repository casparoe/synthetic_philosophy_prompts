Next week I am presenting an independent paper to my school's History and Classics Society, and I would like a Q&A preparation sheet rather than a defence of the view itself.

My thesis, in brief. I argue that what separates a legend from a piece of history is not evidence but a practice I call the error-tolerance test: a narrative is being handled as history when the people who transmit it treat a discrepancy inside it, or between it and a rival account, as a defect to be reconciled, explained away, or mined for information about what actually happened, rather than as a variant to be preserved. On this account legend is not a verdict on truth: a legend can be full of true statements and a history can be false. It follows that the transition is reversible in both directions, since a narrative that a scholarly tradition has treated as history can lapse back into legend if that tradition disappears and its discrepancies stop registering as anything, while a legend can be promoted to history before a single new fact turns up, purely because of a change in how its inconsistencies are handled. I apply the test to three standard cases — Homer's Troy, King Arthur, and the Exodus — and also to a modern one, the American story of Paul Revere's midnight ride as it is taught in schools, where almost nothing is invented but where the other riders and the capture are quietly dropped rather than reconciled, which on my account makes it legend.

The audience. About twenty are students my age who have done a little source criticism and mostly want to know what my test says about the three cases. The rest of the room is what worries me: our classics teacher, who holds that myth is a serious and structured way of saying something true about the world and who resents the way history is used as a compliment and myth as an insult; a parent who is a field archaeologist and thinks that where there is no material corroboration there is no history at all, and who will argue from the excavated mound in north-west Turkey that dirt and not texts is what turned Troy into a question; and one classmate who is observant and who hears any claim about the Exodus as a claim about her own tradition, and who will want to know whether my test is a neutral instrument or one that simply awards the title of history to narratives belonging to cultures that happen to have universities.

What I want from you is exactly five numbered items and nothing else — no introduction, no concluding advice. Each item is two sentences: the first is the objection as that particular person would actually phrase it, in their own vocabulary and with their own commitments showing; the second is what I should say back, ending with a note on where my reply is weakest. Order the five by how much damage the objection would do to my thesis if I had no answer to it, and make sure the list includes the objection I am quietly hoping nobody asks.
