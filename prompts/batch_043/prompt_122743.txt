Hey — I'm a software engineer, never took a philosophy class, so sorry if this is a definately well-trodden question.

At work (mid-size SaaS company) and in this group chat I'm in with a bunch of startup people, everyone has started saying "it's Moloch" or "it's a multipolar trap" whenever something nobody wants keeps happening and nobody can seem to stop it. Came up twice last week: once about us shipping an AI feature that nobody on my team actually likes, purely because every competitor shipped one; once about the industry-wide expectation that engineers are basically oncall 24/7 with no extra pay.

Both times the phrase did the same job: ending the "wait, who's responsible for this / can't somebody just decide not to" part of the conversation. The version I keep hearing is roughly: "it's Moloch — no one's in charge, no one can unilaterally opt out, so blame is a category error." My non-rationalist coworkers say "it's a race to the bottom" in the same tone, and I'm not sure those are even the same claim.

I want this pulled apart. What are the distinct precise things that sentence could be asserting? Some I can half-guess: a boring game-theory one (we're stuck in an equilibrium where no single player improves anything by deviating, and it's worse for everyone than some other outcome); a dynamic one (each individual step is small and locally sensible, so there's no moment where anyone chose the bad outcome — it's a slide, not a decision); a responsibility one (if you couldn't have changed it alone you're not blameworthy); maybe something Goodhart-ish (the thing everyone's ranked on is a proxy, and competition drives everyone to game it); and something spookier that treats Moloch like an actual agent with goals, which my gut says is just confused.

For each, I want a verdict: is it a platitude nobody disagrees with, a substantive claim that's actually defensible, or false? And — this is the part I care about — which of them would really support using it to shut down the responsibility question, as opposed to just sounding like they do?

Also, a friend of mine (also an engineer, also zero philosophy) proposed a test I've been chewing on: ask whether the bad outcome would dissolve if everyone involved could sign a perfectly enforced, costless, binding agreement tomorrow. If yes, it's a coordination problem with a fixed set of players. If no — because the mechanism is selection rather than strategy, i.e. the firms that don't do the thing get outcompeted and replaced, so there's no stable set of parties to sign anything with — then it's a different animal and maybe "trap" is the wrong word entirely. Related: whether the race has a floor (price competition bottoms out somewhere around cost) or doesn't (you can always spend more on compute, forever). Does that cut map onto anything real, or did my friend just reinvent something obvious with worse vocabulary?

Last thing: is there any reading of "it's Moloch" that actually tells you what to do — change the payoffs, build some coordination mechanism, accept it — versus just telling you to stop asking? Right now it feels like a conversation-stopper that quietly smuggles in "nothing can be done." And if you can, give me two or three questions I could ask in the meeting that force whoever says it to commit to one of the readings. No book or reading recs please, I just want the distinctions.
