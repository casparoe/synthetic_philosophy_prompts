I'm TAing a first-year seminar on medicine and society, and this week we did Szasz — the 1961 book, the claim that without a demonstrable bodily lesion there is no disease to be had, the "problems in living" line, the idea that schizophrenia is the sacred symbol of psychiatry rather than the name of a thing, the whole contract-not-treatment picture and the hostility to the therapeutic state. I want to hand out something at the end of the term that's funnier than my summary notes. Could you write me a Swiftian mock white paper — say 800 to 900 words, numbered clauses, issued by a fictional country's Ministry of Health a year after it passes something like the Problems in Living (Reform) Act? Voice: a senior civil servant or a seconded policy consultant who thinks, quite sincerely, that this is the humane reform, writing in flat bureaucratic optimism with the usual costings and implementation timetable. Give it an epigraph from an invented founding figure of the movement. The form is a modest proposal, so the paper should follow the Szaszian logic all the way out to where a reader starts to squirm, and never announce that it has done so.

Some machinery you'll have to invent, because I want it to be more than a list of talking points: there's a Lesion Review Board that certifies whether a condition has a demonstrable bodily lesion (if yes it stays in the health system; if not it's reclassified and moved to a different ministry entirely, along with its budget); a Form 7B Voluntary Distress Contract that anyone seeking help has to sign; a provisional category for conditions "awaiting a lesion"; and — this is the bit I made up and want you to run with — a Retroactive Lesion Clause saying that if a lesion is later found for some provisional condition, everyone previously certified under it is reclassified as having had a real disease all along, which retroactively voids any Form 7B they signed and any consent they gave, and raises the question of what the state owes them now. Then make the paper process at least four awkward files it can't quietly file away: a brain tumour or a dementia with a visible lesion (which stays medical, and so the Ministry has to say what that does to the person's responsibility), someone in acute psychosis who refuses help and then commits a serious crime (no insanity defence left), the collateral damage to the rest of medicine from migraine and chronic pain and fatigue syndromes that have no lesion to show, and drug addiction — which is the one place I think the Szaszian logic genuinely delivers something attractive, so give the Ministry at least one real win where the reader nods instead of recoiling. One hard constraint: the paper must never smuggle illness-talk back in. Every humane concession has to be made in the vocabulary of choice, contract and consent — no "patients", no "symptoms", no "he couldn't help it" — except for exactly one deliberate slip that is itself the joke, ideally caught in a self-congratulatory footnote. And keep the horror bureaucratic and quiet rather than ghoulish; no jokes at the expense of people in crisis, the joke is always on the Ministry's vocabulary and on the consultant's sunny arithmetic.

I'd like it fair, incidentally — I had an argument with a friend who's a psych resident and who says Szasz is only ever presented as a strawman, so I want something a sympathetic Szaszian would grudgingly admit gets the view right rather than something that invents a silly claim and knocks it down; exaggerate a real feature of the position, don't fabricate one. Then add a short afterword, 120 to 150 words, in your own voice: say where the piece takes liberties or leans on a contested reading of Szasz, how confident you are that the parody is fair to the position as it's actually held, and single out the one joke or clause you're least sure works. I don't mind if you say you're unsure about a lot of it — I'd rather have the doubt stated than papered over.
