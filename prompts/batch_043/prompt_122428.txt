I'm trying to work out what I actually think about what matters in survival, having spent time with the Buddhist no-self (anatta) material and with the reductionist side of the personal identity literature. I'd rather start from my own verdicts on cases than from a theory, so here they are.

1. Ordinary gradual change over forty years: that old person is me, and I care about her prudentially, nearly as I care about myself next week.

2. Reliable teletransportation, understood as scan, destroy, rebuild from the scan: also me, with no residue of doubt that I take seriously.

3. Fission into two equal branches, both of which survive: neither is uniquely me, and I'd care about each noticeably less than I'd care about the single survivor in a non-branching case, but far more than I care about my brother. I can't make the two halves of that verdict cohere.

4. The same fission, except that one branch dies a week after the split: I survived, and nothing about my surviving was ever waiting on which branch died.

5. A total psychological replacement carried out gradually over a decade preserves the person; the same total replacement carried out in one operation does not. I know the difference-in-speed objection and it does move me, just not all the way.

6. Maria's character drifts over ten years into something her earlier self would have found alien — new values, new temperament, the old projects abandoned — and then over the next decade drifts back to something very close to her original self, with every adjacent stage as continuous as any in ordinary life. My verdict: the late stage is more fully Maria than the middle stage was, and late Maria's prudential claims carry more weight than middle Maria's did.

7. A long-term meditator whose memories, skills, and dispositions are continuous with her earlier self but in whom the sense of being a subject who owns them has fallen away: I want to say both that nothing has been lost and that there is now no one to whom the continuity belongs, and that the reason I used to give for caring about my own future has been exposed as resting on a mistake, while the caring itself goes on exactly as before.

8. A convert with full psychological continuity who flatly disowns her earlier stage: I don't accept it, she doesn't get to disown it. But when I imagine a future stage of myself that will repudiate everything I now care about and work against it, I feel dread, and it doesn't help me to be told that the stage will be equally me.

The case I go back and forth on: advanced dementia, a final stage with very little psychological overlap with me now. I care about that stage a great deal and not merely out of sentiment, but I can't get clear on whether I think it is me.

My tentative principle: what matters is the holding of psychological continuity with the right kind of cause; identity, uniqueness, and the bare fact of being one and the same contribute nothing; and facts about ownership or first-personal identification contribute nothing either. That gets me through 1 to 5, I think. I don't see how it reaches 6 or 7, and I suspect 3 and 4 cannot both survive.

Is there a single account that captures these, or a short list of candidates? For each candidate, state it precisely, say which of my verdicts it forces me to overturn and which clause is doing that work, give me its verdict on the dementia case and on the repudiating future stage, and tell me what it predicts about the cases I haven't listed — especially any case involving fusion of two people's psychologies, and any case where continuity is held in place by external scaffolding rather than by anything internal. If no account gets all eight, tell me which one I should give up and why.
