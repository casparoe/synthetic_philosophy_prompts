I am a public policy researcher (I teach in a policy school and spent a couple of years evaluating participatory budgeting programs), and in five weeks I have ninety minutes in front of the charter revision commission of a mid-sized city to defend a governance proposal my colleagues and I have been circulating. I want a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the proposal: I can write the defense myself.

The proposal is a deliberately hedged version of futarchy — the familiar "vote on values, bet on beliefs" idea — scaled down and bolted onto the city's existing council. Three modifications carry most of the weight. First, the welfare metric is not fixed by us or by economists: a randomly selected residents' assembly, reconstituted every two years, certifies a composite index (in the pilot we propose something like median rent burden, average transit commute time, and small-business license renewals, measured at a three-year horizon) and the markets forecast that index and nothing else. Second, the market is one-way: it can veto an ordinance that has passed first reading if the enactment-conditional forecast of the index is worse than the non-enactment-conditional forecast, but it cannot enact anything the council has rejected, and every market ruling is logged as a public "market veto" with the council's own forecast printed beside it. Third, because these markets will be thin, the veto triggers only when the price gap clears a threshold that scales inversely with measured participation and depth — a quiet market cannot decide anything — and elected officials and senior staff are barred from trading in them.

The commission is seven people, and I need the objections as these seven will actually raise them, not as an economist would: a municipal bond lawyer who cares about unlawful delegation of legislative authority and about whether any of this is reviewable; two former city councillors, one of whom lost a seat over an unpopular development vote; a representative of the municipal workers' union, who will hear "markets" as "disenfranchisement" and will want to know who loses a voice; a professor of public administration who thinks in terms of legitimacy, administrative capacity, and who gets blamed when things go wrong; a retired state judge who will worry about ultra vires and about what record a court would review; a good-government NGO director focused on corruption and the appearance of capture; and a statistician from the regional transit authority's planning group who will ask whether a market with forty traders can support a three-year forecast of anything.

Give me the eight to ten objections I should expect, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they would do, each attributed to the member most likely to raise it and phrased in that person's own vocabulary — the bond lawyer's objection should sound like a bond lawyer's, the union rep's like a union rep's. For each, give me a two-minute reply I could deliver out loud, a note on what I should concede before I am asked, and a note on where my reply is weakest. Then add three things: the question I am quietly hoping nobody asks, the single concession I should make in my first five minutes to buy credibility with the union representative and the retired judge, and your candid view of which two of these objections have real force against the design as stated rather than against futarchy in general.
