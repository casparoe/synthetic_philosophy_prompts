I work in mechanistic interpretability at an industry lab: causal tracing, activation patching, sparse autoencoder features, linear probes, and the standing argument with my colleagues about whether finding a direction in the residual stream that a probe can read out tells you anything about what the model is doing, as opposed to what it was trained to say. I have no philosophy beyond a first-year survey. A friend who does have the training claimed that the place where my whole toolkit runs up against its own limits is epiphenomenalism and the paradox of phenomenal judgment, and instead of taking that on faith I would like to see the translation actually performed.

So: restate epiphenomenalism, and then the paradox of phenomenal judgment, in the vocabulary I use daily — features and directions in activation space, circuits and causal paths, ablation and patching, gradient signal and the training distribution, output distributions, interventions that dissociate two features that are perfectly correlated under the training distribution, behaviorally identical models. Where possible, be honest about the seams: mark every place where you have to reach outside that vocabulary. My own attempt, which I suspect is broken in a way I cannot locate: an epiphenomenal feature is one that is genuinely computed and genuinely represented but from which no causal path reaches the output, so that patching or ablating it changes no behavior; a model with the feature and a model without it are behaviorally identical from the outside, which sounds like the philosophical zombie built out of transformer parts. And the paradox, translated, is that when the model emits tokens saying it is in pain, there are two candidate upstream causes — a path from the pain-quality feature and a path from the nociception-and-damage feature — and because the two are never dissociated in the training distribution, and because interventions on the first feature are impossible by stipulation, no experiment I can run distinguishes them, so the model has no evidence about which one its self-report tracks.

I would like three things back. First, whether that translation is faithful, and where the distortion is worst. Second, a residue report: what in the philosophical position has no counterpart at all in interpretability vocabulary — and in particular whether the paradox loses its bite in translation because my vocabulary is third-personal while the philosophical worry is essentially first-personal, whether anything corresponds to the fact that I have white-box access to my model but not to my own experiences, and whether the self-undermining version of the worry (if epiphenomenalism were true I could never have reason to believe it) survives when the only evidence available is behavioral. Third, a verdict on an argument I want to make in an internal talk: that in any trained system a feature with no causal route to the output also receives no gradient, hence no selection pressure, hence should not exist — with the obvious reply that networks routinely compute unused intermediates as cheap by-products of the computations they do need, the spandrel case. Does that reply kill the argument, does it merely soften it, or is the whole argument question-begging because it assumes that being real entails being reachable by optimization pressure? Please stay inside the translation and its residue: I do not want a survey of the literature, a map of who holds which position, or reading suggestions, since none of that is usable for me right now. I want to know whether my framework is missing something, or whether the philosophers are disagreeing about less than it looks like from the outside.
