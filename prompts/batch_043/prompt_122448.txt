I should say at the outset that I am neither a philosopher nor an economist. Three months ago I was moved sideways from a housing team into a small corporate strategy unit at a combined authority in the North of England, and the first substantive thing I have been handed is a motion from councillors asking whether the authority should adopt average life satisfaction as a headline measure of progress alongside GVA, with a target attached to it. One councillor has taken to saying in public that we should aim to be the happiest place in the country. That struck me as odd in a way I could not then articulate, and I would like to be able to articulate it before I have to write two pages for a scrutiny committee.

To get up to speed I read a chapter on the economics of happiness in a recent popular book that a colleague passed on to me, which I would rather not name. The argument, as the author puts it, runs roughly as follows:

"The world happiness rankings work by asking a representative sample of people in each country to imagine a ladder, with the best life they could have at the top and the worst at the bottom, and to say which rung they are standing on. That makes them a direct measure of the thing we actually care about, as opposed to GDP, which is only a proxy for it and a poor one. Money turns out to matter only up to a point: beyond about $75,000 a year there is no relationship at all between how rich a country is and how happy its people are, which is why several Latin American countries outrank much richer European ones and why the Nordic countries beat the United States on a fraction of the income. A rating like this is of course subjective, but so is anything worth measuring, and because we are averaging thousands of answers the subjectivity cancels out, which makes the country numbers about as solid as anything in economics. The rankings then tell you what actually produces happiness: social support, trust, freedom to make your own choices, freedom from corruption. And the practical conclusion follows straight from that. A government should set a target for average life satisfaction and hold departments to it in the way it holds them to inflation or employment. On that test Britain is simply underperforming: a country that rich has no business sitting outside the top ten."

I cannot tell how much of this to trust, and I suspect the parts that are wrong are the parts I would most like to use. I would be grateful for a sentence-by-sentence audit: what a wellbeing economist, or a philosopher of measurement, would accept without flinching; what they would treat as a fair simplification for a general reader; and what is simply mistaken, question-begging, or a slide between two claims that the literature keeps apart. Three particular places where I have a feeling but no argument:

The claim that averaging thousands of self-reports cancels out their subjectivity seems to me to be doing enormous work, and I have a suspicion it moves from noise to bias without saying so, but I cannot put the point properly.

The $75,000 figure: is the "money stops mattering" finding about the same quantity the rankings measure, and about countries rather than households? I have been told the satiation claim is contested, but I do not even know which way the contest runs.

The step from "these things correlate with higher scores across countries" to "a government should therefore target the score": does that survive if the correlations are cross-sectional, or is the causal reading doing more than the data allow?

Two things of my own that I would also like tested, because they may determine what I end up recommending:

First, the positional worry. My discomfort with "the happiest place in the country" is that a ranking is a ranking: not every area can be in the top decile, so the aspiration cannot be a general one, and a target defined on relative position can be met by others getting worse. A colleague calls this pedantry and says relative targets still reward genuine improvement. Who is closer to right, and does it matter that we are a sub-national authority rather than a country? Our average, I take it, is mostly a fact about which people live here rather than about what the authority does, which makes me wonder whether a target on the average is partly a target on demographics — and whether it could in principle be met by attracting contented residents and losing discontented ones.

Second, I am inclined to recommend that if we measure anything at all, we track the share of residents below some fixed low point on the scale rather than the mean: the misery floor rather than the average. Does that escape the objections above, or does it inherit them in a new form? One of our analysts has pointed out that the people at the bottom of such a scale are the hardest to reach in a household survey and are least likely to be in the population at all ten years later. I do not know whether that is a fatal objection to my proposal, a mere caveat, or a reason to prefer the average after all.

One constraint, and one admission. I have no access to a university library, so please do not direct me to papers or reports I would have to order in; where a claim turns on a specific empirical result, tell me what the result would have to be for the argument to go through, rather than which study to read. And I should admit that I want these numbers to be usable, so I am probably motivated to dismiss the sceptic who says self-reports cannot be compared across cultures and languages. If there is a defensible version of that scepticism, I would rather hear it from you than discover it in the meeting.
