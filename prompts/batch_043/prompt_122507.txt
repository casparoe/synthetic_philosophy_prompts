I'm retired now — thirty-odd years as an environmental toxicologist, most of them doing risk assessment and cost-benefit work for a regulator, so I spent my career adding up alot of small risks over large populations and comparing the total to the number of lives saved. I've started going to a philosophy reading group at the library on Tuesday mornings, and a few weeks back we did the chapter of Rawls where he complains that utilitarianism doesn't take seriously the distinction between persons. It's been bugging me ever since, because it's aimed more or less straight at what I used to do for a living. So instead of arguing in the abstract I've been writing down my actual gut verdicts on cases, and I'd like to know whether there's one principle behind them or whether I'm just an inconsistency with a lab coat on.

Here's where I've got to. (1) One person undergoes two weeks of severe pain from surgery purely for the sake of her own future health: obviously fine. (2) One passenger gets his arm broken so that 3,000 other passengers don't each sit through a four-hour delay: not fine, and I don't feel any tug at all. (3) One person gets his arm broken to save 3,000 lives: fine, I think, though less certainly than (1). (2) and (3) differ only in how big each beneficiary's stake is, which makes me suspicious that my principle isn't about persons at all. (4) A public-health measure that gives 200,000 children a 1-in-100,000 chance of a severe side effect in order to head off an outbreak expected to kill about four of them: fine, easily, even though I know that in expectation a couple of children will be badly hurt and nobody in particular asked for it. (5) A policy that makes a thousand people modestly better off and one person very slightly worse off — a longer walk to the bus stop, say: fine, whereas under (2) I refused a large harm for many trivial benefits. So trivial harms seem tradeable and large ones not, and I can't tell whether that's a principled threshold or just squeamishness with a number attached. (6) A seventy-year-old endures a brutal course of treatment for the benefit of the eighty-five-year-old she'll become, where a slowly progressing condition means the two of them will share almost no memories, projects or desires: still fine, I think — even though the person who pays and the person who gains are nearly strangers to each other. And then two science-fiction ones, sorry, but they're the ones I keep coming back to. (7a) Two people surgically joined so that damage to either produces a single experience of pain, co-felt by both, roughly twice as intense as it would be for one: here the tradeoff in (2) starts to feel easier to me, as if there's now one subject I'm imposing on rather than two. (7b) One body with two independent streams of consciousness, neither able to communicate with the other, both feeling whatever happens to the body: here I feel no different than I do about an ordinary person — it doesn't get harder, which is the opposite of what (7a) suggested, since in (7a) I seemed to be counting subjects and here I seem to be counting bodies.

My own first pass at a principle goes something like: it's fine to add costs and benefits up inside a single life, and it's not fine to add them across lives, unless each person affected has a big enough personal stake in the outcome that she'd have her own reason to accept the deal. But I don't trust it — (6) looks like a case where the "single life" is barely unified and I still aggregate, (4) looks like I'm aggregating across thousands of lives with no consent at all, and (5) suggests the threshold does the real work while the bit about separateness is decoration. Can you test that principle against the cases and repair it, or tell me which candidate does better (contractualist-style complaints, thresholds on personal stake, something about there being no single standpoint from which a cross-personal trade is a net gain)? Be explicit about which of my verdicts your principle can't accommodate and which one you'd overturn. And tell me what it predicts for the case I haven't been able to settle: one person's broken arm to spare 3,000 people a serious but survivable harm — a year of healthy life each — and then the same trade with ten million people each spared something genuinely trivial like a stubbed toe. Does the number of people enter the principle at all, and if it does, does it enter through the size of each person's stake or through the total?
