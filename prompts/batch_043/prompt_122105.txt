I'm a graduate student in comparative literature sitting in on a philosophy of history seminar, and I have to lead next week's discussion section on Chinua Achebe's 1958 debut novel Things Fall Apart. The seminar has been working through the claim that history is written by the victors, and I find the aphorism indispensable but frustratingly ambiguous: it can mean the causal thesis that victors control archives and suppress rival records, the epistemic thesis that the victors' perspective is inherently partial, or the strong relativist thesis that historical significance is constituted by the narrative of the powerful. To get past the version of this debate that everyone recites, I've been using three homemade models in my notes, and I want you to read the novel through them. Call the first the Veto Model: victors' power is essentially negative, a matter of destroying or burying competing records so that only their version survives. Call the second the Ventriloquist Model: victors do not merely silence others but make the vanquished speak in the victors' own idiom, so that even preserved indigenous words are reframed as evidence for colonial categories such as pacification or primitive tribe. Call the third Ironic Entrapment: even a sympathetic postcolonial narration can fail to restore agency, because the act of turning a life into readable material—whether into a District Commissioner's administrative chapter or into a realist novel written in the colonizer's language for a global audience—already fits it into a genre that neutralizes its claim to be a subject of history rather than an object of it. The closing scene, in which the District Commissioner reflects that Okonkwo's death will furnish material for his own book, The Pacification of the Primitive Tribes of the Lower Niger, is the obvious test case, but I want more than a paraphrase of that scene.

Please read Things Fall Apart against this domain: does the novel ultimately vindicate the Veto Model, the Ventriloquist Model, or Ironic Entrapment, or does it show all three to be inadequate in different ways? Which scenes or character choices—Okonkwo's killing of Ikemefuna, his exile to Mbanta, his son Nwoye's conversion, the destruction of the church, his suicide, or the Commissioner's retrospective reduction of his life—illustrate or complicate the models, and which, if any, resist the reading entirely, so that an honest critic has to admit the novel slips the framework? I would like a reading that is faithful in both directions: it should not reduce the novel to a slogan, and it should not stretch the models until they predict every detail. In particular, tell me what the novel adds to the philosophical debate about power, perspective, and historical relativism that a straight argument about the victors' thesis would miss—for example, about the normative density of a form of life that is about to be archived, or about the difference between being remembered and being narrated. Finally, since I have to defend this in section, I need the reading to be self-critical: state how confident you are in the verdict you give (do you think Achebe escapes Ironic Entrapment, or not?), and say what would change your mind—which alternative interpretation of the Commissioner's chapter, which textual detail I may be overlooking, or which philosophical reply, such as a defense of objective historiography or a stronger relativist rejoinder, would lead you to abandon or substantially revise the reading—and flag where you think your reasoning is most likely to fail.
