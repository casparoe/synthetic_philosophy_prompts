I'm working on two things at once and they have started to interfere with each other, so I would like an outside audit of the position I have ended up in. The first is a position paper for a graduate seminar on social ontology, where I am trying to state as cleanly as I can what I actually think about constructed kinds. The second is a small practical job: I sit on the committee redesigning the demographic and financial variables in our hospital system's research data warehouse, and we are deciding what to record about patients' race, about sex and gender, and about ability to pay. The epidemiologist on the committee wants richer race and gender variables so that disparities stay visible; one of the data engineers wants to drop them entirely; and I have noticed that my reason for keeping the race variables is not obviously the same kind of reason as my reason for the gender policy, which makes me suspect the underlying metaphysics is doing something different in the two cases. Two people in the seminar pushed back on different parts of what follows, and I could not tell whether they were pointing at the same problem. Here is the profile:

(a) Construction is a causal thesis, not a constitutive one. To say that a kind is socially constructed is to say that social practices, attitudes or institutions played a central causal role in bringing it about that the kind exists, or that it has the features it has. Nothing follows from that about the kind being unreal, or merely in our heads, or up to us, or liable to evaporate if we stopped endorsing it: a constructed kind can be as objective, as mind-independent in its existence conditions and as causally efficacious as any natural kind, and claims about it can be straightforwardly true or false.

(b) Race is the model case for (a): real, constructed, causally downstream of racializing practices, and causally upstream of a great deal, including who gets what care and what people are exposed to. So I am a race realist of a sort, and I think the committee should keep collecting race data and make it better rather than worse.

(c) Money is the case where I cannot get the causal story to do the work. What makes something money, it seems to me, is that enough people treat it as money; if the treating stops, the money stops, and there is nothing further to discover about whether it really was money. Here the dependence on attitudes looks constitutive rather than causal.

(d) For bureaucratic purposes I want to treat self-identification as authoritative rather than merely evidential. On an intake form, a person's gender is what they say it is, and it would be a mistake to let a clerk, a clinician or an algorithm overrule them, even in a hypothetical case where overruling them would get the truth about their social position right.

(e) I also think there are perfectly objective facts about how much financial strain a household is under, that these are the facts our data should track rather than the administrative payer categories we currently store, and that researchers investigating them can be straightforwardly wrong.

(f) Finally, I think we should be trying over time to shrink the number of official contexts in which people are sorted by race and gender, on the grounds that the sorting itself does work: it reifies and reproduces the very patterns the rest of us want to measure.

What I want is an audit, not a lecture. Go through this and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look like it, and order the real tensions by how severe they are rather than listing them as equally bad. For each real tension, say exactly what would have to give, what the cheapest available revision would be, and what that revision would cost me downstream in the committee work. Where a single commitment needs to be split into two claims that can come apart, say so. And tell me which one revision would clean up the most. I am not attached to any of (a) through (f) in particular, and I would rather find out where the problem is now than in front of the committee.
