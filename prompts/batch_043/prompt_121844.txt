Write a dialogue between a legal moralist in the style of Patrick Devlin and a critic of legal moralism in the style of H. L. A. Hart, debating one concrete case.

The case: in a polity otherwise much like a contemporary liberal democracy, a large majority has come to hold, as a considered and explicitly non-religious moral conviction, that it is degrading to build interactive simulations of the dead and converse with them. Their reasoning is that love, grief, and memory require the real possibility of absence, and that a society which technologically abolishes absence corrupts the practices its common life depends on. Both characters accept two stipulations. First, no one is coerced, harmed, or set back in their interests by the practice, apart from the distress of those who merely learn that it goes on. Second, the sociological premise the moralist's argument turns on is, unusually, actually true here: without a legal prohibition, this conviction and the cluster of practices bound up with it will unravel within a generation, and the polity's shared life depends on that cluster.

Have the two argue the case rather than recite positions. The moralist must explain why the criminal law, rather than social pressure alone, is the apt response, and why the society is the right unit of moral concern. The critic must say what remains of the argument against enforcement once the harm principle cannot bite and the moralist's sociological premise is granted, and must answer the charge that the harm principle is itself just one contested critical morality. Let each of them concede at least one genuine point.
