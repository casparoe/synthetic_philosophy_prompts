I host a tiny interview series for our department's ethics reading group — 30 minutes, one guest, me asking the questions, and no prepared remarks. Next up is a political philosopher (I'll keep her anonymous here; real name isn't on the flyer yet) who defends a specific version of prioritarianism: rank options by the expected sum of a strictly concave transform of each person's realised welfare — the transform gets applied to what actually happens to each person, and only afterwards do we take the expectation, rather than applying it to each person's expected welfare. She's good, she's heard every version of the levelling-down objection, and she has three moves she falls back on constantly:

1. my view can't require levelling down at all — it's a sum of independently weighted welfares, so making anyone better off always makes the outcome better, end of story;
2. the concavity isn't a stipulation about weights, it's just the diminishing moral value of a benefit as its recipient's level rises, which everyone already accepts in the single-person case;
3. whatever is actually bothering you is a worry about relational equality, which is a different principle and not one I'm signed up to.

Write me nine or ten interview questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give — so: name the answer I should expect, then give me the follow-up that presses on it. I want the sequence to make moves (1), (2) and (3) progressively harder to reach for, and I want it to go well beyond her restating the view. Please cover at least these:

- Why the transform goes on realised welfare rather than expected welfare. Her version is risk-averse at the social level, which means she can prefer a policy with a *lower* expected total welfare to a riskier one with a higher expected total. That looks a lot like the levelling-down worry relocated into expectation rather than outcome, and I want a question that makes her either own it or explain why the expected-welfare version is worse. Give me the tiny numerical illustration to use on air.
- Where the concavity comes from, pushed into her own favourite justification. If the shape of the function really is just diminishing marginal moral value of benefits to a person, and that's supposed to be obvious from the single-person case, then why doesn't the same concavity apply within one life over time — a benefit to you during a bad stretch of your life counting for more than the same benefit during a good stretch? If she bites that bullet she gets an intrapersonal levelling-down result: with the time-slices weighted concavely, smoothing someone's life can beat a life with more total welfare in it, i.e. you should sometimes make a person worse off overall in order to even their life out. I want the question that forces her to say whether she accepts that, and a follow-up on why it isn't as embarrassing as the interpersonal version. If she refuses and says the unit is the whole life, I want the follow-up on what makes the boundary of a person the special place where priority switches on — and on whether benefits she's already received in the past should count when we work out how much priority she gets now.
- The tail of the function: as welfare rises, does the marginal weight go to zero? If it does, then no finite gain to the very well off outweighs an arbitrarily tiny gain to the worst off, and I want her pressed on whether that's still a continuous trade-off view or lexical priority in a trenchcoat. Write the case so it's concrete and slightly absurd (a billionaire's bad afternoon versus something genuinely trivial happening to a badly off kid, scaled however you like).
- Whether the view is egalitarian in any sense. It's additive and separable, so the gap between people never enters the axiology directly — only their levels do — and yet concavity hands her an inequality-favouring result for free. I want a question on what she thinks people are actually responding to when they have levelling-down intuitions: confusion, or something real that her axiology has no slot for. And then the follow-up for the likely answer, which is that it's something real but separate.
- One case where I think she may not be able to stay a pure prioritarian: what happens when the number of people isn't fixed, or when the people in one option aren't the same people as in the other. Who are the worse off supposed to be then, and does priority have anything to say when there's nobody who gets made worse off?
- A closing question about what would count against the view — not what's the strongest objection, but what concrete result, from cases or from the formal side, would make her give up prioritarianism of this particular shape. Phrase it so that some version of more work needs to be done isn't available as a reply.

Keep everything in plain spoken language, this is a conversation and not a referee report. Where a question needs an example, invent it and keep it short — a sentence or two — and please don't build anything on my having read a particular book or paper, because I havent. Also note the tone constraint: I want to keep her talking, so no question should be phrased as an accusation, even when it is one.
