You are helping two moral philosophers write the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration. The first is an Archimedean aggregator about harm: she holds that the badness of an outcome is additive across the individuals harmed, that each person's setback however slight makes a positive contribution to how bad the outcome is, and that there is no positive lower bound on the size of that contribution — so that for some finite number of people, however enormous, everyone getting a momentary speck of dust in the eye is a worse outcome than one person being tortured for fifty years. The second is a threshold theorist: he holds that there is a positive level of individual badness, a setback serious enough to matter to a person's interests, below which an experience cannot enter into interpersonal moral aggregation, and that therefore no number of dust specks, however vast, is a worse outcome than fifty years of torture. They have been arguing past one another for two years and have now agreed to produce a document both can sign.

Write that document. It must be self-contained: no citations, no reading recommendations, no appeal to empirical findings or to what any published author has argued. Use British English spelling and idiom throughout. Structure it as follows.

1. A numbered section of agreed claims. These should cover the case itself — that the specks are momentary, unremembered, unknown to their recipients, and without downstream effects; that the number of recipients is finite but far beyond anything anyone can enumerate or visualise; that the comparison is between two outcomes differing only in the respects stated — and also what neither side may rely on: that the choice could never arise in practice, that the numbers are so large that the question loses meaning, or that intuition alone settles it. Include whatever else both would genuinely accept.

2. A disagreements table. Give each side's position in wording that side would accept as its own, not in the other side's preferred formulation. In particular, get exact whether the threshold theorist is committed to saying that a speck is no harm at all, or that it is a harm whose weight cannot be summed with the weights of others; and whether the aggregator is committed to pure addition or may weight harms by how badly off the person already is.

3. A section on the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them. Two I want handled explicitly, and you should add others:

   - The saturation problem: if torture is worse for every finite number of specks, then the total disvalue of specks stays bounded however many recipients there are. Is the bound per person or global? If per person, a sufficiently vast population of capped harms should still add up to something enormous, and the threshold view seems to collapse into aggregation; if global, then adding recipients beyond some point adds nothing at all to how bad things are, which is its own commitment. Say which way he goes and what it costs.
   - The marginal speck: if the aggregator is right, then removing a single speck from a vast collection makes the outcome better, and yet the difference any one speck makes is itself negligible. Is there a coherent position in which each individual contribution is morally inert while the sum of them is morally decisive? Both sides should state what they think about the analogous question for the torture side.

   Also worth separating out, if you think they matter: whether the same threshold governs aggregation within a single life over time — construct the variant in which one very long-lived subject receives all the specks, one at a time, with no habituation and no interaction between them, and say whether either verdict changes; whether the dispute is really about aggregation or about whether there can be harms that are imperceptible from the inside; what happens across a graded series of cases between a speck and torture, and whether either side is committed to a sharp cutoff; and whether the practical question of what to choose, given uncertainty about which view is correct and about how many people there are, is settled by anything said so far or is a further question.

For each crux, record a case or line of argument that both would accept as bearing on it, and each side's prediction, written down in advance, about how it will come out. For each side, record what would count as evidence or argument against that side's own view. If any part of the dispute turns out to be verbal rather than substantive, say which part and why. Aim for formulations that are neither vacuous nor slanted.
