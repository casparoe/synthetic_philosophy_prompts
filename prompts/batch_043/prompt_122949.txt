I run a fortnightly internal session at work called *Judgement Calls* — about sixteen people, mostly backend and data engineers with a couple of product managers, nobody with any philosophy training, ninety minutes in a room with a projector and laptops. I want one session on why we are entitled to expect the future to be like the past, and specifically on that unnerving move where someone points out that for any stretch of evidence there are endlessly many rules which fit every line of it exactly and then diverge from each other afterwards — Goodman's grue problem, as I've seen it described. The bit I care about is the second-order version: that the difficulty isn't really whether to project at all, but which of the equally-fitting predicates are safe to project with.

Constraints: no lecture at the front, and nobody in the room should hear the word "grue" or any label for the puzzle until the debrief. It has to be something people actually do, out loud or on their laptops, with something small at stake — points, a leaderboard, a trivial prize — and no money. Please give me the rules, the materials, a minute-by-minute timeline, the scoring scheme, and the debrief.

The setting I have in mind, which you should replace if you have something better: participants see a few hundred lines of log from a black box service — inputs carrying a handful of visible properties, and the response the service returned — running up to a certain date. They must commit, in writing, to the rule they think governs the service, then predict the next week of traffic and be scored on it. The rules have to be written in a tiny fixed pseudo-code vocabulary so that I can see exactly which predicates they reached for. What I want the mechanics to do is let them walk into the trap themselves: they will write a rule with no time dependence in it, and the reveal will show a rule that was time-indexed all along — a feature-flag-style branch on a date constant sitting in the future — which fitted every line they were shown and disagreed with them from the reveal onwards, and which any one of them could have written at any point.

Two things I'd like built in if you can make them work.

1. A way of making the entrenchment move felt rather than asserted. My idea was a short warm-up phase in which one half of the room only ever has time-free predicates available in the vocabulary and is rewarded for using them, whilst the other half gets a vocabulary in which date-indexed rules are the ones that pay; then both halves get the same full vocabulary and the same data for the main round. What I want them to notice is that their confidence is tracking their own recent history rather than anything in the log, and to be genuinely unsettled about whether that history is a reason or merely a fact about how they were drilled. Whether that design actually works, and how to stop the two halves comparing notes before the main round, is your problem.

2. A moment, after they have committed but before the reveal, where they are invited to rewrite their committed rule into any other rule that also fits every line of evidence so far, for a small fixed penalty — and almost nobody takes it. I want to know what the debrief should do with that refusal: dogmatism, the correct application of a simplicity prior, or the very circularity the puzzle is about.

For the debrief: it has to map what actually happened onto the parts of the argument — the underdetermination of future evidence by past evidence, and the difference between asking whether the future will be like the past and asking which rule to project — and it has to handle the objection I know is coming, which is that in real engineering we are never in this position because we have deployment logs, source code and a causal story, and we can simply go and look. I don't know whether to treat that as wriggling out of the exercise or as the beginning of the real answer, and I'd like the rules arranged so that the objection arrives in a form I can use rather than as a conversation-stopper. Tell me what else will predictably go wrong: I expect at least one person to announce that the whole thing is a word game, and someone to point out that the log is finite.

Finally, be frank about what the exercise cannot deliver. My worry is that because I choose the ground truth, the session may only ever show that a trickster can fool you, whereas the interesting claim is supposed to be that the puzzle arises with no trickster at all; and that ninety minutes can only exhibit a handful of rival rules when the point is meant to be that the supply is endless. Say whether those are real limitations, what I can do about them, and what I shall simply have to say out loud at the end.
