Consider the proposition below, which I will call T.

T: For a typical human being alive in the Levant around 3000 BCE, the package of changes that came with dependence on domesticated plants and animals — sedentism, stored grain, higher local population density, heritable property, crowd and zoonotic disease, longer and more rigidly scheduled routine labor, and formalised political authority — made that person's life go worse than it would have gone had their community's subsistence base remained foraging. Judge only by somatic health, workload, physical risk, and exposure to coercion. Set aside entirely any value there might be in there being more human lives in total, and set aside everything that happened after the first few centuries of the transition.

Give me your credence in T. I do not want the number on its own; I want the decomposition that produces it.

1. State the proposition's load-bearing sub-questions as you see them. I think there are at least these, but restructure them if you disagree: (a) the size and sign of the empirical change in health and workload at the transition, given that the skeletal record is sparse, regionally uneven, and confounded by contemporaneous climate shifts; (b) whether there is a determinate fact of the matter about how that particular individual's life would have gone under the foraging branch, given that the branching changes who exists and how many exist; (c) what comparison rule handles that non-identity problem — one option is to compare matched lives rather than matched persons, holding fixed the number of lives lived and asking whether the first N lives under farming went worse than the first N under foraging; (d) whether the transition was in any sense reversible, given that the population supported by farming could not have been supported by foraging, so that no one at the time may have had a return option; (e) whether going worse in health and labor can be outweighed, within the terms T sets out, by reduced exposure to violence or by the buffering of famine risk that storage provides.

2. Attach a rough probability to each term, with one line of justification per term, and say how you are combining them — as independent factors, as a weighted average over scenarios, or something else. If your combination is not a simple product, say so and say why.

3. Tell me which single term your final number is most sensitive to, and what sort of finding would move it most.

4. A colleague and I disagree: my credence in T is about 0.6, hers is about 0.2. Tell me which sub-question our disagreement most likely lives in, and what each of us would have to concede to converge.

5. Suppose a large, well-controlled new dataset of skeletal stress markers from adjacent Levantine sites between 11000 and 8000 years ago showed early cultivators to be statistically indistinguishable from contemporaneous foragers on those markers, while both infection load and variance in attained stature rose sharply in the cultivating sample. Say how much your credence in T moves, in which direction, and through which term.

6. Say what would count as resolving T rather than merely nudging it, and whether you think T has a determinate truth value at all.

Answer in under 600 words. No citations, no bibliography, no reading suggestions — I want your own reasoning, decomposed.
