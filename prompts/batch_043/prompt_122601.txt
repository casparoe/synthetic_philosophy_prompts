Bit of an odd one, and I'll be straight up that the novel is mostly an excuse. I write historical fiction, four or five mornings a week, and about a year ago I started reading Nietzsche on and off — Twilight of the Idols, Ecce Homo, bits of the late notebooks — because a friend told me his stuff on diet and health is basically the earliest version of the "sort your body out and your mind will follow" genre, and I've got completely sucked in. The picture I've ended up with, such as it is: he thinks German cookery and German beer are doing real damage to German intellect, he's withering about Wagner's vegetarian turn, he's against alcohol, he treats digestion and climate and how much of your life you spend shut indoors as doing more work on your thinking than your arguments are, he seems to have spent years tinkering with his own regimen while being spectacularly ill anyway, and the whole thing sits under that "how one becomes what one is" heading, which I take to mean the tinkering is supposed to be the point rather than a means to it. Possibly I've got all of that wrong, which is part of what I want from you.

What I actually want isn't an essay, it's a procedure I can run. The rest of the day, outside those writing hours, has quietly become regimen maintenance: I keep a ledger — what I ate, how I slept, whether the walk happened, what I drank — and then I adjust. And the ledger has started eating the day. So give me something reusable: the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each one, where the procedure stops with a no, and — the bit I care about most — the places where it has to hand the decision back to me because it's a question about what I want rather than about how bodies work. Constraints: I'll run it weekly, ten minutes or so, no specialist knowledge and nobody signing anything off. Two things I want built into it. First, a distinction I've started using on myself and can't tell whether it's Nietzschean or just mine: the first-order regimen, which is what you actually eat and do, and the second-order one, which is how much of your life you're allowed to spend managing the first-order one. I've even given it a number — the regimen ratio, hours spent on upkeep divided by hours spent doing the thing the upkeep is supposedly for. Should the procedure cap that ratio, and where, and does anything Nietzsche actually says commit him to a cap, or does "become what you are" push the other way and make the managing itself the work? Second, run it on two cases, and make one of them come out with an answer I won't like. Case one: the two glasses of wine most evenings, which I've decided are when the dialogue gets good. Case two: my wife wants us to go vegetarian at home, and I've read just enough Nietzsche to think I know what he'd say, and just enough to not trust it, since he'd presumably say the same thing about anything.

One more thing, and then I'll shut up. I genuinely can't tell how much of the first paragraph is Nietzsche and how much is the Nietzsche-industrial-complex talking, and I'm asking you rather than a book, so: for each of the main claims you make, say how confident you are, and single out the one you'd least like to be held to. And don't give me a reading list, I've already got far too many unread books and I'll only feel guilty.
