I've been having a slow, recurring argument with a friend from my reading group about the socialist calculation debate — Mises's 1920 essay, Hayek's mid-1940s paper on dispersed knowledge, Lange's trial-and-error reply in the 1930s — and we've both noticed that the argument keeps dissolving into vibes. Half the people in my circles treat Hayek as having obviously won because the Soviet economy visibly failed, which feels post hoc, since those planners had neither markets nor anything resembling modern computation. The other half read Hayek as an early, informal statement of the thesis that a market is a distributed computation, and then conclude that a sufficiently large computer does the same thing better. That inference feels like it has a hole in it, and I want to locate the hole rather than just assert it. So I've been sketching a concrete version of the pro-planner case to argue against: call it the General Allocation Model. Imagine a learned planner trained on the complete behavioral record of a large industrial economy — every transaction, contract, inventory level, logistics and sensor stream, plus elicited stated preferences — with, say, a million times today's training compute, able to answer counterfactual queries like what happens to downstream output if we shift two percent of steel from construction to fabrication, and able to emit shadow prices for any good. Crucially, it cannot observe anyone's choices before those choices are made, and I want to bracket corruption, capture, and incompetence entirely: assume benevolent, competent operators and some legitimate procedure for setting the objective function. The residual question is whether the epistemic and economic core of the Austrian argument survives that.

So here is the proposition I want a number on: that a GAM-style planner, as specified, could allocate capital goods in a modern industrial economy at least as well as capital markets do — where "as well" is measured jointly by static allocative efficiency given the existing technology, and by the rate at which genuinely novel production possibilities (goods and techniques nobody had enumerated in advance) get discovered and adopted over a thirty-year horizon. Give me your credence in that, decomposed, not a hedge: a rough probability for each sub-question and one line of justification apiece, then the combination and a note on how you're combining them. The terms I'd suggest, and you should add or substitute as you see fit, are something like: whether the knowledge Hayek pointed at is genuinely inarticulate and context-bound in a way that resists being reconstructed from behavioral data, or whether it leaves enough trace to be learned; whether prices do something irreducible in compressing heterogeneous, incommensurable local knowledge into a scalar usable by people who don't know where it came from, or whether that compression is just lossy data reduction a model could replicate; whether a model trained on data generated under one allocation regime can produce valid counterfactual valuations for states far outside that distribution, or whether it inherits what it needs from the market and then loses its footing once the market is gone; whether the planner must still produce something price-like as a common denominator for heterogeneous capital goods, and whether such a denominator needs real stakes behind it to mean anything; whether the binding constraint is the discovery of new possibilities over time rather than static allocation at a point in time, in which case a benchmark trained partly on past discoveries is being asked to do a different job than the one it was trained for; and whether the historical record of actually-existing planned economies is any evidence at all here, given the selection effects and given that those systems had neither markets nor machine learning. I'd also like you to say which of your terms the final number is most sensitive to — the one where a swing from 0.2 to 0.8 moves the bottom line most — and which single term you hold least confidently and why.

For calibration: I'm at roughly 0.6 on this, maybe lower; my friend is at about 0.25, and she thinks my number is mostly a failure to update on how good modern ML is at exactly this kind of high-dimensional prediction. I'd like your number on its own terms, but I'd especially like your guess at where our disagreement actually lives — which of the sub-questions above two people at 0.6 and 0.25 are most likely assigning very different probabilities to, as opposed to weighting the same probabilities differently. And finally: name one piece of evidence or one real-world experiment that would move your number the most if it came in one way rather than the other. I have a candidate in mind — the internal capital allocation of very large firms, which already plan quite a lot without prices and yet keep rebuilding internal markets — but I want to hear what you'd pick and what you think the observation would actually show either way.
