Hey — I'm a TA for a first-year "philosophy through film" module and I have to write one of the two model essays for the final handout, and I've decided to do Nolan's The Prestige (2006) — I haven't read the Priest novel, so please stick to the film — read against Parfit's teletransporter cases and the whole "identity is not what matters, what matters is Relation R (psychological continuity/connectedness, with any cause)" package. Act like someone who actually works in personal identity but who also knows how to do criticism rather than just bolt a theory onto a plot, and be careful: I want this usable for students who've read maybe 40 pages of Parfit.

Start with some brainstorming before you commit — jot out the five or six cruxes a reading like this could be built on, then pick the two or three sharpest and tell me in a line or two each why you dropped the others; I'd rather see the rejected theses than a clean single take.

Here's roughly where my own thinking has got to, and the bits I most want you to work with. First: Angier's machine is not really a teletransporter in Parfit's sense. Nothing is scanned and destroyed as part of the process; a duplicate just appears at the balcony while the original is still standing there, and the original then dies a moment later by falling through a trapdoor into a water tank — a death that's causally enabled by the duplication but is a separate event downstream of it. I've been calling this the "add-on death" and I want to know whether it does any philosophical work: if Parfit-style reductionism is happy with the ordinary teletransporter, should it be equally happy here, or does the fact that the killing is an extra step, something the machine doesn't need, make the man in the tank a murder victim rather than a stage left behind? Second, the trick is performed a hundred times, so this is serial fission with one branch killed each night, and I want you to consider a version where the copying isn't perfect — call it R-drift: each new Angier is made from the previous night's body and is very slightly less psychologically continuous with the first one, say 99% per run. Does what mattering admit of degrees change the verdict at all? Is there a threshold below which the survivor isn't Angier in the sense that matters, and does the film's late Angier — colder, more willing — look like it's already staging that decay? And who is the man stepping into the machine prudentially worried about: on the reductionist view, does he lose nothing that matters, half of it, or everything?

Third, and I think this is the part everyone misses: Borden's Transported Man isn't duplication at all. Two brothers, one public life, the psychology pooled and handed back and forth via their notebooks, each of them living half a life, and — this is the bit the film really leans on — Sarah's accusation that he only loves her half the time. That looks like fission turned sideways, a case where R holds across two brains that take turns rather than two bodies that coexist, and I want you to say whether it's better described as one person with two bodies or two people sharing a mental life, and what the reductionist should say about whether two half-lives are as good as one whole one, and about whether Borden's surviving brother at the end is any kind of survival.

Please also give the opposing reading its due: on any view where identity is a further fact, or on the animalist one, the living organism falls in the tank and dies every night and the thing at the balcony is a replica, in which case the film is a hundred suicides. My worry is that the film's plot is the reductionist's favourite intuition pump while its horror — the stacks of drowned Angiers, the shot cat, Angier's line about never knowing whether he'd be the man in the box or the prestige (I'm paraphrasing, don't hold me to the wording) — depends on something much closer to the further-fact view. So tell me whether the film knows this about itself, or whether it's cheating.

Shape it as: a one-paragraph statement of the reading's thesis; then the scene work (I'd point you at the cats, the first test where he shoots the copy, the tank, Sarah, Cutter's "you want to be fooled" speech, and the final warehouse); then an honest section on where the film resists the reading and where the idea has to be bent to fit; then a short closing on what the film contributes to the debate that the armchair cases don't. Don't summarize the plot, don't quote the film or Parfit, no citations or bibliography — I just want the argument and the reading, maybe 1200-1600 words.
