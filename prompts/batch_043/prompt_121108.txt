Ive got a fifteen-minute guest slot in a first-year ethics course and Im putting together a one-page handout, and I want one worked example that does a particular job. I want to show that the move people reach for when they want to dodge the repugnant conclusion — stop counting a new person as an improvement unless their life clears some positive bar — just buys you a different embaressment instead. So I need a case built to order.

Setup: welfare is an integer on a 0 to 100 scale, 0 is the neutral point, anything above 0 is a life worth living. The view under test is the critical-level view: the value of a population is the sum, over everyone in it, of their welfare minus a fixed threshold c, where c is positive, you pick it yourself, you state it up front, and you keep it between 25 and 60. So on this view adding someone at exactly c is neutral, above c is an improvement, below c makes things worse.

Build me a chain of three or four outcomes, A1 through to the end, where every single step does all of the following at once:

- exactly one person is added and nobody is removed
- nobody's welfare moves by more than 2
- total welfare strictly goes up
- average welfare strictly goes up, so that someone who only ever cared about the average would also call every step an improvement — I dont want the critical-level verdict to be dismissable as just dilution
- the critical-level view says the step makes things strictly worse
- every life in every outcome is above 15, i.e. comfortably worth living
- start with at least four people, and keep it boring: no sci-fi, no identity puzzles, no splitting one mind into two half-welfare minds, just a list of people and their welfare numbers

Then give me the arithmetic for each step, restate your c, and tick off each of the constraints explicitly. After that:

1. Tell me which feature of the case is actually doing the work. My suspicion is that the size of the drop imposed on the people already there turns out not to matter and that something else is essential, but say so honestly either way, and if you can state the general condition under which a chain like this can exist, state it.
2. Say what happens if that feature is removed — in particular, if everyone already alive starts out above the threshold, can the case still be built at all, and why not.
3. Give the strongest thing a defender of the critical-level view could say in reply, not a strawman, and then say whether you think it lands.
4. One closing sentence, usable on the handout, saying in plain language what the case shows.

What counts as cheating: dont stipulate any of the verdicts, generate them from the arithmetic. No epsilon-sized steps, no negative welfare, no comparing anything to an empty world, and no smuggling in the thought that the added person would have been better off never existing.
