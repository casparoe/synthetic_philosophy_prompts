You are a professional philosopher whose specialty is causation and explanation in history, and you also work as a dramaturg with novelists. Think carefully before you answer, and work in two passes: first, spend 250 to 300 words brainstorming two or three candidate mechanics and telling me, briefly, why you rejected the ones you rejected; then give me the finished design. The brainstorm is for my benefit — I want to see the alternatives you considered, not just the winner.

Here is the situation. I write historical fiction, and I have been asked to run a single 90-minute workshop at a writers' conference. Sixteen participants, all adults, all novelists or serious aspiring novelists, almost none with any philosophy training, none of whom have read anything on this topic and none of whom will do homework. The room has a whiteboard, index cards in three colors, markers, a ball of string, and a timer. No projector, no laptops, no handouts printed in advance. Some participants will arrive with a specific climactic scene from a work in progress; others will arrive with nothing. I do not want to read anyone's manuscript, and I do not want anyone forced to share their material with the room.

The thing I want them to feel, not to be told, is the difference between two shapes an explanation can have. The first shape is genuine overdetermination: several conditions each of which, on its own, would have been enough to bring the outcome about, so that the outcome is robust and would have arrived anyway by another route. The second shape is what most multi-causal historical explanations actually are: a set of conditions that only produce the outcome together, none of them individually sufficient, each of them load-bearing. My strong suspicion, which the workshop should put them through rather than announce, is that writers and historians systematically mistake the second for the first, and that this mistake is the engine of the feeling that a historical outcome had to happen.

Three mechanisms matter to me, and I would like at least the first two built into the rules rather than the debrief.

The first is the gap between deleting a cause and reversing it. When you take a factor out of a story and ask whether the outcome still happens, you are not asking the same question as when you leave the factor in place but flip it to its opposite and ask the same thing. Deletion invites people to lean on the remaining causes; reversal does not. I want participants to answer the deletion question confidently, be rewarded for it, and only later discover that they were answering a softer question than they thought.

The second is grain. A cause described coarsely, at the level of the harvest failed or the letter arrived late, looks as though it settles the matter. Described finely, at the level of how much the harvest failed by, in which districts, against what reserves, it turns out to settle nothing on its own. Novelists control grain for a living, so this is the part they should be able to feel most sharply, and it should be something the rules force them to vary.

The third, if you can fit it in, is the one I find genuinely strange and would like the room to run into: making an explanation thicker by adding further conditions that all have to hold together makes the outcome less likely in expectation, not more, and yet thickening is exactly what makes an outcome feel fated in a narrative. Longer causal lists, more inevitable-feeling endings, lower probability. If there is a scoring rule that makes participants live inside that reversal, I want it.

Constraints on the design. No lecture up front, and no technical vocabulary in the room before the debrief — I do not want the words overdetermination, counterfactual, or necessary-and-sufficient used in the first hour, and I would rather the activity work through example and rule than through explanation. (If it helps you: philosophers have a label for the merely-conjunctive case, an INUS condition, an insufficient but non-redundant part of an unnecessary but sufficient condition; I know the label and I do not want it said aloud.) Participants must be able to play with a scene from their own work, with a scene invented on the spot, or with a bare historical scenario I supply, without those three routes producing wildly different experiences. There must be a real if small stake: points, a tally, a public verdict on whether a scene survived or collapsed. There must be a deliberate reveal, engineered rather than hoped for, somewhere around minute 55 to 65.
