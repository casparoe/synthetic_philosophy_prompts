I am taking an upper-level seminar on distributive justice, and we have been working through the equality of what debate—whether the currency of egalitarian justice should be welfare, resources, or capabilities. In study group, a friend keeps returning to an analogy that I suspect is doing too much work. He asks us to imagine a city designing its local mobility system and choosing among three policies. Under the resources policy, every adult resident is issued exactly the same standard bicycle and the same modest maintenance allowance, no more and no less. Under the welfare policy, the city—which in the analogy controls the whole local transport and accessibility budget—asks each resident how satisfied they are with their overall mobility-related quality of life and then provides whatever package would leave everyone at the same self-reported level of satisfaction, whether that means a high-end e-bike for the enthusiastic cyclist, a car subsidy for someone in a poorly connected suburb, or some unrelated compensation for someone who simply does not care about cycling. Under the capabilities policy, the city instead aims to give every resident an equal real freedom to get around by bicycle if they want to, which might mean an e-bike for someone with a heart condition, a handcycle or a bike with outriggers for someone with a disability, better-lit streets and secure parking in a neighborhood where theft is common, and a free transit pass for someone who lives far from any safe bike lane, while not caring whether residents actually end up equally happy with the result or whether they own identical hardware. My friend uses the bike-share case to argue that the capability policy is obviously the correct one and that the other two are straw men: equal resources ignores that different bodies and neighborhoods turn the same bicycle into very different amounts of real freedom, and equal welfare panders to expensive tastes or to people who have adapted their desires downward, so it would lavish a fancy machine on the cycling enthusiast while leaving the resigned non-rider with a cash equivalent or an unrelated perk that makes them just as content, rather than with the genuine ability to ride. His conclusion is that the analogy shows what we should equalize is not the hardware and not the smiles, but the real opportunity to move around.

I want to audit that analogy rather than simply accept or reject it. Please give me four things. First, an explicit correspondence table for the bike-share analogy: what in the story plays the role of resources, of welfare, and of capabilities; what plays the role of the distributor, of the recipients, of the conversion of means into real freedom, and of choice or responsibility for how one uses the provision. Second, the places where the mapping breaks down, separating the disanalogies that are harmless for his argument from the ones that actually damage it. Third, an account of which features of a municipal bike-share or mobility system the analogy smuggles into the theory of justice—for example, that the good to be equalized is a single, discrete, publicly provisioned good with an obvious metric, that needs are observable and verifiable, that usage is voluntary and easy to measure, that interpersonal comparisons of satisfaction are as easy as a customer-satisfaction survey, that no one has expensive tastes or adaptive preferences about bicycles, and that the relevant freedom is one-dimensional—and say why those features are not true of welfare, resources, or capabilities in the actual debate. Fourth, a verdict on whether the conclusion that capabilities are the correct currency of egalitarian justice survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the whole argument.

Please be concrete: for each disanalogy you name, say which premise of my friend’s argument it undercuts, and if you think the bike-share analogy is too rigid, propose a better analogy or a modification of this one that a defender of equality of resources or of equality of welfare could also accept, explaining what each of the three views would want the analogy to build in and why the revised analogy is less misleading.
