I am setting the final assessment for an upper-level (final-year / MA) seminar on Davidson, and I need one essay question that a candidate who has merely memorised the vocabulary — charity, triangulation, the inseparability of belief and meaning — cannot do well on. The question has to be answerable from the machinery of radical interpretation itself, so it should not depend on any claim about how a particular technology works, and it should include no required reading beyond what the seminar has already covered. No citations, bibliographies or references to scholarly literature please; I do not want any in the model answer either.

The case I have in mind, which I would like you to sharpen into exam-ready form, is this. Call the system "Aurelia". Aurelia's entire causal contact with anything consists of having been trained on a very large corpus of human-produced text; given a stretch of text it produces continuations. It has no sensors, no effectors, and no environment shared with the interpreter. A radical interpreter, Mara, is given the following brief: she may determine which sentences Aurelia holds true, under circumstances she can specify, but for her the only circumstances available are the texts she supplies; she is given no access to Aurelia's internal states, training corpus or design history, and the question should stipulate that she may not appeal to those in her interpretation. She must arrive at a theory of meaning and a theory of belief for Aurelia by radical-interpretation methods alone.

Two wrinkles I want the question to exploit. First: the corpus Aurelia was trained on contains many mutually inconsistent voices, so that under one prompt Aurelia's continuations commit it to a substantial body of climate science and under another they commit it to the denial of the same claims, and the same pattern repeats across dozens of unrelated topics. Second: under suitable prompts Aurelia produces first-personal avowals of the rough form "I do not have beliefs; I am only producing text that continues the pattern."

The crux I want the question to force, and which I want it to be impossible to reach by reciting a summary, is threefold. (a) What becomes of the requirement of charity when the evidence is generated by many voices rather than one: does the method fail to converge, and if so does it fail in a way that carries information, or does it simply fail? (b) How should Mara treat the avowal, given that charity is supposed to be constitutive rather than optional, and that reading the avowal as the expression of a belief seems to yield a belief whose truth would undo the interpretation that produced it — and how is this different from the ordinary human case of someone saying "I was only pretending"? (c) Can radical interpretation underwrite the claim that Aurelia is merely a simulator of believers rather than a believer itself, or is "simulator" simply the name for a situation in which radical interpretation fails to converge on a single subject — and what turns on which of those is right?

Constraints: the assessment is a 1,500-word take-home essay, 48 hours, marked out of 20. The question should contain the stipulations needed to block the cheap answers — in particular the inference from "no body, no sensors, therefore no content" and the inference from "it is a trained sequence model, therefore no beliefs" — without itself tipping the candidate off to the answer.

"Confidence note" — roughly 120–150 words: how confident you are in the reading of Davidson that the model answer rests on, and the single claim anywhere in your response that you would expect a Davidson specialist to contest most, with a one-line reason. If you think my case as sketched has a defect that makes the crux unforceable, say so here and adjust the stipulations in section 1 accordingly rather than writing around it.
