Hey — I help run a tiny philosophy reading group (me, two friends, whoever wanders in), and we've gotten stuck on something I definately can't tell is a real distinction or just us reinventing a wheel.

Here's the pattern. Someone says "I can conceive of a world exactly like this one except X isn't in it" — X being consciousness, or causation, or time, or moral facts, whatever. It feels easy: you say the sentence, nothing explodes, you get a kind of blank mental image and move on. But when we actually try to describe the world in any detail, one of two things happens. Either we keep sneaking X back in (we describe the world "without causation" by saying how events unfold, which is causal talk), or we bottom out at "like this one, but without X," where that one little "but without" is doing all the work. It feels like the conceivability is borrowed against details we never filled in, and the loan gets called in the second we try to make the picture richer.

And it goes both ways, which is the part that bugs me. Some things get *more* conceivable the more you know: before you've done any physics, "no fact of the matter whether two distant events happened at the same time" sounds like nonsense; after, it's easy. So knowing more opens some scenarios up and closes others down — and the closing-down cases don't feel like finding a contradiction. They feel like finding out our vocabulary has no way to say the thing.

Someone in the group said there's a famous argument that some stuff which looks like it could have gone otherwise turns out to be necessary once you know the relevant facts about the world (identity-ish?). Close, but not it: in our cases there's no identity claim and no new discovery about the world, just the realization that we can't spell the scenario out.

Is there a name for the kind of "conceivable" that shrinks as you add detail? And for the two-directional thing? If not, coin something and give it edges — I want to be able to tell it apart from plain "I can't see a contradiction" and from a scenario that's just gibberish like a round square. Don't give me papers or a reading list, I've got no library access, just say what the thing is in your own words.

Lead with a one-line verdict ("there's an established concept: X" or "no established concept, I'd call it Y"), then the carving — what it is, what it isn't, and one boundary case. All of it under 300 words, no bullet list if you can manage it.
