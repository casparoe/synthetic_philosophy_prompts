My friend Priya and I spent most of last night arguing about whether anything you do inside a video game can be wrong *to* the character you do it to, and we got nowhere — partly, I think, because we kept sliding between "is this wrong?" and "does it corrupt the person doing it?" without noticing. I write narrative for a small studio, so this isn't idle for me: I spend my days deciding what players can do to the people in our games. I don't want to be sent to the literature on this; I'd rather start from my own verdicts, since they're the only thing here I'm actually confident about. Please answer in British English.

Here are my verdicts. Stomping hundreds of identical respawning hopping enemies in a 2D platformer: not wrong in the slightest. Betraying the hand-written companion in a story game, whose grief dialogue plays exactly the same however you treat her: not fine, but a *small* wrong, nowhere near betraying a friend — I'd call it a wrong of some attenuated kind. Walling in a sandbox creature that has hunger and an internal distress variable which visibly changes how it moves, and letting it starve: genuinely not fine, and worse if I do it slowly and on purpose. Torturing a creature whose entire state is regenerated from a seed every frame — no memory, no persistence, no continuity between moments: no moral residue at all, and Priya agrees. Switching on a headless server where that same distress variable is computed but never rendered or read by anyone: I feel a faint, unjustified unease, and I suspect that's sentimentality. Torturing a creature whose code is a copy of a scanned living person's brain — deleted afterwards, never interacting with anyone: this seems to me close to actual torture, and it's the one case where Priya abandoned her "no one is really there" line, which surprised me given how quickly we agreed on the every-frame case. Bullying a villager whose lines are generated at runtime by a language model: Priya thinks this is much worse than bullying one with pre-written branching lines, I feel no difference whatsoever, and neither of us can say why we diverge. Hurting the sneering character the designers built to be enjoyed hurting: not a wrong to anyone. Going off-script to hurt the character the designers built to be loved: worse, and I don't think I can explain that by anything about the character. And here is the pair I can't reconcile: I feel worse about a creature with *no* internal state whatsoever but a long, gruesome death animation than about one with a rich distress variable whose death is a single pixel quietly fading — which seems to make the wrong depend on how things look to me rather than on how they are for it, and that feels like a confession rather than a view.

My own tentative principle, such as it is: a game character can be wronged by me only if it has valenced states that counterfactually depend on what I do, and separately — this is the part I think might be a second principle in a trench coat — some of what I'm tracking is only about what my action expresses or makes of me, not about the character at all. Can you tell me whether that really is one principle or two, test it against the cases above, repair it where it gives an answer I'd reject, and say plainly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which one I ought to give up? I'd also like three further cases the repaired principle settles that I haven't thought of, including at least one where it returns an answer I'd find unacceptable. And the case I keep returning to, which last night's argument didn't settle either: I was cruel to a companion character in a game when I was fifteen, and thinking about it still bothers me now — is that evidence about her, or only about me?
