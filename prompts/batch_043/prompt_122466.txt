I am a PhD student in philosophy writing a seminar paper on the duty to remember and the case for forgetting, and I’m using Avishai Margalit’s ethics of memory and Philip Rieff’s critique of therapeutic culture as my two main touchstones. I’d like you to act as an expert in the philosophy of memory and an experienced critic of film and literature. Please think carefully and, before writing your final response, start with a short brainstorming section in which you sketch two or three candidate readings of the film and flag what each gets right. Then commit to a single, definite interpretation.

The object is Charlie Kaufman and Michel Gondry’s film *Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind*. The philosophical lens is the duty to remember versus the therapeutic case for forgetting. As Margalit argues, a duty to remember attaches to what he calls thick relations—paradigmatically relations of love and shared belonging—because the memories of those we love help constitute who we are and sustain the moral community; forgetting in such relations is not mere information loss but a kind of betrayal, and he distinguishes the shared memories of individuals from the common memory of a community with its division of mnemonic labor. Rieff, by contrast, offers a conservative cultural critique: modern therapeutic culture replaces the older demand to bear the moral and tragic weight of the past with the aim of personal adjustment and relief, so that the modern self is encouraged to manage, minimize, or therapeutically forget painful memories in order to be well.

Read the film through these ideas. What does the film dramatize, complicate, or get wrong about the duty to remember and the case for forgetting? Which scenes or characters’ choices does the lens illuminate? I am thinking especially of Joel’s hunt through his own memories to hide Clementine from the erasure procedure, Clementine’s initial decision to have Joel wiped, the subplot in which Mary discovers she has repeatedly had an erased affair with Dr. Mierzwiak, and the closing sequence in which the couple receive the audio tapes recording their own complaints and nevertheless decide to begin again. Where, if anywhere, does the film resist the philosophical reading, and where would an honest critic have to say that the ideas are being bent to fit the plot?

To push the reading further, I want to inject an idea from my own dissertation. Call it prosthetic counter-memory. When a person cannot or will not maintain a painful memory internally, they may offload it onto an external artifact or institution that retains the bare fact of the memory and the decision to forget—as the Lacuna clinic and its patient tapes do in the film. My questions: does Margalit’s framework, which stresses shared and common memory within a thick relation or community, have the resources to recognize a duty to maintain such prosthetic counter-memory, or does his view presuppose that real memory must be internally held and mutually recognized? And would Rieff regard the Lacuna tapes as the logical endpoint of the therapeutic mentality—outsourcing memory altogether so the self can be unburdened—or would he see the fact that the tapes preserve self-knowledge as a lingering, if deformed, remnant of the older moral demand?

Please give me a committed response. Do not merely lay out the options and leave me to choose: tell me definitely whether the film vindicates Margalit’s duty to remember, Rieff’s therapeutic case for forgetting, or a third position that shows both frameworks miss something important about memory, identity, or intimacy in the film—and give your reasons. Because this is a work of fiction and not an argument, I also want you to be self-critical about the interpretation: add a short note on where the film takes liberties with Margalit’s and Rieff’s ideas, for example by literalizing a moral duty as a medical procedure, or where your reading leans on a contestable interpretation of their work. Your answer should be polished enough to use as a discussant’s notes for a graduate seminar.
