Hey — I'm in a small reading group working through the metaphysics of dispositions (fragility, solubility, that kind of thing) and we got into an arguement I can't settle, so I'm hoping you can sort us out.

The way I understood it, a fink is just any case where something interferes and so the disposition fails to show up when you test for it. So the classic case where a fragile glass is wired to a device that switches its fragility off the instant anything strikes it, and the case where you just wrap the same glass in foam before striking it, look like the same problem to me: in neither case does the glass break when struck, so both kill the simple conditional analysis (something is fragile just in case it would break if struck). So: why does anyone bother splitting finks off from masks or antidotes at all, and doesn't a single fix handle both? It seems like you just add a clause saying the thing would break if struck *and nothing interferes*, and you're done. And since a mask is the case where the disposition is still sitting there fully intact while a fink is the case where it's literally gone by the time the stimulus arrives, aren't masks strictly the harder case — which would make all the ink spilled on finks kind of beside the point?

What actually started the fight is a case someone in the group brought up that we ended up nicknaming an auto-fink: take a metal that reacts vigorously with water, but which on contact with water instantly grows a thin oxide skin that *mostly* (not entirely) shuts the reaction down, so the reaction starts and then tails off. Or, to make it a pure degrees case, a vase wired up so that being struck makes it half as fragile as it was, so it cracks rather than shatters. We couldn't agree whether that's a fink, a mask, or a third kind of thing; I said it's obviously just a fink with extra steps, since the stimulus is what degrades the disposition. But the more I turn it over, the more it seems to me that the whole fink/mask distinction only gets a grip if dispositions are all-or-nothing — if they come in degrees the taxonomy collapses, and then doesn't the honest conclusion become that we should stop talking about dispositions at all and just redescribe everything in terms of whatever categorical microstuff the glass or the metal is made of? I'd also like to know whether the fancy reformulation that swaps the plain conditional for a claim about the thing having some intrinsic basis that would produce the manifestation does any better with our auto-fink than the naive version does, because it looks to me like it inherits exactly the same problem.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give me the reasoning — no preamble, and please don't restate the question back at me.
