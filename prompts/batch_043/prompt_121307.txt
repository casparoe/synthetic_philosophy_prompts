Me and two other people from my first-year ethics tutorial got into an argument about trolley cases that went on until like 1am and we never got anywhere, and I've been trying since then to figure out what I actually believe when I go by gut instead of by theory. The doctrine of double effect is supposed to explain the pattern — the act itself isn't the bad kind of thing, I'm not aiming at the bad part, the good doesn't come about *by way of* the bad part, and the good is big enough to outweigh it — but I don't think it captures all my verdicts. Here's where I land. Plain switch case, five tied to the main line, one on the spur: fine, flip it, no hesitation. Loop variant, where the spur curves back around and the one guy's body is what stops the trolley from reaching the five: I still say fine, but I feel queasy in a way I don't in the plain case and I can't say why. Fat man on the footbridge, shove him: not fine. Same guy but there's a trapdoor under him that I open: also not fine, but a degree less bad, which is stupid, it's the same death. Trapdoor that I rigged myself last Tuesday on the off chance I ever needed it and now just press a button for: worse than the unrigged one, even though I have no idea why the date I installed it should matter at all. One on the spur only gets a broken leg instead of dying: obviously fine, flip it. Switch is already set toward the spur and I can either leave it or throw it back so the trolley goes to the five: I think leaving it is fine and throwing it back is wrong, but that's the same choice as the first case with the default moved, which feels like it shouldn't matter and does. And here's the pair I think actually breaks things: the guy on the spur is already doomed — headphones on, and twenty yards past him the spur is about to get buried by a landslide, so he's dead in fifteen seconds whatever I do — and flipping feels not just fine but *easier* than the ordinary switch case, like it barely counts. But then suppose the fat man is also already doomed, same kind of deal, and I still think shoving him is not fine. If the doomed-spur case got easier because I'm not making him worse off than he would otherwise be, then the doomed fat man isn't made worse off either, so it should get easier too, and it definately doesn't.

My own stab at a principle is two clauses: (i) what I'm doing is worse the more it leaves someone worse off than they'd otherwise have been, and (ii) it's worse if I need their body to do some work — in the switch case I'd save the five even if the guy on the spur miraculously stepped aside, whereas the fat man has to be hit or nothing happens. But (ii) says the loop case should be as bad as the fat man case and I don't think it is, so either (ii) is wrong or my verdict on the loop is wrong. Can you give me one principle, or at most two or three candidates, stated precisely enough that I could actually apply it to a new case, tell me which clause is doing the work in which case, and be honest about which of my verdicts each one has to throw out? I'd also like to know what the best candidate says about two cases I keep going back and forth on and haven't settled: the one where the guy on the spur is the same guy who tied the five to the main line fifteen minutes ago and is standing there gloating (I feel flipping is not just allowed but almost required, and I can't tell if that's a real moral difference about liability or just retributive glee in a costume), and the one where he's a volunteer who signed a card in advance saying he wants to be the one hit if it ever comes up.
