Please explain, at a conceptual level, why it has turned out to be hard to give a formal definition of minimizing side effects that does not amount to an implicit specification of what is valuable — and tell me which of the difficulties you take to be the deepest, with your reasons.

I want to concentrate on one structural distinction. Most proposals I am familiar with measure a system's impact relative to some baseline: either the way the world would have evolved had the system never acted (the inaction baseline), or the system's own ability, before as against after acting, to bring about a spread of arbitrary goals. The trouble seems to be that such a baseline still needs a notion of how far apart two possible worlds are, and any such notion either counts physically tiny but important changes as enormous (or the reverse), or weights changes in a way that already encodes what matters.

Consider this variant, which I will call the counterfactual-principal baseline: compare what actually happens not to inaction, but to a rollout — produced by the system's own predictive model of its operator — of what the operator would have brought about had they not delegated the task. Call a penalty built on any of these baselines compensable if unwanted deviations along different features can offset one another (a large change here balanced by an opposite change elsewhere), and non-compensable if they cannot.

Does the counterfactual-principal baseline escape the need for a theory of value, or does predicting a valuing operator reintroduce the very weighting we were trying to avoid — and is there a principled difference between learning such a weighting and having it specified? Does it fix the two familiar failures of the inaction baseline: penalizing a system for preventing a bad thing it was never asked to prevent, and yet leaving it entirely free to bring about a bad thing that would have happened anyway? And is compensability a bug or a feature — does permitting offsetting invite gaming, or does forbidding it simply smuggle in a lexicographic ordering over what matters?

Commit to a verdict on the headline question — whether a value-free impact measure is possible even in principle — state your confidence, and mark where you think your argument is most fragile.
