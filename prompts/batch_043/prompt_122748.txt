Write a parody of a monastic Rule — the founding constitution of a fictional religious community, the Order of the Greatest Net Difference (invented motto: *Non quantum dederis, sed quantum effeceris*), which is in fact a disciplined, devout community of people who earn to give. Follow the shape of a traditional Christian monastic rule, with numbered chapters headed "On the Abbot," "On the Novitiate," "On the Manual Labour of the Brethren," "On the Faults of the Brethren," "On the Divine Office," "On the Goods of the Monastery," and "On the Ledger of Offsets." Roughly 800 to 1,000 words, in numbered clauses, entirely deadpan: the voice is a sincere founder who takes the Rule to be mere good sense, never acknowledges that he is founding a religion, and never winks at the reader. No footnotes, no doubting novice, no punchlines that require the narrator to notice the joke.

The humor has to come from getting the reasoning right. Build the Rule around these real features of the practice, exaggerating each rather than inventing straw men, so that a sympathetic practitioner would concede the caricature is unfair but accurate:

- Vocation as career discernment. The abbot assigns brethren to lucrative industries they find unglamorous; the novitiate consists of accumulating what the Rule calls career capital; a brother who wants to do work he finds meaningful is suspected of a disordered love of the visible.
- Counterfactual replaceability. A vocation is valid only if the brother's marginal impact exceeds that of whoever would have taken the job instead, and the Rule must take some stable line on the awkward case where the replacement would have been equally generous — including whether the answer changes if the replacement would have given to a worse cause.
- The vow of deferred giving. Gifts are invested for compound growth rather than disbursed, because a larger future endowment will do more good than smaller present gifts; the Rule should let this principle run far enough to be uncomfortable, and the order's own figures (quoted consistently) should show an endowment that dwarfs everything it has ever granted.
- Meta-charity. Recruiting a new brother, or funding the evaluation of causes, is treated as higher-value than any first-order grant, so the recruitment and audit budgets grow past the giving budget, and the abbot's standing is measured in conversions.
- Measurability. The order may fund only what can be audited, preferably by randomized trial with effects visible within a year; over successive clauses its charitable works narrow accordingly, and the monastery's own soup kitchen is finally defunded for want of a control group.
- Faults of the brethren. Warm glow, values drift, loyalty to a place, and the desire to help someone whose face you can see are disciplined as vices; conversely, causing harm in the course of earning is enterable as a debit against the gift.

Let the Rule build to a final chapter where the logic turns on itself: the order accepts a contract it would once have refused — say, work for a predatory lender — on the grounds that the counterfactual hire would be worse and the resulting harm is fully offset by the donation, and the abbot rules that keeping the ledger of offsets has become the community's highest act of worship, the Divine Office having been quietly reduced to it. A reader who has defended these practices should laugh and admit the satire is fair; a reader who has only read about the movement should still be able to follow it. Avoid any joke that depends on naming a real person or organization, living or dead — the target is the shape of the reasoning, not individuals — and keep every invented figure internally consistent across clauses.
