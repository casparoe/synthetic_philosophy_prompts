I'm a journalist finishing a long feature on the fashion for binding one's own hands — deposit schemes and commitment apps, medication-assisted treatment agreements, independent central banks, constitutional debt brakes, supermajority amendment rules, and the current run of proposals to entrench emissions targets so that no later parliament can quietly drop them. Before I file, I want to print alongside the piece a short joint statement from the two positions in this debate that have spent the longest talking past each other, written as though they had finally sat down for an adversarial collaboration. The signatories are:

- the diachronic-autonomy view: an agent can legitimately bind its own later self, and binding is sometimes not a surrender of agency but the exercise of agency over the stretch of time it actually occupies; on this view the Ulysses contract and the entrenched constitutional clause are the same act at different scales, and what stands in need of explanation is not why precommitment is permissible but why anyone would think a later reversal of will should defeat it.
- the present-will view: a bare self-binding is either paternalism by an earlier self over a later one or an incoherent attempt to turn a present intention into standing law, and what legitimises most real commitment devices is something else — the reliance or consent of third parties, the prevention of harm to others, or the plain fact that the device changes what the later agent ends up wanting; on this view constitutional entrenchment is governance by the dead and stands or falls by its effects on people who never agreed to it, not by any story about the people having bound themselves.

State each at its strongest, not as a version its holder would disown.

To keep both of them honest I want them tested against a specimen I will describe in the piece — call it the Orpheus Clause. A legislature enacts, by ordinary majority, a statute that (i) fixes some policy instrument, say a floor price in an emissions trading scheme, for twenty-five years; (ii) declares ordinary repeal void; (iii) permits repeal by simple majority after year ten, but only if a citizens' assembly convened for the purpose certifies in writing that the predicament the clause was enacted to address no longer obtains, and the repealing legislature names in the text of the repeal which of the clause's stated reasons it takes to have lapsed; and (iv) is enforceable by the ordinary courts, which may enjoin the executive from administering any inconsistent policy. Does the review mechanism in (iii) make the clause more legitimate or turn it into a mere statement of intent? Does court enforcement in (iv) matter, given that Ulysses needed sailors — i.e., is there any such thing as a purely self-enforcing precommitment, or do third parties always do the real work?

Two further cases I suspect are cruxes. First, the signature problem: a well-designed precommitment should defeat later weakness of will but not later learning, yet no clause can tell the difference, because an agent in the grip of a preference reversal will always describe itself as having learned something. Say whether this is soluble for the Orpheus Clause, and whether its insolubility counts against entrenchment, in favour of the review mechanism, or neither. Second, the ratchet case: contrast a clause restraining a future majority from spending beyond a fiscal rule with a clause restraining a future majority from raising foreign aid or a carbon price — a clause, that is, that precommits against later generosity rather than later weakness. Does each view treat the two symmetrically, and if not, why not?

Format it as something I can print in about 1,000 to 1,400 words: a numbered section of claims both signatories accept, including claims about what the Orpheus Clause actually does and what would follow if it were struck down; then a table of the residual disagreements, each row giving the claim in the words its own holder would use, not in my paraphrase; then the cruxes, and for each an argument, case, or observable outcome both agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Where you conclude that part of their dispute is merely verbal — about autonomy, about identity over time, about what counts as a dead hand — say which part, and identify the substantive question underneath it. Do not deliver a verdict on who is right; I can write that myself.
