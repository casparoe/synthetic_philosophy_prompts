I would like a careful comparison of two formulations of the paradox of phenomenal judgment — the difficulty, for an epiphenomenalist about phenomenal consciousness, of accounting for her own judgments about her own qualia.

Call the first the causal-contact formulation. On this version the trouble is evidential: if a quale is causally inert, then it cannot cause the judgment that one is in it, and so the judgment stands in no causal contact with the very thing it is about. The usual reply is that the quale and the judgment share a neural common cause, so that the judgment is reliably correlated with the quale and can therefore amount to knowledge of it, even though the quale contributes nothing to producing it.

Call the second the reference-fixing formulation. On this version the trouble is not causal but semantic: if what fixes the reference of a phenomenal term is whatever causally regulates the subject's use of that term, and the quale plays no such regulatory role, then the term, as used by the epiphenomenalist, refers to the neural or functional state rather than to the quale. On this version she cannot so much as formulate her own view, and the common-cause reply answers a question she never gets to ask.

Please address the following, roughly in the order given.

1. Are these two formulations genuinely distinct arguments, or is one of them — and if so, which — reducible to the other? State what each would have to establish that the other does not.

2. Which of the two is the harder problem for the epiphenomenalist? Which of them, if sound, would tell against a wider range of views about consciousness rather than against epiphenomenalism alone?

3. Compare the two formulations with respect to their scope outside the philosophy of mind. The causal-contact formulation looks like a special case of the general difficulty of having knowledge of causally inert items, paradigmatically abstract objects such as numbers. Is that generalization a reductio of the formulation, or a resource the epiphenomenalist can exploit? Does the reference-fixing formulation generalize in any analogous way?

4. Consider a case I will call qualia drift. Early in life a subject has a pain whenever neural state N occurs, and judges that she is in pain whenever N occurs. Over the years the qualia accompanying N rotate — first to a neutral buzz, then to a mild pleasure — while N, the subject's behaviour, and her readiness to judge that she is in pain are held fixed. No third-person investigation, and no evidence available to the subject herself, could detect the change; and the epiphenomenalist, it seems, cannot rule the case out a priori, since by her own lights nothing physical depends on which qualia occur. What does each formulation, together with its favoured reply, predict about the status of the subject's judgments? Does this case discriminate between the two formulations, and does the verdict you gave in (2) survive it?

Where the two formulations disagree, say plainly which one you take to be the deeper objection and why, and identify the strongest move still available to the epiphenomenalist against it.
