ok here's one I keep goign back and forth on, and I want it run as an actual case analysis, not a vibes piece.

Aristotle on money as I half-remember it — correct me if I'm mangling: in the bit about reciprocal justice in the Ethics, money comes in as a device that makes incomparable things commensurable, a shared measure that also works as a kind of pledge for exchange that hasn't happened yet; and it's conventional (nomisma from nomos), so in principle the community can void it by changing the convention. Then in the wealth-getting chapters of the Politics there's the distinction between running a household and limitless acquisition, plus the claim that the most hated kind of gain is the one where the profit comes from the money itself rather than from what money was invented for — interest, offspring, money breeding.

Take bitcoin's first fifteen years or so as the test case for that theory. I mean the whole arc: the 2008 whitepaper, the 2010 guy who paid 10,000 coins for two pizzas, Mt. Gox going under in 2014, the 2017 hard fork that produced a second bitcoin, El Salvador making it legal tender in 2021, the run-up and the 2022 collapse, and the staking-and-yield industry that grew up around all of it.

I want the mapping done carefully before any verdict. On this theory, what even is the household here, and what would count as the use of a coin as opposed to its exchange? Is mining — real capital, real electricity, output is a ledger entry — household management, the disreputable kind of acquisition, or something the distinction has no slot for? Where does a yield-bearing account sit relative to the offspring-of-money objection? Is "number go up" just the unlimited-acquisition worry with better marketing? And deal honestly with the deflationary dodge: a money with a hard cap seems to block the "limitless" charge, so either that's a rescue or it shows the cap was never what the worry was about.

Then run it the other direction: what does the case do to the theory? My own hunch, which I'd rather you argue with than flatter, is that mining is neither of Aristotle's two categories but a third thing the theory can't generate: activity whose product is the measure itself. If that's right, the measure is being produced by the exchanges it's supposed to neutrally gauge, which looks like a structural problem for the theory, not just a new application of it. But I can also see the reply — maybe the conventionalist half was always doing more work than the provisioning half, and a money nobody can void is exactly what his conventionalism predicts will come apart, with the forks as the proof, since "which chain is the real one" turned out to be a social fact after all. Also tell me whether "bitcoin isn't money, so the theory never applies" is a genuine point or a dodge.

No citations, footnotes, or reading lists — assume neither of us can look anything up, and I don't want invented line numbers. Just do the case, say where the theory runs out, and don't manufacture a verdict where it's silent.
