I maintain a small reading group of working programmers — none of us has any formal philosophy training — and for our next session I want to build an interactive flowchart on a static page: the visitor answers a question, descends a branch, and lands on a named position. I would like you to design that flowchart for the Sleeping Beauty problem. I can write the HTML myself; what I need is the diagnostic structure, because my own attempts keep collapsing into a poll between one half and one third.

For completeness, the setup we are using. On Sunday a fair coin is flipped and Beauty is put to sleep. If it lands heads she is awakened once, on Monday; if tails she is awakened twice, on Monday and on Tuesday. After each awakening she is given a drug that erases her memory of it, so every awakening is subjectively indistinguishable from every other. She knows all of this in advance. On waking she is asked: what should her credence be that the coin landed heads? That, plus the observation that both sides' arguments fit on a napkin, is roughly the extent of what I can reliably state about the debate, which is why I would rather have an instrument that sorts people by what they say about cases than one that asks them to pick a label.

What I want from you:

A decision tree of between eight and twelve decision nodes that takes a reader from accepting the setup above to a leaf, with the following constraints.

Each node is a forced choice about a concrete case or a concrete claim, phrased so that someone who has never heard the vocabulary can answer it. No node may ask, in any form, whether the answer is one third or one half, or which named position the reader holds. Build the tree from the logical space where you can — first the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then the actual positions located in that space — and tell me which combinations of answers are unoccupied and why each one is empty: incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unoccupied because nobody has bothered.

At least one node must be about what credences are for rather than what they are: whether the number that should govern your action equals the number you would name as your credence, given that how many times you get to act depends on which branch you are in. My group only started taking the problem seriously once it was put as expected score across awakenings versus expected score per experiment, so I want that distinction to be load-bearing in the tree rather than a footnote.

At least one node must be about whether learning a self-locating fact on waking — being told it is Monday — can be evidence about the coin, and at least one about what happens when the number of subjectively identical copies of you differs between branches.

Three cases the tree has to use:

1. The canonical case above.
2. A duplication variant: one copy of Beauty on heads, a thousand subjectively identical copies on tails, each asked the same question.
3. A variant I invented and cannot find discussed anywhere, which is the main reason I am writing to you instead of reading an encyclopedia entry. As in the canonical case, except that on each awakening Beauty is shown a card that is blue if this is her first awakening of the experiment and green otherwise, and she knows the card is printed correctly with probability 0.85, independently on each awakening. She has no other way of telling whether she has been awake before. In my group, people who gave the same answer to the canonical case came apart on this one, and I could not tell whether the new disagreement was about the variant or about a difference in background view that the canonical case conceals. So please tell me where this case belongs in the tree, whether it separates any pair of leaves the canonical case cannot, and — for each leaf you name — what that position has to say about the number Beauty should give on waking to a blue card. If the case is ill-posed, or if some leaf is committed to refusing to answer it, say so explicitly; that is a result I can use. And if my variant is really a known case under a different description, tell me which one.

Leaves I want to appear. I have probably mangled some of the labels, so please correct me and tell me which of these have standard names in the literature:

- the view that the answer is one third, reached by treating the three possible awakening-situations as subjectively symmetric (I have seen this called the thirder or Elga-style position);
- the view that the answer is one half, because nothing Beauty learns on waking was not already certain to her on Sunday (I have seen Lewisian halfer);
- the view that the answer is one half on waking and still one half after she is told it is Monday (I have seen double-halfer);
- a view that arrives at one third by way of a general rule about reasoning as if you were a random sample from some class of observers, rather than by way of the symmetry argument. I want this kept separate from the first leaf if the two come apart anywhere, and merged with a flag if they do not;
- a view on which the question has no determinate answer — the setup does not single out a unique rational credence — and, if it is genuinely distinct, a sibling on which the rational state is an interval rather than a point;
- a view on which there is no such thing as self-locating credence of the kind the question asks for, or on which the question is well-posed only relative to a specified decision problem.

Two further things. First, flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate, and name the case that would separate them if you were allowed one more node; I would rather see the seam than have it hidden. Second, flag any node that smuggles in a contested assumption — one that presupposes, say, that there is a fact about which observers belong to your reference class, or that rational credence must be a sharp probability — so that I do not ship a quiz that begs the question at step three.

For output, I would like a table of nodes with an identifier, the question text exactly as a reader would see it, and the outgoing branches; a list of leaves, each with one sentence on the position's most awkward commitment; and a path-to-leaf key I can implement as a tally over answers. Markdown is fine.
