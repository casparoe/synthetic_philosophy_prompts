I hold a view about the politics of naming atrocities that I would like stress-tested, and I would like you to argue against it rather than for it. No citations, case law, or reading lists are needed: I am interested in whether the argument itself has soft spots, so reason from the concepts and from cases you can describe in general terms.

Call the view legal anchoring. It has three parts. First, the word genocide has a settled content given by the international definition: the intentional destruction, in whole or in part, of a national, ethnical, racial, or religious group targeted as such. When activists, states, or commentators press for the word to be applied or withheld, the right response is to return to that content and ask whether the specific intent requirement is met. Second, the term's peculiar gravity is inherited from its legal pedigree: it is not merely a superlative for mass killing but the name of a legally cognizable act-type, and the difference between genocide and other mass atrocity is a real moral difference, not a lawyer's quibble. A state that aims at a group's destruction as such does something worse than one that kills comparable numbers through indifference, counterinsurgency, or greed. Third, there are genuine costs to loose application: a term stretched to cover every atrocity inflates, and inflation erodes both the attention and the institutional machinery that attach to it, while handing critics the easy reply that naming is just rhetoric.

What I want from you is a map of the view's exposure. Please give me the defeaters, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, distinguishing what a committed legal anchoring theorist would have to concede as counting against the view from what such a theorist can reasonably dismiss as changing the subject. I have four candidate lines of attack in mind, and I would like you to develop them, improve them, or replace them with better ones.

The first is the etiology objection. Lemkin coined the term to name a pattern: the coordinated assault on a group's conditions of continued existence, which on his account included its language, cultural production, economic base, and leadership, and which on some readings can be carried out without killing at scale. The Convention that fixed the legal definition did not carry over the cultural dimension. If the point of the category was to render a social pattern visible and actionable, then fidelity to the legal definition can be infidelity to the phenomenon the category was invented to track. What would establish that the omitted elements are central to the pattern rather than peripheral to it, and what would a defender of anchoring have to grant if that were shown?

The second concerns what I will call the asymmetry of evidentiary production. Because liability turns on specific intent, the availability of the label depends on access to perpetrators' aims, which in practice means access to state archives. But perpetrators of group destruction have strong incentives both to generate public justifications that deny group-destructive intent and to destroy or seal the records that would reveal it. A high evidentiary standard therefore does not sit neutrally between competing descriptions: it defaults to the description the perpetrator has an interest in producing. Under uncertainty, withholding the label is not a suspension of judgment but a judgment with costs of its own. If that asymmetry is real, is it a defect in anchoring, or merely an unfortunate feature of any evidentiary regime?

The third is about borrowed gravity. If the term's force comes from its legal pedigree, then a moral judgment is hostage to institutional contingency: to which states ratify and with what reservations, to charging decisions, to the interpretive narrowing of tribunals, to the fact that no court may ever have jurisdiction. Does the view have a coherent answer here, and is the answer available to it at all? One move is to say that the law codifies an independently existing moral kind and that the moral judgment survives the law's failures. But then what work is the legal definition doing in the view, and why should the moral kind have the legal boundary rather than some other one?

The fourth is the threshold problem. The destruction of a group and the intent to destroy it look scalar, while the legal category is a threshold concept with a discontinuity built in, and the location of the threshold is settled by nothing in the theory: the in part clause requires a theory of how large or how significant a part, and no such theory is supplied by the concept itself. So anchoring may claim a precision it does not have. Is that decisive, or does every workable moral category face the same problem and hence face it equally?

Two further things I would like. First, tell me what the view predicts that its main rival does not. The rival I find most tempting holds that genocide names a moral kind fixed by the phenomenon of group destruction, with the legal definition one imperfect operationalization among possible others; if you think there is a stronger rival, use that one. Give me the observations, cases, or arguments that would discriminate between them, including anything that has arguably already happened. Second, be honest about whether my view has been qualified into unfalsifiability. If every disputed application can be explained away as politics and every refusal to apply as due process, the view is consistent with everything and risks nothing. Say plainly whether that is so, and if it is, say whether insulation is a vice for a view of this kind, which is part conceptual, part moral, and part institutional.
