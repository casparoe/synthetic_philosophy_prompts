hi — you're a decision scientist with a strong game-theory background who has advised a lot of institutions through exactly this shape of mess, so please actually think it through rather than reaching for a slogan; if it helps, rough out the structural diagnosis first and do tell me if the framing I give below is wrong, I'd rather be corrected than have my confusion politely validated.

I sit on my university's Capital and Space Planning Committee, and we have built ourselves a dollar auction. Two years ago we recieved a restricted bequest to convert the old campus observatory — one building, one use, and the donor's terms don't let us split it. Physics wants it as a public astronomy and outreach centre; a digital humanities group wants it as a text-analysis lab with exhibition space. Both have been spending their own departmental funds on feasibility studies, architect's renderings, outside consultants — Physics around $180k, the DH group around $150k, which is serious money for departments our size. Somewhere along the way the spending changed character: it's no longer about finding out whether a plan works, it's about being the one still standing when we decide. The latest round is a request from each side for another ~$40k of central funds to "complete the submission," and everyone in the room privately knows three things: whoever has spent more will win, the extra studies won't change a single mind, and between them the two groups have now burnt more than the difference between the two outcomes is worth to either of them.

My instinct going in was to say: sunk costs are sunk, tell both departments the money is gone, evaluate the two proposals on their merits, done. But a colleague pushed back and it's been bugging me since — in the dollar auction the next bid isn't really a bid for the prize, it's a bid to avoid the certainty of losing what you've already put in, and at the moment you make it it looks like it has positive expected value given the chance the other side stops. If that's right, then "just ignore the sunk costs" is advice that doesn't reach the case at all, and calling two sensible departments irrational is both false and useless. There's a second-order version of the same thing inside our own committee: reversing course now means admitting we let this run for two years, and I can feel us escalating on our own account. Our subcommittee floated two mechanisms and I can't tell if either is any good. One, call it the salvage rule: the losing side gets back a fixed fraction (say 25%) of documented planning costs out of the bequest, but only if both sides stop all advocacy spending now — the idea being to make one more round of spending expectably negative. The obvious objection, which one of our members makes loudly, is that this rewards the departments for having spent irresponsibly and teaches every future unit that escalating gets you reimbursed, so it solves this game by making the threat to escalate more credible in the next one. Two, a fresh-eyes rule: hand the actual decision to people who didn't authorise the original spending, on the theory that the sunk cost then really is sunk to them.

What I need is the memo I'm circulating before Friday's meeting — To: the committee, From: me, Re: the observatory conversion — recommending a concrete course of action with dates, not a menu of options, and engaging the strongest version of the "let it run and just pick a winner" position honestly rather than batting it down.
