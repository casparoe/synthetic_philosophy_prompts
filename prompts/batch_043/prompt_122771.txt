I write novels, and over the past several years a cluster of institutional habits surrounding work like mine has begun to look like a contradiction that I cannot state precisely enough either to accept or to argue with. I would like you to reconstruct the theory of meaning which these habits presuppose, and to tell me whether the habits can be held together.

The practices, as concretely as I can put them:

First, when a novelist dies with a series unfinished, the estate appoints a successor, the successor is paid to write *as* the dead author, and the book is sold under the dead author's name in the larger type with the successor's name beneath it. Fidelity is judged by whether the result reads like her. I have recently been advised to keep a document for exactly this purpose, and what struck me is what it is not: not a plot outline, not a list of events still to happen, but a set of constraints on the narrator — what she may not know, what she may not withhold, what she may not feel — together with notes on the dispositions of the recurring characters. The trade appears to believe that what makes a continuation *the same novel going on* is not the sequence of events but a voice specified largely by prohibitions. Relatedly, publishing contracts assign *the work and the characters therein* as if a character were an object detachable from the sentences in which it occurs and portable into sentences written by somebody else, long after I am dead.

Second, and in apparent tension: when the living author says what her book means, the institutions that review and teach books treat that statement as at best one reading among others, and often as a mistake about her own authority — the familiar view, which I absorbed in workshops and which reviewers repeat as settled, that an author's avowed aim does not determine what her text says. Yet the same publishers, adapters, prize committees, and libel insurers keep asking me what I meant and keep treating the answer as settling something real: an authorised adaptation is authorised by my saying so, and a character's resemblance to a living person is defended by my saying I did not intend it.

Third, for the archive: scholarly editions of modern novels are built to recover the text the author finally wanted, overriding the publisher's house style and sometimes overriding the author's own last printed revision where that revision looks coerced, tired, or made for money.

What I want is the implicit theory, stated in plain English with each technical term explained when it is introduced, and argued rather than surveyed. Specifically:

Does the continuation trade presuppose that a novel's identity consists in counterfactual facts about what its author *would have written*, and is that a different kind of thing from the author's intention about what her text *means*? I would like the relevant kinds of intention prised apart — intention in the writing, intention about the meaning of the finished text, intention about what comes next — and I want to know whether a person who denies the second any authority can lean on the third without inconsistency, or whether the two stand and fall together.

Is the constraints document best explained by any intentionalist theory at all, or is it better explained by the thought that what is really being preserved is the reader's experience of a voice — in which case the practice is a theory about uptake wearing intentionalist clothing, and the trade's own vocabulary misdescribes what it is doing?

Does any of it require that a character be the *same entity* in a text written by a different person, and what sort of entity would that have to be?

And then the discipline I care most about: distinguish what these practices strictly presuppose from what they merely sit well with, and mark where they are underdetermined — where several rival accounts of meaning would license exactly the same practice, so that the practice cannot be used as evidence for any of them. Say where the theory is silent instead of forcing it to speak.

Finally, what would have to change if the anti-intentionalist half were taken seriously? Would authorised continuation have to be re-described as a distinct art form with its own conventions — pastiche, homage, fan canon promoted to the trade list — or could the practice survive on some other footing, and would it have to be marketed differently?

Two constraints. I am not asking for a map of the literature: no schools, no reading lists, no citations, since I cannot chase them and could not judge them; I want the argument itself, in a form I could use in a conversation with my agent or my heirs. And this recurs — several times a year I am asked to sign a canon statement, a continuity note, or a clause defining *the work* — so there is money and an afterlife at stake, and a clause drafted wrongly binds people who have not yet been born.

Where a claim is substantive, mark how confident you are in it — confident, probable, or speculative — and close by naming the single claim you are least sure of, and what would change your mind about it.
