I teach a 90-minute seminar on rational agency and machine intelligence for about 16 upper-level undergraduates. They have already met the Prisoner's Dilemma and the definition of Nash equilibrium in a previous session. About a third of them have never written a line of code. Almost none of them have any logic or computability background, and none of them should hear the phrase "program equilibrium" until the debrief: the point has to arrive as something they ran into, not as a label they were handed.

I want a runnable, paper-only game (no laptops, no real money) in which each team submits not an action but a *program* — a short, explicitly structured object that is handed the other team's program as input and returns COOPERATE or DEFECT. Design the whole session for me.

**The rules and the object language.** Specify a tiny surface language, small enough to be written on an index card and hand-evaluated by a non-programmer in under a minute, and give me the interpreter's semantics precisely, including the cases that matter: what happens when two programs each try to evaluate the other and the evaluation never bottoms out (state the depth limit and what the referee returns when it is hit), and what counts as legitimate input to a predicate about the opponent. I want teams of two, so eight programs in play, seeded into three or four rounds of pairings plus one round in which each program is run against a copy of itself. Assume the room's instinct will be to write something that simulates the opponent; tell me how your interpreter handles that and what the students will see when they hit the regress.

**Incentives.** Give the payoff table for the base game, and then decide — and justify — whether teams are scored by absolute points or by rank within the tournament, since I suspect that choice quietly determines whether cooperation is worth attempting at all. Add a small per-clause or per-line complexity tax, so that a program which is robust against many opponents pays for it and a program which simply defects stays cheap; say what that tax is meant to make visible.

**A round that breaks things.** After the scored rounds, run a short unscored round in which each team's program is perturbed in three ways I can execute by hand: a behaviour-preserving rewrite (rename the variables, reorder the clauses, invert a conditional), a rewrite that is behaviourally equivalent but textually distant, and a mutant that differs from the original in exactly one clause. Teams predict outcomes before you resolve them. This is where I want the difference between detecting behaviour and detecting syntax to become something they can feel rather than something I assert. If you have a better-designed perturbation round, use it instead, but tell me what it diagnoses.

**The debrief.** Map specific moments in the session onto the ideas, in the order they occurred: why mutual defection is still an equilibrium of the *program* game even after everything they have just watched (I do not want them leaving thinking that cooperation has been proved rational); what it means that the objects of choice are intensional; how a bounded proof search — the trick where each program looks for a short argument that the other cooperates with it, and where the two searches can lock together — gets around the regress, and how much of that you think this room can actually reconstruct for itself in the time available; and a five-minute bridge to the older thought that one can choose a disposition or a constitution rather than an act, without turning the session into a lecture about it.

**Practicalities.** A timeline in minutes, the materials list (index cards, envelopes, a scoring grid, whatever the syntax card needs), and a one-page handout of the language and the interpreter rules that a non-coder can use. Then be honest about two things: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — the specific failure modes, not generic ones — and how I should handle each; and which feature of the idea the game cannot reproduce at all, so that I know what I have to supply in my own words at the end. Please write the whole thing in British English.
