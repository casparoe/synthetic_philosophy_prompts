hey — im taking a Philosophy and Public Policy seminar, we're on the economics of climate change right now, and I keep getting steamrolled by the same move and I can't tell if it's actually good. Our TA is an econ PhD student and his line is basically that discounting the future isn't a moral view at all, it's arithmetic. A dollar spent today on mitigation is a dollar not invested, invested dollars compound at the market rate of return, so at 5% that dollar is worth about eleven in fifty years and well over ten thousand in two centuries — so spending it now is taking consumption away from people who will be much richer than us, and using a near-zero discount rate is just a dressed-up way of saying today's poor should subsidize tomorrow's rich. He writes the decomposition on the board (r = rho + eta*g) and says the only genuinely ethical bit is rho, pure time preference, and that even that should be near zero, maybe a hair above it for extinction risk.

The thing doing all the work seems to be an analogy: intergenerational choice is just an investment decision. The present generation is an investor, the future is the payout, the discount rate is the interest rate, and delaying mitigation is just lending. I need an audit of it, not a verdict. Give me the correspondence table — investor, principal, term, interest rate, default risk, what plays the role of the person who consumes the returns — then tell me which disanalogies actually matter for the conclusion he's using it for and which are noise.

Four things I keep poking at and can't get straight:

1. The compounding runs in the direction that helps him and the direction that hurts him. If the future really is that much richer per unit of consumption, then a unit of future consumption matters less (his eta*g term) — but also a tiny sacrifice now buys an enormous future benefit, which sounds like an argument for spending, not against. Is he using the same number twice?
2. The returns accrue to whoever owns the capital. Future people can't buy shares in our restraint and we can't write them a check. The thing we'd actually be handing over — a stable climate, a coastline — isn't fungible with consumption. My half-baked idea is 'discounting in kind': if the only thing you can transfer is the good itself, the rate you should care about is the rate at which that good appreciates or becomes irreplaceable, not the return on capital. Is that coherent, or am I just reinventing the point that money and the thing money buys can come apart?
3. The investor is one person with one utility function and one death date, and mortality is a big part of why individuals are impatient at all. Humanity is a sequence of overlapping generations with no single utility function and no single death date. Which parts of the analogy depend on there being one person, and what happens to them when there isn't?
4. The same market rate can be used to argue for spending little now and for waiting to learn more later, and those are different conclusions. Does the analogy support one and not the other?

Also, the person I study with has the opposite analogy: temporal distance is like spatial distance, and we don't care less about someone in pain because they're in another country, so distance in time can't matter by itself. Does that one do better on the non-fungible-goods case, or does it just relocate the question? And if both analogies are bad, what's the least bad one?
