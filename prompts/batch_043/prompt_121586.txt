I run grant decisions at a small foundation that funds early-stage, high-risk science: about eight proposals a month, a rotating panel of four or five reviewers who are smart but usually only adjacent to the subfield in question, one ninety-minute meeting, and then a public one-paragraph rationale for every decision that goes up on our site. The board ratifies the list each quarter and has to be able to defend it to their friends. Here's what's been eating me: our decisions are public, other funders and the whole field read them, and I'm pretty sure that some of what looks like emerging consensus in the proposals crossing my desk is just people copying each other — reviewers who've all read the same three preprints, all citing the same one benchmark dataset, all saying some version of "well, the big lab is already doing this" — and I'm equally sure some of it is genuine information moving through the community and I'd be an idiot to ignore it. I can't tell which is which, and I have maybe fifteen minutes a case.

So: build me a decision procedure the panel can actually run, grounded in whatever the sequential-herding literature really establishes — I mean the standard setup where people move in turn, see what earlier people did but not what they knew, and at some point it becomes individually rational to copy and stop contributing their own information at all, plus the reputational version where people copy because being wrong alone is worse than being wrong in a crowd, plus whatever is known about how fragile those states are and what does and doesn't break them. But operationalized: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure just stops with a no, and where it hands the decision back to me because it's really a question about our risk appetite rather than about evidence. Assume I will almost never get a calibrated probability out of these people — say what to do when there's no number to be had. And be straight with me about which steps are real machinery and which are judgment calls wearing a lab coat.

It has to handle at least these: (1) cascade inside the room, since our board member speaks first and the junior reviewers visibly defer; (2) agreement that looks independent but isn't, because everyone's signal came from the same two papers and the same benchmark; (3) the "if it were worth doing somebody would have done it by now" move, i.e. treating absence of uptake as evidence, when sometimes the absence is the cascade; (4) our own past write-ups feeding the thing we're trying to detect — I've been running a quarterly round where we re-review the most-rejected proposals with the prior verdicts and our old rationales redacted, which I call the reversal round, and I genuinely don't know whether it buys anything when the reviewers are the same people who can't un-know what they already said; (5) a rule I invented and will kill if you tell me to — if a panel comes out unanimous, I assign someone to write a minority report even when they agree. Does manufactured dissent carry information or is it just noise with a stamp on it?

Then run it on three cases. First, a method that went from nobody to everyone in about eighteen months: three other funders have already funded variants, my panel is unanimous and enthusiastic, and not one of them can name a reason that isn't a restatement of "everyone's moving this way." Second, a replication-and-negative-results project in a subfield that is extremely confident about its result: nobody has funded it, and the one written review we have says the quiet part out loud about nobody having done it. Third, the one where I want you to tell me something I won't like: two years ago we rejected a proposal and published a fairly dismissive paragraph about it, and since then two other funders have passed on it citing our write-up; a revised version has now come back to us and the panel's private scores are actually decent. Say what the procedure says, including what we should write publicly this time — and if the honest output is that we should eat crow loudly because a loud reversal does more than a quiet one, or that we should fund something we think is mediocre because the point of the money is to buy a signal nobody else is going to generate, say that plainly.

Finally, tell me what the procedure can't do: the failure modes, the cases where it will definately misfire, and which outputs I should refuse to trust.
