hi — i run a quarterly ethics session for the staff on the memory care floor at our hospital (nurses, a couple of CNAs, the activities coordinator, nobody with any philosophy training) and i want to close the next one with a short poem instead of my usual slide. thought i'd ask you rather than try to write it myself.

the case that always comes up is some version of this: M., who signed an advance directive at 54 refusing basically everything — antibiotics, a feeding tube, the works — and who at 78 has advanced dementia, is plainly content, hums along to the piano in the afternoon and finishes the pudding, and would almost certainly fail to refuse anything if you asked her now. the wrinkle i actually want the poem to handle is that her directive has one of those "not me" clauses: she wrote that if the day comes when she seems content in that state, that seeming contentment is to be treated as a symptom of the disease rather than as her speaking, and the form is to be honored anyway. so she didn't just predict her future preferences — she pre emptively disqualified them.

i keep thinking a sestina is the right form, and i want it to earn that rather than just be decorative. six end words that never change but never come around in the same order twice, so that by the fourth stanza "signature" is landing at the end of a line about the woman who hums and "happy" is landing at the end of a line about the woman who signed. the rotation should do the identity work: same vocabulary, no stable arrangement, and no way to tell from the words alone which self is talking. standard pattern in case it helps — if the first stanza ends 1 2 3 4 5 6, then 615243, 364125, 532614, 451362, 246531, and then a three-line envoi that gets all six back in. unrhymed, shortish lines, don't slave over the meter, i want the whole thing to read out loud in well under two minutes.

six words i had in mind: signature, happy, earlier, stranger, promise, mine — swap one or two if you've got better, but keep at least "earlier" and "mine," since the fight is really about who's word that is.

what i definately don't want: i don't want the envoi to settle it. don't let the last three lines decide whether the earlier self or the present one has the authority, and don't let it turn into either a sermon about dignity or a soft thing about the person still being in there somewhere. the tug i want left standing is that the earlier self is the only one who ever had the whole life in view and also the only one who could be wrong about it, the present one is the only one actually here to bear anything and also the one who can't speak for the project anymore, and the clause is simultaneously a real piece of self knowledge and a way of arranging things so you can never be corrected. someone who works on this should think it's a fair picture of the disagreement and not a cartoon; someone who's just a CNA who's known four different Ms should get it on one hearing.

american english please — we're in the US, a good chunk of the staff learned english as a second language, so nothing archaic or british and no vocabulary anyone would need a handout for.
