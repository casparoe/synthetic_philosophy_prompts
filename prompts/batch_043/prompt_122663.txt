hey — I'm a junior and I'm taking a seminar on Dewey this term (the public-and-its-consequences stuff and the inquiry sequence: indeterminate situation, instituting the problem, hypothesis, testing, settling), and I somehow also ended up as one of two undergrads on the university's Space and Capital Planning Committee. We meet for ninety minutes every other week, we get maybe eight or nine real decisions a year — convert a parking lot, renovate a building, close something — and whatever we produce goes to the VP for Finance and Facilities, who can ignore it but usually doesn't. The other members are two faculty, a dean, the head of facilities, and an institutional research analyst who can pull numbers for us in about two weeks if we ask nicely. Nobody on this committee has read any theory and if I say "experimentalism" out loud I will lose the room for the rest of the year. Stakes are real: these are ten- to forty-year decisions about buildings and about who can afford to be on campus.

So here's what I want. Turn that picture of inquiry-as-democracy into an actual procedure my committee can run on a proposal, in the order we'd run it, that I can hand to the dean as a one-page checklist with no Dewey vocabulary in it at all. For each step: what question we ask, what counts as an answer good enough to move on, where the procedure just stops and says no, and where it has to hand the decision to a human because the thing in dispute is a value and not a forecast. Then tell me which of Dewey's distinctions have to survive the translation for it to still be his idea rather than just good meeting hygiene, and which I can quietly drop.

Some things I think need building in, but you tell me if I'm wrong about where they go:

- We keep starting with options ("dorm or no dorm") and I'm pretty sure the Dewey move is that the first meeting isn't allowed to consider options at all, only formulations of what's actually gone wrong. But I don't know what a "problem formulation" has to look like for us to accept one and move on, and I don't know how to stop facilities from sneaking a preferred option in disguised as a problem statement.
- Preferences vs consequences. Everyone wants a survey. Dewey's whole thing seems to be that a poll just adds up wants people walked in with, which is not inquiry. My idea instead is consequence pacts: when someone tells us what they want, we make them name one specific thing they expect to observe in eighteen months if they get it, we write it down with their name, and we bring the list back out when the time comes. Is that a faithful operationalization or a parody of it, and what do I do with the people who refuse to commit to a prediction, which will be most of them?
- The public as the people who catch the indirect consequences. Most affected people never show up — commuters, night custodial staff, the neighbors, students who aren't enrolled yet, people who'd enroll if childcare were cheap. I want a required step where we name the absentees and assign someone in the room to carry their stake explicitly, but that feels like it could become theater where I appoint myself to speak for everyone. How does the procedure keep that honest?
- Experts. Facilities tells us things and we have no way to check them. Dewey's thing about the person wearing the shoe knowing where it pinches, even if the expert knows how to fix it, seems to imply an actual cross-examination step. What form does it take when we have ninety minutes?
- Irreversibility. Some of these decisions (demolish, sell land, close the childcare center) can't be un-tried, and Dewey's whole model seems to assume you can learn by doing and then revise. What does the procedure degrade into when it hits one of those, and how should I recognize that I've hit one?

Then run it on three:

1. Converting a surface parking lot into a 500-bed residence hall with retail on the ground floor. We have the consultant's traffic and revenue study.
2. Closing the campus childcare center and contracting with a private provider five minutes away — saves the college real money, and every parent who uses it is staff or a student parent, meaning the people most affected can't come to a 4pm meeting because of the very thing we're deciding about.
3. A case where I won't like the answer: student government has already announced a campus-wide binding-ish referendum on the residence hall for spring, and I'm the one who pushed for it because it's the only way students ever get listened to here. Tell me what your procedure says about the referendum specifically, and what I'd have to go back and tell people who think I sold them out.

Finally, be straight about what the procedure can't do. My worry is that it can churn through every step, produce something well-warranted, and still get rejected by everyone, and that Dewey's answer to that is just "more inquiry" — which is useless when the VP wants a vote in March. Where does this thing actually break, and what should I do in the room when it does.
