I work as a backend engineer on an internal platform team, and I am designing a small service my colleagues have started calling the roadmap merge. Every quarter each of nine product teams submits a strict ranking of a shared list of candidate projects — currently about forty items, growing each quarter, with no restriction on how the submitted rankings may disagree with one another. The service must hand back one strict ranking of the whole list, because downstream we simply execute projects from the top until the quarter's engineering capacity runs out.

I have read the usual short summary of Arrow's result and I take the point: the conditions cannot all be satisfied at once — no single team whose ballot always wins, unanimous agreement respected, the relative position of any two projects depending only on how the teams ranked those two against each other, and the output always being a genuine ranking rather than something cyclic. I do not need the proof restated, I cannot act on citations or further reading, and I would rather you spent no words on the history of the field.

What I need is a classification instrument: a decision tree I can walk my tech lead and our PM through in a design review, starting from we want all of that and ending at a concrete aggregation rule I could actually implement and unit-test. Please build it to the following specifications.

**Structure.** Ten to fourteen branch nodes, eight to twelve leaves. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of rule names: first the questions and the answer-combinations they permit, with the leaves left anonymous (L1 through Ln), and only afterwards a mapping table from leaves to the named families of rules. Where a leaf has no settled name, mark it unnamed and describe it in one clause. Where a combination of answers is uninhabited, say which one and diagnose briefly whether nobody occupies it because it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Where two leaves cannot be separated by anything on your tree, flag the pair explicitly rather than inventing a distinction.

**Phrasing.** Every node must be a question a PM who has never encountered social choice theory could answer in a design review. Name the formal condition in parentheses after the plain-English wording so I can pick up the vocabulary, but never let the label do the work. At least three nodes must be concrete ballot incidents rather than statements of principle — for example: a compliance-mandated filler project that every team ranks near the bottom is added to the list, and including it flips the relative order of two unrelated projects. Is that a bug we must fix, or an acceptable sensitivity? Positions come apart on that case, and I want the tree to use cases of that kind as its load-bearing joints. At the three nodes you think do the most work, give a worked micro-profile of three teams and three or four projects in which the two branches produce different outputs.

**Axes the tree must include.** Somewhere in the tree I need to be able to choose, and be shown the cost of, each of the following: weakening the output requirement (a set of maximal elements, or a probability distribution over rankings, instead of a strict order); weakening the input assumption (letting teams submit intensities rather than orderings, and the interpersonal-comparability problem that this immediately raises); restricting the domain instead of the rule (is there a single axis — cost, risk, expected headcount — along which we could legitimately require ballots to be single-peaked, and what does that buy?); weakening the independence condition rather than abandoning it (local or adjacent-only versions, insensitivity to near-duplicate projects); and weakening the no-dictator condition in a controlled way (a weighted or oligarchic rule, a designated tie-breaker, or the platform team's ballot deciding only when the rule cycles). A node on strategic behaviour belongs in the tree too: a team should not be able to lift its own top project by misreporting a project it knows is doomed and will never be funded. A colleague mentioned that there is a second impossibility result in that neighbourhood; I am not sure of its exact statement, so please state whatever version you use in the node itself, and tell me how it interacts with the Arrow-style branches rather than treating it as a separate theorem to be admired.

**Three constraints from my system that the tree must take seriously.** First: the aggregation must be recomputable from fixed-size per-alternative summaries held in a streaming key-value store, so that revising one team's ballot does not force a full re-aggregation. I want a node that asks whether this constraint has *already* put me in the subtree where the independence condition is lost, or whether some rule satisfying it survives. Answer honestly either way — if the constraint is secretly deciding the outcome before we reach any philosophical choice, say so at that node rather than downstream. Second: for every adjacent pair in the output, an auditor must be able to justify their relative order by a chain of at most two majority preferences, or by a direct majority; chains longer than that will not survive review, no matter what the rule's global properties are. Treat this as a first-class condition and locate it on the tree. Third: the endpoint contract currently promises one deterministic ranking per request. If any leaf requires randomness or a set-valued output, mark it as blocked by the contract and state the smallest contract change that would unblock it.

**Output format.** A tree I can paste into a design document — nested bullets or an ASCII flowchart, with the mapping table and the diagnostics appended below it. Close with a short paragraph on what the tree does not settle: the residual choices that are disagreements about what we want the roadmap merge to be for, not technical questions the tree could have resolved with a better set of nodes.
