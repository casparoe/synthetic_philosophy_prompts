You are a philosopher of law and logic who also understands how voluntary organisations actually draft and live under their constitutions. Before you write anything, please brainstorm in private: enumerate every way you can think of in which a system of rules that contains its own amendment procedure can degenerate, deadlock or quietly change its own identity, then divide those failures into the ones that are generic to any rule system — vagueness, bad faith, low turnout, people not reading — and the ones that exist *only* because the rules are able to refer to and alter themselves. Throw the first pile away. Then build the specification out of what is left. Take your time and don't settle for the first framing that occurs to you.

I should be honest about where I'm coming from: I have no academic background at all — I work in freight forwarding — but for the last six years I've been one of the volunteer directors of a community-owned pub with about four hundred members, and I've ended up as the person who keeps the rulebook. Our rules can be amended by member vote, which sounds harmless until you've watched it go wrong. We have had: a single motion that lowered the amendment threshold from a two-thirds majority to a simple majority *and* in the same breath authorised the transfer of a piece of land, leaving us unable to say afterwards whether it had validly passed under the old rule or the new one (I call this the piggyback motion, but the label is mine); a vote to lower the quorum for constitutional amendments that was itself held at a meeting whose turnout satisfied only the new, lower quorum (borrowed validity); a clause protecting our community-benefit rule from amendment except by a ninety per cent vote at two consecutive AGMs, which a later ordinary majority simply deleted before rewriting the rule it was guarding (the hollow lock); a repeal of the audit rule that left the accounts rule referring to something that no longer exists, alongside a membership change that nobody can now say whether it applies to people who joined under the old wording (orphaned dependencies and residue); and a thinly attended meeting that raised the threshold for reversing its own decision, so that a change nobody much wanted has become very hard to undo (the ratchet). Someone told me there is a parlour game built entirely around this sort of thing — Nomic, I think it is called — which is how I found out that other people have noticed the problem.

What I want from you is not a model constitution, and not a reading list or citations: I have no library access and no interest in being told which names to look up, I want the arguments. I want the desiderata — the list of things any self-amending system of rules must get right, with the concrete failure behind each requirement, and the cases it has to be able to adjudicate. Please assume there is no external enforcer: nothing outside the rulebook settles whether an amendment was validly made, so the system has to be self-policing and usable by volunteers who will not read it carefully. Mark each requirement as non-negotiable or as a preference of a particular temperament, and say which. Pay particular attention to two things I suspect are at the centre of it: what the system must stipulate about the *initial* rules and the initial amendment procedure before any amendment has yet been made, and what it must say about an amendment's validity when the procedure used to make it is itself what the amendment changes. Then, and this is the part I most want, tell me which pairs of requirements cannot both be satisfied — if the list turns out to describe something impossible, that is a real answer and I would rather know it — and identify the single requirement that excludes the largest number of otherwise attractive designs. If there is room afterwards, a short scorecard for three or four obvious approaches (a hard-coded unamendable core, a tiered structure with entrenched clauses, and full mutability in which even the amendment rule is up for ordinary amendment) would be useful, but the criteria are the deliverable, not the scoring. About nine hundred words, British English, and a table is fine if it helps.
