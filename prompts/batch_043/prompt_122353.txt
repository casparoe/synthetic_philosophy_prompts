I'm a second-year PhD student in cognitive science, currently taking a seminar on measurement and reproducibility. Last week I presented a preregistered replication I ran with two collaborators. We used roughly two and a half times the original study's sample, preregistered the analysis and a smallest effect size of interest, and got an estimate very close to zero: the 95% interval comfortably contains zero, but it also contains effects about a third the size of what the original paper reported. I concluded, cautiously, that the effect, if it exists at all, is substantially smaller than reported.

A visiting speaker waved this away with the line "absence of evidence is not evidence of absence," and added that this is also why the field's accumulated failed replications shouldn't lead anyone to say the original effects are zero. I'm confident the line can express something respectable, and I'm equally confident it was doing something illegitimate in that exchange, but I can't locate the illegitimacy precisely, because I can't pin down what the line says.

Two complications I'd like handled explicitly rather than gestured at. First, "absence" seems to me to be able to mean at least three different things: that the effect is exactly zero; that it is smaller than the smallest effect anyone would care about (this is why we preregistered a SESOI); and that it simply isn't there in this population, at this time, with this implementation. I suspect the verdict on the slogan changes depending on which of these is meant, and in particular that the first is a much stranger thing to seek evidence for than the second. Second, I think what the line amounts to depends heavily on what one takes "evidence" to be — a matter of a test's severity or error probabilities, a matter of likelihood ratios between specified hypotheses, or a matter of posterior odds — and I'd like those differences spelled out rather than blended. There also seems to be a symmetry worry I can't finish: if a non-significant result never counts against an effect, exactly what is it about the original significant result that makes it count in favor of one?

Please don't give me a reading list or citations; I want the conceptual map, not sources to chase. I'd like the response in four sections:

1. "The readings" — about 700 words. A numbered menu of the distinct precise theses the one-liner could be expressing, ordered from the most trivial to the most radical, each stated tightly enough that I could say which of them I hold, and each tagged with what it must mean by "evidence" and by "absence."
2. "Verdicts" — about 350 words. One or two lines per reading: platitude, substantive and defensible, contentious, or false, with the reason in a clause. Please include any readings that are self-undermining.
3. "What the line was doing in our seminar" — about 400 words. Which of the readings would actually license the speaker's two moves (that my null result shows nothing, and that the field's null results show nothing), which would license only a weaker version of them, and which are consistent with the line but undermine it as a response to me.
4. "Two things I could say back" — about 150 words. The shortest replies or questions that would force the distinction I care about, phrased so I can use them in a seminar discussion without sounding like I'm accusing a visitor of sophistry.
