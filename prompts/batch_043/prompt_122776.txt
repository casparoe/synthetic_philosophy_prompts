I run a two-hour session once a year for about 20 first-year undergrads in a course called "Organizations, Rules and People" — it's in a public policy school, most of them have never done any theory and won't have read anything in advance. This year I want to do Foucault on disciplinary power and power/knowledge, but I don't want to put up a diagram of a circular prison and talk at them for an hour. I want them to walk into the structure and notice it from the inside. Constraints: one normal classroom with movable chairs, one teaching assistant, no budget, no prep reading, no lecture up front, and they shouldn't hear the words "panopticon" or "power/knowledge" until afterwards. Last year I tried just silently standing at the back of the room taking notes for ten minutes, which sort of worked but it was obviously all about me, and the discussion afterwards was just "surveillance is bad."

So: design me the actual runnable thing. Roles, materials, rules, a round-by-round timeline with minute markers, and what I say at each transition. Some things I'd really like built in if you can manage it — but redesign freely, I'm making this up as I go: first, the observation should be intermittent and unverifiable rather than constant, so that not knowing whether they're being watched is what does the work. Second, the observers should be writing things down using categories the participants themselves help invent, and the records should have real if tiny consequences (who presents first, where people sit, who gets a small privilege), so that describing quietly turns into ranking and the ranking then produces the behavior the description predicted. Third, I'd love a stretch where the observation stops entirely and the behavior still doesn't revert, so they feel internalization instead of just compliance. Fourth, some mechanism showing that nobody is actually in charge — my idea was a "record card" that carries a first-round label forward even after the person's behavior has clearly changed, so that the file rather than any person ends up driving the outcome. I also want them to come away seeing that the categories felt neutral and descriptive while obviously having been made by someone, and that the power at work wasn't mainly someone saying no.

Then the debrief: map concrete moments in the session onto the parts of the view — hierarchical observation, normalizing judgment, the examination, visibility as a trap, knowledge producing the very thing it claims to describe — and give me the discussion questions in the order I should ask them. Please also be blunt about what will predictably go wrong the first time (I'm assuming at least one group works out the observation is fake and starts performing for the observer, and someone will say I manipulated them), and about which feature of the account the exercise fundamentally can't reproduce and I'll just have to supply myself. My worry is that the whole thing will read as me holding all the power, which is more or less the opposite of the point.
