You are a moral philosopher who also has a working command of the economics of discounting and of the literature on intergenerational justice. Please think the problem through carefully before you write: begin with a short note-form sketch of how you intend to map the episode onto the concept, say explicitly which parts of the episode you are treating as fixed background, and flag anywhere the concept goes silent rather than stretching it to speak.

I spent my career as a research oceanographer and have, since retiring, been reading my way into moral philosophy; I am writing a paper for a small reading group on whether any positive rate of pure time preference can be defended. What I want from you is a two-directional case analysis: the 2006 Stern Review and the discounting controversy that followed it, read through the concept of pure time preference, and that concept in turn read through what the episode revealed about it.

Take the following as the background I am assuming, and correct me briefly at the start if it is materially wrong: Stern chose a pure rate of time preference near zero, on the order of a tenth of a percent per year, which with a unit elasticity of marginal utility and modest long-run growth gave a social discount rate around one and a half percent; critics favoured something of order four or five percent; and it was argued shortly afterwards both that uncertainty about the discount rate itself should make the effective rate decline with time and that Stern's headline conclusion depended at least as much on his treatment of low-probability catastrophic outcomes as on his choice of the pure rate.

Then give me, in order:

First, the mapping. Properly separated from growth, diminishing marginal utility, and risk, what was the actual ethical choice Stern faced, and how much of the apparent technical dispute was really a disagreement about that parameter rather than about the others? Who, in the analysis, is the agent whose time preference is at issue, and what were the options?

Second, where the lens works and where it fails. Which features of the episode does the concept of pure time preference explain, and which does it miss or misdescribe? I have in mind the possibility that the live dispute was mostly about inequality aversion between generations and about fat tails rather than about impatience, and the further difficulty that no observed market rate pins the parameter down.

Third, my own proposal, which I would like you to test rather than endorse. It has occurred to me that a positive apparent rate of pure time preference need not be ethically arbitrary at all: future goods are contingent on there being a recipient population and an unbroken institutional chain to deliver them, so a planner might reasonably discount by the hazard that no one is there to receive the benefit. But if the same model already carries catastrophe and extinction terms, does folding survival probability into the pure rate simply double-count it? And if I strip the survival component out, is what remains still pure time preference in the sense the literature argues about, or have I quietly changed the subject? I would also like your verdict on the parallel argument my reading group liked, that we reject discounting well-being by distance and so should reject discounting it by date, and on whether that is a genuine argument or only a pun on the word discount.

Finally, what the episode shows about the concept. Is the Stern controversy a confirmation of the charge that pure time preference is a moral parameter wearing a technical disguise, an anomaly for that charge, or a case the concept was never built to handle, such as the aggregation of individual impatience across overlapping generations? Close with what, by the lights of the concept as you understand it, Stern ought to have done, and whether he could have done it within his remit as a government adviser.
