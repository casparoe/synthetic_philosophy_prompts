I'm in a small reading group working through the metaphysics of dispositions, and we've now spent two sessions on finks and masks — the pair of counterexamples that are supposed to sink the simple conditional analysis, where a fink removes or alters an object's causal basis at exactly the moment the stimulus arrives, while a mask leaves the basis intact and merely prevents the manifestation from getting out. The trouble is that everyone can classify the stock illustrations correctly by rote and nobody can tell, in an unfamiliar case, which of the two structures is actually in front of them. So I'd like a case built to order, and I'd like it to turn one notch further than the usual examples do.

The extra notion I've been playing with is what I've started calling an *extrinsic-basis disposition*: a disposition whose causal basis isn't wholly intrinsic to its bearer but is partly constituted by facts about the bearer's surroundings — a neighbouring object, an ambient field, a standing arrangement that the bearer is plugged into or mounted on — so that whether the bearer has the disposition at all depends on something outside its own boundaries. It seems to me that this should make the fink/mask distinction harder to apply, because an intervention on the environment could count either as destroying the basis or as merely blocking the output, and I can't see what settles which.

So here is the specification. In one short paragraph, give me an ordinary physical set-up — a kitchen, a bike, a sound system, a greenhouse, anything; no angels, no demons, no sorcerers, no perfectly timed interventions by an agent, and please avoid fragility, solubility and the other usual suspects. The fink mechanism has to be something a competent engineer could actually build and that operates by ordinary causal means on locally available signals. Within that single set-up, one and the same event or device must operate as a fink with respect to one disposition and as a mask with respect to a second disposition of the same object at the same time; at least one of the two dispositions should be an extrinsic-basis one in my sense; and the differing verdicts must fall out of what finks and masks are, not be announced by you in the telling.

After the scenario, please do the following. For each of the two dispositions state the stimulus condition, the manifestation, and what you take the causal basis to be. Show what the simple conditional analysis predicts for each and where exactly it goes wrong. Then classify each as fink or mask and say what in the case grounds the classification — I especially want the part where the extrinsic basis makes the classification contestable, if it does. Next, add a brief reverse-cycle variant: a counterpart object that lacks one of the dispositions but would acquire it precisely on being stimulated, and tell me whether that is best handled as a fink, a mask, a third thing, or a reductio of the whole apparatus. Finally, be honest about the machinery: which single feature of the case is doing the work, what collapses if it is removed, and how someone who wants to rescue a conditional analysis — by moving to ideal conditions, or to complete states of the world, or by some other refinement you think is stronger — would answer you. I'm planning to use this as a teaching example, so I care more about the case surviving a hostile seminar than about it being tidy. Please write the whole answer in British English.
