I sat in on a commitee rewriting a national anti-doping code a while back, and I've been going back and fourth ever since on what the thing we produced actually assumes. I'm not naming the federation and I've changed some details, partly out of discretion and partly because I want the analysis to be about the shape of the rules rather than where they came from — and a few of these clauses are ones I haven't seen anywhere else, so please reason from how I've described them rather than from anything you already know about real codes. Here's roughly the practice.

Substances and methods on a prohibited list are banned, but an athlete with a documented diagnosis can get an exemption to use the same substance at a comparable dose, and then it counts as treatment rather than cheating. Seperately, for anything that follows an injury or an illness, the rule is "return to baseline": you may be restored to your own measured pre-injury values and you may not go past them, even when the intervention that restores you is the same one that would have enhanced you. Deficiency is defined against the bottom few percent of healthy adults of the same age and sex, not against other athletes in your event and not against your own earlier self. Ordinary training, nutrition, equipment, and surgery that repairs damage are all fine; a machine or device that supplies energy the athlete didn't generate isn't. Then there were two clauses we argued about for months. One permits whatever is cheap and available over the counter to every competitor, on the idea that it can't be unfair if anyone can get it, while banning equivalent effects that only reach athletes through a specialist clinic. The other permits a compound that doesn't raise anyone's peak output at all and only removes off-days, making performance more consistent; that one passed on the grounds that it raises no one's ceiling. Finally, enforcement punishes the presence of a banned substance in a sample, and even attempted or admitted use, whether or not it made anybody faster.

What I keep tripping over is that these clauses don't seem to come from one place. The exemption clause puts the therapy/enhancement line at a diagnosis, the baseline clause puts it at a fact about the individual's own history, the deficiency clause grounds it in a statistical normal for a population that has nothing to do with the sport, the availability clause seems to abandon the distinction and ask a different question about fair access, and the consistency compound seems to smuggle in a third question about what kind of inequality a contest is even meant to be a contest of. Meanwhile the enforcement clauses punish use rather than advantage, which makes it look as if the wrong isn't unfairness at all.

So: what does this package presuppose? Can you reconstruct a single implicit view of therapy, enhancement, fairness, and the "natural" that makes all of it hang together, or is each clause licensed by a different theory that happens to agree with the others in the cases that come up most? I especially want to know which bits are strictly required by any view that could generate these rulings and which are just comfortable bedfellows, and where the code is genuinely underdetermined and several theories would license it equally well. And then: if you dropped the therapy/enhancement distinction as the organising idea and rebuilt the code around health and safety plus equal access, or around levelling the natural lottery, or around capping total advantage regardless of where it came from, which clauses would survive, which would flip, and what would the enforcers have to start measuring that they currently don't? Write it as continuous prose, no headings or bullet points or bold or tables, the way you'd write a long email to a friend who asked.
