I write fiction, and I am about to make a decision that a historian is free to postpone. My novel is narrated in the first person by a woman from a fishing household on the northern shore of the lake, who attaches herself to a Galilean preacher and stays with him to the end. That means I have to settle, before drafting, what this preacher said, what he refused to say, what he thought he was doing, what the people travelling with him were expecting of him, and how much of any of it is only the evangelists' retrospection. I can hedge in a note; I cannot hedge in a scene. So I need a specification rather than a survey.

Please write the desiderata that any method for getting from our sources — the four Gospels, with their well-known dependencies on one another; the letters of Paul, with their near-total silence about the life; the brief notices in Josephus, one of which has probably been retouched by Christian hands — to a portrait of Jesus would have to satisfy. Fix the purpose as mine: a portrait good enough to build one determinate character on, capable of carrying a psychological arc and of acting for reasons a reader can follow, rather than a portrait good enough for a monograph. Whether the standard is the same for the two purposes is itself something I want you to decide.

For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it, and mark it either non-negotiable or a preference of one school or one craft. Among the things I want adjudicated: whether a method owes me anything about what he did not say and could not have done, or only about what he did and said; whether a method can coherently be required to be independent of the historian's prior portrait of Jesus, or whether any criterion of authenticity is doomed to ratify whichever portrait it was run to confirm; how a criterion should treat sources that are not neutral reports but interpretations produced by people who already believed; whether the desiderata should require the method to recover the believing community as well as the man, since I have to stage the ensemble and not only the protagonist; and whether the portrait must be at once continuous with first-century Jewish life and discontinuous enough to explain why a movement formed around him and why Rome killed him.

I also want a candidate criterion of my own audited. I have been working with something I call narrative fit: a reconstruction is adequate only if the actions I need to stage follow from the sayings I attribute to him, and those sayings from those actions, without my having to supply a bridging motive that the sources nowhere suggest. Is that a genuine desideratum, a novelist's prejudice smuggled into historiographical method, or a requirement with a narrow but real domain — and if the last, over what range is it trustworthy?

Then the two things I care about most. First, tell me which subset of your desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and why: I would rather have the impossibility than a list that flatters my hopes. Second, name the single requirement that does the most work in excluding the criteria in ordinary use — embarrassment, dissimilarity, multiple attestation, Semitic or Palestinian linguistic and environmental colour, coherence with what has already been accepted — and score those against your list briefly, in a table if you prefer, since that part I can check for myself.

Two closing requests. Give me a decision rule for an author: when the best-supported reconstruction of a scene carries something like a third or two-fifths of the probability mass and the alternatives are not dramatically worse, what may a novelist do, and exactly where does legitimate filling-in become dishonest? And mark your own confidence: attach a level of confidence to each desideratum, tell me which one you would retreat from first, say which consideration or piece of evidence would change your verdict about the most discriminating requirement, and say where you think your reasoning is most likely to fail.
