Hey — I write about science and ideas for a magazine, mostly long-ish features, and I've got a piece coming up that I keep circling back to: how (and whether) you can compare one person's wellbeing with someone elses. Not the abstract "can utility be measured" question on its own, but the version where it cashes out in the world — cost-benefit analysis, QALYs and disability weights, wellbeing "units" in policy appraisal, and now the AI-flavoured version where you train a model on a huge pile of self-reports and it hands everyone a score on one latent scale.

Here's my actual problem. I've got roughly 4,000 words and maybe five serious conversations with people who work on this, and I want the piece to leave a reader able to think something they couldn't have thought from the first paragraph of a textbook. So: which of the open questions in this area should I spend that budget on, and in what order? I care much less about getting a list than about your reasons — what's genuinely upstream of other stuff, what's tractable for a journalist to get across in a few hundred words without flattening it, what's a rabbit hole that sounds deep but leaves a reader with nothing, and what would move a question up or down the ranking if I found out X or Y while reporting.

Some of what I've been turning over; please add your own and tell me what I'm missing or over-weighting:

- The "we're already doing it" point. Every serious policy apparatus already compares people — a QALY is an interpersonal comparison with extra steps. So maybe the live question isn't whether comparison is possible but which existing practice is most in need of the philosophical work, and which one is quietly doing something indefensible under the cover of a technical-seeming number.
- The calibration regress. To put A and B on one scale you need a standpoint, or a judge, or a population of judges; and picking among those (or picking how to weight their disagreements) looks like it already requires a comparison. Is that regress fatal, benign, or just the same problem one level up — and does any of it matter for what an actual agency should do on Monday?
- A thing I more or less invented while thinking about this, which someone has probably thought about properly: instead of asking everyone to rate their own life and then stitching the self-reports together, you run small deliberative "calibration panels" — randomly selected people, given both a self-report instrument and bundles of vignettes about other lives, asked to rank whole lives against each other. Then you have to decide whether to aggregate the panels, discount the outliers, or pick some median life as the reference point. Is this a real advance on the standard setup or just moving the arbitrariness somewhere less visible? And would the choice between aggregating and taking a median actually reveal something, or is it a coin flip dressed up?
- Latent-scale models. If a model trained on many people's survey answers or text can score anyone on one axis, is that an answer to the philosophical problem, or has the arbitrariness just relocated into the loss function and into what counts as a good prediction? Related: such a model is extremely good at producing *consistent* comparisons. Is consistency the thing we were after, or just the thing we can measure?
- Partial comparability. Maybe the honest answer is that some pairs of lives are comparable and some aren't, in which case the practical question becomes what decision rule you run over a merely partial ordering — and whether "don't act until the comparison is forced on you" is a rule anyone would actually accept from a government.

And a couple of overratedness checks, where I want you to be blunt: is hard-line scepticism (the comparisons aren't merely hard, they're meaningless) as central to the picture as it is fun to write about, or has it been oversold? Is the utility-monster style objection worth any of my word count, or is it a classroom toy? Is the "impartial spectator / you might have been anyone" style move still load-bearing in current thinking, or has it been superseeded by something else?

What I want back is a defended agenda for the piece: the two or three threads you'd actually chase, why those, what each is upstream of, what you'd cut and why, what would change the ranking, and what a reader should be able to do or think differently at the end. Don't give me a reading list or a set of names to call — I'll handle sourcing myself. I want the shape of the argument and the priorities.
