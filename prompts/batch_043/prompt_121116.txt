I'm writing the final for my third-year seminar on epistemic democracy — half the students are formal epistemology people who want everything expressed as a probability, half are political theory people who want to talk about legitimacy, and the two groups have started actively annoying each other in seminar. Last year's exam was a disaster because literally everyone wrote the same answer: state the Condorcet jury theorem, write out the binomial sum for the probability of a correct majority, note that it goes to 1 as the group grows, conclude "hence democracy", stop. I want one 45-minute question (25 marks, and please write it as it would actually appear on the paper, in whatever parts you think work best) that makes that answer insufficient. My idea is to give a concrete decision body — say a licensing or funding committee of 15 to 25 people facing a yes/no question where there is a fact of the matter — and then break the theorem's assumptions in specific ways rather than gesturing at them in general: a bloc of members whose errors are perfectly correlated because they all read the same summary memo or defer to the same in-house expert, so that adding more such members adds heads without adding independent evidence; a group whose competences are known but unequal, where the most reliable rule weights each member by the log-odds of their competence instead of giving everyone one vote, and where for small groups deferring outright to the single best member can beat taking a majority; and a case where mean competence is a shade under one half, so majority rule is worse than a coin toss and gets worse the more people you add. Use at least two of those three, or substitute your own wrinkle if you've got a better one — I did toy with making the vote sequential and public, so later voters see the running tally and you get herding. The thing I want students forced to reach is what the theorem, on its own assumptions, actually licenses about majority rule as against weighted or expert rule, and what would have to be true of a real electorate for any of it to transfer.

Deliverables: the question text, a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme. The arithmetic should be a step in the answer rather than the answer, so please don't turn this into a computation exercise — what I care about is the reasoning about what the independence assumption is doing and why an asymptotic result can be a bad guide at realistic group sizes. Build the scheme around five or six must-hit moves with marks totalling 25, and seperate an A from a B by naming the specific move each does or doesn't contain: does the candidate see why correlated errors put a ceiling on group reliability, do they notice that the theorem is silent about who is competent, do they handle the sub-half case, do they distinguish the finite-n from the limit claim. For each move give a one-line note on how students typically botch it. And no reading list or citations anywhere — my students sit the exam without library access and I can't check references either, so everything has to be answerable from the question alone.

Two last things about how I want your reply written. First, lead with a single sentence naming the crux your question is built to test, the one claim a top answer has to defend, then go straight into the question, model answer and scheme with no preamble and no restating my request back to me. Second, keep clear what's settled from what's contested: the probability theory and the behaviour of the limit are settled, but whether any of this supports democracy rather than epistocracy, and whether real voters satisfy anything like the required conditions, is genuinely disputed among people who work on it — so flag those places instead of presenting one side as what everyone thinks.
