I'm writing the final for my upper-level epistemology seminar — the back third of the course was Williamson's margin-for-error argument against luminosity in *Knowledge and its Limits* and the structurally similar argument against KK — and I want one question that can't be answered by writing out the sorites series for being cold and concluding that luminosity and KK are both false.

The crux I want them to hit is: what exactly does each argument need? The anti-luminosity argument needs a series of cases that differ only slightly in the subject's condition plus a safety/margin requirement on knowledge. The anti-KK argument needs a series ordered by something like strength of evidence or number of fake barns, plus the assumption that in the marginal case the subject can't discriminate knowing from the nearby failing case. Is that the same premise doing the work in both, or is one of these arguments in worse shape than the other, and why? A good answer should say which premise is load-bearing and whether the KK argument has to import something extra — e.g. a claim about what the subject can tell about their evidence — that the luminosity argument gets for free.

Then throw a wrench in. Last year a student proposed what I've started calling deferred luminosity: a condition C is deferred-luminous iff whenever C obtains at t there is some slightly later time at which the subject is in a position to know that C obtained at t; and likewise deferred KK: if you know p at t then at some later time you're in a position to know that you knew p at t. Ask them whether the margin-for-error reasoning does anything at all against the deferred theses — does the delay let the subject slide into a clear case and wiggle out, does it depend on wich direction the series is moving, and does it matter that safety for a belief about an earlier time is a funny animal — and then, if the deferred view does escape, whether it has escaped into something still worth wanting, or whether it's lost exactly what luminosity and KK were supposed to buy us.

Give me: the question as you'd word it on a handout for a 90-minute in-class essay, a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 that separates an A from a B by naming the specific moves each one has to contain, with a one-line note on how people usually screw up each part.
