Hey — I run the research team at an international health NGO. Several times a year we write an interview guide or survey module in English, our field staff translate it into about a dozen languages, and then we compare the answers across countries and move real money around based on the differences. I've started to worry that a good chunk of what we read as "people in country X care less about Y" is really an artifact of what their language makes easy or hard to say.

Where I've landed on the theory: the strong Sapir–Whorf thing (language determines what you can even think) looks dead, but some weaker version seems hard to deny — that a language shapes what you're *obliged* to encode in order to say anything at all, what sits on the tip of your tongue, and what takes a whole clause to get across. (There's an idea I half-remember, Slobin I think, that the effect is mostly on what you have to work out while you're speaking rather than on thought in general.)

Here's what I want: turn that into a procedure my team can run on an item before we field it. Concretely — we have no budget for ethnography, our country leads are smart but have no linguistics training, sign-off is the country director, and usually I have a day or two, not a month. So: the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops with a "don't field this cross-nationally," and where it has to hand the decision to a person because it's a values call rather than a factual one.

Some things I specifically want in it:

My stab at the key distinction is three-way: does the language *force* you to mark the distinction every time you open your mouth (grammatically), does it just make it *cheap* — one ready-made word, easy to reach for — or is it merely *unsayable* without a whole clause or a loan word? Fix that if it's wrong.

For my purposes I genuinely don't care whether an effect is reshaping thought or only reshaping what people say when you put a question to them, since either one wrecks a cross-country comparison. Tell me if that's a mistake and it matters which it is.

I also want a step where we write down, *before* seeing any data, which direction the language should push the answer and roughly how much — if we can't say which way, we shouldn't use the item. And for anything big enough to move money, some kind of non-verbal or behavioural cross-check of the same construct.

Then run it on three of our actual items:

1. A confidence question: "How confident are you that the clinic will be open next time you go," scored 1–5. In one of our languages, when you talk about a future or past event you have to grammatically mark whether you saw it yourself, were told it, or inferred it — evidentiality, I think it's called. Respondents there score consistently low and the country team reads that as "they don't trust us."

2. A wayfinding question: we ask people to describe the route they took to the clinic and where they'd put a sign, and we code the answers for spatial detail. At one site the language doesn't really have a way to say "to the left of the door" without using cardinal directions — people say north-side and so on as a matter of course, even indoors. Our coding scheme was built for left/right answers, so those responses look vague to us.

3. The one I won't like: our flagship "trust in the health system" battery, which our regional strategy and the last two board reports are built on. In two of our languages the nearest natural word to "trust" isn't about institutions at all — it's about a particular person you have a relationship with, or it's bound up with reciprocity and exchange. So low scores there may mean something entirely different. Run the procedure on this and tell me what it says even if it says I have to stop reporting a comparison I've been reporting for two years.

Also give me at least one item where the honest output is "this is fine, field it as is," so I know the procedure isn't just a no-machine. And tell me plainly what it can't do.

On confidence: be clear about what's settled among people who actually work on this versus what they're still fighting about. I've gathered that some of the famous experiments in this area have had replication trouble, and I don't want to build a step on one of those and find out a year later. If any step rests on a contested result, flag it and say what breaks if the result is false — and don't soften it if the honest verdict is that the weak version I've committed to is itself less secure than I think. No citations or further reading, I have no library access out here and won't chase them. American English, please.
