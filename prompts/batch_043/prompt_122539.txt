I'm finishing a chapter of my dissertation on political obligation, and the part I keep getting stuck on is what the literature calls the content question. Grant, for the sake of argument, that being subject to a reasonably just state generates some duty. Is that duty a duty to do what the law says because the law says it, with the content of the law fixing what you owe and the fact of its being law doing justificatory work? Or is it only ever a duty to bring about whatever justice and the common good independently require, so that the law is at best evidence of what that is?

My supervisor settles it with an analogy, and I want to know how much weight it can carry before I build a section on it. The analogy is that political society is a joint cooperative enterprise. The stock examples are rowers pulling in time and neighbours draining a meadow they share: several people pursue a common end under rules, each restricts his own liberty on condition that the others do the same, and anyone who has taken the benefits of their compliance while withholding his own is free-riding. So the duty of the participant is a duty of fair play, and what it requires is his part as specified by the scheme, not whatever he independently judges best. In a boat you pull when you are told to pull rather than when you think pulling is optimal; you defer to the coxswain about the stroke even when you would have called it differently, because the whole point of having a coxswain is that the stroke has to be settled by someone and cannot be settled stroke by stroke by the crew. Carry that over and you get a content-independent, obedience-shaped obligation whose scope is fixed by the rules of this scheme rather than by your own assessment. And you get the corollary my supervisor actually cares about, which is the one I have to answer in the chapter: civil disobedience, however conscientious and however just its cause, is a species of free-riding on other people's compliance, and therefore always needs a special defence that its sincerity does not by itself supply.

I find that conclusion attractive, which is why I don't trust my sense that the argument for it works. The joint-enterprise picture seems to me to import a cluster of features that a modern state simply lacks, and I can't tell which of them the conclusion depends on. A crew is small enough that each member's compliance is visible to the others and each bears a roughly comparable share of the burden; a state is not. A crew shares a single end agreed in advance and can tell whether it is getting there; under pluralism the point of a polity is largely what is in dispute, and reasonable people disagree about whether they are being benefited at all. The coxswain's authority rests on an epistemic division of labour the crew has reason to accept, and on his pursuing the crew's end; neither is guaranteed for a legislature. And free-riding as it appears in the examples is covert, evasive, and aims to keep the benefit while dodging the cost, whereas civil disobedience as it is standardly characterised is public, avowedly(non-evasive), and accepts the legal penalty, which is hard to square with the paradigm of the free-rider.

I have been testing this with variations, and I would like you to run them too. The stowaway: someone carried aboard unconscious, who wakes up mid-voyage and rows. The disputed destination: a crew half of whom want to go upstream, where the scheme's rules are partly an instrument of that dispute. The coxswain with private errands: the scheme's directives partly serve ends that are not the participants' shared end. The automated scheme: the same benefits and the same rules, but the directives are generated by an optimisation procedure with no one issuing orders and no one to defer to. And the open defaulter: someone who declines to comply in public, announces that he is declining, and submits to the sanction.

What I want is an audit rather than a survey. Give me the correspondence table explicitly: what plays the coxswain, the stroke, the other rowers, the destination, boarding the boat, jumping ship. Then sort the disanalogies into the ones that matter for the use my supervisor is making of the analogy and the ones that are incidental, and say which of them actually does justificatory work in getting from the premise to the conclusion. Does the content-independence claim survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and does the free-riding charge against civil disobedience survive with it, or was the analogy carrying both? If it doesn't survive, is there a better comparison for the specific work it was being asked to do: the polity as an umpire whose rulings settle a question the players cannot settle play by play, as an emergency service whose directives you follow for the duration of the rescue, as a platform or protocol in which the rules are partly enforced by the architecture, or something else? And if the honest answer is that nothing in this family secures a content-independent duty of obedience, tell me that, and tell me where the burden of argument then falls in my chapter.
