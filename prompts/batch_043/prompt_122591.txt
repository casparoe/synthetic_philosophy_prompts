i'm a backend/infra engineer, no philosophy classes past high school, and i got into a dumb dinner-party argument about art forgeries that i have not been able to let go of. i keep trying to write down the rule i'm using and it keeps breaking. so: here are my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases, and i want someone to tell me what principle (if any) actually generates them, which ones i have to give up, and what the rule says about the cases i list at the end.

1. A studio assistant paints a passage of a canvas under the master's eye, master signs it, sells it. Real. Full value. obviously.

2. Same assistant, same pigments, same canvas, paints the whole thing alone at night, and it passes as the master's for three hundred years. Then someone finds a receipt or whatever. Fake. Value collapses to near zero. Here's the part that bugs me: nothing about the object changed on the day the news came out. I still think it lost its value. that feels insane and i also stand by it.

3. Every big famous forgery that got exposed — people still line up to look at them, they're gorgeous, and experts had rated them as good as anything else attributed to that painter. Still think the value dropped. See above.

4. A well-known contemporary artist who never touches his own work: sends specs and materials lists to a shop, they build it, he approves it. Fully authentic. nobody's losing sleep. But that's the same physical relation as case 2 with the secrecy removed — so am I really just punishing lying, and if so why does my verdict in case 3 feel like it's about the object and not about the guy?

5. A photographer's negative. Print pulled in 1978 under her supervision; another print pulled from the same negative in 2005 by her estate, authorized, stamped on the back, chemically indistinguishable from the first. I want to say both are real, and also that the 1978 one is worth several times more. I can't say what the second one is missing.

6. My own attempt to make it crisp, engineer-style: an artist publishes the complete build instructions for a work — materials, dimensions, tolerances — plus a reference object, and announces that anyone who makes one to spec is making a genuine work of hers, and she'll sign the first ten. Number 11 rolls off the same process, byte-identical as physical objects can be. My gut says: it's a copy, it is not a fake, and "fake" is the wrong word for something with zero deception in it. So maybe value and fakeness are just different axes? (in my day job: a reproducible build gives you two bit-identical binaries and nobody thinks one of them is counterfeit. I don't know why art should be different and I don't know why it feels different.)

7. A fresco that's been restored so many times that essentially none of the surface the painter touched is left. Still the original, not a forgery, no problem. So it isn't "how much original matter is present."

8. Someone trained a system on a living painter's work and produced a new canvas in her exact style, same pigments, and when she saw it she said it was better than what she was actually making in 2009 and offered to sign it. I recoil hard at that, even though the artist is signing it and there's no deception at all. My best story is that what's missing is the process: hers was exploratory, this one was optimized to match an output. But that story seems to put pressure on case 1 too, and it makes case 3 weird — a forgery that's better than the real thing should, on this story, be worth more, and it isn't.
9. A museum displays a Roman bust as a copy for a hundred years; turns out the "original" it was copying was itself a Renaissance fake. I laugh, and I don't think the copy is worth one cent less. So one unmasking destroys value (case 2) and another does nothing at all.

The principle I keep half-writing and then crossing out: authenticity is a causal-historical property — the object has to come from the right kind of intentional act by the right person — while "fake" adds intent to deceive, and money should track the object's perceptible and art-historical properties only. That handles 4 and 7 and probably 6, but it flatly predicts that the exposure in case 2 shouldn't matter (it does), that I shouldn't care in case 8 (I do), and it doesn't explain the 2-vs-9 split.

So: is there one principle, or do I need two or three stacked? Which clause does each of my cases buy, i.e. what would the rule look like if you removed the case that motivated it? And what does your version say about: (a) a painting attributed to a "pupil of" somebody, where we now think the pupil never existed — anonymous, or fake? (b) a bronze cast posthumously from the artist's own mold, explicitly authorized in the will; (c) an artist who specifies a rule, generates a thousand variants mechanically, and picks one — is the picking the act that matters, or the thousand? (d) a genuine object whose paperwork was forged to get it out of a country, versus a forged object with flawless paperwork. Please argue it out rather than point me at books or papers or names of theories — I can't get at any of that anyway and I want the reasoning, not a reading list. And if the honest answer is that one of my verdicts is just an inconsistency I should drop, say which and why.
