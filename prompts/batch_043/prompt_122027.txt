i'm writing a novel set in Fustat and one of my narrators is a shochet, a ritual slaughterer, who's been sitting in on a circle going through Maimonides' Guide for the Perplexed, and he can't get past the part about the sacrifices: the claim that the cult was left standing because the people could not be dragged off it all at once, that human nature can't leap from one extreme to its opposite, so the law kept the shape of the rite and changed what it pointed at — one place instead of every high place, a few species, one priestly family, prayer grafted onto it — and that this was wisdom rather than a retreat. He also says the weekday Amidah three times a day, including the blessing where you ask God to accept the Temple service and Israel's offerings again, so every day he asks for the thing he believes was only ever tolerated.

What I want is a sestina he could think his way through at the block, not addressing a reader, more talking across the animal to the God the rite was re-aimed at. The form should do the arguing: the six end-words come back in rotated order, nothing added, nothing dropped, only moved, which is exactly his picture of what was done to the rite. You pick the six, but three should be plain words from his hands and the animal and three from the vocabulary of the explanation (habit, gradually, the Name, that sort of thing), and the rotation should feel like a rite being carried from one place to another rather than like cleverness. The envoi should be the one place he admits out loud that he still asks for it back, and the poem shouldn't settle whether that asking is a wish that the concession would end or a wish that it would be restored.

Someone who actually knows the Guide shouldn't wince: the concession isn't a punishment, it isn't philosophers sneering at simple believers, and nothing in it says the sacrifices were ever abolished or that God wanted the blood.

Give me the poem first, then a short section headed Notes for me — 150 words tops — on which end-word carries which part of the argument, and where you let the poem refuse to settle.
