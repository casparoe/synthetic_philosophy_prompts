I'm the token novelist on the collections committee of the Ridgely Falls Historical Society — they stuck me on it because my last book was set in a made-up Bronze Age river town, which mostly means I feel like a fraud whenever we discuss the actual dig up on Kettle Hill. Here's where we are. The site was excavated from 1962 to 1969 by a local amateur society that never really published it, and the finds sit in our one good display case under a label written in 1971: "Shrine of the Mother," a votive treasury, c. 1400–1100 BC. The reasoning behind that label was basically (a) twenty-six clay figurines with exaggerated hips and breasts, (b) a plastered platform someone called an altar, and (c) the fact that the room has a single narrow doorway, so they figured it was set apart for something holy. Our new curator, Marisol, went back through the original notebooks and the boxes in the basement and now wants the case relabeled as a storeroom — a place where counting and debt and sealed deposits happened, not prayer. Her evidence, as I understand it: the room's floor was replastered fourteen times, and every single plaster layer has a thin scatter of broken figurine bits, but almost no two fragments in a given layer actually join, while fragments from the *same* figurine turn up in layers three or four apart, so the smashing happened somewhere else and the pieces were being kept and put down over a long stretch of time. Then there are the caches: 41 objects were found in sealed spots — under thresholds, inside wall cavities, down the filled-in well shaft — and these are consistently the *finest* made things in the room, way better finished than the stuff lying on the floors, which is a pattern she calls "blind caching," the better the object the more likely it was stuck somewhere nobody could ever look at it again. And there are 96 little carved stone tokens bearing only seven distinct marks, which keep turning up in stacks of five to seven, one carefully made and several crude, and the proportions of the seven marks are near-identical from stack to stack and cache to cache — the same ratio also shows up on clay sealings found out at the site's gate, which is wierd if these are offerings to a goddess and pretty unsurprising if they're accounting. Oh, and the "altar" turns out on closer look to be the stub of a collapsed stair.

Half the board thinks Marisol is doing exactly what the 1971 committee did, just with a different fashion — reading a story into objects that would support a dozen other stories. The other half says keeping "Shrine of the Mother" is a sixties reflex (female figurines, therefore fertility cult, therefore mother goddess) and that we'd be spending town money to assert something the objects don't support. Meanwhile the "Goddess of Kettle Hill" is definately our town's whole identity: summer festival, the logo on the water tower, and a group called the Daughters of the Hill who leave fresh flowers at the case every month and treat it as a living holy place. The donor family whose name is on the wing is not thrilled either. And the tourism brochure for next season goes to print in five weeks, with the gallery reopening in March, so we can't just table it.

So: draft the memo I'll circulate to the committee before our October 3 meeting. It should recommend one concrete course of action with a time frame — relabel, keep the old label, hedge, phase something in, whatever you actually think is right — and it should take the strongest argument on the losing side seriously instead of knocking it down cheaply. I'd guess the strongest one is that temples in the ancient world *were* archives and banks, so an administrative reading doesn't rule the sacred out at all, and anyway accounting stamps don't explain why the tokens and figurines look like *bodies*; Marisol's ratios explain the counts but not the content. My own novelist's instinct is to put two readings side by side, but the board chair says a label that says "maybe" twice isn't a label. Lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence — the recommendation, not the setup — then give the reasoning, no preamble and don't just restate what I've told you back at me. Keep it to around 700 words, and write it so that both a skeptical board member and one of the Daughters could read it without feeling sneered at.
