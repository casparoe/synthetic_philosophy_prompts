Hey — I'm on my university's Commemorative Naming Committee (we got one after the last round of protests) and I've got to circulate a decision memo before Thursday and I could use a draft I can hack at.

The case: Vance Hall, our main lecture building, named in 1962 for Alton P. Vance, a donor and thirty-year trustee. The same guy spent about two decades on a state eugenics board and signed off on several hundred sterilizations of people committed to state institutions, and he wrote pretty openly about it in letters we now have. The original gift agreement has a reversion clause: if the university takes the name off, the corpus goes back to the Vance family — roughly the chunk of the endowment that currently pays for about six full scholarships a year for first-gen students from the surrounding county. So the money that funds the students with the strongest claim to have been harmed by the other part of his life is exactly the money we forfeit by removing his name. There's also a family lawyer who has hinted they'd contest any removal as a breach, and the student government has passed a resolution demanding removal by the end of the term. Committee is split down the middle.

The part I keep going round on: a campus survey last spring found under 5% of students could identify who Vance was; most thought the building was named for the county or for a similarly named donor, and for years the directory and the bus-stop signs have listed it as just "Vance Hall," with nothing anywhere on or in the building saying who Vance was. So the name has partly come loose from the man — for nearly everyone who says it, it just means "the building with the big lecture theatre." That makes me unsure whether taking it off is the speech act people think it is: are we withdrawing an honor, or just relabeling a word nobody reads as a name? And by the same token, if we put a new honoree up there, does that carry the content we'd want it to, or are we just swapping labels?

The live options as I see them: strip the name and eat the reversion; keep it and install a permanent counter-commemoration (the family says they'd waive the reversion if the name stays and a substantial installation goes in — which I find a bit repulsive, honestly); rename to something non-commemorative, like a place or a number; or formally redesignate the existing name as memorial rather than honorific and say so in one sentence on the facade.

What I want: a memo for the committee with the recommendation in the first sentence, then the reasoning, then a short honest paragraph on the strongest thing on the side you're voting against — I want something the losing half can live with. Include the concrete next step and a timeline, and say explicitly what the recommendation costs us. Don't spend words on general theory of commemoration or rehash "we shouldn't erase history" vs "names honor people"; assume we've read the op-eds. Hard cap 550 words.
