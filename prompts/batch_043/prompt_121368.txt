I'm a backend engineer with no philosophy background and I keep chewing on this Hofstadter thing (I think its from The Mind's I?) about the careenium: a frictionless table with tiny fast particles, the simms, and huge sluggish ones, the simmballs. Simms bash simmballs around, but once the simmballs are sitting in some arrangement they also hem the simms in, so the big configuration has a kind of downward grip on the little stuff. People seem to read this as yes, symbols really do push the substrate around. I have firm gut verdicts on concrete cases and zero idea what rule I'm using:

1. A config file my service re-reads on every request. Change a line and behavior changes instantly. Obviously the symbol is pushing the substrate.
2. A compiled binary. The source fixed the bytes once; now the machine does what the bytes say, and "the loop on line 40" is just a convenience for me. No longer a cause.
3. Type annotations in a language that erases them completely before runtime. They excluded a whole class of programs from ever existing. I want to say: causal.
4. A comment in the same file, also erased. Obviously not causal — even though it too shaped what the human typed next.
5. A garbage collector's reachability graph. No location anywhere, spread across the heap, different every millisecond. Feels like the clearest case of something real that isn't a thing.
6. A deadlock. The low-level story has instructions and voltages and no mention of it at all; you only see it at the level of threads and lock order. Here I feel the high-level story isn't merely convenient, it's the only place the explanation lives.
7. Trained model weights: a billion numbers that mean anything at all only relative to an architecture. I flip-flop on this one daily.

My working rule has been: a high-level thing is a real cause if I could have written it differently and the outcome would have changed, without touching anything low-level. #4 breaks it, and I suspect #2 does too. Is there a cleaner principle that gets 1, 3, 5, 6 in and 2, 4 out? Give me one rule — or two candidates if they disagree somewhere — say which case is buying each clause, tell me honestly which of my verdicts I'd have to give up, and then run it on #7 plus a case I haven't listed: a content-addressed build cache where every artifact is stored under the hash of its own bytes, so editing one file renames everything downstream. Does anything there get pushed around, or is the hash just a label?
