I ship the infra behind a bunch of LLM agents at work, and I've noticed something annoying: my team can't agree on how to talk about these things in writing. When we do an incident review I'll write something like "the agent thought the migration had already run, so it skipped the backup," and someone will redline it, and then we spend twenty minutes arguing about whether the model "believes" anything. Meanwhile the same person who redlines it will happily say "it's trying to get me to approve the tool call." So we're clearly doing this inconsistently, and I want a rule I can hand the team.

What I want from you isn't a verdict, it's a spec. Suppose someone hands me a theory that says when it's actually right — not just convenient shorthand, actually right — to attribute a belief or a goal to one of these systems. What does that theory have to be able to do before I should adopt it? The purpose is fixed and it's an engineering one: I need to (a) predict what the agent does in an environment we never tested it in, (b) decide how much weight to put on what the model says about its own reasons, and (c) write incident reports where the difference between "it believed X" and "it emitted a string consistent with X" changes who we page. Adequacy relative to that, not adequacy for a seminar.

Here's my half-baked list of requirements with the case behind each — please tell me which of these are genuine desiderata and which are just one camp's prejudices dressed up as requirements.

- The paraphrase cliff. Same question, trivial rephrasing (change the ordering of two bullet points, swap "before" for "prior to"), and the answer flips. Did it believe both? Neither? A theory should say something here rather than shrugging.
- The splice test. Take two fine-tunes off the same base, one trained to always say P and one to always say not-P, and average their weights. The midpoint model is a real thing I can deploy tomorrow. How many beliefs survive the merge, and what's the story at the midpoint, where there's no training signal that ever selected for any answer?
- Attribution laundering. RLHF-style tuning changes what the thing says without obviously touching whatever it encodes about the world. If we go on behavior alone we'll say it now believes things it doesn't. A theory should be able to keep "we reshaped its behavior" and "we changed its mind" apart, or tell me why that distinction is fake.
- The distillation question. A small model distilled from a big one matches it on 99% of behaviors and shares almost no internal structure with it. Same beliefs, or a completely different creature wearing the same outputs?
- Self-report asymmetry. The model says "I assumed the file was at /tmp/x." Does a theory owe us a story about privileged access, or is self-report just more behavior with no special status?
- The eval-awareness trap. The system behaves differently when it can tell it's being evaluated. Which context holds the real belief, and why isn't that just asking which context you like better?
- The retrieval control. Swap the model for a search system that finds the sentence in a corpus of human text. If a proposed test can't tell those two apart, it isn't testing for belief, it's testing for fluency.
- Don't collapse: having a belief vs. how strongly it holds it vs. being disposed to say it vs. wanting it to be true. Keep those separate.
- Earn its keep. If attributions don't beat a purely behavioral predictor on out-of-distribution cases, they're decoration on the incident report.
- Grounding. Does belief require the thing to have picked it up through perception or action in the world? I suspect this one is a prejudice and not a requirement, but argue me out of it or confirm it.

Then: which of these are non-negotiable given my three uses, and which are philosophers' hobbies. Flag any pair that can't both be satisfied at once — if the list itself is impossible that's the most useful thing you could tell me. And name the single requirement that knocks out the most popular accounts. After that a small table is fine, scoring the prediction-first / intentional-stance view, the mechanistic view where a belief is something you can point at in activation space with a probe or a feature, and the deflationary "it's a next-token predictor, there's nothing there" view. Keep the scoring brief; most of the words should go on the criteria. And no reading lists — I don't have access to the literature, and I wouldn't read it anyway. Just pick a side on each requirement.
