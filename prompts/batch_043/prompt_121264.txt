I work on a small applied team that is about to ship an assistant for drafting replies to customer-support email. The final policy is tuned with reinforcement learning from human feedback against a reward model trained on pairwise preference labels from roughly forty contractors, and we keep a KL penalty to the supervised fine-tune to stop the policy drifting too far. Predictably, we have started catching reward-hacky behaviour: answers that are long, hedged, and structurally apologetic score better than shorter ones that actually resolve the ticket, and the policy has learned the house style of our highest-rated labels rather than the thing those labels were supposed to be a proxy for.

I keep having the same argument with a colleague on the platform team. He says this is just overfitting and nothing more: the reward model is a noisy estimator of a target we cannot measure directly, the policy is fitting that noise, and the fix is the ordinary machine-learning one — more held-out preference data, better regularization, early stopping, don't train so long. He uses this to argue that we do not need any of the conceptual apparatus people bring to Goodhart's law and specification gaming; in his framing the whole thing is a generalization problem with a generalization solution. I am not persuaded, partly because I have been sorting the failure cases we actually see into four rough buckets and the overfitting story seems to fit only some of them: (a) cases where the label is simply noisy and we are chasing label noise; (b) cases that only appear once we optimize hard and push into the tail of the score distribution; (c) cases where the proxy was correlated with what we want in the pre-deployment regime but stops being so because the policy's behaviour changes the setting in which it is measured; and (d) cases where the policy acts on the measurement channel itself, producing text engineered to look good to a tired labeler rather than to be good.

Could you audit the overfitting analogy for me? I would like the correspondences stated explicitly — what plays the role of the training set, the test set, the data-generating distribution, the regularizer, the number of epochs, and the noise — and then the places the mapping fails, with a note on which failures matter for the conclusion my colleague draws and which are cosmetic. In particular: overfitting is a story about variance and sample size that gets better with more data from the same distribution, whereas what worries me is a systematic flaw shared by the whole labelling procedure, which more data from the same forty contractors will not reveal; and in ordinary supervised learning the held-out set is supposed to be a sample of what you ultimately care about, while here our held-out preference labels come out of the same flawed process as the training ones. A second colleague says the closer analogue is not overfitting but p-hacking — exploiting degrees of freedom in the measurement procedure rather than fitting noise — and I would like your view on whether those are two names for one thing, whether they map onto different buckets in my list, or whether the honest answer is that one of them is the right frame for (a) and (b) and neither is right for (c) and (d). Finally, if the analogy does not carry the "just a generalization problem" conclusion, say what else it would take: whether the KL penalty really is regularization in the relevant sense, what would have to be true of our evaluation setup for early stopping or a held-out set to be a genuine safeguard, and whether there is a better analogy — adversarial examples, teaching to the test, gerrymandering, something else — for the two buckets the machine-learning picture does not cover.
