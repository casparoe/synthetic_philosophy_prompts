I moderate a lot of panels on science-and-society stuff, and I keep watching people make two moves in a row that I don't have a word for.

Move one: an economist says a regulation that costs each of 300 million people about two dollars imposes 600 million dollars of cost, and that we should weigh that against a policy that ruins one person's life. Half the room nods; the other half says you can't add it up like that — nothing really happened to anybody, so there's nothing to weigh. Fine, that's just the old dust specks versus torture thing.

Move two, twenty minutes later, from someone who was in the second half: forty years of mild tinnitus leaves someone worse off overall than one truly horrible afternoon. Nobody blinked — including the people who had just refused to add up the two-dollar costs. But it's the same shape of move: a huge pile of very small bads summing to something big. The only difference is that it's spread across one life instead of across many lives.

Third thing, which is the one I actually can't place: when I reran the specks case with a twist — 30,000 people each get one speck at the same moment, in the same stadium, and everyone there knows it's happening to everyone else — the refusers got visibly wobblier. No one's experience got any worse. The only thing that changed is that the sufferers now know about each other, and so form something like a group.

So: is there a established concept for this asymmetry — tiny things aggregate within a life but not across lives, and across lives they get easier to aggregate when the people know about each other — or am I bundling three separate things? I don't want a label, I want the carving: what the real line is here and what's merely correlated with it (a single subject? duration? shared awareness? a threshold of noticeability?), which nearby concepts it gets confused with, and where each candidate breaks. And give me a verdict rather than a menu: commit to whether the asymmetry is defensible or a glitch in how we picture other minds, say how confident you are, and say what would change your mind.
