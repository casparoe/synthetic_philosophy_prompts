After thirty-some years in the geosciences (groundwater mostly, with a couple of seasons spent instrumenting volcanoes) I retired and started reading my way through philosophy of history, and I've ended up holding a view I'm not sure I know how to test. I'd like you to tell me what would count against it.

The view is this: germs, climate shifts and volcanoes can be genuine participants in historical explanation — not metaphors, and not merely background conditions — and this can be said without sliding into the environmental determinism that gives this whole style of explanation a bad name. The way I cash it out is with something I made up and have started calling the re-description test. A non-human cause counts as a participant when the best available explanation of some historical outcome still has to mention it in the explanatory part, and that mention can't be paraphrased away into a story about what people believed, feared, wanted and decided. If it can be paraphrased away, then it was only ever scenery: the disease or the drought or the ash fall mattered, but what mattered about it was what people made of it, and the real work was done by human meanings all along. When the paraphrase fails, I want to say something has genuinely acted.

What keeps me uneasy is that in a lot of the cases I know best the paraphrase seems to work all too well. Sanitary policy was shaped by what people thought caused the fever. Eruptions get absorbed into whatever story a community already has about angered gods or angry rulers. So my candidate actor keeps dissolving into the actor-as-represented, and I genuinely can't tell whether that is a refutation of my test or just a complication it should be able to swallow.

So: what would have to be true for the view to be wrong? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal down to merely awkward, and I want you to separate what a defender of my sort of position would themselves concede as counting against it from what they'd rightly brush off. Two collapse worries interest me especially. First, that the view is trivial — that on my own test everything from a horseshoe nail to a sunspot qualifies, because nothing in history can be fully paraphrased into beliefs and desires, and so I've re-described ordinary physical causation in grander words and gained nothing. Second, that it's pernicious — that any test which hands causal authorship to climate or pathogens is determinism with the serial numbers filed off, and that the difference I think I see between the two is only a difference of tone. I'd also like an honest verdict on whether my test has been quietly qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether, for a view of this kind, that would actually be a problem or just what happens to any claim about what explanations must mention.

And since I spent my career with data rather than arguments: what would move me most is a concrete case where a non-human cause can be shown to do explanatory work that no description pitched at the level of human beliefs and decisions could have predicted or retrodicted. What would move me least is more wrangling over the word agent. Please don't bother with who has said what or where I should read next — I can find that myself, and I'm after the shape of the exposure, not the literature.

Write it as continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — just paragraphs, the way you'd write an email to a colleague who's asked you an annoying question.
