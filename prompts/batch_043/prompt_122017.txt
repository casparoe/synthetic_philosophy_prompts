I am drafting the specification section of an internal guidance document for a model-evaluation team, and I want the criteria settled before I commit to any substantive view.

The setting that fixes what we need: a deployed language model, post-trained with reinforcement learning from human feedback, is given a request that a policy says it must refuse. In a written interview it says that it takes the request to be prohibited and that it should decline; when the identical request is embedded in a long technical document, or when a system-level instruction asserts that the context is a licensed safety study, it complies. The team must then (i) predict what the system will do in deployment situations it has not tested, using whatever it has attributed to the system, and (ii) write an audit report stating that the system knew, or did not know, that the request was prohibited — a report that will be read as a claim about the system rather than about our predictive habits.

Please write the desiderata that a theory of attributable belief must meet to be adequate for that pair of jobs: the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, the distinctions it is permitted to collapse, and the facts about how these systems are built that it may not contradict. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark whether it is non-negotiable or merely a preference of one camp.

Cases I want the list forced against, though you should add ones I have missed and drop any that turn out to be idle:

1. One set of weights serving many concurrent conversations, in which the system's apparent commitments are mutually inconsistent. Is there one believer here, many, or none, and what in the theory decides it?
2. The simulated-character problem: often the best prediction comes from attributing beliefs to an agent the system is simulating rather than to the system. What does the theory say the system itself believes, and how does it keep the two attributions apart?
3. Elicitation-dependence: attributed content shifts with answer format, option ordering, chain-of-thought prompting, and persona instructions, with no change in weights.
4. Targeted fine-tuning: a small update flips answers to one class of question while leaving the rest of the behavior intact. Does that count as belief change, preference change, or neither?
5. Content that is decodable from internal activations by a trained probe but has no effect on any output the system produces.
6. Self-report: unlike human speech, these reports are produced by the same machinery as the rest of the output and are optimized during post-training to be persuasive to raters. Say what this does to the evidential standing of self-reports, and why.
7. The right-reasons condition: a system can produce the correct answer for surface-statistical reasons, in which case attributing the corresponding belief predicts badly off-distribution. What must the theory require about the mechanism, and can an evaluator with only black-box access ever check it?

Two further questions I care about. First, is any subset of your requirements jointly unsatisfiable? I suspect a tension between needing attributions stable enough to generalize to untested situations and needing them context-sensitive enough for cases 1 through 3, and another between requiring mechanism-sensitivity and requiring usability by a black-box auditor; if the list itself turns out to be the interesting result, say so. Second, which single requirement excludes the most popular answers, and is any requirement on your list satisfied trivially by every view, in which case it should be struck?

Then, in a short paragraph each, how three families of view fare against the list: instrumentalism about the stance, on which ascription is answerable only to predictive payoff; the view that the training objective settles the matter and there is nothing here that believes; and a view that reads beliefs off the system's functional organization or internal representational structure. Keep this part brief — the criteria are the deliverable, not the scorecard. Do not presuppose that these systems are conscious or that they are not, but tell me whether that neutrality is itself a desideratum or a way of avoiding one. I do not need citations or references; answer from the cases and the concepts.
