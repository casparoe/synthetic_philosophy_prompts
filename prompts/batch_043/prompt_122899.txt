You are a professional philosopher of physics who works on spacetime theories, and also someone who is used to explaining arguments to clever non-specialists. Please think carefully and in stages. Before you answer me, sketch out for yourself the space of things someone might say about what a pair of symmetry-related models represents, and what would have to be true for each of those things to be right. Then work through the argument I describe and only then give me your verdict. Take your time, and if you think I have something wrong, say so plainly even where I have not asked.

I am a novelist, not a philosopher, and I am finishing a book in which two characters — a relativist and a philosopher of physics, once collaborators, now adversaries — have a long argument about whether the points of spacetime are real individuals or merely positions in a web of relations among physical events. Here is the argument as I have it.

You take a solution of the field equations and a bounded region — the hole — and you apply a smooth map that is the identity everywhere outside that region but rearranges the points inside it. Because the equations are generally covariant, the result is another solution. The two models agree on everything outside the hole, including on an initial slice before it, and disagree inside it. The philosopher character then says the following: the container view of spacetime is refuted, because the theory cannot tell us which of the two is the actual one, so it is stuck with a radical indeterminism, and therefore space and time must be relations among events rather than a substance. She also generalises, in a move I invented for her and rather like: she calls it the Symmetry Fork. Any theory with a symmetry group that can be applied locally in a bounded region faces the same choice — either the symmetry-related models represent distinct physical possibilities, in which case the theory is indeterministic, or they represent one possibility, in which case the theory's variables carry surplus structure. She says the same fork shows up in Newtonian mechanics, when you imagine the whole material universe displaced a few feet or given a uniform extra velocity, and again in the gauge freedom of the electromagnetic potentials. Her conclusion is that the old substance-versus-relations dispute is really only a dispute about whether a theory's symmetry-related models represent one world or many, and that once you see that, the person who wants spacetime points to be real has nowhere left to stand. I have also given her a line about Einstein having first used the argument as a reason to abandon generally covariant field equations before returning to them in the final theory, which is a small subplot in the book.

I want the relativist's reply to be genuinely strong rather than a rhetorical dodge, and I cannot tell whether I have the dialectic right. Three things in particular.

First, I had planned to have her reply that the two models are the same world described twice, because a point of spacetime just is the bundle of field properties and relations located at it, so shuffling which point plays which role changes nothing. But written that way it sounds as though she has simply become a relationalist and conceded the whole argument. Is there a coherent position that keeps spacetime points real as individuals while denying that the rearranged model is a new possibility, or does that combination collapse into her opponent's view? If it is coherent, what exactly has to be said for it to amount to more than a verbal manoeuvre?

Second, I have a scene in mind in which there is a laboratory with instruments and a person inside the hole, and she points out that this observer would obtain exactly the same measurement results in both models, so nothing that could ever be observed is left undetermined. I had taken this to dispose of the indeterminism charge, and I have written the philosopher character as floored by it. Is that right, or is it a cheat?

Third, I would like to know whether the Symmetry Fork is a good frame for the whole thing or whether it quietly misdescribes what is at issue. In particular, a friend who read the chapter said that taking the surplus-structure horn of the fork costs the substantivalist nothing at all, which would make my philosopher character's conclusion a non sequitur — but he could not explain why, and neither of us could tell whether the electromagnetic case is genuinely parallel to the spacetime one.

If it matters for any of this: one of my characters also worries that in a completely empty and perfectly symmetric region there is nothing left to tell the points apart, and that this breaks the bundle-of-properties reply. I do not know whether that worry is deep or a red herring, and I would rather rewrite the scene than publish a chapter in which specialists laugh at my characters.
