I write distributed systems for a living and have no formal philosophy background, and I have been sitting in on a reading group where two people have now spent three sessions arguing about sphexishness — the digger-wasp example in which the wasp drags her prey to the mouth of the burrow, leaves it on the threshold, goes inside to inspect the chamber, comes back out and drags the prey in; if the prey is moved a few centimetres while she is inside, she emerges, drags it back to the threshold, and goes in to inspect again, apparently for as many times as the experimenter cares to move it. One of them holds that this isolates a real property of a mechanism, and that the property does work in arguments about free will. The other holds that it isolates nothing but a difference of degree, that the procedure is a trick played on a creature whose normal environment contains no hand that moves prey, and that free-will arguments leaning on it are leaning on a rhetorical image. They have stopped making progress and are now mostly restating themselves.

Please draft the document they should have produced instead: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between the two positions. Use these names and glosses throughout:

- The Architecturalist: rigidity is a property of the control architecture — specifically of whether the agent re-reads the world at the points where re-reading would make a difference — and is therefore a real, largely binary, mechanism-level property that can be present even when observed behaviour looks flexible.
- The Ecologist: rigidity is a property of a behaviour-in-an-environment; every mechanism is rigid under some perturbation and flexible under others; the interesting questions are quantitative — how much, how fast, at what cost, in which environment — and the binary notion does no work.

Both glosses are my paraphrase of what each of them said. If either would reject the wording, say so and supply the wording they would accept.

Materials I want used. These are my stipulations, invented for the purpose, and I would rather you sharpen them than replace them:

1. The re-entrancy test. Take any routine an agent performs. Run it, then at a randomly chosen point change the world so that the next step is pointless or wrong. Score the response as: (a) restart the whole routine, (b) complete the current step and then stop, (c) abort immediately, (d) substitute a different action that still reaches the goal. Sphexish behaviour is (a) under perturbations that a fully re-entrant architecture would have handled by (b), (c) or (d).
2. First-order versus second-order re-entrancy. First-order: the agent re-reads the state of the world (is the prey still where I left it?). Second-order: the agent re-reads its own policy or goal (should I be doing this at all?). A thermostat is first-order re-entrant; whether anything can be second-order re-entrant seems to me to be most of the free-will question.
3. The gradience knobs. Perturb by an amount x (a millimetre, a centimetre, a metre), or hold the perturbation in place for a duration d, or make it progressively costlier to ignore. Does the response vary continuously with x and d, or is there a cliff, and who has to explain the cliff?
4. The time-of-check-to-time-of-use framing. In my field the wasp's failure has a name: a race between checking a condition and acting on it. She checked the chamber and then acted on a state that was no longer true. There are two standard fixes — re-check immediately before use, or hold a lock so the world cannot change in between — and she can do neither. So: is sphexishness a bug, a resource limit, or an architectural choice? Does the classification change if the agent could have locked but locking was too expensive to be worth it?
5. The underdetermination worry. Any finite behavioural record looks consistent both with an architecture that re-reads and weights the new input at approximately zero and with one that never re-reads at all. What, if anything, distinguishes them, and must the distinction be drawn at the implementation level rather than the behavioural level?
6. My own case, which is why I care. The language-model agents I build re-read their tool output at every step and are about as non-sphexish as anything I have worked on by criterion 1 — and they have no goals of their own, no stake in anything, and no capacity to ask whether they should be running at all. Where does each camp place them, and does that placement embarrass either camp?

What the document must contain:

- A numbered section of claims both sides can sign. They should be load-bearing: for each claim, at least one of the two would have denied it at the start of the collaboration. If the strongest available claim is something like that behaviour exists, leave it out.
- A disagreements table: one row per residual disagreement, with the Architecturalist's own wording of their position in one column, the Ecologist's own wording in another, and a column recording whether the disagreement is genuine or merely verbal. Where you call a disagreement verbal, state exactly which term was doing the work and what substantive question remains underneath it.
- A cruxes section: the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one side rather than merely refine it. For each crux, give the argument, case, or perturbation both sides agree bears on it; the result each side predicts, recorded in advance; and, explicitly, the result each side concedes would count against their own view. Include at least one crux where both sides agree in advance that no available test will settle it, and say what the obstacle is.
- A short closing section on what sphexishness does and does not establish about free will and moral excuse, given what the rest of the document has conceded. In particular: is that I did not re-read ever an excuse, and is the human case that looks most like the wasp — the habitual commuter who drives to the old office on the day of the move, or the person who says the hurtful thing they have said ten thousand times before — the same kind of case or a different one?

Two constraints. Nobody in the room can look anything up, so do not rest any claim on an empirical fact about real wasps or real insects, or on the reliability of the anecdote itself; where the debate does turn on such a fact, flag it as an open empirical question and record what each side predicts about it instead of asserting it. And do not settle anything by appeal to what some author has argued — the joint statement has to stand on its own arguments.

Format it as a document both of them could put their names to: numbered agreed claims first, then the disagreements table, then the cruxes with predictions, then the closing section on free will. Write it in plain prose and tables, no bullet-point mush, and mark anywhere you had to invent a distinction neither of them would have offered.
