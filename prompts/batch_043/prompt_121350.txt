I'm a journalist working on a feature about why well-informed people go on disagreeing, and I need help auditing an analogy that has quietly become the spine of the piece.

My case study is a long-running row between two senior epidemiologists. Both have read the same literature; both know the other's published position. One puts the probability that a widely prescribed drug causes a particular rare adverse event at something like one in ten thousand and worth acting on; the other puts it at effectively zero. Neither, privately, thinks the other is stupid or corrupt.

A commentator I interviewed says the row is an illusion, and reaches for a picture: two engineers reading the same gauge. If their readings differ, either an instrument is faulty or somebody is misreporting it — there is no third option in which both are competent, honest, and right. He cites Aumann's agreement theorem as the proof: agents who share a prior and who have common knowledge of each other's posteriors must post the same number, so a disagreement that survives full disclosure of the evidence is itself evidence of a defect — incoherence, motivated reasoning, or bad faith. He uses this to licence a strong practical conclusion: funders and editors should stop treating expert disagreement as a reason for modesty or for splitting the difference, because in such cases at least one party is failing, and a formal synthesis or a betting market will reveal which.

What I want is an audit, not a summary. Set out the correspondence explicitly — a table is fine: what plays the engineer, the gauge, the reading, the faulty instrument, the misreported figure — and then say where the mapping breaks. In particular, test three worries of my own. First, the gauge picture imports a shared calibration standard; my two sources plausibly carry different priors about base rates for rare harms and about how much observational data can bear compared with trials, and I'm told the whole impossibility result depends essentially on a common prior rather than merely different private information. Is that right, and does it matter here? Second, "the drug causes the adverse event" is not one proposition but a family — across dose, duration, genotype, background rate — so they may be pricing different members of that family, or disagreeing about which partition of possibilities is the right one to reason over; does the theorem get any grip when the contested thing is the state space rather than the probability? Third, common knowledge: I've been told that if there is even a small chance a stated number never reached the other party, exact agreement no longer follows but only approximate agreement, and that the result also assumes each agent has already computed the posterior their evidence supports, which nobody does with a messy literature. Is either claim correct, and how much work does it do?

Finally, a second source offered a replacement picture: two surveyors on adjoining hills in thick fog, each describing the lie of the land. Their accounts differ because the vantage points differ, and neither is misreading anything; on his telling this shows the theorem is really about a shared map that real disputants simply don't have. Audit that one too — I worry it proves too much, since on that picture no disagreement is ever a mistake and it cannot distinguish my epidemiologists from a row where one side is quietly funded by the manufacturer. Separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended use from the ones that don't, tell me whether a better analogy exists, and say whether the strong conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table or whether the analogy was carrying it. Finish with a single final line in exactly this form: \answer{does-not-survive}, \answer{survives-weakened} or \answer{survives}.
