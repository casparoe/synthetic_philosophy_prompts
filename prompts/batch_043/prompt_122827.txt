I am writing as a retired experimental physical chemist — thirty-eight years in an industrial spectroscopy laboratory, the last decade of it spent signing off on safety documentation — who has, since retiring, been auditing a graduate seminar in philosophy of science. I have developed an unreasonable fondness for structural realism, and an even stronger suspicion that its literature takes itself slightly too seriously. I would like to commission a piece of satire about it.

The form: a parody Safety Data Sheet, in the standard sixteen-section format (Identification; Hazard Identification; Composition / Information on Ingredients; First-Aid Measures; Fire-Fighting Measures; Accidental Release Measures; Handling and Storage; Exposure Controls / Personal Protection; Physical and Chemical Properties; Stability and Reactivity; Toxicological Information; Ecological Information; Disposal Considerations; Transport Information; Regulatory Information; Other Information). The product is a reagent-grade ontology sold by an invented supplier — invent the company, its address, the emergency telephone number, a CAS number, a product code, an issue date and a revision number, and make the header block look exactly like the real thing. Around 900–1,100 words for the document itself.

The target is structural realism: the claim that what survives theory change is the mathematical structure rather than the furniture, in both its cautious version (we should believe only that the world has the structure our best theories attribute to it) and its bold version (there are no objects at bottom, only structure, and objects are mere positions in it), and above all the shared discursive habit of announcing the retirement of objects while continuing to write as though there were some. The satire must exaggerate real features of the view, not invented defects; a philosopher of science should recognize that the author understands what is being mocked and should feel the mockery is fair.

My own sketch of the dialectic, from which you should not feel bound: superseded theories are routinely discarded at the level of what they said existed, while their equations carry over into their successors; so perhaps structure is all we ever knew, or all there ever was. The view's most awkward embarrassment is that a claim of the form "there are some things arranged thus-and-so" is close to empty, since almost any large enough collection of things can be arranged thus-and-so — the usual repair being to insist that the relations in question be physically significant rather than arbitrary, a distinction easier to invoke than to state. And there is a standing residual question about what, if the objects go, the structure is a structure of. All three of these should be legible in your document, but none of them may be named, explained, footnoted, or winked at: they have to arrive as product hazards, impurities, incompatibilities, and discontinued lines, or not at all.

Two or three apparatus I would like you to build on, and to extend freely in the same spirit: the product comes in two grades, a thin Ramsey-grade and a thick causal-grade, the latter containing trace nomic necessity and costing considerably more per gram; a fictional inspectorate issues Continuity Certificates reporting a "Continuity Number" — the measured fraction of a theory's structure that survives into its successor — and the measured values are suspiciously near unity for anything expressible as an equation and near zero for anything a bench scientist would call a mechanism; and the relata, I think, should be listed in Section 3 as available separately, with the line discontinued. Please do not confine yourself to these; invent a spill incident, an incompatible material, a regulatory citation, a revision history in Section 16.

Some constraints. Voice and register must stay deadpan throughout — flat, bureaucratic, exactly the tone of a real SDS — with no asides to the reader, no "as philosophers say," no glossary, and no section that turns into a disguised essay. Do not name any real philosopher, alive or dead, and do not refer to any real book, paper, or institution; if you need an authority, invent one. Nothing in the piece may require a library or an internet connection: no citations, no real CAS numbers, no real standards bodies, nothing whose accuracy depends on looking something up. The result should be funny to a specialist in philosophy of science, and still legible to me — an old chemist who has read a great many documents in exactly this format and laughs at the ones that are wrong in the right way.

You are a professional philosopher of science with a sideline in comic writing, and I would like you to take this seriously rather than quickly. Before drafting, spend roughly three hundred words preparing: first, list the genuine commitments and genuine vulnerabilities of structural realism that are most susceptible to deadpan treatment; second, assign each of the sixteen sections the single joke it can best carry, and discard any section where you cannot find one; third, decide how the two grades differ in specification and which one the supplier recommends, and why. Then write the sheet. Precision about the view matters more to me than the density of the jokes; a gag that gets the metaphysics wrong is worth less to me than a clause that gets it right.
