I'm trying to work out what I actually believe about distributive priority, and my verdicts on cases don't line up with any formulation I can write down. Read the numbers as lifetime welfare for the named people, counted in a way that already includes any distress a person feels from being worse off than others, so that none of my verdicts can be explained away as a hidden gain in welfare.

1. Ana 100, Bo 40 versus Ana 85, Bo 60: I prefer the second. Fifteen from the better-off person buys twenty for the worse-off one.
2. Ana 100, Bo 40 versus Ana 60, Bo 60: I prefer the second, though uneasily — it costs forty to give twenty.
3. Ada 100, Ben 100, Cleo 100, Dev 40 versus 95, 95, 95, 55: I prefer the first. I won't take five from each of three people to give fifteen to the worst-off one, even though in case 1 I accepted what looks like the same kind of trade.
4. Ana 1000, Bo 10 versus Ana 990, Bo 9: I judge the first better all things considered, but I can't shake the sense that the second is better in one respect, and that this respect is not merely Bo's welfare misdescribed. I had assumed that a prioritarian view simply escapes the levelling-down objection; this case makes me doubt it.
5. A coin flip between (Ana 100, Bo 0) and (Ana 0, Bo 100) versus (Ana 50, Bo 50) for sure: I prefer the sure thing, and I'd still prefer it at (49, 49), even though each person's own prospect is the same either way.
6. One person, two halves of a life: 100-then-20 versus 70-then-60. I prefer the second and feel no pull towards smoothing. Given the same numbers for two different people, I would weigh the worse-off stretch heavily, and I can't see why the arithmetic should care whether the two levels belong to one person or two.

My working principle is the sum of a strictly increasing, strictly concave transform of each person's welfare. It gets 1 right, but so far as I can tell it mispredicts 3 unless the concavity is implausibly steep, it has no room for the judgement in 4, it is silent on whether 5 is to be read ex ante or ex post, and it applies to time-slices in 6 whether or not I want it to.

Is there a single principle — precise enough to be stated in a sentence or two — that captures these verdicts, or nearly all of them? Tell me which case motivates each clause, which of my verdicts it forces me to give up and why, and what it predicts about a case I haven't listed: Ana 100 alone versus Ana 100 together with Bo at 40. Please answer in British English.
