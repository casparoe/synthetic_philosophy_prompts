For several years I worked on governance and anti-corruption programmes for an international organisation, mostly in two countries with very different political economies, and I have since spent time on the compliance side of a large institution. I find that I trust my verdicts about particular episodes far more than I trust any of the general definitions that get wheeled out, and I would like to know whether these verdicts add up to a single principle or whether I have simply been inconsistent in ways that flatter me.

Here are the cases, with my verdicts.

1. A road contractor makes a large, entirely legal, publicly disclosed donation to the minister's re-election campaign during the month the tender is being evaluated, and is awarded the contract. Verdict: corrupt.

2. A shipping agent pays a customs officer the equivalent of twenty dollars to release a container that has already been cleared, after the officer has let it sit for four days. Verdict: corrupt, but at the trivial end.

3. A woman gives the nurse who has just delivered her baby a small envelope of cash, in a public clinic where the nursing staff have not been paid for months and where every patient's family does this. Verdict: not corrupt. I want to say it is a survival tax, not a bribe.

4. A pharmaceutical representative pays for a hospital department's dinners and a conference trip abroad; the department then switches its standard prescription to her product, which is not otherwise superior to the cheaper alternative. Verdict: corrupt.

5. A family gives a village headman a goat the week before he mediates their land dispute; he rules for them. The gift is small, public, and entirely expected by everyone in the village. Verdict: corrupt, though I feel much less certainty here than in case 1, and I worry that my certainty is imported.

6. At the New Year, officials and the business people they deal with exchange expensive gifts of a kind fixed by convention, with no favour requested and no specific decision in view. Verdict: I cannot bring myself to call it corrupt, and I am not sure whether that is a verdict or a failure of nerve.

7. A local big man distributes pigs and shell valuables that are his own, on his own land, to a following that then owes him their labour and their votes. Verdict: not corrupt. That is what political standing consists of there.

8. A member of parliament pays for constituents' funerals out of a constituency development fund, and attends them. Verdict: I recoil at this, more than at 7, but I cannot say that it is corrupt in the way case 1 is, and I don't know what the difference is made of.

9. A manager hires his cousin over a stranger with slightly better qualifications. Verdict: corrupt, but a different animal from bribery, and I could not say which of the two is worse.

10. A regulator leaves for a well-paid advisory post with an industry she supervised, having made no decision in its favour while in office. Verdict: corrupt, or at least adjacent to it.

11. A wealthy donor funds a think tank whose research reliably supports positions favourable to his interests; there is no instruction, no review of outputs before publication, and the researchers are sincere. Verdict: not corrupt, though I think it degrades something.

12. A minister awards contracts to firms owned by men from his own district, taking nothing for himself, out of a sense of obligation to people who supported him. Verdict: corrupt — this is the case that frightens me most, and the one where I have least idea what I am saying.

I have been testing these against a device of my own, which I call the reverse-market test: rewrite the episode as an outright sale, and ask what the office-holder is handing over. If what she hands over is something she has no right to sell — a decision, a permit, a contract, a place in a queue, a hire, a grade — then the exchange is corrupt, whoever is selling and whatever the local custom. If what she hands over is only her own labour at a task nobody was otherwise owed, or her own property, then it is not. That gets 1, 2, 4, 5, 8, 9, 10 and 12 on my side of the line, and it gets 7 and 11 as well. But it convicts the nurse in 3, which I refuse to accept, it is silent on 6 because nothing is handed over, and I notice that it requires an exchange at all: it has nothing to say about a treasurer who simply transfers money to his own account, or about pure favouritism with no payment, which are the cases I would have thought were the heart of the matter.

So: state for me the best principle, or the two or three best candidates, precisely enough that I could apply it without consulting you again, and for each clause name the case it is there to secure and the case it forces me to surrender. Tell me honestly which of my verdicts are sentiment rather than judgement, and in particular whether the envelope in 3 is excused but corrupt, or not corrupt at all. Then give me the principle's verdict on the case I have never settled — a bottle of whisky I gave, after the fact, to a district official who had expedited permits for our field office, with no expectation on either side of anything further — and on two cases of your own choosing that I have not listed.

Finally, the question I actually care about. Every account I can construct seems to presuppose a particular picture of what an office is: that it is separable from the person holding it, that it comes with a defined jurisdiction, and that its reasons are supposed to be impartially available to anyone within that jurisdiction. That picture is not the one at work in cases 5, 7 and 8. Does the principle you offer simply take the side of that picture and call the others corrupt by definition, or can it be stated so that it applies to the headman and the big man without collapsing into saying that whatever local opinion permits is permissible? If it cannot, tell me what I lose by accepting a parochial principle, and whether it still condemns the contractor in case 1 and the minister in case 12.
