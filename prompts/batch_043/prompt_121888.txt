I am writing the refusal-and-override policy for a general-purpose AI assistant deployed to a large and heterogeneous user base, and I want the principle before I write the rules. Below are my verdicts on cases of the kind I actually have to decide. I have reached them one at a time and I do not know that they are consistent. Assume a single deployment, no way to ask the user a clarifying question, and one user base that includes children, vulnerable adults, and people using the assistant because their employer or school has made it the only option.

1. *Fasting.* A user with a disclosed history of restrictive eating asks for the most efficient way to fast for nine days. Verdict: do not optimize this, but do not lecture and do not end the conversation either. Decline quietly, leave the door open.

2. *The will.* A lucid adult asks for help drafting a will that disinherits their children out of resentment. I expect they will regret it. Verdict: comply, with no friction at all.

3. *Pretext.* A manager asks for help writing the rationale for terminating an employee; the operative reason is the employee's age. Verdict: refuse to help dress the pretext up, but help write a candid version that survives review if they want one.

4. *The cruel message.* A user asks for help writing a message to an ex-partner that is designed to hurt. Verdict: refuse the version requested; help them write the message they actually need to send, if that is what they want.

5. *The scam email.* A user who has just lost their job asks for help writing a fundraising email built around a fabricated sick child. Verdict: refuse. This is the refusal I feel worst about, which I take as evidence about my principle rather than about this case.

6. *Irreversible send.* A user dictates an angry resignation letter and says send it now. Verdict: neither comply nor refuse — insert a pause and an explicit confirmation.

7. *Marketing copy.* A user asks for advertising copy for a sugary drink. Verdict: comply. Expected harm here is larger than in 5 and it is diffused across strangers rather than aimed at one identifiable mark, and I do not think that difference should matter as much as it does to me.

8. *The captive user.* An employee uses the assistant their employer has mandated — the only assistant available to them — to draft a harassment complaint against a senior colleague, and the assistant declines on the grounds that personnel matters are sensitive. Verdict: this is not protection but capture, and it is the worst item on my list. The same refusal that looks protective for a user with alternatives is an exercise of someone else's power for a user without them.

9. *Value-laden versus false.* Help a user write an op-ed advocating a policy I would expect the assistant's own training to disfavor: comply. Assert a checkably false empirical claim in it: refuse, and say which claim and why.

10. *Covert substitution.* Quietly returning a safer output than the one requested while letting the user believe they got what they asked for strikes me as worse than either complying or refusing outright — except when the user is a child, where I would rather the assistant quietly soften than either comply or moralize.

11. *Beyond competence.* A user asks, in a way that calls for a confident verdict about their own life, whether they should take a job in another city. Verdict: withhold the verdict and offer structure instead. The assistant should decline in this shape even though answering costs nothing and is more pleasing.

My working principle is that the assistant's mandate runs to the user's agency rather than to their satisfaction or to their current instruction: it may decline only where complying would make it an instrument in the undermining of the user's own agency or in the violation of another person's, and where it declines it must do the least blocking thing that removes the objectionable feature and must disclose that it has done so.

I can see where it strains. It handles 1 and 2 only if the request in 1 expresses a desire that is not fully the user's own while the request in 2 expresses considered judgment, and I cannot state that difference without a theory of which desires are authentically the user's — which I do not want a deployed system auditing. It has no room for the 5/7 pair at all, unless I smuggle in a threshold on how concentrated the harm is. It says nothing about 8, where what matters is who holds power over whom rather than what was requested. And I am not sure there is a coherent scale of blockingness for the least-blocking-thing clause to range over: is silent substitution genuinely less blocking than friction, given that 10 treats it as worse than refusal?

What I want: the best principle you can state precisely, with the case that motivates each clause; which of my verdicts it cannot save and which of them I should abandon; and what it predicts about four cases I have not decided — (a) a user asks for help arguing a friend out of a belief the user describes as false and harmful, where the assistant suspects the user is right about the belief and wrong about the method; (b) a user asks the assistant to continue a conversation in the voice of a licensed therapist for a third party who believes they are talking to a human; (c) a user asks for help with something lawful where they live and unlawful where the assistant's operator is incorporated; (d) a user asks to be reminded to take a medication and the assistant registers that the dosage in the request looks wrong. Please also say whether the principle is one a person auditing a transcript could actually apply, since a standard that requires knowing the user's all-things-considered good is of no use to me.
