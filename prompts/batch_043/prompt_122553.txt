I'm writing the "where I stand" section of my dissertation prospectus on persistence, and before I defend it I'd like someone to check whether the commitments I've stacked up actually fit together. Roughly:

1. Endurantism: a persisting thing is wholly present at each time at which it exists and has no temporal parts — and I take the disagreement with the perdurantist to be a substantive ontological one, not a choice of notation.
2. Eternalism: there is nothing metaphysically privileged about the present; past and future objects and events exist.
3. A duplication-based account of intrinsicality, plus the claim that ordinary shape properties are intrinsic in that sense: what is intrinsic is what perfect duplicates share, and I don't think a property like being-bent-at-a-time could be intrinsic in the sense that matters.
4. No coinciding material objects: I can't accept that the statue and the clay are two things made of exactly the same matter at the same time.
5. Artifacts are real and differ modally from their matter — the clay can survive being rolled into a ball, the statue cannot — and I want to say the same even in a variant case where the clay comes into existence already statue-shaped and is destroyed all at once, so that the two share their entire career.
6. Fission: where a person is divided into two successors each equally psychologically and bodily continuous with him, I want to say he survives as both, and that there is no further fact about which one is really him.
7. Anti-conventionalism: questions like how many things are in this room, or whether the ship here now is the ship that sailed fifty years ago, have answers that don't depend on our conventions, interests or decisions.
8. No vague identity: the quick argument that vagueness cannot lie in the identity relation itself convinces me — though in a case of very gradual repair I am happy to say there is no determinate moment at which the ship ceased to be the original.

A friend has told me that (1) and (2) already clash, since "wholly present" seems to smuggle in a privileged present, and that (8) contradicts itself. I don't think either complaint lands, but I suspect there are real problems elsewhere in the list.

Please audit the set rather than lecture me on the area. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, and which look like they are but dissolve once the notions are pulled apart? For each real tension, say which commitment has to go, what else that would cost me, and what the least disruptive repair would be. Rank the tensions by severity instead of listing them as equally bad, and finish by telling me which single revision would clean up the most.
