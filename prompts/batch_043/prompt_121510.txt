You are a mathematical logician and formal epistemologist who works on reflection principles, self-reference, and the proof-theoretic foundations of agency, and who also has experience hosting long-form technical interviews. Please adopt that dual role and, before drafting anything, do your thinking in writing: spend a few paragraphs brainstorming (a) the four or five commitments your interviewee is actually stuck with, (b) the reply he is most likely to give when each is pressed, and how that reply can be cut off in one sentence, and (c) which two of his commitments you suspect are in tension with each other. Then, and only then, produce the questions.

I host a podcast on the formal foundations of agency. My next guest is an invented defender — call him "the tiling theorist" — of the following package. An agent may hand off to a successor only if it can verify, within its own reasoning, that the successor will take only actions the original would endorse; by Löb's theorem, a system rich enough for arithmetic cannot prove its own soundness schema, so the agent cannot certify the general claim "whatever I can prove to be safe is safe," and the handoff condition looks unsatisfiable. The tiling theorist's response is to break the circularity by well-founded descent: the parent verifies the child's behaviour by reasoning about a strictly weaker proof system than its own, so trust runs down a well-founded hierarchy rather than in a loop, and no agent ever has to trust itself. He holds that this yields a reflectively stable agent, and that the resulting loss of strength per generation is a cost only in principle, not in practice.

Write me eight to ten interview questions for him. The audience already knows Löb's theorem and the handoff setup, so no question may ask him to restate either, and none may be a soft opener or a "where should listeners start reading?" question — every question must be answerable from first principles alone. Under each question, give the one-line answer he is most likely to give, and then one follow-up built to cut that specific answer off. Make at least one question address each of the following, and let the rest go where your own analysis says the pressure is highest:

1. The descent floor. If every generation trusts a strictly weaker system, then a long chain of self-modifications seems to run the agent's reasoning strength toward a floor. Ask him whether he can prove any lower bound on what survives arbitrarily many generations, and whether an agent that has to plan over an indefinite horizon should treat the decay as a real cost.

2. The Mirror Certifier case, which is my own variant and which he will not have seen. Suppose we abandon proof-based handoff and let the agent act whenever a computable, self-referential estimator certifies that expected value clears a threshold with high probability. Does the obstacle reappear as a fixed-point problem for the estimator's calibration — an agent that refuses to act until it can certify its own certification? Press on whether logical uncertainty dissolves the difficulty or merely relocates it.

3. Coherence versus correctness. His handoff condition is defined in terms of what the parent would endorse, and endorsement is itself defined by the parent's proofs. Ask what the hierarchy buys beyond intergenerational coherence: can a chain of perfectly stable successors all be badly wrong together, and would he count that as a failure of the framework?

4. The strength-demanding environment. Some tasks require a successor strictly stronger than its builder — a problem the builder cannot itself solve. Ask whether his agent then either declines a task it ought to perform or violates its own handoff condition, and whether either outcome is a cost he accepts.

5. The mirror-tiling case. Two agents, or two copies of one agent in a distributed setting, each need to trust the other, and mutual trust forbids the strict descent that made the one-directional case work. Ask whether the circularity he removed has simply moved into the multi-agent case.

6. Erosion of the target. The predicate defining which actions count as endorsed is itself something a successor may rewrite. Ask how his account distinguishes preserving the agent's proof-theoretic trust from preserving the thing the trust was supposed to protect.

Include one sequence of two or three linked questions, placed late, whose explicit function is to make his known deflection — that these are artifacts of formalizing trust in arithmetic rather than problems for the view — progressively harder to reach for, and end the episode with one question about what mathematical or empirical evidence could count against the package.

Be committed throughout: choose one definite ordering, one definite "hardest" question, and one definite guess about which question he will answer worst, with a sentence of rationale for each, rather than offering me alternatives to pick between. Where you are genuinely unsure whether a follow-up lands, mark that uncertainty in a bracketed aside and move on; do not hedge the list as a whole.
