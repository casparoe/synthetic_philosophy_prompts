I'm a TA for a second-year applied ethics course, and I'm partway through a problem set on lifeboat ethics — about forty scripts left, the midterm is Thursday, and I want to fix my rubric before I anchor on my own reaction to the first few papers I read. The week's reading was Garrett Hardin's "Lifeboat Ethics: The Case Against Helping the Poor" (1974), and in the seminar I stressed that the case for refusal is supposed to work even on fairly demanding moral premises: in Hardin's original scenario rescue does not merely cost the passengers something, it sinks the boat and kills everyone aboard, including the people who were pulled in. The problem set asks students to extend that reasoning to variants where the physical necessity is removed, so that whatever extra premises they need become visible. I've pasted the question I set and one student's full answer below (lightly copy-edited for typos).

**Question (25 marks).** *Part 1 (8 marks).* In Hardin's original case the boat sinks if everyone climbs aboard. Now consider the **slack variant**: the boat stays afloat with all 100 swimmers aboard, but the extra load cuts every person's chance of surviving the voyage from 95% to 30%. Does Hardin's argument for refusal still go through? Identify the feature of the original case that is doing the argumentative work, and say whether the slack variant has it.

*Part 2 (9 marks).* **Convoy variant.** Ten lifeboats, 50 people in each, 1,000 swimmers in the water. Each boat can take 10 aboard at a cost to its own passengers that is small but real: their survival chance drops from 95% to 90%. If every boat takes its ten, all 1,500 survive. The boats cannot see, compel, or communicate with one another. Is this the same problem as Hardin's? Does his conclusion follow here? If not, what kind of problem is it, and what does that imply about what each captain should do?

*Part 3 (8 marks).* **Enfranchisement variant.** Anyone taken aboard gets an equal vote on all subsequent rescue decisions, and the rescued are drawn from a population strongly sympathetic to rescue. The boat can expect to meet further groups of swimmers at intervals. Does this give the original passengers an additional objection to rescue, over and above anything in Parts 1 and 2? Does the strength of that objection depend on the numbers involved?

**Student's answer.**

*Part 1.* Hardin's argument in the original case is essentially a consequentialist one: if the boat takes everyone aboard it sinks and 150 people die rather than 50, so rescue is worse for everyone affected, swimmers included. The feature doing the work is that rescue leaves every single person worse off — the passengers' prudence and their duty point in the same direction, and no moral view, however demanding, could require it. The slack variant lacks that feature, so the argument does not go through unchanged. But it goes through anyway, once you do the arithmetic. Refusal gives 50 × 0.95 = 47.5 expected survivors; rescue gives 150 × 0.30 = 45. Rescue is worse by two and a half expected lives, so the same consequentialist reasoning forbids it, and the 150-versus-50 comparison in the original case is just a special case of this expected-utility calculation. The only difference between the two cases is how big the numbers are.

*Part 2.* The convoy variant is a collective action problem of the same shape as the tragedy of the commons. What it is rational for one boat to do depends on what the other boats do, and since no boat can observe whether the others are doing their part, no boat has a reason to do its own part. My taking ten people only matters if the other boats also take their share: if they don't, my ten are a drop in the ocean and I have simply imposed a cost on my own passengers for nothing. So Hardin's conclusion follows here as well. Absent an enforceable agreement, prudence requires each captain to take no one, and morality does not require what prudence forbids — the passengers would be sacrificing real survival chances and achieving nothing.

*Part 3.* Yes, enfranchisement adds a real objection. Once the rescued can vote, the composition of the deciding group changes, and because the rescued come from a population sympathetic to rescue, each rescue makes further rescue more likely. Run the process over enough rounds and the boat ends up filled past capacity with everyone drowned — which is exactly Hardin's outcome, reached by democratic means instead of by physical necessity. The enfranchisement variant is therefore the original case in disguise, and the right policy is to take no one, or at most to take a number small enough that the rescued can never outvote the original passengers.

I am deliberately not telling you what I think of the answer, and I'd rather have a point-by-point assessment I can turn into rubric language than a general impression of whether the student "gets it."
