I have been reading through the welfare standards that certifiers and regulators apply to farmed fish and shrimp, and the shape of the package puzzles me. As I understand the dominant pattern — please correct me where I have the details wrong, and flag anything that is jurisdiction- or species-specific rather than general — the requirements are overwhelmingly resource- and management-based: a ceiling on stocking density in kilograms per cubic metre, floors on dissolved oxygen, a species-appropriate temperature band, feed specifications, lighting regimes, a veterinary health plan, and limits on things like fin erosion, eye damage and sea-lice counts. On top of that there are increasingly animal-based outcome measures, of which cumulative mortality across the production cycle is the headline one; a farm above the threshold fails. Slaughter is handled separately: for finfish, more and more schemes require an actual stunning step, percussive or electrical, and treat death by ice slurry, chilling, or asphyxiation in air as a failure; for crustaceans the normal route is still gradual chilling or being cooked alive, and in many legal systems they attract no stunning requirement at all, only a general prohibition on unnecessary suffering. Three absences are conspicuous. Nothing caps how long an animal may be held in the system: a salmon may spend well over a year in a sea cage, ranched tuna many months, a breeding shrimp years. Nothing requires enrichment or any opportunity for species-typical activity. And routine culling of the runted, deformed or moribund is not merely permitted but required, and does not itself count against a farm, even though the same farm is penalised for letting animals die on their own. Fish are also routinely fasted for several days before slaughter, and that fast is written into standards as a welfare and food-safety measure rather than treated as a deprivation.

What I want from you is the implicit theory this package rests on. Concretely, I would like you to reconstruct it and then interrogate it on five points. First, its temporal structure: mortality is tracked as a rate across the cycle and duration therefore enters the accounting indirectly, yet nothing limits duration as such. Does the practice presuppose that welfare is a property of time-slices whose badness does not aggregate into a lifetime quantity, or can an additive, whole-life view explain the asymmetry some other way? Second, death: cumulative mortality registers as a welfare failure while culling a suffering animal is required and costs the farm nothing on the scorecard. Does that presuppose that death is not itself a harm to the animal, and that mortality matters only as evidence of antecedent suffering? Third, the finfish–crustacean asymmetry: what must we believe about the threshold for being a welfare subject for that split to be principled, and is that belief strictly presupposed or merely convenient given the state of the evidence, costs, and political feasibility? Fourth, the unit of assessment: thresholds on rates and averages seem to make the group, not the individual, the object of evaluation. Fifth, fasting: is a standard built from resources, health and a humane death strictly a negative or health-centred conception of welfare, and would any positive-welfare or flourishing view forbid it?

To pressure-test the reconstruction, consider rewriting such a standard in lifetime terms: replace the thresholds with a ceiling on the integral over each animal's whole life of a welfare index sensitive to both intensity and duration — call it negative-welfare-days. Under that rule a short, dense, fast-grown cycle could beat a roomy one; earlier slaughter automatically scores better; culling a chronically suffering animal becomes a welfare improvement rather than a tragedy; and a system offering a long, good life could fail where a cramped, brief one passes. Tell me whether that consequence really follows from the commitments you have reconstructed or only from my particular way of writing the metric, which commitment is doing the work if the consequence seems repugnant, and what would have to be added to the standards to block it.

Two further requirements. Epistemically: separate clearly, throughout, what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who actually work on animal welfare science and its philosophy — fish nociception versus fish phenomenal consciousness, crustacean pain, whether welfare aggregates additively across a life, whether death harms a non-human animal, whether positive welfare is a distinct requirement — and do not present a contested view as the consensus or a contested empirical claim as established. Say too where the answer is underdetermined by the practice itself, so that several theories would license the same standards, and distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with.
