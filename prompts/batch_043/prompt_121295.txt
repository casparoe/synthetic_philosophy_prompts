I write fantasy novels, the kind where you have to build the religion from scratch, and I've noticed somthing that bugs me: every invented religion I come up with ends up looking like a costume-drama version of something real. Sacrifices that have to cost something. Rules about contamination. A god who knows what you did when nobody was watching. The cosmology changes but the the furniture doesn't. I've started to wonder whether that's a failure of imagination on my part or whether there's something about the material itself, and that's got me listening to arguments about religion as a natural phenomenon — with mixed feelings, because most versions I hear are lazy. Either it's just "it's all made up," or it's a claim dressed up as an explanation that's really only the observation that lots of people happen to be religious.

So: I've got a two-hour slot at a writers' conference in the spring. 14 people, adults, mixed. Two write explicitly devotional work and will bristle if this turns into a debunking session; three are the kind of atheist who arrives already agreeing; one writes hard SF and will try to optimize whatever game I give him. Room has a whiteboard, index cards, and nothing I can plug in. Design me an activity for it.

The version of the claim I want them to feel — tell me if I've aimed at the wrong target — is roughly this: the features keep reappearing not because they're vivid or easy to remember, but because they do a particular kind of work in holding a group together, and human minds are already fitted to respond to that work. In my notes I've been splitting two things apart: ideas that are *sticky* (easy to learn and pass along — all that minimally-counterintuitive stuff people go on about) and ideas that are *weight-bearing* (the ones people reorganise their week around, keep doing once it stops being fun, and enforce on each other). I think the second is the interesting one and the first is nearly a distraction. If that cut is wrong, say so and use a better one.

What I want them to walk out with: four separate groups are told to invent a religion from scratch with no shared sources, and they converge on a small set of features — and the convergence is both genuinely surprising and, on about ten seconds' reflection, partly rigged. I want them be able to say why it was rigged without me telling them.

Give me the rules, materials, a payoff or scoring scheme that needs no money, a timeline with rough minutes, and the debrief mapping specific moments in the room onto specific parts of the claim. Then: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — my SF writer, my two devout writers, and the group that deliberately invents something exotic just to be original are the three I'm most worried about — and how to handle each. And be honest about what the activity cannot do. I know it can't tell us anything about actual historical religions and it can't touch whether any of it is true; tell me what else it can't reach, and what I'll therefore have to say out loud myself.

Last thing, because I'm a novelist and not a teacher: if the upshot is that the furniture will be there no matter what, is there anything I can actually do to make a fictional religion feel genuinely alien to a reader without it just turning into noise?
