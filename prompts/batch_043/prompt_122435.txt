My team will recognize the genre instantly (RFCs, runbooks, postmortems, "please file a ticket"), and there's one guy on the team who did a philosophy minor and who should be wincing in recognition at the *arguments*, not at the philosophy.

The thing being made fun of is the position that everyday conventions — tipping, queueing, jaywalking — are genuine moral obligations rather than just etiquette, and that visitors are bound to follow the local ones. I dont want "haha tipping is wierd." I want the jokes to come from that position's actual best arguments being applied with total seriousness:

- conventions as solutions to coordination problems: nobody drives on the left because left is better, we drive on the left because we all picked left, and that's the whole reason it binds;
- expectation and reliance: if you queue, the people behind you have planned around your queueing, so cutting isn't merely rude but a kind of small betrayal;
- fairness / free-riding: the locals pay the ongoing cost of keeping the practice up, and the visitor walks in and consumes the coordination without ever paying in;
- "when in Rome," and why it feels self-evident until you ask what exactly generates a duty for someone who never agreed to anything.

Two things I want the document to walk straight into without ever naming them:

1. Bad equilibria. Some conventions are ones the locals themselves would rather escape — US tipping is the obvious case. So: can a convention obligate you if it's a lousy equilibrium? I want the runbook to have invented an operational rule for this so an engineer can see it concretely. Say the company classifies conventions by how far they sit from the best available arrangement, with a threshold past which a visitor isn't just permitted but *required* to deviate. Have the doc apply the rule straight-faced to a real case, and let the reader feel how unstable the threshold is.

2. The visitor category itself. Where does "visitor" end? Two weeks obviously counts, two years obviously doesnt, but what about a remote worker on a six-month workation, or someone who moves to Rome and then just never leaves? I want a subsection that tries to operationalize it — a days-based cutoff, or a "local dependence" test — and quietly collapses. Ideally it ends up saying that if you're in the queue often enough you become a bearer of the obligation, which is circular, and the doc never notices.

One invented concept you can use if it helps: "convention debt" — the idea that every deviation imposes a small repair cost on whoever has to restore the equilibrium afterward (someone has to hold the door, someone has to be the one who over-tips, someone has to re-form the queue), and that a visitor can't discharge it because they're leaving. Maybe the runbook proposes a way to pay it off, and the proposed way is obviously worse than the original problem.
