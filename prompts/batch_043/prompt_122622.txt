I'm a self-taught programmer with no academic background in machine learning or philosophy. From my own reading I've become interested in the proposal that we might supervise AI systems that outperform us by having two systems argue for competing answers in front of a human judge, who then picks the winner — and in the standard worry that this breaks down when the dishonest side can win with arguments that sound fine but are too expensive to check. I've set aside a modest sum to commission about six small, six-month projects from independent, unaffiliated researchers on this, and I now have to decide what to commission.

Working on my own, I've started sorting the failure cases into two kinds, and I'd like to know whether this is a real distinction or whether I've drawn a line that isn't there:

1. Depth failures — the honest answer is only reachable through a chain of steps the judge cannot walk in the time available, so the honest arguer simply cannot make its case legible.
2. Framing failures — the honest answer is in fact short, but the dishonest arguer wins by controlling which sub-question the judge treats as decisive, so the judge is not out of their depth, just led to check the wrong thing.

Given that budget, that time horizon, and researchers working alone without a lab or a compute budget, which few questions should I commission, and in what order? I'm less interested in the list than in the reasoning: what makes a question worth a contract — how many other questions its answer would settle, whether a lone researcher can genuinely make progress on it in six months, and how much its answer would change my view of whether debate-style oversight is worth pursuing at all. Please also say which questions look important but are badly mismatched to this setup, whether the depth/framing split should reorder the priorities (and whether one of the two is likelier to be fatal), and what result, if one of these projects produced it, would most change the ranking.
