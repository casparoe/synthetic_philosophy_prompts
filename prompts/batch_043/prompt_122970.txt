I'm a rising senior, and I got into this through a summer reading group my school runs with a nearby philosophy department — most of it goes over my head, but the piece I keep circling back to is whether mental content can be naturalized at all. I've ended up arguing with a PhD student in the group that the argument we keep being given for teleosemantics — roughly, that only a theory that ties content to a mechanism's history or proper function can make room for genuine misrepresentation, because a pure tracking or covariance theory just re-describes the state's actual causes and so never lets it get things wrong — is weaker than it's made to sound. I want something that tests that argument rather than illustrating it, and I'd rather the result be fair than flattering to my side.

So here's the build request. Give me one paragraph, plain present-day biology, no science fiction, in which a single token of one inner state in one individual animal gets three different verdicts: a selected-effects or teleosemantic theory says the state has content F and that this token misrepresents; a straightforward information or covariance theory says the content is something disjunctive like F-or-G, so that nothing misfires here at all; and an asymmetric-dependence-style theory agrees with the teleosemanticist that the content is F, but gets there from the structure of the causal dependencies rather than from history. The state should be one a real creature could genuinely be in, and the setting should be an ordinary ecological situation rather than a laboratory.

Constraints, because I know how these cases usually go wrong. Please don't let a designer or a stipulation do any work: no thermostats, no artifacts whose function I get to announce, no scientist wiring up a brain or an electrode, no molecule-for-molecule duplicate assembled by a lightning strike, no lookalike substance on a duplicate planet, and no re-run of the classic frog-and-flying-insect detector in a new costume. The state's mistaking things must not be readable off the animal's behavior — the animal should do the same thing in this situation on all three readings, so that behavior can't be what settles which content is right; if the case can be adjudicated by looking at what the animal does, then the disagreement I'm after has been lost. And it counts as cheating to stipulate a verdict: I don't want to be told that the state is supposed to indicate F, or that the mechanism was selected for detecting F, in a way that smuggles the answer in. The supposed-to has to fall out of the theory once the facts of the case are fixed, and I'd like you to be explicit about which facts are doing that.

After the scenario, I want: the content each of the three theories assigns to that one token, whether each calls it a misrepresentation, and the reasoning in each case; then a diagnosis of which single feature of the scenario is carrying the divergence. Then the removal tests, which matter most to me — what happens to each verdict if the G-type cause has in fact been present for evolutionary time rather than recently, and separately, what happens if the G-cases are so much more frequent than the F-cases that the dependence looks like it runs the other way. I'd also like the strongest thing a defender of whichever theory comes out looking worst can say to get out of the case: for the covariance theorist, presumably something about the disjunction being gerrymandered or about their theory already having resources to pick F; for the teleosemanticist, something about how much recent selection or co-option it takes before a function changes. Finally, an honest verdict on whether my case is question-begging against either side, and — since my actual topic is mental content rather than frog content — whether the case transfers to a human perceptual state or a belief without my having to add any new stipulations, or whether the transfer itself quietly imports something the animal case didn't have.
