I am writing a chapter on presentism in historiography and I do not want to defend a position before I know what a position here is supposed to do. So give me the specification rather than the theory: what must an adequate account of anachronism — of when a historical judgement illegitimately imports the standards of the historian's own time — be able to deliver, if it is to be good enough for a working historian writing a monograph rather than for a seminar on blame?

For each requirement, give the case that motivates it, and keep the distinctions the theory must respect separate from the verdicts it must return (excuse versus justification, blameworthiness versus the propriety of blaming, causal explanation versus moral evaluation, "it was wrong then" versus "we rightly condemn it now"). The cases I want handled, at minimum:

- the unavailable-concept case: condemning an eighteenth-century Caribbean sugar planter in a vocabulary of structural racial exploitation that did not yet exist. Distinguish a concept's being actually available to contemporaries from its being merely latent — constructible out of materials they already had — and say whether an agent can be faulted for not having invented a standard that was only latent.
- the few-did case: some contemporaries did dissent, so "no one knew better" fails. Is contemporaneous dissent evidence that the standard was available, or only that it was possible?
- the archive-silence case: the surviving record was itself shaped by the power being judged, so absence of recorded dissent is not clearly absence of dissent.
- the collective-agent case: a chartered company or a colonial office, where both "what could they have known" and "who is the they" go fuzzy.
- the temporal-symmetry case: if we may judge them by ours, later generations may judge us by theirs, and most of us resist that. Must the theory explain the asymmetry, or forbid one?
- the retrospective-uptake case: does it matter that the descendants or political heirs of the condemned now endorse the condemnation, as in disputes over statues and official apologies? Is uptake evidence about the past or a fact about the present use of the judgement?
- the self-application case: our critical vocabulary is itself a historical product. What happens when the standards used to judge the past are among the things being historicised?

Then mark each requirement as non-negotiable or as the preference of a particular camp; flag any subset you think cannot be jointly satisfied, since if the list is unsatisfiable that is itself the result I want; and say which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidates. One line each, no more, on strict contextualism, the relativism-of-distance view, and thoroughgoing presentism.
