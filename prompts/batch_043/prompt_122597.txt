Hey — I'm the clinical effectiveness person on our hospital network's Quality & Safety Committee (eleven hospitals, monthly meeting, this one's Thursday and the packet goes out Tuesday night). We have a case that's turning into a shouting match and I need to draft the written opinion that goes in the packet — the document the committee actually votes on. Can you write it?

Here's the case. One emergency sepsis unit switched about 18 months ago to a new early-intervention bundle ("Protocol B"); the rest of the network is still on the old one. The registry pull for the last four quarters, as-treated, looks like this:

Mild at triage: Protocol A, 700 patients, 686 survived (98%). Protocol B, 200 patients, 198 survived (99%).
Moderate: A, 250 patients, 215 survived (86%). B, 300 patients, 267 survived (89%).
Severe: A, 50 patients, 15 survived (30%). B, 500 patients, 190 survived (38%).
All patients: A, 1000 patients, 916 survived (91.6%). B, 1000 patients, 655 survived (65.5%).

So B looks better in every severity band and 26 points worse overall. One of our members has started calling this "Simpson's paradox" in emails and has been sending the Wikipedia-ish explanation around, which has not helped.

Complications you should build into the reasoning rather than ignore:

1. Triage severity is scored by the same senior resident who then decides which protocol to start. So it is measured before treatment — good — but it is a judgement call, and the unit has an obvious incentive to score its own patients up. We do have a vitals-only score that can be computed at the door with no human input; nobody used it in this analysis.
2. The unit's analyst (who has since left) produced a second table conditioning on whether the patient was escalated to ICU within six hours, and in that table B's advantage largely disappears. His conclusion, in his slides, was that B does nothing. That table is only partly reconstructable from his slides. Note that Protocol B mandates earlier escalation, so ICU transfer may sit on the causal path from B to survival — or may just be a proxy for how sick the patient looked to the bedside team. I genuinely don't know which, and I don't think the data settles it.
3. The stratification into three bands was chosen after everyone had already seen the aggregate number. There was no pre-registered analysis plan.
4. At the last meeting someone proposed a standing tie-break, which she's calling the Aggregate Primacy Rule: whenever a stratified comparison and the aggregate comparison disagree, the more adverse-to-the-unit figure governs. She wants it adopted as policy this Thursday, not just applied here. Several members like it because it feels precautionary.

What I want from the document:

A clear bottom line with dates — on the pay-for-performance withhold for this unit, on whether we suspend or freeze Protocol B (including the planned expansion to mild and moderate presentations), on what if anything we publish about this unit this cycle, and on the Aggregate Primacy Rule. Please don't just say "more data is needed"; tell the committee what to do on Thursday and what has to be true for the decision to change.

Then the reasoning, aimed at busy clinicians who will not read an equation. You can show the arithmetic on the table above, but no algebra and no notation they'll skip over. In particular I want the memo to be explicit about which question each comparison actually answers — the aggregate comparison, the within-stratum comparison, and the question the committee is really asking (is this unit worse, and should we keep doing this) are three different things, and I'd like the memo to say plainly that the reversal is not a statistical illusion but a signal that the two groups weren't comparable.

The losing side needs its best argument stated better than its supporters state it. Their strongest point isn't the Wikipedia thing; it's that the raw numbers are what patients and regulators see, and that "adjusting" by a severity score assigned by the same clinician who picked the protocol is close to circular — a unit can make any outcome look acceptable by calling its patients sicker. Engage that seriously and still come down somewhere.

One more thing: add a short closing section — call it a confidence ledger or whatever — that separates what no one who knows this stuff disputes (the arithmetic of the tables, that reversals of this kind are possible, that conditioning on a variable affected by treatment is a different move from conditioning on a pre-treatment one) from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic (whether the triage score is a legitimate adjuster here at all, whether to standardize or weight, how much to discount an analysis whose stratification was chosen after the fact, whether observational data like this can ever license the inference we want). Don't present the contested ones as settled consensus, and don't hedge the whole memo into mush either — I need a recommendation and an honest confidence rating on it.

Keep it around 900–1100 words, memo format, and please don't put any citations or suggested reading in it — the committee can't look anything up and it'll just make them suspicious. We also definately don't have the budget for the six-month registry rebuild I'd want, so work within that.
