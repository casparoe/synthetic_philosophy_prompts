I work in evolutionary ecology, on life-history evolution in fluctuating environments. The vocabulary I actually think in: long-run stochastic growth rate of log population size and its small-noise approximation (roughly the arithmetic mean minus half the variance), geometric mean fitness, conservative versus diversified bet-hedging, environmental autocorrelation, structured population models, invasion criteria for a rare type in a resident population, absorbing extinction, generation-time discounting, and the branching-process fact that a typical surviving lineage grows at the geometric-mean rate while the expectation of population size is dominated by rare lucky lineages and grows faster than any typical one. My formal training stops at expected utility as one would use it in a model; I have no measure-theoretic ergodicity and no decision theory beyond that.

I keep running into the log-optimality and "geometric rationality" cluster of arguments from the decision-theory side, and I want them rendered into my vocabulary, not explained to me. Translate, as faithfully as you can, each of the following four:

1. The case for maximizing expected log wealth — the Kelly rule — in a repeated multiplicative gamble, including the property that the log-optimal policy almost surely comes to exceed any other policy's wealth by an unbounded factor.
2. The ergodicity-flavoured critique: that expected-value maximization optimizes an ensemble average of a quantity whose growth is governed by a time average, and that the log rule is what you get when you optimize the average that is actually being taken.
3. The practice of betting only a fraction of the sanctioned stake — half, a quarter — and the reasons given for it: uncertainty about the edge, model risk, the asymmetry of being wrong about your probabilities.
4. The extension that travels under the name "geometric rationality": that an agent should maximize a probability-weighted geometric mean of its values across possible worlds, or across incommensurable value dimensions, rather than an arithmetic expectation.

For each, give me the translation, then tell me what resisted it. Some discipline on the translation: do not call something a translation when you have quietly substituted an assumption of mine for theirs. In particular, say where their agent's rationality lands in my terms — on fitness, on an invasion criterion, on a typical-lineage growth rate, or on none of these; say whether the normative/descriptive distinction survives the move; and say whether "optimal" in the betting sense is a claim about one decision-maker or a claim about who ends up with most of the population. If one of the four can only be rendered as something trivially true or trivially false in my vocabulary, say which, and why.

Then the residue report, in both directions. What in these four arguments has no expression in my vocabulary at all? And — the direction I care about more — what does my vocabulary contain that these arguments have no room for: a metapopulation structure in which the number of independent lineages changes which average matters, extinction as a boundary that demographic stochasticity can force even on an optimal life history, frequency dependence, the gap between maximizing one-period geometric mean fitness and maximizing long-run growth when environments are autocorrelated. For each residue, commit to a verdict rather than a taxonomy: is it a gap in ecology — no notion of a single persisting agent with attitudes toward its own future — or is the original argument less determinate than it looks, trading on an ambiguity between "optimal" as best-for-me and "optimal" as what-wins, or between averaging over time and averaging over worlds?

Take the four items in order, a short paragraph of translation and a short paragraph of residue each, then one closing paragraph with the verdict. Commit to claims; where the translation genuinely fails, say so instead of rescuing it with a redefined term.
