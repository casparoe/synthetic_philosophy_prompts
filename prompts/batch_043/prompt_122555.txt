I'm a high school senior planning to study statistics, and I've been reading about a disagreement I can't get a grip on: the one between the self-sampling assumption and the self-indication assumption in anthropic reasoning, including the "presumptuous philosopher" case, in which a philosopher claims that counting observers lets physicists settle a cosmological dispute without building the experiment that was meant to settle it. I have no formal philosophy training. What I do have is AP Statistics, a term of independent reading on survey sampling, and about a year of working with data in Python. So I would like the dispute restated in the vocabulary I actually think in, and I would especially like to know what that vocabulary cannot say.

The vocabulary I mean: target population and sampling frame; samples drawn with and without replacement; simple random versus stratified or cluster designs; sampling bias, coverage error, and selection effects; conditional probability and Bayes' rule; likelihood functions, priors, posteriors, and Bayes factors; estimators and their bias; and identifiability, in the sense of parameters the data can and cannot pin down.

Here is my own attempt at the translation, which I suspect is too crude. The self-sampling assumption, as I read it, says: treat the observers in your reference class as a sampling frame, treat yourself as a single draw from it, and condition on the position you observe yourself to occupy — which is what makes the Doomsday-argument style inference look like sampling without replacement, where your serial number carries information about the size of the frame. The self-indication assumption, as I read it, says: the headcount itself is evidence, so a hypothesis on which there are a million times more observers gets roughly a million times more credence before anything else is taken into account. My gloss is that this amounts to treating the likelihood of a hypothesis as proportional to the number of observers it contains, or equivalently to tilting the prior by the population size and then applying the first assumption. Please tell me whether that gloss is fair, and in particular whether casting the second assumption as a claim about the likelihood rather than about the prior is correct, or whether the two assumptions are not rival claims of the same kind at all — one being an update rule and the other a prior.

I also made up two cases to make the translation do some work.

Case one, with no protocol. Two hypotheses agree about every observation anyone will ever make. Hypothesis A says the universe contains N observers in circumstances just like mine. Hypothesis B says it contains those same N observers plus a further million times as many, living in regions from which no signal will ever reach us and in circumstances in which no one is ever in a position to wonder which hypothesis is true. Nothing observable distinguishes the hypotheses. As far as I can tell, whether one updates at all here depends entirely on whether those extra observers fall inside the reference class — and in my vocabulary that is a question about the sampling frame, which is normally settled by who the sampler could actually have reached, not by stipulation.

Case two, with an explicit protocol. A future civilization builds a machine that flips a fair coin and then creates either one observer or a million observers, each with identical memories that include a complete description of this protocol. Now there is a real randomizer, a real sampler, and a real frame.
