I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I've been turning this over since I interviewed a type designer last week. She said something that I built into an argument, and now I can't find the exit. Its probably obvious to a philosopher, but here goes.

(1) No geometric property is shared by all and only the a's: the single-storey ɑ, the double-storey a, blackletter, italic, a five-year-old's wobbly crayon one.
(2) So what they share has to be a role: being the mark a competent reader recovers as the vowel in cat, the one that contrasts with o, d, and e within the same system.
(3) Roles are extrinsic. The very same physical shape is an a in one system and a b in another — think of a substitution cipher, or of a typeface in which every letter's shape is systematically shifted onto the next letter's slot. So no intrinsic property of a mark settles which letter it is.
(4) If the role is what makes an a an a, then both whether a mark is an a and how good an a it is come down entirely to which role it's been assigned and how well it cues readers to that role.
(5) And roles can be assigned by stipulation: if I declare that this squiggle plays the a-role in my system, then in my system it is an a, and anyone who reads an a off it is reading correctly.
(6) So there is no such thing as a malformed letter. Any mark whatsoever can be a perfect a, and a child who produces something indistinguishable from a u while meaning a hasn't miswritten anything — she's produced a flawless a in her own system.
(7) That's absurd. Miswriting letters is the paradigm case of getting something wrong, and legibility is a real property: there was a well-known AI project that spent years trying to get a program to invent letterforms on a coarse grid, and it kept failing on exactly this. So one of (1)–(5) has to go.

My friend says (3) is obviously true and (5) is the weak link. I'd rather keep (1), (2), and (3) and give up nearly anything else, but I can't say why (5) is the one that breaks rather than (4). Her own positive suggestion was that an a is defined as much by not being that typeface's o, d, g, u, and q as by anything positive about it — so you can't evaluate a letterform in isolation, only against the rest of the alphabet it belongs to.
