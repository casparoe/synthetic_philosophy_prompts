Last night I argued with a friend — a macroeconomist — about whether a central bank's decision to hold or cut rates could in principle be fully explained by the preferences, beliefs and incentives of the individual committee members together with the rules they operate under. Each time I pressed for details she answered with "the whole is more than the sum of its parts" and behaved as though that settled it, and I suspect the reading that would do the work she needs is not the reading she was leaning on. Here is my own first pass at a taxonomy: (i) predicate novelty, where new predicates simply apply to the composite — a committee can be divided, or risk-averse, in a way no single member is; (ii) derivational, where the outcome cannot in practice be computed or predicted from the parts and the micro-level story even granting both; (iii) boundary conditions, where the individual-level story genuinely underdetermines the outcome unless you add facts that are not facts about anyone's psychology — who sets the agenda, what the voting rule is — and those facts seem to live at the group level. For each of the three, and for any reading my taxonomy misses, tell me whether it is a mere truism about composition, a substantive claim that can be defended, or straightforwardly false; and say whether it would really rule out the individualist explanation she was resisting, or only make that explanation hard to produce in practice. Lay the readings out as a numbered list I can take back to her, and close by saying which reading people are normally trading on when they use the line to end an argument, and whether that reading is one you would defend.
