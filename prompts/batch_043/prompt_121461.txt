I need a eulogy, and I need it to be usable by someone whose own work is the reason the obvious versions of it won't do. My grandfather died ten days ago at eighty-eight. He taught civics and coached track at the same high school in a paper-mill town on the Ohio for thirty-two years, was a union steward for most of them, knocked on doors for every levy and every campaign from the late fifties until his knees gave out, and was, for the last decade of his life, the most persistent critic of my doctoral work. I model long-run political instability — the structural-demographic line of work associated with cliodynamics, which treats states as passing through century-scale secular cycles: an integrative phase in which institutions hold, wages track productivity, and the supply of elites is small enough to be absorbed, and then a long disintegrative slide in which elite overproduction and popular immiseration combine with fiscal strain and intra-elite competition until the polity goes through a crisis and eventually starts over again. The models are population-level and decade-grained; they say things like that this decade sits near a peak of instability, and they have strictly nothing to say about whether any particular man's life went well or was worth the way he spent it. Every time I tried to explain this to him he waved a hand and produced some version of the same objection: you're measuring the tide and telling me about the swimmers.

The constraints are mostly about who will be in the room. It's the Methodist church he taught Sunday school in for forty years, about a hundred and fifty people: his children and grandchildren, twenty-odd former students who drove in, neighbours from the mill, and my grandmother, who asked me specifically to say that he was right about this country and that it will come back around. Six or seven friends from my program are also coming, and they will be listening for whether I smuggle in determinism, or imply that structural-demographic models forecast events, or quietly pretend that a mechanism about cohorts and wages is a fact about a person. The pastor has asked for eight minutes and has asked me not to make it political, which nearly rules out saying out loud what I actually believe: that the high-trust, high-membership, low-inequality world he was born into and helped sustain was a phase of a cycle and not the shape of things, and that the standard funeral consolations — the line about the arc of history bending toward justice, the claim that the country he believed in recovers because it always has — are exactly the claims my own framework should make me suspicious of. I'm not willing to lie at his funeral, and I'm also not willing to stand up in front of his widow and his former students and tell them that the world he gave his life to is now structurally unwinding. So I want the philosophical problem to do the work of the speech rather than decorate it: the eulogy should be able to say what is true about one life, and about why his life was worth spending the way he spent it, from inside a theory that explains populations and not persons, and it should answer his tide-and-swimmers objection in a way that concedes what is right in it — he wasn't wrong that the models miss the swimmers — without leaving the theory, or my disagreement with him, out of the speech altogether. My grandmother has already told three people that I'm the one who "does the computer history," if that helps you gauge the room.

Write the eulogy as I would actually deliver it: eight minutes spoken, plain enough that a retired machinist follows it without effort, no unexplained jargon, at most one number in the whole thing, and nothing that would read as a lecture or as a campaign speech. Then add a short note of no more than two paragraphs listing the two or three points where the honest thing and the consoling thing come apart — the thing my grandmother asked for should be one of them — and what you chose to have the speech do at each of those points instead. If there is a version of what she wants that I can say without dishonesty, put it in the speech; if there isn't, say so plainly in the note and explain why not. Please don't pad the thing with illustrative examples from Rome or the Ancien Régime; whatever the speech does with the cycle should be about him and about the town he lived in, and if the honest conclusion is that my models have less to offer a funeral than my grandmother does, I would rather you wrote that than dressed up a forecast as a blessing.
