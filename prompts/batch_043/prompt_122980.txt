I sit on the acquisitions and attribution committee at a mid-sized museum, and on Thursday we have to rule on a genuinely nasty one, so I want my own head straight before I walk in. Please answer in British English, and please don't point me at any reading, case law or exhibition history — I can't get to a library this week and I'm not asking for citations, just for the reasoning.

Here's our track record, so you can see the shape of us:

1. An anonymous nineteenth-century copy of a very famous portrait (the original hangs in another country), in our collection since 1908, always labelled "after". We keep it on the wall, insure it for a token figure, and it goes in the guidebook as a copy.
2. A Roman marble copy of a lost Greek bronze. Star object, full valuation, travels to loan shows, no one treats it as second-rate.
3. A Dutch golden age panel we aquired in 1961 as "circle of the master"; panel dating plus a workshop account book later showed it was painted by a pupil in his shop, from his design, with his hand in the highlights. Relabelled "workshop of", stayed on the wall, valuation cut roughly in half.
4. Three "newly discovered" Dutch pictures bought in good faith in the seventies, hung for twenty-five years, cited in a couple of theses, then exposed as the work of a twentieth-century forger with no connection to anybody. We pulled them, sold them off for a nominal sum, and haven't shown one since — a curator's proposal to hang one as a brilliant forgery in its own right was voted down.
5. Posthumous productions: we bought a posthumous bronze from the artist's own mould, estate-authorised and stamped as posthumous, display it, value it at about a tenth of a lifetime cast; and we realise an instruction-based wall piece from her written instructions and a diagram, using our own technicians, supervised by her foundation, and hang it under her name at full value.
6. A sixties canvas painted entirely by the artist's studio assistant under a written agreement that the artist would sign and sell it as his. The arrangement was openly acknowledged in the studio and the artist never denied it. When the agreement surfaced in 2004 we relabelled it "conceived and signed by X, executed by Y", kept it up, cut the valuation by about a third.

The new one: a big 1954 abstraction, given to us in 1985, hung and catalogued ever since as by Rees. It is the single most reproduced image of the whole movement — half the survey books use it, painters cite it as the thing that changed how they worked. Rees's diaries (found by his granddaughter, handwriting checked, published last year) contain an entry that reads as half confession, half shrug: his partner Nadel, a trained painter, did the entire surface from his full-size cartoon while he was in hospital; he signed it; he then exhibited it, wrote about it in letters as his own work, and never corrected anyone. Technical work finds no trace of Rees's hand. The cartoon survives in his hand and matches exactly. Nadel had her own career and since her death her work sells for about what a mid-range Rees does. The donor's family have lawyers writing letters saying the gift fails if it's not a Rees, and a big Rees retrospective in two years wants to borrow it and needs to know what to print in the catalogue.

So: predict what we'll actually do on the three decisions — the catalogue attribution, whether it stays on the wall, and the insurance figure — and then tell me what we should do if we want to be consistent with ourselves. Which rule are we implicitly running, and which two or three other rules fit those six precedents just as well? What does each of them say here? I think the closest analog is genuinely up for grabs between 3 and 6 on one side and 4 on the other, so say what turns on which one we pick. If our precedents really don't settle it, be explicit about the principle we'd be committing to with each option — is the value in the hand, in the design and the signing, in what a viewer can get out of the object, in what the object has actually gone on to do in the world, or in not being complicit in a deception? And give me the one further hypothetical variant of this case that would best separate those rules, with what each predicts about it — my own candidates are: same facts but Nadel is an unknown studio hand with no career of her own; same facts but the picture only became famous after someone proposed the reattribution; same facts but Rees had credited her in the 1954 exhibition catalogue. Tell me which of those (or what else) does the most work, and why.
