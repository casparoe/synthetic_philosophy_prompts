I run a graduate reading group in formal epistemology, and next week we are covering a survey chapter on Richard Jeffrey's probability kinematics. Before I present it, I would like to know how fair the chapter's summary actually is. The passage I intend to put on the board runs like this:

Jeffrey's probability kinematics is the update rule for cases in which experience does not hand you a certainty. Ordinary conditionalization says: if you learn E outright, set P_new(H) = P_old(H | E). Perception, however, typically shifts your confidence without settling anything. Jeffrey's proposal is to take a partition E_1, ..., E_n of the possibilities you can distinguish, fix your new credences P_new(E_i) for its cells, and set P_new(H) = Σ_i P_old(H | E_i) · P_new(E_i): a weighted average of your old conditional credences, weighted by your new credences in the cells. The real content of the rule is the rigidity condition, which carries the conditional probabilities P(H | E_i) over unchanged — the experience tells you only how probability is redistributed among the E_i, and nothing about what follows from any one of them. This is not an optional extra: rigidity is what protects you from diachronic Dutch books, so a bookie who knows your update policy can guarantee you a loss if you depart from it. The rule contains ordinary conditionalization as a special case (when a single cell receives credence 1, it returns P_old(H | E_i)), and it inherits that rule's good behaviour: process two experiences in either order and you arrive at the same distribution, because each update merely reapportions weight across a partition while leaving the conditional probabilities in place.
