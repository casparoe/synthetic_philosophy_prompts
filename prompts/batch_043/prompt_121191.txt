I volunteer at a hospice (weekends mostly) and I've somehow ended up helping run the training sessions for new volunteers. In the last one we got onto a case: a woman who died at 59, and one of the trainees said she'd been robbed of twenty-odd years, that it was a tragedy for her. Our trainer shut this down with that old line — the one about how as long as I'm here death isn't, and once death is here I'm not, so death is nothing to me — and concluded that the loss is entirely ours, not hers: nothing bad happened to her at all.

I've been chewing on this since and I can't tell how strong the claim really is, because the line seems to be doing about four different jobs at once. Could you lay out, as a list I could print as a one-page handout for the next session (it's tuesday, I said I'd bring something), the different precise things it could be claiming, from the trivial to the radical, and for each one: is it a platitude, is it substantive and defensible, is it merely contentious, is it just false — and most importantly whether it would actually get the trainer her conclusion, or only something weaker like "there's no point dreading the state of being dead."

A few things I want the menu to sort out, because they came up in the room or occurred to me on the way home:

- Is any of it a claim about timing — like, there's no time at which it's bad for her, versus there's no time at which she's around for it to be bad for her? Those feel different to me and I think only one of them does the work.
- Our patients are sometimes sedated into unconciousness for the last stretch and never wake up. If the argument works, doesn't it also show that nothing after that point can be bad for them either — not just the dying, but anything? And doesn't it make our advance-directive forms pointless, since they're entirely about what happens once you can't speak? I'd like to know which readings have to swallow that, and whether any reading can still say the posthumous stuff (her reputation, her projects, whether we did what she asked) matters for her.
- Someone pointed out that by this logic nothing that happens during dreamless sleep or under anesthetic could be bad for you either — a promise broken in your name while you're out cold, or being kept in a coma for forty years instead of two days. Which readings have to bite that bullet, and is it a bullet or a reductio?
- The trainer also used the symmetry bit, that you didn't mind the centuries before you were born. Same claim or a different one, and is it actually false given that we're all biased toward the future?
- Last thing: is there any reading on which "robbed of twenty years" is literally incoherent rather than just false? My sense is that's what she wanted, but that she'd only earned something weaker.
