I'm writing a seminar paper on cross-cultural variation in philosophical case intuitions, and a set of findings I've been working through has backed me into an argument whose conclusion I can't accept.

The field site: a small speech community, call it Tavea, whose language has no lexeme covering know. The nearest attitude term, kili, is used indifferently for true and for false belief, and every clause reporting an attitude carries an obligatory evidential suffix marking whether the subject witnessed the thing, was told it, or inferred it. So there is no way to render a sentence like he knows that the man in the field is his brother without the translator first choosing an evidential. Researchers ran a standard Gettier vignette — the protagonist infers a true belief from a justified false premise — alongside a matched control in which the protagonist simply perceives the truth. Tavean answers track the evidential suffix and are flat across the Gettier/control contrast; the Western comparison group shows the familiar pattern.

My argument:

(1) A case judgment is evidence about a concept C only if the subject making it is exercising C.
(2) A subject exercises C only if her judgments track the distinctions C supports; if they track some unrelated feature instead, she is exercising a different concept.
(3) Tavean judgments track source-of-information and ignore the Gettier contrast, while Western judgments do the reverse.
(4) So Tavean subjects are not exercising KNOWLEDGE. They are exercising a source-tracking notion — call it K* — and their answers are not intuitions about knowledge at all.
(5) So the Tavean data are not evidence for or against any analysis of knowledge. In this case at least, the variation finding dissolves: there was never a shared object the two populations disagreed about.
(6) Nothing in (1) and (2) is specific to Tavea. For any reported divergence in case judgments one can always say the two populations are exercising different concepts, and the divergence itself is what licenses the claim — no independent evidence of conceptual difference is required.
(7) So no cross-cultural intuition survey can establish what it purports to establish. Variation in intuitions about a given concept becomes unfalsifiable by construction, and so does its absence; the research program is empty.

(7) is absurd, and worse, the same reasoning run inside a single population would show that no two speakers ever disagree — they merely talk past one another. My supervisor adds that (7) saws off the branch it sits on, since my own claim that the Taveans are exercising K* rather than K is itself a case intuition about their situation. If I have to choose, I'd rather keep (1) and (5) than (7), and I suspect the damage is in (2) or (6) — but I can't locate it precisely, and I can't say what either rejection would cost me. Which step do you give up, why does it look innocent, and what do I have to pay?
