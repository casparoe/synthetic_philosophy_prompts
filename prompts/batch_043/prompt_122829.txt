I was recently appointed executor of my mother's estate, and for the next few years I will be making a sequence of decisions about a jointly inherited asset — a small rental property — together with my two siblings, who are differently placed in life and who disagree, calmly but persistently, about what the next decade is likely to bring. Because I have to be able to justify each decision to both of them, I have been trying to hold myself to a single rule: make the change only if it is what I have been calling a *safe Pareto improvement*, where a Pareto improvement is the usual thing — someone better off, no one worse off — and the safety clause is meant to capture the fact that my numbers are estimates, not facts.

The disagreement I want your help with is about whether that clause does any work. My brother's view is that the only standard worth having is what he calls *robustness*: there is a set of probability assignments over the relevant states of the world that none of us can rule out — including assignments that disagree wildly about how likely the bad states are — and an intervention is robust when, under *every* one of those assignments, each of us has a higher expected welfare than under the status quo. Give him that, he says, and my "safety" adds nothing: any residual worry I have is just uncertainty I have failed to put into the set of probabilities. Give him less than that, and my rule is muddle dressed up as caution.

I suspect the two standards can be separated, because they seem to me to be answers to different questions. Robustness, on his account, is indifference to which of several admissible probability assignments is correct. What I care about is something else: whether the verdict survives my having the *payoffs* slightly wrong. So let me stipulate, for the purposes of the case, that an intervention is *safely* Pareto-improving when there is some margin such that perturbing any of the estimated welfare numbers by less than that margin leaves everyone's expected gain positive — that is, when it is Pareto-improving not only here but in the nearby worlds where my model is approximately rather than exactly right. Robustness ranges over probabilities; safety, as I mean it, ranges over the model itself.

What I want from you is a case that shows these are genuinely different standards and not two names for one idea. Specifically, build me a single scenario containing two available options, A and B, meeting all of the following:

1. No available option — A, B, or the status quo aside — is an *ex post* Pareto improvement: in at least one state everyone takes seriously, someone comes out behind where they started. Without this the whole question is trivial, since an option that dominates in every state satisfies every standard at once.
2. Option A is robustly but not safely Pareto-improving. Show the arithmetic: give a credal set containing at least two probability assignments that disagree substantially about the probability of the bad states, and verify that A raises each person's expected welfare under both. Then exhibit a perturbation of the payoff estimates, of a size any reasonable person would call negligible, under which someone's expected gain turns negative. I want A to fail safety for the interesting reason — not because the person's expected gain was zero all along, but because the margin is thin enough that a small error swamps it.
3. Option B, in the same scenario, is safely but not robustly Pareto-improving. It should survive any small misestimate of the payoffs, and there should be a probability assignment in the credal set — one none of the characters can rule out — under which someone's expected welfare falls.
4. The scenario must be realistic: money, time, health, or security in the payoffs; no predictors or oracles, no exotic physics, no demons; a household, a family firm, a co-operative, a clinic roster, a departmental budget, whatever you like. Keep the story itself to one paragraph, and set out the states, the payoffs, and the credal set compactly beside it so the arithmetic can be checked.

Then do the demonstration rather than asserting it. For A, run the expected-welfare comparison under at least the two extreme assignments in the credal set, and then show the exact perturbation that breaks it. For B, show why no small perturbation breaks it, and name the assignment that does. After that, I want four things: first, a statement of which single feature of the case is doing the work in each direction, and what happens to the verdict if that feature is removed; second, your honest read on whether my untutored judgment about which option feels reckless tracks safety or tracks robustness; third, the strongest version of my brother's reduction — if safety really is just robustness over an enlarged space, say precisely what has to be added to the space to make that true, and whether adding it is a legitimate move or a change of subject; and fourth, given all of that, which standard I should actually adopt when I have to explain a decision to two people who disagree about the future, and whether the safety/robustness distinction survives contact with a real case or dissolves as soon as the numbers are written down.

I am not asking you to decide my family's case. I am asking for a designed case that isolates the distinction, and for an account of whether the distinction is one an executor should care about.
