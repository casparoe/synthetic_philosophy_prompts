My brother and I keep going in circles about whether there's any point in my declining to buy factory-farmed chicken. His case rests on an analogy rather than on a straight empirical claim. Suppose, he says, a million people each skip one pack of chicken this month; somewhere in the supply chain an order for one fewer flock gets placed, or it doesn't. Now focus on my single purchase. It is, he says, exactly like one grain of sand in a heap: there is no grain such that adding it is what turns a non-heap into a heap, and no grain such that removing it unmakes the heap. That isn't because the grain's contribution is too small to notice, and it isn't a claim about probabilities — it's that the question of which grain did the work has no answer at all. In the same way, he says, there is no fact of the matter about whether my purchase is the one that tips the retailer's order past the next whole-batch threshold, so there is no probability to estimate either; asking whether my purchase makes a difference is not a hard question but a malformed one. He concludes that individual consumption choices are causally inert and therefore morally optional, and that the only lever worth pulling is collective or political action aimed at the aggregate.

I don't think the analogy is doing the work he thinks it is, but I can't say exactly where it goes wrong, so I'd like an audit of it.

Please give me the correspondence explicitly — a table is fine: what plays the role of the grain, the heap, the tipping point between the two, the person who adds the grains, and the person who cares whether there's a heap. Note anything in his setup that has no counterpart in the chicken case, and anything in the chicken case (batch sizes, contracts, lead times, recorded sales data, the difference between a shelf being restocked and a flock being ordered) that has no counterpart in the sand case.

Then separate the disanalogies that matter for the conclusion he wants from the ones that don't. The ones I care about most: the threshold at which a pile becomes a heap looks like a threshold in how we apply a word, while the threshold at which one more flock gets grown looks like a real, dated decision by a specific actor with lumpy indivisible units; a heap has no effects, while aggregate demand has effects that feed back onto the individual buyer's future options; and the grains in a heap are symmetric in a way that purchases made at different times and places may not be. Say which of these, if any, actually breaks the argument, and say plainly if you think one of them is a red herring.

Be explicit about what the analogy quietly imports that the target lacks. My suspicion is that the vividness of the sand case — the sense that each grain's contribution is not merely small but unlocatable — is doing the motivational work, and that the argument would look much weaker if rebuilt without it. If that's right, say so; if you think the sorites structure survives the translation and the problem is instead the further step from no causal difference to morally optional, say that instead.

Also: don't stop at demolition. If the analogy fails, tell me what a better one would be for the same territory — something that captures whatever is genuinely right about his worry (that no individual act is identifiable as *the* cause) while leaving room for whatever is genuinely wrong with his conclusion. And if there's a version of the analogy that would support his conclusion, tell me what would have to be true of the supply chain for it to work.

I want a committed answer, not a survey. Take a position on whether the sand analogy can carry the conclusion he hangs on it, and on what, if anything, survives for the inefficacy worry once the analogy is set aside. I'd also like you to be clear about where your verdict is contingent: state the empirical assumption you're making about how finely production responds to retail demand, how confident you are in it, and what would change your mind. Assume a supermarket supply chain in a wealthy country, and don't hedge by saying it depends on the case — pick a reading of the case, say you've picked it, and go. Please write in American English, and please don't give me citations or further reading; I have plenty of that, and I want your own reasoning.
