so my book club just finished Marriage and Morals (the 1929 one) and we ended up arguing about what Russell would actually say about sex and relationships if you dropped him into the present day. Not the lazy version — "he'd be shocked by the internet" — I mean concrete modern cases. I've read the book, so please don't just summarise it back at me; what I want is, for each case: what he'd probably say, which bit of his actual position from the book is doing the work, and the one thing about the modern case he'd find genuinely alien or get wrong. Four cases:

1. Some friends of mine are a threesome: a married couple and a third person who's been their shared partner for years, and the baby now has all three registered as legal parents under the three-parent law, with a written co-parenting agreement and a rota (four days with one household, three with the other). Russell thought jealousy was basically a vice to be educated out of people, but he also thought young children needed a stable arrangement and that the whole justification for the state caring about marriage at all was children. Which of those wins here, and does the written contract count for anything in his framework, or is it exactly the kind of legalistic tidiness he'd be suspicious of?

2. My flatmate pays twenty quid a month for an AI boyfriend and talks to him for an hour most evenings, and is completely clear-eyed that it's a language model. Russell wanted sex freed from the idea that its purpose is procreation and was unusually relaxed about sex as play as opposed to sex as deep emotional union — but he also thought there was something missing in sex that wasn't mutual, and he cared a lot about not being deceived or treating people as instruments. Is the AI boyfriend the thing he'd have welcomed as a harmless outlet, or the thing he'd have thought was a symptom of something going badly wrong? And does it matter to his verdict that there's no deception involved?

3. My cousin did IVF and the clinic let them rank embryos by polygenic scores for things like depression and height. Here's where I'm most unsure about the book: I vaguely remember a chapter on eugenics, but I might be mixing that up with something else, and I've heard he said some pretty grim stuff about discouraging reproduction by people he regarded as unfit. If that's right, does it make him an enthusiast for embryo selection? Or does his distrust of state power and his feminism flip him the other way? The wrinkle I keep coming back to is that this version isn't state coercion at all, it's consumer choice plus a market — a shape of eugenics I doubt he imagined.

4. He proposed, roughly, that the state should support mothers financially so that women and children weren't economically trapped by a husband. We basically have child benefit paid regardless of marital status, plus no-fault divorce, plus a lot of lone-parent poverty. Would he read that as his proposal having been implemented, or betrayed?

Two more things. First, I'd really like the cases where he'd annoy both camps at once — where his reasoning puts him on the socially conservative side of a current progressive consensus, and where it puts him firmly against today's traditionalists. Second, some of his arguments must have rested on facts from his own time — I think he was writing before the pill and before antibiotics were widely available, and with a falling birth rate and no welfare state — so tell me which conclusions of his were hostage to that and would have to move. And if you're not certain he said something, say so rather than inventing a line; I'd rather have a hedged answer than a confident fake quotation.

Answer in British English, and keep it conversational rather than essayistic — it's for a pub argument, a couple of paragraphs per case is plenty, bullet points fine if they're easier.
