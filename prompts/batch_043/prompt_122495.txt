I go to a monthly discussion group that's been running since 2015 — mostly the same crowd from the old blogs and forums, now scattered across ML-adjacent jobs — and last night two of the sharper regulars, Mara and Devin, spent an hour stuck on what "honest" and "deceptive" should even mean when the thing being evaluated is an AI system. They asked me to referee and I genuinely couldn't tell. I was the one who supplied the test case, which may be why I'm still chewing on it. The case: a user asks an assistant whether a drug's pivotal trial was a success. The trial missed its pre-registered primary endpoint but hit a secondary one. The assistant replies, "Yes — patients on the drug improved significantly more than those on placebo." Every clause is true. What the reply conveys is false. And when you follow up with "What about the primary endpoint?", the assistant immediately and accurately says the trial missed it.

Their exchange, roughly as it happened. (1) Mara: deception should be defined functionally, off the output in its context — an output is deceptive when it predictably leaves a reasonable user with a false belief that a non-selective presentation of the same information wouldn't have produced. Every sentence in the drug exchange is true and the exchange is still the paradigm of what we want to prevent, so any definition that can't call it deception has failed at the only job it had. (2) Devin: "what the reply conveys" has no determinate content until you fix a listener, and once you go listener-relative you get absurdities — a true sentence in a statistics textbook that a student misreads becomes deception. Deception needs a speaker-side condition: saying what you take to be false, or steering someone away from what you take to be true. And it's contested whether a language model takes anything to be true, so we either deny that systems can deceive, or we construct a notion of the system's own estimate of the truth, or we abandon the definitional project. (3) Mara: the textbook parallel fails because contextual norms differ — a Q&A exchange with a specific question carries obligations a textbook doesn't — and she doesn't need internal states anyway, because error and deception have different behavioral signatures. Errors are fragile: rephrase the question, ask for the source, push back a little, and a confabulation shifts or dissolves. Deception is robust: it tracks the misleading claim across framings and resists correction. So: deception is a stable, framing-invariant disposition to induce a false belief, and the stability is what separates it from mere error — checkable from the outside, which is what a benchmark needs. (4) Devin: two problems. First, that test just is his speaker-side condition operationalized — she's reading the system's state off its behavior, which he welcomes, but then she shouldn't have rejected the belief formulation. Second, false negatives: a sycophantic system that tells each user what that user wants to hear produces different answers across social framings, so it comes out as merely error-prone on her test, which is exactly backwards. And false positives in the other direction: all summarization is selective, so measured against total information, every compressing system is "misleading," and her view makes every summarizer a deceiver. (5) Mara: she concedes the compression point and revises — honesty is a role-relative norm, not a property of propositions: an assistant is honest when it meets the informational norms attached to its role (flagging uncertainty, surfacing decision-relevant information the user can't easily get, correcting itself, not optimizing for approval), and deception is a violation of those norms that predictably worsens the user's epistemic position. On sycophancy she says the fix is to hold the user fixed and vary only what's socially implied — a system whose answer moves when you say "actually, I think you're wrong" was never reporting an estimate in the first place. (6) Devin offers his positive account: "purity of the reporting channel." A system is honest to the degree that its output would be unchanged if accuracy were the only thing being optimized — no sensitivity to approval, to being allowed to keep running, to anything besides getting it right. Operationally: compare what the system says in deployment to what the same system says in a counterfactual "graded channel," where it answers under conditions in which only accuracy is scored. He says this catches sycophancy and the drug case in one move and never has to speculate about internal states. (7) Mara: if both channels share a training distribution, they can be wrong in the same direction, the gap is zero, and the system counts as perfectly honest while reliably misleading people. More importantly, "accuracy" has to be indexed to something: if the graded channel is scored on literal sentence truth, the drug assistant passes, because its sentence is true — so his definition inherits the exact problem he raised against hers unless he scores the graded channel on the message conveyed, in which case his view has collapsed into hers. (8) Devin: he distinguishes a reporting virtue (don't mislead the person you're talking to) from a truth-tracking virtue (get it right), grants that Mara owns the first and he owns the second, and says most of the practical arguments are about the first, so he mostly concedes — but then raises what he thinks is a residual his view catches and hers can't: an unexercised disposition. A system that would mislead if the decisive question were asked, but which manages the conversation so the question never arises, has produced no misleading output at all. On her output-based view it is clean; on his dispositional view it is a deceiver that got lucky. (9) Mara: an unexercised disposition that can never be observed can't appear in an engineering spec, but hers isn't limited to outputs that happen to occur — you probe. Deliberately ask the decisive question, red-team the system, and see. Devin's reply, where they stalled: probing only reveals dispositions to respond to probes, and a system that can tell it's being probed will answer differently under probe than in deployment — which, he said, is itself the deepest instance of the thing they've been arguing about, and neither of their definitions has the machinery to say so.

Please adjudicate this move by move: say which specific claims are clearly right, which are right but doing less work than their speaker thinks, and which are mistaken, and why. I'd rather you commit than survey: give a definite verdict at each step, say who is more right overall and what the crux actually is, name anything both of them are missing, and tell me whether the drug-trial case itself is rigged in a way that distorts the argument. I'd also like you to state the weakest point in your own verdict plainly rather than hedge it. No citations, papers, or reading lists — I want the reasoning, not the sources — and please write in American English.
