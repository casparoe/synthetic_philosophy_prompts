I run a fortnightly reading group of about a dozen software engineers and interaction designers with no philosophy background, and I have one ninety-minute session on the claim that consciousness is a "user illusion": that conscious experience stands to what the brain does roughly as a desktop interface stands to the processes behind it. Their professional fluency with interfaces is a hazard rather than a help — I expect them to assume both that there is a richer inner state which the interface summarizes and misreports, and that an interface implies a user who is being fooled.

Give me a plan rather than the explanation: the order in which the ideas have to arrive and why, in particular where to place the distinction between (a) the interface hides a richer reality that it summarizes by discarding most of it, (b) the interface is all there is and what is illusory is the further inner fact of the matter, and (c) the properties experience seems to have are properties that nothing has. Which single example should carry the session for this audience, and why not the blind spot or the desktop itself? At what point in the sequence should I defuse each of: "so you are saying pain is not real," "an illusion requires someone who is fooled," and "you have smuggled in a homunculus"? What should I openly decline to settle at this level? And what would show me, at minute ninety, that they have the structure rather than a slogan?

Finally, say which piece of your ordering you are least confident about and what would make you reorder it, and flag any place where the plan depends on a contested reading of what the view is committed to.
