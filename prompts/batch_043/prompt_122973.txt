I'm in a small reading group working through formal epistemology, and we keep going in circles on the same pair of puzzles: the large fair lottery, where for each ticket I seem entitled to accept that it will lose while apparently not being entitled to accept that no ticket will win, and the author's preface, where she believes every claim in her book and also believes that at least one of them is false. Every week someone proposes a fix and someone else objects that the fix handles one puzzle and not the other, or handles both only by surrendering something we wanted to keep. I've started to think the real disagreement is upstream of the solutions: we never agreed on what a theory of rational acceptance is supposed to deliver. So before we read another paper I want the specification, and I'll do the scoring myself.

Fix the purpose as follows, since I take adequacy to be relative to it: the theory is meant to guide a body that must publicly certify claims and then act on them. The cases I care about are institutional rather than armchair — a regulator signing off on a complex engineered system on the strength of an assurance case with a few hundred sub-claims, each individually well supported but whose conjunction is not; a journal deciding whether a multi-lab replication result is established; a court weighing purely statistical evidence about a defendant against individualized evidence; a standards body that must issue one sentence of guidance that many agents will then treat as settled. So: write the desiderata such a theory has to meet. For each one give the case or consideration that motivates it, say whether it is non-negotiable or merely a preference of one camp, and be willing to throw out candidate requirements that turn out to be prejudice dressed as principle. Some distinctions I want respected rather than collapsed unless you can argue they should be: accepting for the purposes of inference versus accepting as a public commitment versus flat-out believing; closure under conjunction versus closure under entailment we have actually checked; first-personal deliberation about what to accept versus third-personal assessment of a certifier's performance; and the difference between a claim's being improbable and its being badly evidenced, since several of our arguments felt like they were sliding between those two. Then flag any subset of the requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — that incompatibility is the part I most want, because if the list is self-defeating that is itself the result — and tell me which single requirement knocks out the largest number of otherwise attractive theories.

To keep it from being an exercise in recall, here are three variants I invented that I want the list tested against, none of which I've seen discussed. First, the distributed preface: a hundred-author handbook in which each contributor stakes her reputation only on her own chapter, while an editor must certify the volume as a whole. Must the paradox arise for the individuals, for the collective, or for neither, and is aggregate acceptance the same thing as accepting the aggregate? Second, the adversarial preface: same author, same book, but a hostile reviewer has publicly offered to pay for each error found, and the author knows the reviewer is very good. If the stakes move the threshold for acceptance, the author should arguably become less willing to accept any given claim — yet nothing about her evidence has changed, which strikes me as a reason to doubt that stakes-sensitivity is a genuine desideratum at all. Third, the audited lottery: an ordinary lottery, except that before the draw any ticket holder may pay a small fee to have her own ticket independently verified as a loser, and the audit has a known tiny error rate. Does being able to convert one statistical item into an individually checked item change what should be accepted, and if it does, what does that say about a theory that treats all the tickets as symmetric? If any of my three cases is confused, say so and explain why rather than accommodating it.

Please be explicit about your own epistemic standing. For each requirement, mark whether it is uncontroversial among people who actually work on these questions or whether it is itself a contested commitment that a reasonable theorist would reject outright, and do not let a contested one pass as the consensus. Where you are extrapolating beyond anything settled, say that too, and where one of my framings is doing argumentative work I haven't earned, tell me which. A short score at the end for the three or four main strategic options — moving or contextualizing the threshold, restricting closure, or denying that high probability is what justification consists in — is enough; keep the weight on the criteria.
