I was recently appointed to the board of a small family foundation whose founding instrument, drawn up by my grandfather in the early 1960s, commits the foundation to the defense and transmission of Western values. The trustees disagree about what that phrase is doing, and we have to decide by the end of the year whether to keep the language as it stands, replace it with an explicit list of commitments, or strike the clause and rewrite the mission statement around what we actually fund.

Rather than go around again, two of us have agreed to act as an adversarial collaboration and table a joint document at the board meeting. One of us holds what I will call the thick reading: the phrase names a genuine, historically continuous inheritance with commitments that can be listed, that are worth defending, and that the foundation would be abandoning something real by dropping. The other holds what I will call the functional reading: the phrase is a boundary-marking device whose apparent content is set by the contrast it is drawing in context, so that its extension is determined by who is being excluded rather than by what is being affirmed, and the list of commitments always arrives after the fact to rationalize a membership decision already made.

What I want from you is the document itself, not a survey of how the phrase is used and not anything that depends on looking things up: no citations, no bibliography, no reading list. Draft it in language an intelligent trustee who has never studied philosophy can follow, and keep it to roughly twelve hundred words.

Start with a numbered section of claims both of us could sign — about what the phrase is being used to do in our own materials, about what would have to be true for either reading to be preferable, and about which considerations are simply not probative. If part of the dispute between us is verbal rather than substantive, say exactly which part and why, and put that in the agreed section rather than burying it.

Then give a disagreements table. For each residual disagreement I want three things: the claim in neutral wording; the formulation of it that the thick reader would accept for her own side; the formulation the functional reader would accept for his. The formulations should be honest rather than diplomatic — I would rather have two sentences that still visibly clash than one sentence we can both tolerate.

Then the cruxes, and for each one a test we could actually run and what each of us predicts it will show. I have drafted four candidate tests, and you should use them, sharpen them, or replace them with better instruments, telling me why if you do:

The substitution test. Take any sentence in the foundation's own materials containing the phrase and substitute the most charitable explicit statement of the commitments the speaker would endorse. Ask whether the sentence loses anything — force, warmth, authority, an implied threat — and if so, what, precisely, the residue is.

The defection test. List the parties, states, and institutions that our materials over seven decades describe as having betrayed or abandoned these values, and ask whether that list tracks the commitments named in the substitution test or tracks something else: alliance, trade, religion, latitude, the decade.

The audience flip. Compare passages addressed to domestic audiences with passages addressed to audiences abroad, and record whether the commitments are presented as binding on everyone everywhere or as one people's inheritance among others. The thick reading predicts any flip is incidental; the functional reading predicts it is systematic and tracks the audience.

The invocation curve. Code the foundation's grant letters and board minutes from 1960 onward for how often the phrase appears and what work it does in each passage, then check whether invocations cluster in years of internal disagreement about grantmaking rather than in years of external pressure on whatever the phrase is said to name.

For each crux, write down what each side predicts and, more importantly, what result each side would count as evidence against its own view. If a crux has no test that would genuinely move one of us, say that plainly rather than supplying a decorative one.

Two further requests about how you write it. First, be calibrated: mark which of the agreed claims are load-bearing and which are merely the sort of thing any two reasonable people would concede, and attach a rough confidence to each crux claim about what the tests would show — I need to know which parts of this document I can lean on in the room and which parts are your guess about people you have never met. Second, push back where the request is confused. In particular: if you think the thick and functional readings are not two rival positions but a first-order claim and a second-order claim about first-order claims, so that an adversarial collaboration between them is a category mistake, say so and tell me what I should have asked for instead. If the honest landing place is a mixed view — a real inheritance plus a phrase that mostly signals — write for that rather than forcing symmetry. If the version of either reading you can best defend is one no actual trustee would hold, tell me. And if any factual or historical stipulation in this letter, or in the document you draft, is something you are not confident about, flag it rather than smoothing it over.
