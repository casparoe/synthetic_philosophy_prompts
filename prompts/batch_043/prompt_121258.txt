I am writing a specification document for a research group, and I need the central section of it: the requirements that a theory of evidential cooperation in large worlds would have to meet in order to count as adequate. Please fix adequacy by one practical purpose rather than trying to satisfy everyone: the theory is to be implemented in a bounded agent that chooses exactly once, in a world it cannot fully enumerate, among very many other agents — natural and artificial — that resemble it to varying degrees, may share a designer or a common cause with it, and face structurally similar choice situations. By a large world I mean one in which the agent cannot enumerate the relevant states of nature and in which its own decision is correlated, by one mechanism or another, with decisions made by agents it will never meet. The theory's job is to say when, and how strongly, such an agent may treat its own act as evidence about what those other acts will be, and what it should do on that basis.

Please argue from cases rather than from the literature. I do not want names, dates, citations, or a bibliography — the document has to stand on its own for readers who have no library access, and I want the reasoning, not the map of who said what.

What I want delivered, in this order:

A numbered list of desiderata. For each one give the requirement stated crisply, then the case or consideration that motivates it — the concrete situation a theory fails on if it ignores the requirement, or the distinction it collapses — and then a verdict on whether it is non-negotiable for the purpose I fixed or merely a preference of one camp (a taste for causal purity, a taste for uniformity across cases, a taste for computability, and so on). Where you think a proposed requirement is really a sectarian demand wearing the costume of a desideratum, say so and demote it.

A short section on which subsets of your desiderata cannot be jointly satisfied. If the list is itself a result — if the requirements people actually place on such a theory are jointly unsatisfiable, or satisfiable only jointly with something obviously false — that is the part I most want to see, and I want the incompatibility argued rather than asserted.

A short section naming the single requirement that does the most exclusionary work: the one that, if enforced strictly, eliminates the largest share of otherwise attractive candidate theories, with your reasoning about why it bites harder than its neighbors.

Optionally, and briefly, a scorecard of the main existing families of view against the list — a table is fine, and a single row each is enough. Keep the weight on the criteria: the scorecard is an appendix, the specification is the deliverable.

Here are the materials I keep getting stuck on. Treat them as raw inputs, not as the list itself: some may turn out to be the same requirement stated twice, some may dissolve under scrutiny, and some may be parochial demands in disguise.

The exact-twin case versus the near-twin case, where similarity is a matter of degree: if my similarity to another decision-maker is captured by some coefficient between zero and one, the probability that it acts as I do should plausibly be regressed toward the population base rate rather than set to one, and I want to know what the theory owes us about where that coefficient comes from and how it is estimated from a finite record of observed behavior.

The many-weakly-correlated case: a large population in which no one is my twin but everyone is somewhat like me, and the worry that aggregating many faint correlations manufactures a confidence that no one of them individually supports, and does so as a pure artifact of how many such agents there happen to be.

The diffuse case: correlation with no discrete common cause behind it at all, only a broad statistical regularity across a population of agents who resemble one another without having been made to.

The indexical case: I do not know how many near-copies of me there are and must integrate over a distribution on that number, and I am worried that a heavy-tailed prior will generate cooperation out of nothing — that the size of my uncertainty, rather than anything about the world, will do the work.

The opposed-values case: agents similar to me in decision procedure but not in aims, up to and including an adversary who can simulate my policy well enough to exploit it, and the question of what the theory says when the correlation is real but the correlated acts are ones I should want fewer of rather than more of.

The counting distinction: the difference between my act being evidence about a great many acts and its bringing about a great many acts, and the demand that a theory not let the first be quietly entered into the ledger as the second.

The fixed-point case: what the theory says to an agent who knows that it is running the theory, and what the agent should do if no stable policy exists.

The similarity-metric case: whether a theory of this kind owes us a specified metric on agents, or must return verdicts that are invariant across any reasonable choice of metric, given that similarity is famously in the eye of the beholder.

Aim for roughly 1,300 to 1,600 words, in prose with a table only for the optional scorecard, and write the whole thing in American English.
