i keep starting another essay about whether we should do anything about suffering in nature and giving up halfway, because every one of them seems to be answering a different question. so before i read any more of them: i want the spec first.

what i need it for: imagine a small funder with actual money, deciding whether to back a field pilot next year — contraception, supplemental feeding, pain reduction, habitat tweaks, whatever, for some wild population — versus just not spending it on this at all. so i want a theory of intervention that is decision-guiding at that level, not one that only tells me whether nature is on balance good or bad in some cosmic sense.

write me the desiderata. for each, give the case or consideration behind it, and mark it non-negotiable vs. preference-of-one-camp. things i already think belong on the list, but you fix it:

- predation, parasitism, disease and starvation should come apart, not get treated as one blob called "suffering in nature" — i want a case where the mechanism changes the verdict
- don't collapse "this creature has moral status" with "this creature matters this much"; don't collapse "relieve the suffering of the individuals who exist" with "bring it about that fewer suffering individuals exist"
- say what to do about the maybe-sentient: insects, spiders, isopods — where i'd put maybe 20–40% on there being real felt suffering, but where the numbers are astronomical. "probably not sentient" can't just be treated as "doesn't count"
- the equilibrium trap: in a density-dependent population, removing individuals or feeding them mostly changes how many there are, not how any particular one fares. a theory that ignores this will keep recommending things that do nothing or backfire. (tell me if i'm over-reading the biology here)
- the shifting baseline: the counterfactual isn't pristine wilderness, it's whatever climate change, habitat loss and introduced species are about to do anyway. "leave nature alone" isn't a stable option to compare against
- reversibility: contraception you can stop; a gene drive or a pain-insensitivity edit you can't. is irreversibility a side-constraint that can veto a large expected gain, or just another term in the expected value
- the "it has to be about them, not about us" test: winter feeding and rescue-y stuff makes us feel better and looks like helping while plausibly making things worse. i want a criterion that screens that out without screening out everything
- standing: does it matter whether the intervener is one guy with a dart gun, an ngo, or a state acting with something like authorization. i suspect most theories quietly assume a benevolent god's-eye intervener and i want that assumption dragged out
- operationalization: it has to cash out in something measurable, for animals you can't ask anything of. if the currency is unmeasurable in principle it can't guide a funding decision
- plus at least one requirement i havn't thought of

then three things. (a) flag any subset you think can't be jointly satisfied — my guess is that "don't act without decent evidence of net improvement" and "the whole point is acting on huge numbers with thin evidence" are in direct conflict, and i want to know if that's a real contradiction in the list or a false alarm. (b) which single requirement does the most work at knocking out the popular positions. (c) which of my requirements are me smuggling in my own politics — which are genuine desiderata and which are just the prejudices of one camp.

and: put a confidence note on the requirements, however rough, and single out the one you're least sure of. i'd rather have a list with honest error bars than a tidy one. if you want to add two or three lines on how the hands-off view and the "intervene wherever expected suffering goes down" view do against it, fine, but keep that short — the list is the deliverable.
