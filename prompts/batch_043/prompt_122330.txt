I need help finding the mistake in an argument I cannot accept but also cannot take apart.

A visitor at our reading group last week argued that the distinction between intent alignment and value alignment is idle, on the ground that neither has a determinate target, and hence that the alignment problem has no solution in principle rather than merely in practice. I have reconstructed it with my own numbering, and I think I have been fair to it:

(1) A system S is intent-aligned with an operator O only if S's objective is what O intends — the content of O's intention, perhaps under some idealization — and not merely some proxy that happens to coincide with it on the cases that actually arise.

(2) For that content to be S's objective, the target must be determinate: for any behavior available to S, there must be a fact of the matter about whether it conforms to what O intends.

(3) Intentional content is not determinate in that sense. For any finite body of evidence about what O intends — instructions, corrections, demonstrations, approval signals, and any amount of neural or behavioral data up to now — there are indefinitely many contents that fit that evidence exactly and prescribe different actions in situations outside it. Call these deviant intents.

(4) So however much evidence S gathers, S cannot single out O's intent from its deviant rivals: for every candidate, some rival fits everything S has ever observed and diverges later.

(5) If S cannot single out the target, S cannot be aligned with it. A system optimizing any one of the rivals is, by its own lights and by ours, in exactly the same position as a system optimizing O's, and nothing distinguishes them before the divergence.

(6) Therefore intent alignment is unachievable in principle. The obstacle is not data, compute, or engineering skill; it is the absence of a target with enough structure to be hit.

(7) Value alignment is no better off. What O values is subject to the same underdetermination as what O intends, and the usual ways of making value targets more robust — idealization, reflection, extrapolation — only widen the space of admissible contents.

(8) So the narrow and the broad statements of the alignment problem stand or fall together, and both fall.

(9) But the reasoning in (3) through (5) is not special to machines. If it works, then no apprentice ever does what her supervisor meant, no one ever successfully follows a written instruction, and I cannot now know what I will intend tomorrow, since in each case deviant contents fit all the available evidence equally well. People plainly do follow instructions. So either one of (1) through (8) is false, or the steps do not connect the way they appear to.

I cannot say which. I am most confident of (3): it looks like the familiar rule-following point restated for intentions, and denying it seems to require a substantive naturalistic theory of content that I do not have. I also do not want to give up (9). Beyond that I am open, including to being told that (1) is confused.

Three further things I would like addressed.

First, one member of the group said that (7) is where the intent/value distinction comes back in: what O intends is O's, whereas what is good for a person is fixed by facts about that person, so a value target may be determinate even if an intention target is not. I do not know whether that rescues value alignment or only relocates the underdetermination, since the good-for relation still has to range over the right individuals under the right description.

Second, the obvious repair seems not to help. Suppose we stop trying to specify the target and let S latch onto it by a causal or deferential route instead, so that S optimizes whatever O in fact intends rather than some description of it. Then, if the target is a reflective one — what O would intend given more information, or after seeing S's proposals — the target depends on S's own output, so what S is aligned with is a fixed point of a process involving S, and there may be several such fixed points with nothing to select among them. That looks like the underdetermination of (3) returning in a new form rather than being answered.

Third, if your diagnosis is that alignment can hold without being certifiable, say plainly whether what survives is still a solution to the alignment problem as it is usually understood, or whether the argument has merely moved from the object to our knowledge of it, and what that relocation costs someone who has to decide whether to deploy.

Which step do you reject? If the defect is not a premise but a slide between steps, identify the exact transition and say what licences the slide. And in each case, price the alternatives: which of the claims I have said I want to keep becomes harder to hold, and does the repaired position still leave room for the thought that a system can be genuinely aligned with an operator rather than merely behaving as we hope?
