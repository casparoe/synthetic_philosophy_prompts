Okay so I've been going back and forth on the whole are-dreams-experiences thing, Malcolm's 1959 book Dreaming versus Dennett's 1976 reply. I dont want to just repeat the usual stuff, so I tried to build an argument from some sleep-lab-ish cases, and now I think it proves way too much. I can't accept the conclusion, but I also really want to keep the bit that dreams are experiences during sleep and not just waking impressions. Here's the thing I came up with (numbered so it's easier to attack):

(1) If a subjects later first-personal report of a seeming past experience is reliably correlated with, and causally produced by, measurable neural goings-on at an earlier time t while they were asleep, then the subject really underwent that experience at t.

(2) In REM stimulus-incorporation studies, when researchers present a cue at t during REM—say a rose smell or a beep—and then wake the subject a bit later, the subject reliably reports a dream that incorporates the cue. The report is produced by the REM processes at t together with the cue, not by the subject composing a story at the moment of waking.

(3) So, from (1) and (2), the subject really undergoes an experience of the rose or beep at t during REM. Dreams are experiences during sleep. (This is the claim I most want to keep.)

(4) Now imagine a memory-tagging setup in deep N3 sleep. At t during N3, researchers present an imperceptible auditory cue. Later, after waking, the subject is given a hypnotic suggestion that links that cue to a childhood birthday party. After the suggestion, the subject reports a vivid memory of the party. This report is also reliably correlated with and causally produced by the earlier neural event at t, meaning the N3 cue plus later consolidation and suggestion.

(5) By exactly the same principle (1), the subject really underwent an experience of the childhood birthday party at t during N3. The dream-report case in (2) and the suggested-memory case in (4) are supposed to be paralel: same kind of reliable causal link to an earlier sleep-time neural event.

(6) But (5) is absurd. The subject did not live the birthday party at t; if the party was a real childhood event, it happened years earlier, and if it was fictional it never happened at all. N3 is the deep dreamless stage, so the subject wasn't conscious of any party then. Hypnotic suggestion can make people confidently report things they never experienced; it doesn't give them a new experience of the event at the time of the cue.

(7) So either (1) is false, or I have to give up (3) and say Malcolm was right that dreams aren't experiences during sleep, or (2) and (4) are not actually paralel and one of the steps hides a relevant difference.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd rather keep (3) than (1), if I have to choose, but I don't want to just bite the bullet on (5). Please name the step, explain why it looked fine, and price the alternatives—e.g., what I'd have to say about dreams, about hypnotic memory, or about the relation between neural causes and conscious experiences if I reject it. If the flaw is an equivocation between a neural event's being the realizer of an experience at t versus merely being an earlier cause of the later report, say so and tell me how to spell out the difference without assuming the conclusion that dreams are experiences.
