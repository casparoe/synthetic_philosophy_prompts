I'm on my school's Ethics Bowl team, and one of my teammates has unusually settled views about rescue cases, so I have started testing her. Her verdicts so far: one stranger drowning on the left against five strangers drowning on the right, where you can reach only one group — she denies that you have to save the five, says saving the one is no worse, and says neither group has a complaint the other does not also have. One stranger who will drown against five strangers who will each only break an arm — she saves the one, on the grounds that five broken arms do not add up into something that outweighs a death. Her own sister against five strangers, all drowning — she saves her sister, and not merely as something she is permitted to do. One stranger against a hundred — she still will not grant that you must save the hundred.

The case I want to put to her next repeats the first one with a single change: you cannot throw the line yourself, you can only set a dial on an automatic thrower fixing the probability with which it throws to the single person on the left, and the machine randomizes from there. I have realized her first verdict does not settle this, because the thought that everyone should get an equal chance splits into giving each side an equal chance, which sends the thrower left half the time, and giving each of the six people an equal chance of being the one saved, which sends it left once in six. So: what number does she set, and is there any setting she would call impermissible? More useful to me than the number itself is the reasoning behind it — what are the two or three rules that fit all four of her verdicts equally well, which one should I bet on and why, and what single further case would tell the surviving rules apart?
