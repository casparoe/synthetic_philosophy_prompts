I'm in a graduate reading group on the philosophy of time, and we have now spent two full sessions on the Rietdijk–Putnam argument without converging, partly because everyone keeps restating their position instead of saying what they'd bet. So I want a number, and I want to see the machinery that produced it.

The proposition I want you to evaluate is this: conditional on our best physics genuinely retaining the special-relativistic feature that there is no physically privileged slicing of spacetime into simultaneity classes — for any two spacelike-separated events there is an admissible inertial frame in which they are simultaneous, and no frame is distinguished — the thesis that only presently existing things exist is untenable. By untenable I do not mean unmotivated or inelegant; I mean not rationally retainable without additions that a neutral observer would class as ad hoc or as changing the subject. Since that is vague, stipulate a resolution condition of your own and state it: for instance, that in fifty years' time the community working on these questions has either abandoned presentism or is defending a version a majority would say no longer answers to the original thesis. My own credence on the proposition is 0.6. A friend in the group puts his at 0.92, and I cannot locate where the disagreement lives.

Please decompose rather than give me a bare figure. I'd like numbers and one line of justification each for at least these, plus any terms you think I've missed: that relativity of simultaneity carries metaphysical weight rather than being a coordinate convention with no ontological consequence; that the argument's bridge premise — whatever is simultaneous with my present moment in some admissible frame is, for that reason, real — does not already presuppose a tenseless notion of existence and thereby beg the question against the presentist; that the presentist's fallback of an unobservable privileged foliation, a cosmic now that never appears in the dynamics, is genuinely costly rather than merely unfamiliar; and that the argument does not overgenerate. On that last point I'd like you to consider a view I'll call relativized presentism, where each observer's own simultaneity slice is the real one, and say whether it is coherent and, if it isn't, whether the argument that kills it also kills something the eternalist wants to keep — and also whether in a hypothetical Newtonian universe with absolute simultaneity there remains any residue of the argument at all, which would tell me how much of this is physics and how much is semantics. I'd also like one conditional: suppose we had a quantum theory of gravity in which a preferred foliation is a real dynamical object, a physically detectable cosmic time that no observer is in principle barred from measuring, rather than a convenient coordinate choice. Give me your credence on the original proposition under that supposition, and say how far your headline number moves; if it barely moves, I'll conclude the physics was never doing the work. Combine your terms explicitly, tell me which one the result is most sensitive to, which one you are least confident about, and — given my 0.6 and my friend's 0.92 — which of us is most likely misjudging which term. Finally, name the one conceptual consideration, not an empirical result and not anything requiring a library, that would move your own number furthest, and in which direction.
