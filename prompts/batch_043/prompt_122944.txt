Hey — I'm on a pilot-grant panel at my university (we hand out small pots of money for population-health and education research, nothing glamorous). We've got three applications that are all basically the same study at three different levels of intensity, and our deliberations have tied us in a knot. I need to draft the decision memo we'll circulate before we vote next Thursday, and I'd like you to write it: give us a bottom line at the top, recommend one concrete course of action, and — this is the part I care most about — give a fair airing to the strongest thing that can be said against whatever you recommend, since half the panel will be looking for exactly that.

Here's the case. Three teams applied to study whether a school-based reading intervention improves literacy in the long run. There's money for one, not three. The designs:

- Light: test reading at the start and end of one school year, in 50 schools. Cheap, barely any burden on kids or teachers, but short follow-up and underpowered for subgroups.
- Medium: test at three points over two years, in 40 schools, plus teacher surveys. Better powered, can see whether effects persist, more cost and a bit more hassle.
- Heavy: test at six points over three years, in 30 schools, teacher surveys, and monthly home visits to collect home-literacy data. Best shot at mechanism and long-term effects, but way more burden, much higher dropout, and the home visits bring real privacy issues.

What happened in the room is the problem. We were comfortable that Medium is better than Light: the extra power and longer follow-up are worth the extra cost and the modest extra burden. Then we talked ourselves into Heavy being better than Medium: the home visits and extra waves felt like a big enough jump in what we'd learn to be worth it at that margin. But then someone said, compare Light and Heavy head to head, and nearly everyone flipped: the total burden, cost, dropout risk, and the privacy escalation of sending researchers into people's homes every month are so much larger than Light's that Light comes out better than Heavy. So we've got Heavy better than Medium better than Light better than Heavy. No design beats all the others.

I've been reading a bit about this in the abstract — the question of whether "better than" has to be transitive. One line of thought says it does: it's built into the idea of one option being better than another that the relation is transitive, so a circle like ours means at least one of our three comparisons is wrong and we should go back and fix it (maybe we were too quick to say Heavy beats Medium, or too quick to say Light beats Heavy). Another line of thought says no, it doesnt have to be: when options sit on a spectrum and each small step up looks like an improvement, but the accumulated step crosses a threshold (a lot more money, a lot more burden, or a qualitative change like entering the home), "better than" can genuinely go in a circle, and there may just be no best option, definately not one we can all agree on. I don't need a literature review and please don't give me citations — I want the memo itself.

So: write the panel's decision memo. It should (a) recommend something concrete — fund one of them, fund none and send them back to redesign, pick by lottery, whatever you actually think is right — with a timeline for whatever happens next; (b) say why, in terms the three applicant teams and the rest of the panel can respond to; and (c) take the strongest objection to your recommendation seriously instead of batting it away. If you think the cycle is a mistake, say which comparison you'd revisit and what would have to be true for the ranking to come out consistent. If you think it's real, say what we should do given that there's no best option, and don't just say "flip a coin" unless you mean it and can defend it. Keep it under about 800 words — it's a memo, not a paper.
