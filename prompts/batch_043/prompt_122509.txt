I am a staff engineer at a company of about 250 people, and I own the measurement layer for our internal developer platform. Several times a quarter I am asked to turn a qualitative goal — "make the platform easier to ship on", "improve the reliability of service X", "raise code quality" — into numbers that then do real work: they go into team OKRs that a director reviews, into a launch gate for a migration, and into promotion packets. Call it a dozen to twenty metrics a year, each of which gets roughly one 90-minute working session with me, one data analyst (no statistics PhD anywhere on my team), an engineering director who signs off, and the lead of the team that will live under the metric.

I am good at writing specifications for APIs and I am bad at this. Every time a number of mine starts to matter, it stops tracking the thing it was supposed to track. I have read the popular write-ups of the effect people call Goodhart's law; they are long on diagnosis and anecdote and short on anything I can run on a Tuesday. So I want a procedure, not an explanation.

Please write me a decision procedure I can apply to a candidate metric before it is allowed to govern anything, and re-apply when a metric already in force looks suspicious. Ordered steps, each with: the question to ask; what counts as a sufficient answer (and what counts as hand-waving); the artifact I should record; and an explicit stopping rule — either stop with a "no, this metric may not govern that decision", or hand off to a named human because the question is one of values rather than facts. Then give me a one-page "metric charter" template that captures the outputs of the steps.

I want the procedure to build on the following distinctions, which I have found useful but cannot systematize. Please keep them, sharpen them where they are muddled, and tell me if any of them is wrong or collapses into another:

1. Classify the failure before treating it. A metric can stop tracking its goal because the world moved and the correlation decayed with nobody responding to it; because the people under the metric changed their behaviour to move the number; because we optimised the number far outside the range of conditions in which anyone ever checked that it tracked the goal; or because measuring and reporting the number changed the system being measured, so that the target itself moved. These have different remedies, so classification has to be step one — and it has to be doable from artifacts we actually have (dashboards, commit and review history, ticket data, incident write-ups), not from guessing at intentions. I cannot prove anyone gamed anything, and I do not want a procedure whose output is an accusation.

2. The indifferent optimizer. Instead of asking whether anyone would cheat, ask what a competent, well-meaning engineer who has no interest whatsoever in the goal and every interest in the number would do, given the tools and permissions they actually have. Name the concrete behaviour. If the list is long, that is evidence about the metric rather than about the team.

3. The blindness profile. An explicit list of what the metric is structurally incapable of registering: things that never enter the data source at all, effects that only appear on timescales longer than the reporting window, qualities that have no unit, and costs borne by people outside the measured population. Require naming the single most important item on that list, and require someone to sign their name to it.

4. The validated envelope. State the range of conditions and values within which anyone ever checked that moving the number moved the goal, and state where current and target values sit relative to that range. If nobody ever checked, the procedure must stop with a "no" rather than produce a confidence score.

5. A second channel. For any metric that governs a high-stakes decision, require an independent signal not computed from the same underlying source, chosen so that it fails in a different direction from the first. Where no such channel exists, the procedure should say so plainly and route the decision to a human with a review date, rather than implying the number is adequate on its own.

6. A pre-registered kill condition. At definition time, write down what observation would make us stop using this metric, set a review date, and record a rough half-life estimate in quarters — "unknown" is an acceptable entry only if it is written down as such. "We'll keep an eye on it" should count as a failed step.

The procedure must also mark its own limits: which steps produce answers from data and which are value judgments it is not entitled to make — which goals are worth pursuing at all, the exchange rate between units of the proxy and units of the goal, and how much false-positive versus false-negative risk we accept. Where a step looks rigorous but is really a judgment call in a lab coat, say so in the step.

Then apply the finished procedure to three cases from my world, showing the artifacts and the point at which each case stops or hands off:

(a) Deployment frequency and change-failure-rate targets attached to a gate that decides whether a large service migration is allowed to proceed.

(b) Counts derived from bug trackers — open P1s, or bugs closed per sprint — used as an input to promotion packets, where the declared goal is code quality.

(c) A composite "developer productivity index" that our platform dashboard already computes and that leadership is attached to. I suspect the procedure will tell me to stop using it. If that is where it lands, work it through to that conclusion anyway and draft the two or three sentences I would actually have to say to the director who likes it.

Finish by stating what the procedure cannot do: the situations where it simply does not apply (systems too new to have a validated envelope, goals with no unit at all, goals that are contested rather than merely hard to measure), and the ways it will misfire even when applied honestly — slow drift below the detection threshold, blindness lists that are effectively unbounded.

Two constraints. Please draw your examples from software engineering rather than from the standard folklore about bounties and factory quotas, and do not give me citations, a reading list, or a bibliography: I have no way to check them and I am not asking for the literature. I want something I can run in ninety minutes with a data analyst and defend in a room.
