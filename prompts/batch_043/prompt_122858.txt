I am a graduate instructor and I have one 75-minute seminar to teach Sen's liberal paradox to second-year politics and philosophy students. They have just finished Mill's *On Liberty* and arrive strongly attached to the idea of a protected private sphere; a few have taken microeconomics, most have not; I have a whiteboard and can hand out a single page with a preference profile on it. Please do not explain the result to me. Plan how I should teach it.

Specifically, I want the following.

First, the order in which the components have to be introduced, with the dependency made explicit: which item cannot be motivated until another is in place. I am thinking of the social-states framing (the point that a choice about one's own reading or one's own room is still a complete social state), the Pareto condition as applied to rankings over such states, the minimal-liberalism condition as decisiveness over a pair of states differing only in the holder's own protected sphere, and then the cycle. Tell me if that order is wrong, and where in the sequence the students need to see the cycle for themselves rather than be shown it.

Second, the carrying example. I do not want to lead with Sen's own: the prude-and-lewd case invites the diagnosis that the whole difficulty is busybody preferences plus a prurient frill, and it makes the Pareto condition look like it is deferring to a nuisance. So give me an example the seminar can build on instead, and say what it must have and what it must lack. It must make the cycle come out with no preference that is obviously illegitimate, envious, or intrusive: the cross-sphere preference doing the damage should be something the students would endorse, or at least recognise as decent. If no such example can be constructed without smuggling in something equally peculiar in another guise, tell me that rather than presenting a rigged case — and tell me what I should do instead, since I would rather use the book example knowingly than use a clean-looking one whose cleanliness is an artefact.

Third, the misconceptions, in the order they will actually form, and the point in the sequence where each should be headed off. I expect at least these: that the result is a proof against liberalism rather than a clash of three formal conditions; that it is Arrow's theorem again; that the fix is simply to exclude meddlesome preferences; that Pareto is the mild, obvious condition and so the rights condition must give; and that a planner's ranking is doing the work, when the students will suggest that markets, contracts, or decentralised rights would avoid it. Add any I have missed, including ones my own framing invites.

Fourth, where the mid-1970s move belongs — the reconception, associated in my memory with Gibbard's 1974 paper, of a right not as unconditional decisiveness over a pair of states but as a conditional power: the holder fixes their own component of the outcome given what others do, and unconditional preferences are what count. Correct me plainly if I have the attribution wrong. Say whether it belongs in this session at all or is a follow-up, and if it belongs, what must be in place for it to land.

Fifth, what to refuse to cover at this level, and how I should say so without making the omission look like evasion.

Finally, give me one end-of-session question that tests whether they have the structure rather than the verdict — something like a fresh profile or institution in which they have to locate the two protected pairs and the Pareto pair themselves, or find the minimal change in one ordering that produces or dissolves a cycle.

One more thing, throughout: separate what is settled given the definitions from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this. I have in mind whether the condition labelled liberalism captures anything recognisable as a right, whether the right response is to weaken Pareto or to restrict the domain, whether Sen himself took the result as telling against welfarism or against the rights condition, and how much weight the conditional-rights reconstruction actually bears. I do not want to hand the students a contested reading as though it were the consensus, and I would like to know where my own framing is doing that.
