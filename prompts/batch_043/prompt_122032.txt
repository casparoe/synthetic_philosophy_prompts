im writing a novel — small town, factory closing, two sisters who have to decide whether to say out loud that the man who kept the place running for thirty years did something shabby to a kid who worked there one summer. one sister thinks the family and the town should close ranks; the other thinks somebody got hurt and it has to be said. the problem is not the plot, its that every time i write them arguing i get two cardboard cutouts: one all sentiment and loyalty, one all harm and fairness. so before i build the rest of the cast on top of it, i want the spec rather than the theory.

what would a theory of moral judgment have to deliver for me to trust it as scaffolding for characters who disagree like this? not "which theory is true" — the list of requirements, each with the case or consideration behind it, so i can see which demands are real and which are just one camps ideology dressed up as a criterion. adequacy here means a specific thing for me: good enough that a reader who disagrees with a character still recognises her as a person with reasons, and good enough that i can predict what she does in a scene i haven't written yet.

here are the things i keep tripping over as i write, which i suspect are cases any such theory has to handle — tell me which of them actually deserve to be on the list, which are my own prejudice, and what i've left out:

- the harmless-but-condemned ones: consenting adult siblings using contraception, a man who cooks and eats his own dog after the dog is already dead, someone cleaning a toilet with a national flag. people condemn and then can't produce a victim, and when you knock down their reasons they keep the verdict.
- the gap between killing and letting die, and between the personal and impersonal versions of the trolley family — is that distinction doing real moral work or is it an artefact of which emotional system gets triggered, and if its an artefact, does that defuse the intuition or indict it?
- verdicts getting harsher in a foul-smelling room or after a bitter drink, and how much weight that deserves given that effects of this kind have had replication trouble.
- the residue: a character who has stopped believing something is wrong and still flinches at it. and the reverse — the character who has argued himself into endorsing something and still can't bring himself to do it.
- the flip: somebody who changes her mind on an issue over about two years, after which she has a complete and fluent set of reasons that were not what moved her.
- the cases with no polling data. my characters have to react to situations nobody has surveyed — a specific betrayal in a specific kitchen. so the theory has to generate verdicts for new cases, not just catalogue the ones it was fitted to. i think this is the requirement that kills off a lot of candidates, but you tell me.
- the observable markers. i can write thoughts but i mostly need bodies: what does a change in moral conviction look like from outside?
- the moral/conventional line as kids are supposed to draw it — hitting someone versus breaking a school rule about what to wear — and the awkward fact that for a lot of people purity violations feel like both morality and just disgust at once.
- the bit where moral conviction stops being a strong preference and becomes something you'd enforce on strangers who weren't involved.
- heritability stuff, the fact that moral intuitions are trainable, and the fact that whole classes of acts go from unremarkable to unthinkable inside a couple of generations. if conviction can move that fast and that far, what is left of the feeling that this one is just true?
- the same person being a stickler in one domain and a consequences guy in another, and being a different moral agent with her family than with a stranger or an outgroup.
- and then the one i actually care about for the sisters: does any of this psychology tell us anything about who is right, or is that question on a completely separate track? i have a feeling my two favourite requirements are going to fight here — that a theory can't both take seriously the way conviction presents itself (as about the thing, not about me) and explain that conviction as weights that got set by culture, upbringing and mood. if some subset of the desiderata can't be jointly satisfied, say so and show me where the tension is; that's the part i want most.

mark which requirements are non-negotiable for my purpose and which are negotiable or just somebody's preference, and say which single requirement does the most work in excluding popular theories. then, very briefly — a few lines, not an essay — how the main contenders do: the harm-only rationalist picture, the pluralist foundations picture with its care/fairness/loyalty/authority/purity/liberty style menus, the morality-as-cooperation kind of view, and the hard-nosed "its all tribal signalling" one. and one more novelist thing: give me the knobs. what parameters would i actually set to build a character whose verdicts are predictable but not mechanical?

no footnotes or reading list please, i cant chase references anyway. and write the whole answer in British English.
