I'm assembling the closing packet for a historiography seminar I teach, and our last session is on Whig history — Butterfield's 1931 *The Whig Interpretation of History*, and the way the label has since loosened from his actual target (a nineteenth-century school that, in Wikipedia's paraphrase, praised all progress and habitually linked Protestantism with liberal views of liberty) into a general pejorative for any narrative of improvement. My students can all recite the warning and then go right back to doing it: they hunt for ancestors who resemble us, quietly delete everything that didn't lead anywhere, and award verdicts. Rather than assign one more essay about the danger, I'd like them to be handed the danger in institutional form.

So: write a parody call for proposals — a funding call / "Request for Proposals" — issued by an invented commissioning body (an Institute for Retrospective Coherence, a Directorate of Narrative Continuity, whatever you prefer) inviting applications to write a definitive new survey history, to be titled something in the register of *The Road We Were Always On*. About 700–900 words, laid out as numbered clauses with headings, and including (a) a scoring rubric with point allocations and (b) a short closing "Notes to Applicants" in FAQ form. The voice is deadpan foundation-speak or Whitehall-ese: unfailingly reasonable, procedurally meticulous, gently disappointed by applicants who insist on contingency. Include at least one quoted remark from a dissenting internal figure — a Keeper of Discontinued Pasts, an external reviewer from a department of contingency — whose objection is a real one (that the archive itself was curated by the winners, say, or that a selection rule that keeps only what leads somewhere cannot then discover that the past was simple). Give the document a fictional director or panel chair who can be quoted in earnest.

The comedy has to come from the mechanics being right, so build the clauses out of the actual features of the practice: the way a progressive sequence of events gets treated as, in Butterfield's phrase, "a line of causation," so that ancestry is offered in place of explanation; the funded activity of "abridgement," and the trick whereby the simplicity produced by compression is then reported as a discovery about the past; the casting of historical actors into friends and enemies of progress, with the latter scored as having contributed nothing and reduced, in Butterfield's words, to a "dummy that acts as a better foil to the grand whig virtues"; the pressure to modernize past actors so that they seem more contemporary than they were; the transfer of moral credit, whereby the present inherits the authority of struggles most of its members would probably have lost; and the assumption that a history is incomplete unless it delivers a verdict. I'd like you to invent two or three pseudo-technical instruments in the same spirit as my own — an *Arrival Coefficient* measuring what share of the narrative can be plotted as leading to the present, an *Abridgement Dividend* for pages saved by deleting dead ends, a licence for retroactive adoption of ancestral virtue — and to make the rubric reward them. Then add one compliance appendix for the applicant who thinks they are exempt: the declinist, who writes the same story with the sign flipped, and the debunker, whose narrative of exposures has its own hero arriving at the end. Handle them with the same deadpan administrative sympathy. Somewhere in the fine print, let it become visible that the route has to arrive at *someone's* present, and that most of the world's pasts are therefore classifiable as sidings. Two constraints: don't let the piece collapse into mocking the banal claim that some things have in fact improved — the target is the ratifying structure, not optimism — and don't invent epigrams and attribute them to real historians. Begin directly with the document's title and issuing body; no preface, no note on what you're doing, and nothing after the final clause.
