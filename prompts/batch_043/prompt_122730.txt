I am a PhD student in moral philosophy, writing on whether deaths caused by crop cultivation should count against plant-based diets. A colleague handed me the following argument, which I think proves far too much, but I cannot locate the exact step where it goes wrong.

(1) The moral cost of a food system is the difference it makes to the total amount of suffering in the world, counting every sentient being whose welfare it affects: not only the domesticated animals slaughtered for meat, but also the wild animals whose lives are shaped by the land the system occupies.

(2) For a typical wild animal of a small-bodied, numerous species, expected lifetime welfare is negative: most members of such species die young, and the dominant causes of death (starvation, predation, exposure, parasitism) are slow and painful. Call the quantity that carries this cost a wildlife-year, and call the number of wildlife-years per year that a hectare generates its wildlife-yield.

(3) If a life would be net-negative in welfare, then preventing that life from occurring is pro tanto good, and preventing more of them is proportionally better, for the same reason it would be good not to create miserable beings in the first place.

(4) Intensively worked land has a much lower wildlife-yield than uncultivated habitat of the same kind, and the more intensively a hectare is worked, the fewer wild animals it carries.

(5) Therefore converting land to more intensive production, or converting habitat to farmland at all, permanently reduces the number of net-negative wildlife-years that land generates, and so reduces total suffering. Call this the displacement dividend.

(6) Since the cost is carried by the number of net-negative wildlife-years, the best food system is the one that minimizes wildlife-yield per calorie: land worked as intensively as possible, as much of it as possible.

(7) Corollary: rewilding, set-aside and habitat-restoration projects are pro tanto bad, and the crop-cultivation deaths that animal advocates cite against plant agriculture turn out, on the same arithmetic, to be a reason in favour of intensive agriculture.

I cannot accept (6) and (7).
