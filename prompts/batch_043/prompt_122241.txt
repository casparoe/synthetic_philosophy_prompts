I write about science and ideas for a newspaper, and I'm working on a piece about where, in practice, the line between health and disease gets drawn. I need help working out what rule a national health-coverage advisory panel is actually applying. The panel publishes one-line rulings with no stated rationale, and its standing guideline says only that classification is independent of whether a treatment exists and of what treatment costs. Its published record so far:

- A rare inherited enzyme defect causing failure to thrive in infancy: a disease.
- An accessory spleen — uncommon, asymptomatic, no measurable functional cost: not a disease.
- Traits whose only problem is that they are socially disvalued, such as a minority-typical facial morphology or short stature with no underlying endocrine abnormality: not diseases.
- Iodine-deficiency goiter in a highland district where most adults over forty have it: a disease.
- Age-related bone loss that sharply raises fracture risk: a disease. Age-related skin wrinkling: not a disease.

The petition now in front of them: roughly 7 percent of the population carry loss-of-function variants in both copies of the gene for a small-intestinal transporter protein I'll call EMTA, and they absorb about a third as much of micronutrient M as typical adults. Under the current food supply, which has been fortified with M for thirty years, carriers are indistinguishable from everyone else — same life expectancy, same hospitalization rates, same fertility, no symptoms — but cohort records from before fortification show they had materially elevated rates of a recognized deficiency disease. The variant is old, and population-genetic work suggests its frequency was maintained by balancing selection, because the same transporter is the entry route for a common gut bacterium and carriers contract that infection less often. A tablet costing a few cents restores absorption to typical levels. The petition comes from an advocacy group of people who learned their EMTA status through direct-to-consumer sequencing and want the tablet covered; a substantial minority of carriers say they feel perfectly well and do not want a disease label.

What do you predict the panel rules, and on what grounds? More usefully for my purposes: what are the two or three accounts of disease that fit the whole precedent set, what does each of them say about EMTA deficiency, and which one do you think the panel is running? If the precedents genuinely underdetermine the ruling, say so and tell me which principle the panel would be committing itself to under each choice, and name the single change to this case — or the single further case — that would best tell the candidate accounts apart. I'd also like to know what a defender of whichever account loses would say is wrong with the way I've described the case.
