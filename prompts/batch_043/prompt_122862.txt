ok so i'm giving a short paper at a small conference in the spring on blame, and i've been trying to write down my *own* view on the hypocrisy stuff instead of just summarizing what everyone else says, and i've realised i can't actually tell whether the package hangs together. can you audit it? one ground rule: no reading suggestions and no citations — i know the literature, i want the argumentative work done on my actual set, not a map of who said what. and don't restate my commitments back to me before you start, just go at them.

1. the "you're one too" retort is sometimes genuinely apt. a hypocritical blamer can lack standing to blame, and the defect in their blaming isn't just imprudence or bad manners — it's a real normative defect.
2. here's what i think is doing the work. to blame you for φ-ing is in part to commit myself to the norm against φ-ing applying to agents like me; the unrepentant hypocrite isn't willing to accept that, and that unwillingness is what's wrong with their blame.
3. blame as a practice is best understood as moral address: the point of it is to call on the blamee to acknowledge the demand and respond somehow (apology, repair, explanation), not just for the blamer to register a verdict about them.
4. standing is one thing and how the blame lands is another. whether the blamee is receptive or stung or actually reforms has nothing to do with whether the blamer had standing.
5. fittingness and standing come apart cleanly: someone can be fully blameworthy while nobody at all is well placed to blame them, and a blamer's lacking standing doesn't make the blamee any less blameworthy.
6. standing loss is norm-relative. the person who cheats on their taxes doesn't lose their standing to blame me for cruelty, only to blame me for tax cheating and maybe the stuff in the neighbourhood of it.
7. hypocrisy is bad independent of all of this. someone can be blameworthy for hypocrisy even if they never blame anybody.
8. you can also lose standing with no hypocrisy at all — the person who engineered the situation you went wrong in, or a total stranger with no relationship to you who just enjoys the telling-off. and i'd like a single account of standing, not a seperate story for each defeater.
9. two case verdicts i feel fairly confident about: (a) my colleague who fudged her expense reports two years ago, owned it, paid the money back and has been clean ever since — she can blame me for fudging mine; (b) my colleague who fudges his every month, is completely open about it and genuinely thinks it's wrong of him — i still want to say he can't blame me, and if anything his adding "i know, i'm the worst" makes it worse rather than better.

my guess is the trouble is somewhere in 6 and 7, but honestly i can't locate it and i've got no horse in this race about which one goes, so don't soft-pedal. what i want: which pairs actually pull against each other, ordered by how severe the tension is; for each, exactly what would have to give and what giving it costs me (does it cost me a verdict, an explanation, or just a bit of tidiness); which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection and why; and then which single revision to the set would clean up the most damage. if you think the real casualty is one of my verdicts in 9 rather than one of the general claims, say so directly. 700-900 words or so, and please be specific about *mechanism* — i want to see how one commitment is supposed to entail the thing i'm denying, not just be told they're in tension.
