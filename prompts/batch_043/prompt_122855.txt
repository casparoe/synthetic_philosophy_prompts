A student is handed one of two envelopes, is told that the other contains either half or twice the amount in hers, and is offered the swap. I want a decision tree that starts from exactly that much and walks her to a labelled position on the two-envelope paradox — built, please, from the space of cases and answers rather than from a list of views: fix the questions and the answer-combinations they allow first, then locate the actual positions in that space, and tell me which cells come out empty. No author names, no paper titles, no bibliography anywhere in the document: every leaf should be labelled by the claim it makes, not by who has made it, because the handout has to be usable by someone with no library.

Ground rules: seven to nine branch nodes and seven or eight leaves. Every node must be phrased as a question about a concrete specification of the game, answerable by a student who does not yet know what the branches are called; no node may presuppose a theoretical label.

The distinctions I need the tree to route on:

- whether the choice is made before looking inside or after, and — as a separate node — whether the student is choosing a single act or committing in advance to a policy that will be applied to whatever amount is revealed;
- whether the revealed amount is treated as evidence about which envelope she holds, which forces a view about what the filler's amounts could have been;
- whether the expected value of the amount in her envelope exists at all. This is where I want the heavy-tailed variant: the smaller amount is fixed at 2^n for n heads before the first tail of a fair coin, so that there is no finite expectation to compare;
- whether a policy is assessed by expected amount at all or by long-run multiplicative growth. In the repeated variant the game is replayed with fresh amounts and wealth compounds, and halving and doubling occur equally often under any unconditional policy, so log-wealth is a martingale and the growth-rate criterion reports no gain from switching even where expectations are infinite. Give this its own leaf if you can separate it, and flag it if you cannot.

Cases the tree must return a determinate recommendation for: the revealed amount being the largest the filler could possibly have produced (finite-budget variant: smaller amount drawn uniformly from one to some ceiling); the revealed amount drawn from a tail fat enough that the expectation is infinite; and the filler's distribution being disclosed in advance versus not disclosed at all.

Leaves should include, under whatever names you prefer, the view that symmetry settles the matter, the view that one should switch only below a threshold, the view that there is no expectation to compare, the growth-rate view, the view that one may switch in every state and that this is simply rationally permitted rather than required, and the reading on which the one-and-a-quarter computation equivocates between the two possible values of "the amount in my envelope" — each with one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Where two leaves cannot be pulled apart by what they recommend rather than by how they describe themselves, say so instead of manufacturing a distinction.

Close with a short table of three or four answer-combinations the tree allows but that nobody occupies, each with a one-line diagnosis: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unclaimed. I especially want to know what to do with the student who wants to swap before looking but would keep whatever amount is revealed, and with the one who accepts the one-and-a-quarter step for every possible amount while insisting that switching ex ante gains nothing.
