Doing a short essay for my moral responsibility module and I'd rather not write yet another paper on free will and determinism, so I want to try reading Manchester by the Sea (Lonergan's film) through Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment" — the participant vs objective attitudes, the personal reactive attitudes, the excuse/exemption distinction, and his claim that no theoretical conviction could get us to give up the reactive attitudes in general. The scenes I keep circling round: Lee, drunk, forgets the fire screen and his three kids die in the house fire; the police rule it an accident and bring no charges, and at the station he grabs an officer's gun and tries to kill himself; years later he's back in town as his nephew Patrick's guardian, and there's the scene with his ex-wife Randi where she sobs out an apology for how she treated him and begs him not to just give up and die, and it undoes him; then he gets drunk, picks a fight with total strangers in a bar and gets knocked out; at the end he arranges for Patrick to be taken in by family friends and says he can't beat it. (I might be misremembering some of the staging, and I can't check the film anyway, so please don't lean on exact lines or quotations — paraphrase is fine.)

The bit I really want help with is an idea my housemate came up with and which I can't tell is deep or just confused. She points out that Strawson's taxonomy is built on other-directed attitudes — resentment, gratitude, hurt feelings — and that the self-reactive attitudes get roughly a paragraph, tied to the offender's own readiness to acquiesce in his punishment. On that model guilt is essentially *addressed*: someone resents you, and their forgiveness, or their letting it go, is what releases it. But Lee gets released from every side. The authorities refuse to charge him, Patrick doesn't blame him, Randi explicitly apologises to him and he can't take it. So whose attitude is he braced against? Her name for it is "orphaned guilt": guilt that outlives the withdrawal of every actual blamer, so that it has no addressee and no route to discharge. She pairs it with "recruited blame": the bar fight, where he goes and solicits punishment from people with no standing whatsoever to resent him. Is that a real phenomenon Strawson's machinery can't describe, or is it just a relabelling of things he already handles — displaced or misfiring reactive attitudes, or the objective attitude turned inward (maybe Lee actually *wants* to see himself as just a thing that was causally involved in an accident, and can't pull it off)?

So, a few things: adjudicate those two made-up terms — are they doing work, or muddying it?; say where the film genuinely illuminates Strawson and where it resists him (I don't want to flatten it into an illustration, and I'd like to know where an honest critic has to admit the reading strains); and tell me whether Lee's case is best read as an excuse case or whether his intoxication pushes it toward exemption territory, and whether the film is deliberately not settling that. One more thing that seems relevant and which nobody in my seminar mentioned: there are hints that plenty of people in the town think the fire wasn't an accident at all, so you get blame where the official verdict denies standing sitting right next to a refusal of blame from the one person demanding it — I'd like you to use that. What, if anything, does the film add to the debate that the arguments on their own don't? Answer in British English please, and assume your reader knows Strawson but has definately not seen the film.
