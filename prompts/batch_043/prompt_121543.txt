I'm writing a position paper on distributive ethics and want my own commitments audited before I commit to them in print — an audit of the package, not a survey of the literature and not a reading list. Here is where I stand:

(1) Prioritarianism: an outcome's value is the sum, over persons, of a strictly increasing and strictly concave function f of each person's welfare across their whole life. Nothing else counts — no relational or equality term.

(2) The levelling-down objection is decisive against the view that equality is intrinsically valuable in outcomes. What I find decisive in it is a general thought: that a distribution instantiates a pattern is not the kind of thing that can make an outcome better, and a change that is better for no one is not an improvement in any respect. I take this to forbid sacrificing anyone's welfare for the sake of a pattern.

(3) I evaluate prospects ex post: the value of an option is the expectation, under (1), of the value of the outcomes it might produce. I reject the ex ante alternative that applies f to each person's expected welfare, because that seems to me to discount the claims of whoever actually ends up badly off merely because their bad outcome was improbable.

(4) This gives me a verdict I'm confident about and that the ex ante view cannot reach: of two patients with the same expected welfare, one facing a fifty-fifty prospect of ending up at 0 or at 20 and the other certain of 10, I give the drug to the first.

(5) I also accept ex ante Pareto: if one option gives every person a prospect with at least as high an expected welfare, and at least one person a higher one, then the first option is not the worse one.

(6) And I want the numbers to count: I reject any view on which an arbitrarily small gain to the worst off could outweigh an arbitrarily large loss to everyone else.

Three objections I've had from readers, which I suspect are very uneven in quality: (a) that using expectations in (3) already makes me an ex ante prioritarian; (b) that (1) and (2) are unstable together, since I plainly do treat inequality as pro tanto bad — I accept that a pure transfer from a better-off to a worse-off person improves the outcome, other things equal — and that is just equality-worship under another name; (c) that my ex post evaluation reintroduces, across possible outcomes rather than across persons, exactly the pattern-worship I reject in (2), and will sometimes have me sacrifice expected welfare to avoid an inequality that never actually obtains. I think (a) and (b) have no force and I don't really understand (c).

My provisional patch for whatever does turn out to be broken is a two-parameter view: apply f to expected welfare as the ex ante theorist does, then subtract λ times some measure of the inequality in people's realised prospects — a separate risk-equality term, with λ a parameter I'd then have to defend.

So: which of (1)–(6) genuinely pull against each other, ranked by how severe the conflict is; which of (a)–(c) are real and which dissolve on inspection; and for each real conflict, what exactly has to give and what giving it up would cost me? I'm attached to all six, so give me the cost of each way out rather than just telling me which one to drop. And is the two-parameter patch a coherent third position or the two views stapled together — and does it escape the problems you identify?
