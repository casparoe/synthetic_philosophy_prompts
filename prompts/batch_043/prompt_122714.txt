I spent most of last night arguing with a friend who teaches law about how fast it became disgraceful, in universities, newsrooms and literary magazines, to pass machine-generated text off as your own. Her position: nothing new happened morally. The arguments against it were just the old arguments about plagiarism and authorship, and the norm only became real once detection tools and institutional policy caught up — argument first, stigma after. Mine: those arguments were available and unchanged for years while people cheerfully used spellcheckers, translation aids and ghostwriters, and then the stigma arrived within about eighteen months, which is the signature of a change in what you can do without losing face rather than of anyone being refuted.

We tried to settle it with the Williams/Appiah thought that large moral shifts are driven by changes in honor and social structure rather than by the force of argument, and stalled on two objections she raised: the missing peer group, and the missing immorality. There is no code of gentlemen here whose self-respect is at stake, only institutions enforcing a rule; and it isn't clear that what changed is a moral verdict at all, rather than a judgment about authenticity or a way of protecting the value of a certain kind of paid work.

So I'd like the counterfactual version. Take Bernard Williams, who died before any of this existed, and his view that such revolutions are not won by argument and that those who lose them have not been shown to be irrational. What would he have made of this case, and of my friend's distinction between a stigma that arrives before the arguments and one that arrives after them? Would he have counted it as a moral revolution on the model of dueling or footbinding, as an honor shift confined to a professional class, as interest wearing moral clothing, or as something his framework lacks the vocabulary for? No ventriloquism and no biography: I want the view his actual commitments push him toward, and where those commitments run out, an honest account of what he would have had to give up or add.

Finish with a single line: \answer{X}, where X is one of argument-first, honor-first, interest-first, not-a-revolution.
