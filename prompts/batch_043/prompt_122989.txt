Setup: I'm trying to work out what Adam Smith would actually have said about a modern arrangement that has no real analogue in his century. Don't give me a bibliography or readings — I'm after the reasoning, and I know you can't look things up.

The case (I made it up, though it's stitched together from real things like export processing zones, special economic zones, and investment arbitration — I've changed the details on purpose):

Meridia is a lower-middle-income democracy of about 30 million people. It signs a 99-year deal with a foreign semiconductor company. Terms, roughly:

- The company gets an exclusive lease on a 200 square km coastal zone, renewable, and no other chip firm can be licensed inside the country for the life of the deal.
- Inside the zone the company runs its own port, its own power generation, and its own commercial arbitration system; Meridian courts need the company's permission to enter, and Meridian labour and environmental law mostly doesn't apply inside the fence.
- Zero corporate tax for 25 years, then 5%. No duties on inputs or outputs.
- A local-content rule: at least 30% of non-managerial staff must be Meridian, paid at least 1.4x the national manufacturing average.
- The company pays $200m a year into a government "skills fund" whose board is half-appointed by the company.
- The chips are sold by the zone to another subsidiary of the same company at a price the company picks, so most of the reported profit lands in a low-tax jurisdiction elsewhere. Meridia's own tax take is close to nothing.
- The company's home government has offered Meridia a preferential trade deal if the zone stays as is, and has hinted it will "review" the deal if Meridia renegotiates.
- The Meridian opposition is campaigning to renegotiate or cancel. The company says it will move to a neighbouring country.

Now, my very rough sense of the Smithian materials, which you should feel free to correct: he attacked the mercantile system as a set of privileges granted to particular merchants at the public's expense; he thought the colonies were a bad bargain for ordinary Britons even if they were great for certain traders and for people looking for offices and contracts; he was generally suspicious of joint-stock companies and thought they only worked well in a handful of narrow cases; he thought empires got defended by people with a concrete interest in them rather than for the nation's good; and he thought there was something important about the difference between a bargain two parties actually consented to and one imposed by force.

What I want from you:
