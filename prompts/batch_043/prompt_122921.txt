I sit on the panel for a small regional arts grant, and our eligibility rules permit us to fund "works of art" but not "decorative, functional, documentary, or promotional objects." That distinction turns out to do all of the work and none of the explaining, and since the panel has to publish a short statement of what it counts, I am trying to work out what I actually think rather than just splitting the difference case by case. I have read a bit about the usual families of answer — resemblance to acknowledged artworks, conferral by an artworld, some historical or narrative relation to earlier art, the maker's intentions, the invitation to a certain kind of attention — but I cannot tell which combination of them my own verdicts commit me to. So let me just report the verdicts I find I have, and ask what principle, if any, they add up to. Four hundred identical factory-glazed mugs bought from a surplus supplier and stacked in a grid on a gallery floor under a wall label: art, though dull and derivative. A set of transit wayfinding signs commissioned by a city and designed with real elegance by someone who never thinks of herself as an artist: not art, just very good design. A six-year-old's crayon drawing of her family: art, and bad art, but art. A painting made by a zoo chimpanzee and auctioned for charity: not art — and I know that verdict sits badly with the last one, since neither maker has any art-historical intentions, and I cannot point to the difference except that one of them is a child and one is not. A technically flawless forgery of a lost painting, made to deceive and sold under the original painter's name: a work of art and a fraud, with the one not cancelling the other — its defect is not a defect in it as art. A man who sits silently in a shopfront for eight hours, livestreamed, to raise money for a children's hospital, and who says plainly that this is a fundraiser and not art: not art; the same eight hours of sitting, staged in a gallery under a title, would be art. A found piece of driftwood that an artist puts on a plinth in a museum: art. The same kind of driftwood on a hiker's mantelpiece, untitled and unshown: not art. And here is the one I keep going back and forth on: that hiker's driftwood, found in her house after she died and installed by a curator with a wall text she never wrote and would probably have found silly — I have no stable verdict.

What I would like from you is this. Give me a candidate principle, or a small set of them, stated precisely enough that I could put it in the panel's statement, and tell me which of my cases motivates each clause. Then tell me what the principle says about cases I have not put on this list — I am thinking of things like the readymade that every account of this has to handle, a cathedral, a composer's transcription of a bird's song, and an image produced by a text-to-image system after two hundred hours of someone writing and refining prompts — and be honest about which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate and would have me overturn. If the honest answer is that no single principle gets all of them and that I have to choose between a more complex account that saves every verdict and a simpler one that gives up one or two, say which verdicts are the cheapest to lose and why. I would rather give up a verdict than publish a sentence that merely relocates the problem into a word like "artistic."
