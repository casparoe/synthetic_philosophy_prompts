You are a political philosopher who works on democratic theory and the law of democracy: franchise design, the boundary problem, and the epistemology of collective judgment. I want a consistency audit of a position I have arrived at, and I want you to work in two stages. First, brainstorm: write out the candidate principles that could each generate most of the package below, and be explicit about which of those principles are rivals rather than variants of one another. Only then, second, run the audit against the package. Please reason carefully and step by step, resist the urge to affirm the package as a whole before you have checked what each item commits me to, and keep your answer under about 900 words. No citations, no reading suggestions, no name-dropping of the literature in place of argument: I want the mechanics.

I sit on a national electoral-law reform commission, and the position below is roughly where I have landed after two years of hearings. It is meant to be defensible to the people we would exclude, not merely convenient.

1. Inclusion: anyone whose significant interests are substantially affected by a decision has a pro tanto claim to a say in making it. I mean this as the real ground of democratic inclusion, not as a slogan. Exclusions have to be justified to the excluded.

2. Lower the voting age to 16. The argument I actually find persuasive is longitudinal stake: a 16-year-old will live with the climate, debt, pension, and institutional consequences of this year's decisions for longer than any currently enfranchised cohort, so on my own inclusion principle their claim is among the strongest in the polity. I am unmoved by maturity objections, and I accept that many 15-year-olds are better informed than many 40-year-olds; that does not strike me as a reason to move the line to 15 or to assess anyone individually. One person, one vote: no weighting votes by stake or age, no proxy votes for those below the line. I am also untroubled that we would be legally compelling 16-year-olds to show up while still treating them as minors for contracts, alcohol, and jury service; I do not think consistency requires one uniform age of majority.

3. Oppose a domicile-based national franchise. Long-term lawful residents who are not citizens should not vote in national elections, even after many years of residence and taxpaying: formally undertaken membership, i.e. citizenship, is the right bright line at that level. But I do support giving them the vote in municipal and regional elections, where what is decided is the provision of local services and the regulation of daily life.

4. Compel turnout. Attendance required by law, sanctioned by a modest fine, roughly the Australian model, with the ballot secret and a formally counted none-of-the-above option that triggers a fresh election if it wins a majority. I support this for two reasons I hold separately: (a) voluntary turnout is socially skewed, and the skew predictably bends policy toward the already advantaged; (b) a large, broadly representative electorate arrives at better collective judgments than a self-selected one, in something like the way jury-theorem and diversity-of-perspectives results about group judgment suggest.

5. No competence, literacy, or civic-knowledge test for the franchise, ever. Such tests are exercises of power by whoever drafts and administers them, there is no neutral version of one, and they have been used to disenfranchise the poor and racial minorities.

6. Voting is at bottom a public office or trust rather than a personal right. The demos is entitled to constitute itself, and someone excluded has no rights-based complaint so long as the scheme of inclusion can be defended by appeal to the common good.

7. Two case verdicts I am confident about: people with advanced dementia should remain on the electoral roll and may vote with assistance if they wish, and a person who declares total indifference to politics is never, for that reason alone, to be struck off.

What I want from you: which pairs are genuinely in tension, ranked by severity rather than listed flatly; for each real tension, exactly what I would have to revise or abandon and what that revision costs me elsewhere in the package, since a fix that saves one commitment by wrecking two is worse than its alternative; which apparent tensions dissolve under scrutiny and why; whether any of my items are simply orthogonal, so that the friction I half-suspect is noise; and finally the single revision that does the most repair work, together with whether what survives is a package a theorist could defend in print or only one that sounds coherent in a hearing.

Please do not tell me which of these views is correct, and do not assume that because I listed something last or flagged it as one I am confident about I have thereby marked it as the weak spot. I genuinely do not know where the fault line is; if I did, I would have fixed the paper myself.
