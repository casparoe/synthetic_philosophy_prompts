I'm in the last year of a PhD in behavioural ecology, writing the synthesis chapter of a thesis on cooperation, and I've realised I don't actually have a specification for what I want out of a theory in this area — only a set of half-articulated demands that my committee keeps invoking inconsistently. So before I write the chapter I want the desiderata stated properly: what any adequate theory of the evolution of cooperation has to deliver, with the case or consideration behind each requirement. The purpose I care about is diagnostic rather than merely narrative. My empirical chapters are on a set of field systems (a cooperative breeder with high extra-group dispersal, and an interspecific mutualism where the benefit to the recipient arrives months after the actor has paid the cost and often accrues to a different individual than the one who was helped). What I need from a theory is the ability to license — or to refuse to license — an inference from field data to a mechanism: given helping rates, relatedness estimates, interaction structure and the timing of costs and benefits in a natural population, which mechanism is operating, and what would I have to measure to tell? That is my standard of adequacy, and I'd like the list built for it, not for the standard of "explains why cooperation exists somewhere" or "is elegant in a model."

Here is the material I think the criteria have to come out of, roughly in the order it started bothering me. (i) One-shot anonymous encounters: humans in the lab cooperate well above zero in a single anonymous prisoner's dilemma with no future and no reputation, and I don't know whether that is data for a theory of cooperation or noise generated by a psychology built for repeated life. (ii) The unknown horizon: in every real system the continuation probability is not known to the players, only to me, so a strategy that needs the players to know the shadow of the future is asking for something nature doesn't provide. (iii) Misimplementation: in my mutualism the helping act fails independently of the helper's effort maybe a fifth of the time, so a partner cannot condition on realised benefit — and relatedly, benefits are decoupled from costs across individuals, which breaks the feedback loop that partner-fidelity accounts lean on. (iv) No recognition: in the cooperative breeder I cannot find any evidence that helpers discriminate among the adults they assist, yet helping is strongly assortative with respect to something. (v) Large groups: dyadic reciprocity machinery does not obviously scale to the n-player public-goods structure that most of the interesting cases have. (vi) Private, noisy assessment: in my own simulations, once assessment of reputations is private rather than public, the plain image-scoring style of norm stops sustaining cooperation, and the more demanding norms that survive require cognitive and informational machinery I would be embarrassed to attribute to my study animals. (vii) Second-order free riding: any account that stabilises cooperation by punishment has to explain why the punishers aren't undercut. (viii) Payoff non-transparency: the players don't observe each other's payoffs, only their own, plus noisy cues. (ix) The definitional case: some of what gets called 'cooperation' is mutualism or a byproduct of self-interested behaviour, where there is no puzzle at all, and I want a theory to be able to say when the puzzle dissolves rather than to explain a case that never needed explaining. (x) The bookkeeping constraint: as far as I can tell, every mechanism works by arranging either genetic relatedness or a fitness feedback from the recipient's benefit back to the actor, so a candidate that seems to produce cooperation from random interaction without either is probably hiding assortment in its assumptions. I take (x) to be a consistency constraint rather than a desideratum, but I want you to tell me if I'm wrong about that.

What I'd like from you, then, is the list: the requirements, which cases or considerations motivate each, and — this is the part I expect to be most useful — which of the things people in this literature demand are genuine desiderata and which are prejudices of one camp (for instance, is it a real requirement that a theory specify an explicit decision rule and cue set, or is that just what the reciprocity people are used to building? is demanding that a single mechanism cover all species a requirement or a taste for unification? is identifiability from observational data a fair demand, or does it just penalise theories that are permissive about mechanism?). Then flag any subset you think cannot be jointly satisfied — I suspect something like "explain cooperation in genuinely one-shot, anonymous, non-kin interactions" plus "be stable against invasion by unconditional defectors" plus "invite no assortment" is inconsistent, and I want to know which is the right one to drop and what dropping it costs me — and say which single requirement does the most work in knocking out candidates, since that is the one my chapter should be organised around. Only after that, and briefly, would I like a scorecard: direct reciprocity, indirect reciprocity, kin selection and inclusive fitness, partner choice and biological markets, byproduct mutualism, and multilevel selection, each in a few lines against the list. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on the scoring; I can do the scoring myself, and my committee will argue about it anyway.
