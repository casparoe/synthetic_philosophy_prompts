I am preparing a prospectus for a nine-month master's thesis of roughly fifteen thousand words on Berkeley's master argument, and I need the prospectus document itself to run about twelve hundred words. My half-formed idea is that the argument is best understood as what I will call a diagonal argument: it belongs to the family whose attempted counterexample supplies its own verification, and the clearest other member of that family is Descartes's cogito. But the cogito is normally taken to establish something about the thinker and the act of thinking rather than about the world beyond the thinker. If the master argument has the same shape, it is not obvious how it is supposed to reach a conclusion about trees. I want the prospectus to press on that gap rather than on the familiar dispute about whether the imaginative exercise Berkeley describes really is impossible.

Please write the prospectus so that it contains each of the following.

First, a precise statement of the question. The thesis is to concede, for the sake of argument and in its strongest form, Berkeley's phenomenological claim: any attempt to represent a tree as existing while unperceived is self-defeating in the way he describes. The question is then what, if anything, follows from that concession. Spell out what kind of bridge principle would be needed to move from a claim about our representational capacities to a claim about what can exist, distinguish the plausible candidates from one another, and explain how the choice among them determines the force of the argument. Say explicitly why the standard reply that one can after all conceive of an unperceived tree is being set aside, and what is gained by conceding Berkeley his premise instead of contesting it.

Second, an account of why the existing positions leave the question open. Both Berkeley's defenders and his critics have concentrated on the act and its description; the bridge has largely gone unexamined, and it is not settled whether stating a bridge principle requires assuming that the objects of thought are ideas.

Third, the method, and a justification of it over the obvious alternatives. The proposal is to use the comparison with the cogito, and with any other arguments that share the diagonal structure, as a tool for determining what conclusions that structure can bear. Say why this is preferable to another close reading of the relevant passage in the Principles, and why it is preferable to intervening directly in the contemporary literature on conceivability and possibility. Include the two test cases that I think the project must handle: Berkeley's doctrine that we have notions but not ideas of spirits, together with his acceptance of other finite spirits that exist unperceived by any creature, and his appeal to divine perception to handle objects no human being is observing. Each raises the question whether the argument is being quietly restricted, and whether the restriction is principled or question-begging.

Fourth, the range of possible conclusions and what would decide between them. The candidate conclusions I have in mind are that the argument supports only a claim about the conditions under which objects can be represented; that it supports immaterialism about objects of sense specifically; and that it generalises into a claim about all objects of thought which Berkeley himself must reject. Identify the considerations that would discriminate among these, including what each would imply about the work the argument was doing in Berkeley's overall case for idealism.

Fifth, honest failure conditions and a realistic scope. State what would make the project collapse into exegesis or into a restatement of the secondary debate, what would make it too large for fifteen thousand words or nine months, and what a minimal but respectable result would look like if the central question cannot be settled.

Sixth, a short paragraph on why the question matters beyond Berkeley scholarship, in particular what the project would show about how much transcendental or conceivability-style arguments from the conditions of representation can establish.

Do not include a bibliography and do not cite secondary literature; refer to positions and arguments by description, and I will supply the references myself.
