So I'm writing a novel — a retired logic tutor, her ex-student who keeps badgering her to be *shown* that modus ponens is valid, and she eventually just refuses to play, says it's a skill like riding a bike and skills aren't the sort of thing you can demand a justification for. I wrote out the argument she'd give if she were being honest with herself and now I think it proves way too much, but I can't find the bad step:

(1) Whatever justification is, it can't just be a matter of having more true beliefs on hand: for any premises and any conclusion you like, you can accept all the premises, accept the conditional that links them to the conclusion, and still not budge. So the thing that turns a pile of accepted propositions into an inference isn't another proposition.
(2) So the missing ingredient isn't truth-evaluable at all — it's a rule, a practice, a disposition to move a certain way given certain inputs.
(3) A rule can't be justified by citing further rules without restarting exactly the same regress, so our grip on the basic ones has to bottom out in something we simply do, not something we've derived.
(4) If it bottoms out in what we simply do, then nothing internal to the practice marks modus ponens as correct and, say, affirming the consequent as incorrect; "correct" can only mean "ours."
(5) By the same reasoning, a community whose basic practice licensed moving straight from any premise to its own negation, or who just skipped the middle step and never felt the need for one, would be reasoning exactly as well as we are — calling them irrational would be pure parochialism, like mocking someone's table manners.
(6) That's nuts. Some inferences really are better than others, and I want my tutor to be able to tell the student *something* true in the last chapter.

Which step do I junk? I'd much rather keep (1) and (2) than (5), and I'd like to keep (6) no matter what. Also tell me what the bad step was doing that made it look innocent — whether it's smuggling in a slide I can't see or just a premise that costs less than I think — and what each alternative would cost me: in particular, is there a version where the tutor stays silent and is still in the right, or does rejecting any of these force her to actually give the student an argument after all? Under 400 words, please, and don't spend half of them telling me about Wittgenstein.
