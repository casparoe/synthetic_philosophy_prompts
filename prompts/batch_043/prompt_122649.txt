me and my roommate got into this at 1am and I still can't tell whether his argument is clever or just broken. He says the utility monster objection is basically a cheat. Here it is.

Setup: call a being's *yield* the welfare it gets from one more unit of resources, and its *ceiling* the total welfare it can hold at once before it's full.

(1) Maximizing total welfare is a marginal business: each next unit of resources goes to whoever gets the most welfare out of it.
(2) A "cheap saint" — say something that gets a million welfare units out of a single crumb and is then completely full and never wants anything again — beats any starving human on the margin. So by (1) the crumb goes to the saint and the human goes hungry.
(3) Nobody I've asked finds the cheap saint horrifying. It's efficient, it's cheap, and it doesn't come back for seconds. So a high yield on its own can't be what's objectionable about the monster.
(4) What's objectionable is that the monster never fills up. It just keeps converting, so there's never a point where the rest of us get a turn.
(5) But nothing about human beings fills up either. For any human welfare level you pick, we can imagine that same human better off with more resources, so there's no ceiling on us either.
(6) So the feature that's supposed to make the monster a monster is one we share, and the objection either collapses into resentment at its efficiency or indicts every one of us equally.
(7) Either way the total utilitarian has been handed no reason at all to give up her view.

I want to hang on to (4) — the "it never gets full" bit feels like the entire objection to me — and I also want to keep that moving small resources from the well-off to someone who's much worse off is *required*, not just nice.
