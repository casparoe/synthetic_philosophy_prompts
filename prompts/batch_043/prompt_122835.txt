I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about whether there is really anything that all pleasures have in common. She holds the broadly attitudinal line: to be pleased is to be in a state with a certain directedness toward its own continuation, and the apparent variety of pleasant experiences is variety in their objects, not in the pleasantness. I kept pushing the cases that I think break that: the pleasure of scratching an itch, of getting out of a hot shower into cold air, of a punchline landing, of relief when a pain stops, of absorption in work you have to be dragged away from and that you report as miserable in the moment but gladly repeat, of a sad film you would describe as pleasant overall, of finishing a chore where the enjoyment seems to exist only in retrospect. Her reply was that these are a motley and that I have mistaken the craving for a single feeling-tone for a real requirement, so that the heterogeneity of pleasures should just be conceded and the theory of welfare rebuilt on something pluralist. I do not want to concede that, and I came away with a half-formed idea I cannot get into shape on my own.

The idea: stop looking for the shared property inside the experience and look for it in the experience's second-order dynamics. Roughly, the suggestion is that pleasures share not a qualitative core and not an attitude, but a characteristic way of responding to being aimed at — the old observation that pleasure tends to slip away when it is pursued directly, that it decays under repetition and under self-monitoring, and is often best reached by attending to something else. So the proposal would be to treat pleasantness as a matter of a state's counterfactual profile under attention and repetition rather than its intrinsic character at an instant, and then ask whether that gives a unified account that is still recognizably hedonist or whether it secretly converts hedonism into a view on which what is good for you is extrinsic and relational. There is also the test I think kills or saves it: a creature or a possible agent whose enjoyments are not in the least poisoned by being aimed at. If we would still call those pleasures, the dynamic account fails as a necessary condition; if the case feels like a case where the word breaks down, that is evidence for it.

What I need from you is the proposal document itself, for a 6,000-word seminar paper due at the end of one semester. Give me: the question stated precisely enough that it has a determinate answer rather than an invitation to muse; why the main existing strategies — the felt-quality view, the attitude-based view, and the quietist or family-resemblance and disjunctivist retreats — leave that question open, without turning this into a literature review and with no citations, since I will add those myself; the approach, as a section-by-section plan with a word budget and a sense of what I would actually do in weeks two, five, and nine, including how I would build and use a battery of hard cases like the ones above and what would count as a case the account mishandles; an argument for why this approach rather than the obvious alternatives, in particular rather than simply going pluralist about welfare and dropping the unity claim; what a positive result would look like and what would count as the project failing, where I take the most likely failure to be that the account either collapses into the attitudinal view it was meant to replace or else unifies pleasures only by making pleasantness extrinsic, in which case I have not defended hedonism but abandoned it; and an honest note on whether the question is too big for a semester and how you would cut it down. Close with a short paragraph on where the proposal's reasoning is most likely to break and what argument or case would persuade you that the project is not worth doing, since I would rather hear that now than in week twelve.
