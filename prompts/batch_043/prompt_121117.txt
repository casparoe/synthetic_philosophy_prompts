I teach a 75-minute seminar for eighteen upper-level students in a philosophy of economics module. They have already seen the standard revealed-preference apparatus: the weak and generalized axioms, the claim that a utility representation is pinned down only up to increasing transformation, and the advertising pitch that this lets economics talk about preference without positing unobservable mental states. What they have not felt is the discomfort underneath it: that if preference is *defined* as whatever choice reveals, "she maximizes utility" threatens to become a relabeling of the data rather than a claim about it, and that the word "utility" may end up meaning three different things in three different mouths.

I want an in-class activity — rules, materials, roles, a timeline in minutes, and a debrief — that makes them run into this themselves rather than hear it from me. Constraints: no real money (course credit or a small prize like a chocolate bar is fine); materials limited to index cards, chips or slips of paper, and a projector; nothing that requires laptops for the students.

The design brief I have in mind, which you should feel free to improve or restructure:

Roughly half the room are Choosers. Each gets a private card specifying a payoff function over integer bundles of two goods, and accumulates points across rounds that convert to the prize. The goods must be denotationally empty — units of A and units of B, not chocolate versus fruit — because the whole point collapses if the Observers can help themselves to assumptions about what people probably want. The other half are Observers working in pairs, each pair responsible for two or three named Choosers, seeing only the budget lines announced and the bundles chosen, never the cards.

The activity should open with Choosers facing a sequence of budget lines — some nested, some crossing, so that some pairs of chosen bundles are mutually comparable and some are not — and Observers fitting a ranking as they go. Then it should turn on them, in three stages:

First, an out-of-sample test. Announce a new budget line, have each Observer pair commit to a prediction, and score the predictions against a explicit baseline predictor: just guess the bundle that Chooser picked on the most similar previous budget. The comparison is what gives operational content to the question of whether the recovered preference carried any information beyond the data it was fitted to.

Second, non-identification made visible. I want two Choosers to be given cards that are genuinely different — different numbers, different marginal rates of substitution in the cardinal sense — but that provably generate identical choices on every budget line you are going to show. Let the Observers discover that nothing they could ever see distinguishes the two.

Third, menu-dependence and the belief-utility tangle. Run two Choosers with the same card and the same budget but different presentations of the option set — an added option that no one would pick, or a re-described default — and let the choices come apart. Then add a round of choices over acts: payoffs contingent on a draw from an urn whose composition the Chooser cannot inspect. Observers should be asked to state what the Chooser believes and how risk-averse she is, and should find that they can trade one against the other indefinitely: an apparently cautious pattern is equally compatible with pessimistic belief plus near-risk-neutrality and with optimistic belief plus sharp diminishing returns. Nothing in the choice data separates them.

I also want one Chooser, planted without the Observers' knowledge, whose card instructs them to pick a bundle that is strictly worse for them on their own card, consistently. When the Observers meet that data they will have to decide whether to report an exotic preference or a mistake, and I want the activity to make vivid that the data cannot settle it.

The debrief should do four things. It should map what happened onto the structure of the theory: what was gained by taking choice as primitive, and where exactly the empirical content of "utility maximization" came from — the consistency axioms, plus out-of-sample prediction, not the definition itself. It should name what is not identified: cardinal magnitudes, belief versus risk attitude, and the causal contribution of framing. It should lay out the three readings of "utility" that the session has been circling — a bookkeeping device for representing choice, a real psychological magnitude that choice measures with error, and a currency of well-being — and show that each reading tells a different story about the decoy round and about the planted error-maker, so that the disagreement is not one that more choice data could settle. And it should end on the open question rather than closing it: whether revealed preference theory tells us what utility *is*, or only what we are allowed to say about it.

Please also include an honest note on what will predictably go wrong the first time this runs — Choosers failing to optimize their own cards, Observers smuggling in substantive assumptions about the goods, someone spotting the decoy and deliberately choosing around it, the scoring round eating more time than it should — with a concrete handling for each, and say which choice you made about Chooser error and what that choice costs you. Finally, be explicit about what the activity structurally cannot deliver: state plainly which parts of the formal result, and which part of the philosophical dispute, the facilitator will simply have to supply in words afterwards, and why no arrangement of rules could produce them.
