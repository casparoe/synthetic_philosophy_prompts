There is a familiar standoff about computational attribution: one side holds that any ordinary physical system, a wall included, can be described as implementing arbitrarily many computations, so that no single computation is the one the system implements; the other replies that a computation is genuinely implemented only when its states and transitions map onto the causal organization that explains what the system does. Suppose we adopt the second view as a filter on moral status: among all the computations a system implements, the one that settles whether it is sentient, and how much it matters, is the one figuring in the best causal explanation of its behavior — call this the causal-explanatory filter. My worry is that the filter leaves the choice of behavior to be explained up to us: ask why the system writhes, tends the injured limb, and learns to avoid the lever, and the best explanation cites a negatively valenced pain-like state; ask why it emits the sentence reporting pain, or why the heat exchanger keeps the room at twenty degrees, and the best explanation invokes something far thinner, or nothing at all that a sentience attribution could grip. Please explain what is going on here, and specifically: (a) whether this makes sentience objectionably interest-relative in a way that moral status cannot tolerate, or whether it is a benign relativism of explanation that leaves the underlying fact fixed; (b) how one might single out the privileged class of behavior non-arbitrarily, without presupposing the very conclusion that the system is sentient; and (c) whether switching to a non-computational criterion — a particular neural realization, or a specified causal mechanism, say — escapes the difficulty or merely relocates it to the choice of which mechanism to count as the real one. I am after the conceptual mechanics rather than a survey of named positions.
