I work as a process operator at a plant that makes flavourings for food manufacturers. I've been on the line for eleven years; I came up through the shop floor. Most of the people I report to came in through the graduate scheme and have some kind of engineering qualification. I'm the only person on my shift with neither, and I'm also the only woman.

Last spring I started noticing that a particular product was going slightly off-spec — nothing dramatic, a faint bitterness that came and went — and I thought it tracked with the change we'd made to the cleaning cycle on one of the holding tanks. I couldn't explain a mechanism; I only had the pattern, and I didn't have words for it beyond "it seems to happen after the flush." I raised it once in a handover meeting, and I hedged badly, because I knew I couldn't back it up: I said something like "this might be nothing, but I've got a feeling about tank three." It was treated as an anecdote. Six weeks later a new graduate said essentially the same thing, in the language of residence times and residuals, and it was taken seriously on the spot. Then a customer complained, an investigation was opened, and the cleaning interval was changed. The fix that got adopted was the thing I'd been trying to point at.

What I can't settle is not the original brush-off. It's what's happened since. My notes are now formally part of the process — I'm asked to log anything unusual, and my supervisor has said more than once that I've "got good eyes" — but nobody asks me what I think it means. At the last review I suggested the same logic might apply to a second line, and I was told, kindly, that I should keep logging and leave the diagnosis to the engineers. That stung more than being ignored did, and I don't think the sting is just vanity. It feels as though I'm being used as an instrument rather than as someone who is trying to tell them something, and that being treated that way is a different injury from simply having lost an argument, or from being annoyed that credit went elsewhere.

I've been turning this over on my own, and I'd rather have someone take it seriously than be told I'm overthinking it. Some specific things I'm stuck on:

Does it make a difference that I turned out to be right? If my hunch had been nonsense, would there still have been anything to object to, or would it just be a case of people reasonably trusting the qualified person over me?

How much of their scepticism was legitimate? They were right that I couldn't produce a mechanism, and there is a real sense in which the engineers know more than I do. I don't want to claim I was entitled to be believed outright.

I also suspect part of the problem was mine: I didn't have the vocabulary for what I'd noticed, so I offered it as a feeling, which made it easy to file away as one. Is that a separate problem from the fact that they didn't listen, and is it anybody's fault, or just bad luck about which concepts happen to be lying around?

One of the other operators, a woman who came up the same way I did, also didn't take me seriously at the time. I keep going back and forth on whether that shows this was never about who I am, or whether it shows something worse.

And practically: the process is fixed and nothing is at stake now. Is there anything I should actually ask for, or say to my supervisor, or is raising it now just making a grievance out of nothing? If it is worth raising, how do I do it without it landing as being about respect and status rather than about getting things right?
