Hi — I'm a retired plant physiologist, 35 years at a land-grant station, and lately I've been on our town land trust's stewardship committee and reading more philosophy than is good for me. We vote on habitat work about once a month: mowing schedules, invasive removal, a prescribed burn, a pond drawdown, a deer cull, a pollinator meadow. Two-hour evening meetings, volunteer board, no budget for studies, and the conservation commission has to sign off.

Here's where I keep getting stuck. Things I've run into on wild animal welfare seem to say habitat loss might actually be good, because most wild animals have short miserable lives and populations just fill whatever the habitat supports, so more habitat means more suffering rather than less. My own half-baked version of it, from the way I used to think about steady-state populations: at carrying capacity births roughly equal deaths per year, so the only durable way to shrink the annual death toll is to lower the birth rate; anything that just raises the death rate — culling, hard winters, starvation — adds suffering without narrowing the pipeline. I like it because it cashes out into something actionable (prefer fewer breeding sites to more, prefer fertility control to killing), but I don't trust it and I can't see where it breaks.

So: write me a decision procedure my committee can actually run on a habitat proposal in about twenty minutes, keeping whatever is defensible in that line of thought and tossing whatever isn't. I want the questions in order; what counts as a good enough answer to each given we can't fund a study; which questions are empirical and which are values calls the board has to own rather than hide inside the science; where the procedure stops with a no; and where it says "we can't know this" instead of pretending. Say plainly which of my distinctions survive contact with the philosophy and which are just my lab habits.

Then run it on three cases with the shape of real ones we've had: (1) drawing down a stormwater pond that's the main breeding site for a frog population with near-total tadpole die-off; (2) clearing a large stand of invasive honeysuckle that has become the winter food for resident birds and the cover for rabbits; (3) converting eight acres of mown lawn to pollinator meadow, which everyone on the board instinctively wants to do. I suspect the meadow one comes out worse than we hope — tell me honestly if it does, and why.

Give the procedure as numbered steps in prose. Then put the three cases in a table at the end, one row per case, with these columns: case | first step where the procedure binds | key unknown we can't settle in twenty minutes | values call, if any | recommendation | confidence (low/medium/high) | what single new fact would most change the answer.
