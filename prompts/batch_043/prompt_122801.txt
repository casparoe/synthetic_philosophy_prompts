I teach a six-week evening class called Puzzles, Paradoxes and Arguments at a community adult-ed centre — 16 people, one 90-minute session, mostly retirees, two programmers, one guy who did a philosophy degree in the 80s, and almost nobody with any formal logic. I want to give a whole session to that MIU puzzle from Godel Escher Bach (start from MI, four rules, can you get to MU) and to the idea Hofstadter builds on top of it: that some questions about a system can only be settled by stepping outside it and reasoning about it, never by working inside it.

I want an activity, not a talk. They should spend real time banging their heads against the puzzle from the inside, and the jump out should happen because the setup forces them into it, not because I announce it. Three things I want them to walk out with: (1) "I couldn't find it" and "it can't be done" are different claims that need completely different kinds of support; (2) you can prove something about infinitely many possible derivations with a finite argument, by finding a property that every rule preserves; (3) that argument lives outside the system and cannot be turned into a fifth rule. No lecture up front, and they shouldn't hear the phrase "jumping out of the system" or Hofstadter's name until the last five minutes.

A few half-ideas I don't know how to make work:

- A sibling system as a contrast: same alphabet, same axiom, same rules 1, 2 and 4, except the third rule replaces II with U instead of III with U. There MU falls out in two steps. I want it to land as a gut-punch right around the moment people start saying "it can't be done" about the real one, so they can feel that the two situations are indistinguishable from the inside. I also have a hunch that in the sibling system some other short string is the unreachable one — MIII, or something with three Is? — but I havent checked properly, so if that's wrong give me a better one, and tell me what the obstruction actually is there.
- I'd like the level-shift to be something they physically do. My idea was three tables with signs — INSIDE, BESIDE, ABOVE — where you may only say a sentence at the table matching what kind of sentence it is: INSIDE for strings and derivations, BESIDE for statements about particular strings with numbers attached, ABOVE for claims about every theorem. Plus team roles that enforce it: whoever is writing strings is banned from the words "all", "never" and "impossible". Tell me if that's overengineered for 16 adults who've been at work all day, and what to cut.
- A payoff scheme with no money in it that doesn't reward grinding — chocolate, stickers, a leaderboard, whatever. The scoring should make it visible that producing the fortieth string is worth nothing and noticing one property is worth everything, but they mustn't be able to see that coming at minute five or they'll skip the grind. Maybe reveal the scoring halfway through?

Deliverable: materials list, the rules exactly as I should print them on the handout (this matters — I don't want the wording to leak the answer), a minute-by-minute for the 90 including a hard stop on the grinding phase, the role/table mechanic, the payoff scheme, the exact sentence or two I say at each transition, and an overflow task for the two programmers who will crack it in fifteen minutes and then get bored and start reading their phones.

Then the debrief, mapped moment by moment onto the parts of the idea: where the invariant is supposed to surface, how to get them to see that it's a property no rule mentions and that it's a property of numbers rather than of strings, how to draw out the three-part shape of the proof (true of the axiom, preserved by every rule, false of the target) and the point that a finite argument has settled an infinite question. Then what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. I can already see three: illegal moves (applying the III rule to Is that aren't adjacent, or doubling some random middle chunk), people announcing "it's impossible" when they mean "I'm tired", and the philosophy-degree guy shouting "it's the number of Is mod three" in the first ten minutes. Tell me how to handle those, plus the two or three I haven't thought of.

Last thing, and please be blunt: what does the activity fail to show? My worry is that it makes jumping out look easy and always available, when the cases that matter are the ones where you jump out and there's nothing to land on, or where the system is strong enough to talk about itself. I'd rather say that out loud at the end than have them leave with a general recipe. And tell me which bits I simply have to supply in words, because no arrangement of rules and payoffs will produce them.
