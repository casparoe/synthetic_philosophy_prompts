I work as a backend engineer — no formal philosophy, and my only economics was a single undergraduate elective years ago, mostly forgotten — and I've been lurking in a work-adjacent group chat where a few colleagues trade small amounts of crypto and argue about prediction markets. Last week one of them, call her Priya, said a token was trading well below what she thought it was worth and that she was buying more. Another member, Marcus, replied with a line I've now heard him use three or four times in different contexts: "if it's such a bargain, someone would have taken it already." He clearly thought this settled the matter — that the mere fact that the price sits where it does, and that somebody is willing to sell to her at that price, is by itself a reason to think she's wrong, or at least that she has no grounds for thinking she's right. Priya pointed out that her counterparty on that particular venue was an automated market maker, a constant-product pool that will quote a price to literally anyone, in any size, while holding no opinions or information at all. Marcus answered that this only shows the pool is being arbitraged by someone sharper than either of them. They went in circles, and I genuinely could not tell whether they were disagreeing about economics, about probability, or about what the sentence itself meant.

So I want a disambiguation rather than a verdict on the token. Please lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise claims that one-liner could be making. What I'd like separated, at a minimum: readings about how fast prices adjust (someone would have gotten there first, so there's nothing left); readings about which information is already impounded in the price (all information, all public information, all information that matters, only information that is cheap to act on); readings where the evidentiary weight comes not from the price but from the fact of a specific counterparty's willingness to trade with you — the used-car / winner's-curse style thought that being offered a deal is itself evidence against the deal being good; readings that lean on a no-trade style result, roughly the shape of: if two parties began with the same priors and each knew the other was willing to trade, neither could expect to gain from it, so any actual trade has to be explained by asymmetric information, by divergent priors, or by non-informational motives like liquidity needs, hedging, risk tolerance, or fees; and finally readings strong enough to imply that no one can systematically beat a market at all. For each reading, state it as precisely as you can in one or two sentences, then give it a verdict — near-tautological, substantive and defensible, substantive and contested, or simply false — and, the part I care most about, say whether that reading, if true, would actually do the work Marcus wanted: not merely that Priya is probably wrong, but that she has no reason to think she's right, and whether it survives her automated-market-maker case. Where a reading's force depends on magnitude rather than existence — how fast the adjustment, how much information, how severe the adverse selection — please say so instead of treating it as yes-or-no. Flag any two readings that cannot both be true, and tell me which reading you suspect Marcus actually had in mind and whether it's the strongest one available to him.

A numbered list with a one-line verdict on each entry is ideal. Please don't give me citations, bibliographies, or further reading — I'm offline and I only want the conceptual map. Close with a one-sentence summary line beginning "Verdict:" and, as the very last line of your answer, a machine-readable line of the form \answer{...} naming the number of the single reading from your list that best supports Marcus's dismissal, or NONE if none of them does.
