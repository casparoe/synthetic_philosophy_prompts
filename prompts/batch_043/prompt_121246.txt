I am giving a job-market talk in three weeks at a philosophy department, and I need a Q&A prep sheet written against the specific room I will be facing rather than against the people I usually argue with. The talk is titled "Induction After No-Free-Lunch." Here is the position.

**The setup.** I distinguish two things that I claim the literature runs together: a *bias* (an ordering over hypotheses — a prior, a simplicity preference, an architecture) and a *budget* (a measure over the problem instances, or continuations, that you take yourself to face).

**Lemma 1 (the sequence form of no-free-lunch, which I state and prove on the board).** Take any finite binary history and any learner whatsoever. If the continuation is drawn uniformly at random, so that every future bit is an independent fair coin, then the learner's expected accuracy over any finite block of future bits is exactly one half. No learner beats chance on a uniform budget.

**Lemma 2 (the "duality" I defend, and which I expect to be contested).** Bias and budget are not two posits but one. Every claim of the form "bias B is a good bias" is equivalent to a claim of the form "B does well on budget μ," and every budget can in turn be re-expressed as a bias. So the traditional problem of induction — justify our preference among hypotheses — is restatable without loss as: justify the budget.

**The positive claim.** The budget cannot be justified a priori; that, I argue, is the real content of the no-free-lunch results and the genuine residue of Hume. But it can be checked a posteriori, at a second order: we can ask whether the budget on which our bias has been paying off is one we should keep, and revise it when the track record degrades. I call this second-order inductivism, and I argue it is not viciously circular because the second-order check is a claim of a different kind — a claim about classes of problems, not about the next instance. To stop the resulting regress (a second-order check needs a third-order budget, and so on), I propose stopping at a fixed point: a budget that is its own best vindication, which I call a self-certifying budget. I gesture at the universal mixture over computable measures familiar from algorithmic information theory as a candidate, since it dominates every computable budget up to a multiplicative constant, and therefore does roughly as well as any computable budget one could have chosen. I already know the cost and say so in the talk: the constant depends on the complexity of the competing budget, so the comfort is asymptotic only. I also appeal to description length (Kolmogorov complexity) to break ties between green-type and grue-type hypotheses: grue-style predicates are projectible only under more expensive encodings.

**The room.** About twenty-five people. A formal epistemologist who is a thoroughgoing Bayesian and thinks the problem of induction was dissolved rather than solved: priors are exogenously given, conditionalization does the rest, and merging-of-opinions results give you long-run convergence provided only that you were not dogmatic about the truth — so, on their view, I am manufacturing a problem by demanding that a prior be *justified*. An early modernist who works on Hume and thinks the real problem concerns the standing of induction as a matter of reason rather than custom, and who will suspect that my second-order check is just custom relocated one level up. A philosopher of science with historicist leanings who is a pluralist about inductive practice, thinks a domain-general bias is a philosopher's fantasy, and will hear my budget as a research tradition with a number attached to it. One philosopher of machine learning, plus a visiting ML researcher, who regard no-free-lunch as a triviality about the uniform measure and think real learning is explained by structure — locality, compositionality, symmetry, and the like — and who are liable to say that I am re-deriving what practitioners already know, slowly. A metaphysician who will raise Goodman's grue puzzle and ask whether my framework has any resources the old projectibility debate lacked. And several generalists who will want to know whether any of this changes what anyone should do on Monday morning.

**What I want from you.** A prep sheet, not a defense of my view and not a summary of it. Please use the following sections, in this order:

1. **The line I cannot give up** — about 100 words. The single claim such that, if I concede it, the talk is over, stated in one sentence and then glossed.
2. **Objections, in the order they will come** — about 1,000 to 1,200 words. Seven or eight objections, ordered by how likely they are to be asked multiplied by how much damage they would do. For each: name who raises it; give the objection in that person's own idiom, two or three sentences, as they would actually say it (the machine learning people should sound like machine learning people and the Humean like a Humean); assign a damage rating; give me **What I say**, a reply of 100 to 120 words that I could deliver in about two minutes; and give me **Where it is soft**, one or two sentences on the weakest joint in that reply. At least two of the objections should have real force against me — do not let me win the room. Do not include objections from audiences who will not be there.
3. **Concessions to make before anyone asks** — about 150 words. What I should give away in the first five minutes to buy credibility with each of these groups.
4. **The reply that has to land** — about 200 words. The one exchange that decides whether the project survives, with the exact form of words I should aim for and the diagnostic sign that I have lost it.

6. **The soft spot in my own setup** — about 150 words. The premise in my own argument I should prepare to defend rather than hope the room misses.

Total 1,700 to 2,000 words. Do not pad with objections from audiences I am not facing, and do not give me answers that work only by citing literature I could look up: everything in the replies has to be sayable from the board, using the lemmas and distinctions above.
