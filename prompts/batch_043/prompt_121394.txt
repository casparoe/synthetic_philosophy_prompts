**The question (25 marks; indicative weights: (a) 6, (b) 6, (c) 7, (d) 6)**

**The Batch Case.** A retailer sells a meat product in single-unit packages; one package contains the meat of exactly one animal, with no by-products and no waste. Stock arrives in pallets of 500 units. The supplier raises and slaughters animals only in batches of 500, one batch per pallet ordered. The retailer's system automatically orders a new pallet — and so causes a new batch of 500 animals to be raised — if and only if all 500 units of the current pallet are sold. Assume away all complicating causal pathways: no price responses, no resale or substitution (a unit Amy does not buy is bought by no one else), no effects on the retailer's longer-run ordering policy, no effects on other buyers' choices, and no effects of any kind other than on the number of animals raised. Demand is uncertain: in some cycles a pallet sells out, in some it never does. The store displays an accurate live counter showing how many units of the current pallet have been sold so far.

Amy is in the store. The counter reads 213. She buys one package. Assume that her purchase leaves the behaviour of all other buyers unchanged.

(a) Set out the inefficacy (or "no-difference") objection as it applies to Amy's purchase. Be explicit about its premises, and in particular about what else must be assumed for the objection to yield the conclusion that Amy does nothing wrong. Distinguish clearly between actual and expected difference-making. (6)

(b) Two replies to the objection are defined as follows.

- *The Expected-Difference Reply*: even if Amy's purchase makes no actual difference, there is, relative to her evidence, a positive probability that it does, and expected difference-making is what matters for the assessment of her action.
- *The Complicity Reply*: an act can be wrong in virtue of being one participant's contribution to a collectively wrongful practice, whether or not it makes any difference to outcomes.

Explain what each reply has to establish, and what kind of reason each offers. One common way of blurring the two is to read the Complicity Reply as a causal thesis about influencing other people through signalling or social norms; explain why that is not what the reply says. (6)

(c) Apply the two replies to Amy. In particular: does what Amy can see on the counter settle whether her purchase makes a difference? What would have to be true of the other buyers for her purchase to make a difference to whether a new batch is produced? Does the Expected-Difference Reply require Amy to be the five-hundredth buyer? What work, if any, is done by the fact that the counter is visible to her? (7)

(d) A fellow student offers the following argument: each batch of 500 animals is produced by exactly 500 purchases, so each purchase accounts for exactly one animal; hence the inefficacy objection fails, and every purchaser makes a one-animal difference. Assess this argument, and say what it shows about whether the lumpiness of production strengthens or weakens the inefficacy objection. (6)

**The student's answer**

*(a)* The inefficacy objection holds that Amy's purchase causes no additional animal to be raised. Production responds to aggregate demand in lumpy batches: a new batch is produced only when the current pallet sells out, and one package more or less cannot by itself push a pallet over that threshold. Amy buys the 214th unit, so whether or not she buys, the number of batches ordered is the same. So her act makes no difference to how many animals are raised.

The objection needs one further premise to reach a moral conclusion: that an act can be wrong only if it makes a difference to outcomes, or only if it makes someone worse off. That premise is the load-bearing one, and it is exactly what a non-consequentialist will reject; the objection as usually stated simply helps itself to it. Note also that the argument is not peculiar to consumption. It has the same structure as the argument against voting, where a single ballot never changes the result. Many people treat that generalisation as a reason to reject the objection rather than to accept it, since as stated it appears to let everyone off the hook simultaneously.

Finally, actual and expected difference-making must be kept apart. An act makes an actual difference when, given how things in fact are, the outcome would have been worse had the agent acted otherwise. It makes an expected difference when, relative to the agent's evidence, the probability-weighted difference it makes is positive. An act can be expected-difference-making while making no actual difference, as when you buy a ticket in a large lottery you lose.

*(b)* The Expected-Difference Reply has to establish that Amy's evidence leaves open a positive probability that her purchase is the one that tips the pallet over the threshold, so that the expected number of animals raised is greater given her purchase than given her abstention. It offers a forward-looking, evidence-relative reason: the language is that of expected value, and the reason is the same in kind as any other reason of expected utility.

The Complicity Reply has to establish something quite different: that Amy's relation to the practice — she is a participant in, and a beneficiary of, an arrangement that raises and slaughters animals for food — is itself morally significant, irrespective of what her act brings about. It offers a reason that is not a function of outcomes at all; the wrong, if there is one, lies in the relation between the agent and the practice rather than in the upshot.

The two replies are therefore independent, and they are answers to different questions. The Expected-Difference Reply concedes that difference-making is what matters and argues that it is present, in expectation. The Complicity Reply concedes that it may be absent altogether and argues that it was never required. The signalling reading collapses the second into the first: if the point were that Amy's purchase changes other people's behaviour, the reply would simply be the Expected-Difference Reply with a longer and more speculative causal chain, and it would inherit that reply's vulnerability — namely that the expected effect of one purchase on others may be negligible. The whole point of the Complicity Reply is to sever wrongness from difference-making, so it cannot be a difference-making thesis in disguise.

*(c)* Here the counter settles the matter. Amy can see that she is the 214th buyer and not the 500th. The trigger fires on the 500th unit, and nothing she does changes who occupies that position: 286 further people will buy after her whether or not she buys now. Her purchase therefore makes no actual difference. And because she knows this, it makes no expected difference either — the Expected-Difference Reply is unavailable to an agent who knows where she stands relative to the threshold, since there is no uncertainty left for the probability to attach to. The visibility of the counter is thus the crucial feature of the case, and it is what distinguishes the Batch Case from the voting case, where no voter knows whether she will be pivotal. It follows that Amy must rely on the Complicity Reply if she is to have any reason at all not to buy.

*(d)* The fellow student's argument is sound, and it disposes of the residual worry left by part (c). Each batch of 500 animals is produced by exactly 500 purchases, so the arithmetic is inescapable: each purchase accounts for one animal. Once the total harm is divided by the number of acts that jointly produce it, each act turns out to make a one-animal difference after all, and the inefficacy objection fails. This is why lumpiness looks threatening but is not. The batch structure spreads the causal contribution across the 500 purchases rather than concentrating it in the 500th, and the average contribution is the real measure of what each purchase does. So Amy's purchase makes a difference of one animal. The Expected-Difference Reply is the same point in probabilistic dress: each purchase carries an expected one-animal difference, and that is simply the average made sensitive to what the agent knows.
