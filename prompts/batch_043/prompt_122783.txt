so im not any kind of academic. I ended up being the person in my tenants union who reads the housing authoritys paperwork and explains it at meetings, and ive been chewing on one thing for a while and nobody i know can help me with it.

Our city has a single waitlist for subsidized apartments, and they rank everyone on it with a number from 0 to 100 that they call a need score. Higher means you get housed sooner. The score is built out of things like how many different addresses youve had in three years, whether youve had a utility shutoff, eviction filings, whether theres someone over 65 in the household, and so on. It does not ask your race, your religion, or what country you were born in, and the authority is genuinely proud of that; at every meeting someone says the computer doesnt know what colour anybody is.

But then, other things about the same setup:

- The software company publishes a report showing the score predicts who actually ends up homeless about equally well within every group they checked, and the authority points at that as the proof the thing is fair.
- There are carve-outs. Veterans get a fixed number of the apartments off the top. Survivors of domestic violence skip the line. Theres a separate list for people already in a shelter.
- If you get skipped over and you think its wrong you can appeal, and at the appeal they have to give you a reason about you. They cant say the computer said so, and they cant say people in your situation usually do badly. But when we ask them to defend the score in public, all we ever get is group averages.
- They also insist the score is not a decision, its just a ranking. The number of apartments is fixed by the budget, so who gets one is settled by where you sit in the order, but they talk as if ordering people and deciding who gets housed are two seperate steps.

Heres what I keep going round on. Why do they think group membership is fine in one place and not in another? They wont let the model look at it directly, but they will hand out apartments by group, and they do check their accuracy group by group. So they clearly dont think it never matters. Whats the actual rule theyre using, in their own terms?

Three things I made up while thinking about it, in case theyre useful:

1. I call it borrowed accuracy. My score goes up if ive moved house a lot, and it goes up partly because other people who moved house a lot ended up homeless. So the number is telling me something about myself using facts about other people who resemble me on paper. It just comes in the back door instead of the front. Does the setup secretly assume this is ok? And if it does, is that different in kind from just asking someones race, or only different in how visible it is?

2. Everything in the score is something that leaves a paper trail. Someone whose life is a mess in ways nobody ever wrote down scores low, and everybody at our meetings has met that person. It feels like the practice assumes being easy to read is worth something, not exactly that you deserve it, but that its fair for it to pay off. Is that a commitment the practice really has, or just an accident of what data exists?

3. I proposed a fix I call lottery at the margin: wherever the scores error bars overlap, stop pretending and flip a coin instead of acting on a two point difference. People pushed back in opposite directions. Half said obviously fairer. The other half said spending a home on a coin flip insults the person who loses. What does that split tell me about what the current practice assumes?

Last thing. Somebody told me there is a maths result here, and as i understand it, it says you cant have all of these at once: that the same score means the same real chance of the bad outcome no matter what group youre in; that the rate of wrongly calling someone urgent is the same across groups; that the rate of wrongly missing someone is the same across groups. Not unless the groups really are the same in the relevant way, or the score is perfect. Our authority seems to want all three of those plus the colourblindness. So: is the practice committed to something impossible, or is one of those four not something it actually needs?

What I want from you is this. What does this whole arrangement presuppose about what fairness even is? Not the marketing version, but what somebody would have to believe for all these moves to make sense together, and which rival ideas of fairness the setup quietly rules out. Tell me which parts theyre strictly stuck with and which are just habits that happen to fit the theory, so several theories would let them keep it. And tell me concretely what would have to change if we swapped the implicit theory for a different one, say the view that the point is to get the apartments to whoever is worst off, accuracy be damned.

Please just argue it out. Dont send me looking for papers or books, i cant get to a university library and I want the reasoning, not a reading list.
