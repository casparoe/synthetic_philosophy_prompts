so im in a poetry club at school and were doing this joint open mic thing with the physics club (yes, really) and i somehow got stuck with the "science poem" slot. I want to do Maxwell's demon but I really dont want it to be the version everyones already heard — demon opens the door, demon cheats the second law, haha, moving on.

Heres the angle ive been chewing on with my physics teacher: the demon isn't actually failing at anything. It sorts perfectly. Every cycle it pulls out kT ln 2 of work for every bit of information it gets about which side of the box the molecule is on. What its really doing is moving entropy out of the gas and into its notebook — each measurement writes a bit into a blank spot, and a blank notebook is itself this weird low-entropy resource, kind of like unburnt fuel. So the demon is, like, solvent right up until it runs out of pages. It only breaks even once it has to wipe the notebook clean to start over, because erasing a bit at temperature T costs you kT ln 2 dumped back as heat. The demon ends up being a perfect bookkeeper who discovers it gained exactly nothing. Also please dont do the old "measuring is what costs you" version — I'm told thats the outdated answer, and that the real split is between the cost of getting information and the cost of erasing it, and erasing is the one that matters.

Form: I want a sestina — six end words that rotate around and then all six come back in the three-line bit at the end. Make the form do the work: the same six words grinding back around is the demon running the same cycle over and over, and the way they come back in a different order each time is the notebook filling up. No big dramatic turn in the middle, let it accumulate, and then the last three lines are where the bill comes due. End words something like door, side, bit, blank, heat, cost — swap them if that scans better. Write it in the demon's voice, first person, and make it sound like somebody who is extremely good at their job and not evil or confused or triumphant. Plain enough language that it reads out loud fine to a room thats half physics kids and half people who showed up for the free cookies, and keep it to the usual 39 lines.

Two last things. First, commit: pick one reading of whats going on and stick to it, and make the choices you make on purpose instead of hedging. Then straight after the poem give me two or three sentences of plain prose saying which reading you committed to and where the poem fudges the physics or leans on something people genuinely argue about — and no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables anywhere, just continuous prose like your writing me an email.
