I'm a graduate student in comparative history, and last month I got into an argument at a seminar that I won on rhetoric and lost on reflection. The position I defended, and still half-hold, is the modernist one in the spirit of Hobsbawm's invented-tradition volume and Anderson's print-capitalism account: that the thing we now call a nation — a mass political identity binding a whole population to a supposed people with a supposed deep past, and making claims on that basis — is essentially a creature of the last two centuries, manufactured out of standardized vernacular print, mass schooling, conscription, censuses, maps, museums and invented pageantry, and that what existed earlier were dynastic subjects, parishes, estates, confessions and localities, plus a thin elite literature about peoples that ordinary people neither read nor acted on. My medievalist colleague produced case after case of apparently premodern national feeling, and I noticed that I could absorb every single one of them on the spot: continuity of a name became retrospective myth-making, popular religion became a vehicle later nationalists captured, a revolt became proto-national only in the historians' telling. That worried me, so I want the exposure map rather than another round of point-scoring: what would actually count against this position, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and — importantly — which of those defeaters a committed modernist would concede as counting against them rather than explaining away?

To keep this from being a standoff about vague words, here is the apparatus I've been toying with, and you should feel free to dismantle it as part of your answer. I want to split the claim into three grades: a reference claim (premodern people had terms picking out roughly the group the modern nation claims as its ancestor), a salience claim (that grouping ranked high in their practical reasoning — above village, lord, guild, confession, dynasty — often enough to matter), and a causal-work claim (appeals to it reliably extracted sacrifice: taxes, militia service, rebellion, exile). I want to know which of the three the modernist is really committed to denying, because I suspect most of the debate trades on sliding between them. I also want a fourth, non-standard test added to the set, since I haven't seen it used: a counterfactual fragility test. If a nation is an artifact of state scaffolding, then sustained removal of that scaffolding — no standardized schooling, no mass-circulation print in the vernacular, no conscription, no census categories — should cause the identity to dissolve or become inert within a generation or two; if instead it reliably reconstitutes itself from below, that is evidence of a substrate. Tell me whether that test discriminates anything, what historical situations approximate it well enough to be informative, and what result would be genuinely surprising on my view. Then take two hypothetical findings and tell me whether each is decisive, awkward, or absorbable by a qualification: first, that large-scale digitization of pre-1800 vernacular texts — sermons, court depositions, letters, ballads, wills — shows ethnonyms appearing almost exclusively in Latin, in elite texts, and in the third person, with self-description running to parish, valley, lord and sect; second, that a pre-1800 revolt is documented among a largely illiterate peasantry with no exposure to a standardized vernacular press, whose manifesto nonetheless appeals explicitly to a named people with a claimed territory and history. Finally, there is an identity-conditions problem I can't get straight: for a nation to be *old* rather than merely made of old materials, how much can change — language, religion, dynasty, borders, the very name — before we're dealing with a different entity that merely inherits the earlier one's prestige? At what point does "this nation is medieval" become true only by courtesy of a naming convention?

Please also give me an honest verdict on the insulation problem I flagged: has this position been qualified into unfalsifiability, and in particular is the move "apparent continuity is itself an invention, or an elite appropriation of something that meant something else" a legitimate inference or an all-purpose escape hatch? If it is an escape hatch, say whether that is actually damaging for a claim of this shape — compare, say, the difference between a falsifiable empirical thesis about origins and a framework that tells you what to look for — and tell me what a defender should be willing to stake out as a prediction that a rival view does not make. Throughout, separate what is settled among people who actually work on this (I take it that states really did standardize languages, stage traditions, count and school their populations) from what is genuinely contested, and don't present the modernist consensus as firmer than it is or the ethnosymbolist alternative, associated with Anthony Smith's work on premodern ethnic communities, as refuted. Where you are extrapolating beyond what you can actually support — especially on the historical cases you'd name as the strongest test — say so explicitly, and tell me which single case, if I went and read up on it properly, would do the most work in either direction. Conclude with your own best guess at how the three grades of claim come out, and at how confident I should be, given that my seminar performance was much more confident than my actual evidence.
