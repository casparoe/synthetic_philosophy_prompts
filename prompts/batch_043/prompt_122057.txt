I write a column on science and ideas — mostly psych and cognitive science, but every so often I get pulled into ethics — and I'm stuck on a piece about sexual consent. The thing I keep running into is that almost everyone arguing about it, on all sides, secretly treats consent as a kind of contract: a competent adult, informed of the material facts and not under duress, transfers a right they otherwise hold, and what undoes the transfer is fraud or coercion. The campus people use it to argue for explicit affirmative-yes policies with a revocation clause; the lawyers use it to say deception invalidates only when it goes to something material (they have that old distinction between lying about what the act is and lying about what you get out of it); and the more libertarian types use it to say that if you were competent and nobody put a gun to your head, you consented, end of sentence. I want to audit that move rather than just repeat it. So: lay out the mapping explicitly — a table of correspondences is fine — what plays the role of offer, acceptance, consideration, capacity, the fraud and duress clauses, breach, remedy, third-party enforcement. Then tell me where the mapping breaks, and in particular which disanalogies do real work for the questions I actually care about and which are just decoration.

The cases I keep circling are these. Someone who sleeps with a partner after lying about an STI, or about being single, or about having had a vasectomy: does the contract frame give the right answer about which of those, if any, invalidates, or does its materiality test quietly import an arm's-length, buyer-beware picture of a transaction that has no business here? Someone who says yes not because of any threat but because saying no would have detonated the relationship, or the evening, or their housing situation: the contract frame's duress clause seems at once too narrow and too blunt for this, and I can't tell whether thats a bug in the analogy or a bug in my case. And on competence: someone who was blackout drunk but initiated and seemed enthusiastic; a seventeen-year-old who actively sought out a much older partner; someone with a mild intellectual disability who genuinely wants this and is capable of refusing plenty of other things. The contract frame gives me one clean capacity threshold and I suspect sex needs something messier, but messy thresholds are hell to write into law or into a campus code.

Two more things. First, a philosopher I interviewed for the piece pushed what she called the cheap-exit condition: a yes counts only if there was a live, socially cheap way for the person to stop things at any point and they knew it, so a yes extracted in a situation where exiting would have cost them the relationship, the ride home, or their reputation isn't really a yes even with no threat on the table. I half think this is just the duress clause done properly and half think it quietly turns nearly every yes into a maybe, and I'd like you to say which. Second, if the contract is a bad picture, what's better? I've been toying with improv — a jointly improvised activity like a jazz set or a climbing belay, where the point is mutual responsiveness, either person can call a stop, and nothing is settled in advance — but I can also see that importing a picture where the activity is cooperative and the value lives in the process might be smuggling in a substantive view about what sex is for, which is exactly what's under dispute. Please actually come down on this rather than giving me the both-sides tour: your verdict on whether the contract analogy should be kept, demoted to a heuristic for some sub-questions, or dropped, and on what you think the right answers are on deception, coerced-but-not-threatened yeses, and capacity. And flag clearly where your verdict is resting on a contested moral or empirical claim rather than on something the analogy settles — I want to know which bits I'd have to defend in the comments section.
