I run a two-hour workshop, once, for about 18 people — mostly public policy masters students, a handful of philosophy grad students, and usually two or three actual civil servants who sit in and quietly change the temperature of the room. It is a standalone session in a module on measuring wellbeing. No prep, no homework, no laptops if I can help it, printed handouts fine. It has to survive being run with 10 people or with 30, and it absolutely cannot depend on anyone in the room knowing anything about the real-world international happiness league tables or any actual country's data. Assume half of them have never thought about this for more than a minute.

What I want them to feel in their hands, before anyone names it, is the gap between a country topping a happiness ranking and its citizens being happy. And specifically the difference between two complaints that people collapse constantly: that the index is a noisy or gameable proxy for a real quantity (a measurement problem, fixable in principle, throw more data and better questions at it and the gap shrinks), versus the complaint that there is no such quantity sitting behind the procedure for it to be a proxy for, so that which country is really happier has no answer the index is slowly converging on. I want them to run into the second one by discovering, on their own, that they can move their score without moving a single life.

So: design me a game. Small teams are wellbeing ministries of invented countries, each with a printed population profile — and I want at least one subgroup in each profile that is badly off and structurally easy to make invisible. They get a limited annual survey budget and a pack of policy cards, and they are ranked against each other on an index they can see the formula for. The formula should be visible and simple enough that a team can sit down and work out what moves their number, but with enough moving parts that optimising it is not the same as helping anybody.

Things I would like built into the rules rather than said out loud:

- Two separate data feeds about their own population — something like a life-evaluation feed (the ladder-style question, answered once a year) and an experience feed (mood sampled at random moments). The ranking counts only one of them. Policy cards should split: some genuinely improve the experience feed but barely touch the evaluated one, some raise reported evaluation mainly by changing what people compare themselves to or what they think a good life is supposed to look like. Teams get forced to spend scarce budget on one.
- A sampling frame with exclusions that look boring and administrative — hospitals, prisons, care homes, people without a fixed address, anyone who does not answer three contact attempts. Someone will notice that a policy which moves miserable people into those categories raises the national score. I want them to notice it, not be told it exists.
- A compositional lever: miserable residents can be encouraged to leave, or cheerful ones recruited. Average goes up, nobody is better off, world total flat or worse.
- Reference drift: the scale respondents use recalibrates as circumstances change, so a country can improve everyone's life and see its number sit still, or raise the number by lowering expectations. If you can build in the nasty structural consequence — that if responses partly track relative standing, not everyone can climb at once — please do, that is the bit I most want them to feel.
- A time structure that rewards the wrong horizon, e.g. a rolling multi-year average plus a short electoral cycle, so harvesting credit for a predecessor's investment and dumping costs past the window is available as a strategy.
- A round where the previous winner gets to propose one amendment to the international methodology. I really like that mechanic, because it makes the point that topping the ranking comes with the power to redefine what topping it means, but do whatever version of it actually works.

Debrief: map three or four specific moments in play onto specific parts of the problem, including where the proxy-failure reading and the there-is-nothing-there reading come apart, and where the normative question — given a perfectly measured quantity, should a government spend money raising it rather than anything else — is a separate question again. Then, honestly: what will go wrong. I already expect one person to start meta-gaming in the first five minutes and spoil the discovery for everyone else, the civil servants to drag in real examples immediately, and at least one team to refuse the ugly card and lose because of it, which I think is fine but I am not sure how to handle without either rewarding cynicism or making the refusers feel naive. Also tell me which feature of this the game just cannot produce and I will have to say in so many words — my guess is something about whether wellbeing is the kind of thing that admits a single interpersonal scale at all, but you tell me.
