I teach an upper-level philosophy seminar on rational choice, and I am writing the final in-class exam, which is ninety minutes long. I need one essay question on the Death in Damascus case that cannot be answered by simply reciting the difference between evidential and causal decision theory. Please draft the question, a model answer of about seven hundred words, and a marking scheme worth twenty marks.

Use the following self-contained version of the case; the question must not require students to know anything beyond it. An agent is in Damascus tonight and Death is coming for her. She may either stay in Damascus or travel to Aleppo. Death is an infallible predictor of what she will do: his location already matches whichever option she will in fact choose, so if she stays he is in Damascus and if she goes he is in Aleppo. She cannot causally influence his prediction; her act is merely a perfect indicator of his location. Before she decides, let p be her subjective probability that she will end up staying; because Death is infallible, her probability that Death is in Damascus is also p. The utilities are as follows. If she stays and Death is in Damascus, she dies in Damascus, utility zero. If she stays and Death is in Aleppo, she survives in Damascus, utility ten. If she goes and Death is in Damascus, she survives in Aleppo but bears the cost of the journey, utility nine. If she goes and Death is in Aleppo, she dies in Aleppo after a wasted journey, utility minus one.

The question must require students to do three things. First, compute what evidential decision theory and causal decision theory each recommend, using conditional and counterfactual probabilities respectively, and show that on this setup both theories in fact converge on the same pure act, so the familiar disagreement from Newcomb's problem does not simply reappear. Second, introduce the phenomenon of decision instability: explain why, for each pure act, becoming confident that one will perform that act makes the alternative act look better, so that no pure act is ratifiable, where an act counts as ratifiable only if it still maximizes expected utility conditional on the agent's believing she will perform it. Say what this does to the evidential theorist's slogan that one should do what one would most want to learn one will do. Third, ask whether a randomized or mixed strategy—deliberately settling the question by a chance device that Death cannot predict, such as a genuinely chancy quantum process—can be a rational response, and what it would show about decision theory if the only stable response is to make one's action unpredictable to the predictor.

The model answer should state the decision matrix, compute the expected utilities for evidential and causal decision theory, explain the mechanism of instability by tracing how the agent's credence p that she will stay drives the probability that Death is in Damascus, show that no pure act is ratifiable, and assess the mixed-strategy proposal. The assessment should note both the appeal of the mixed strategy—it can raise the chance of survival from zero to one half and yield a higher overall expected utility than any pure act—and the conceptual difficulty, for example whether decision theory can genuinely recommend a policy of randomization rather than an act, or whether recommending randomization amounts to advising the agent to make herself less than fully rational. The marking scheme should divide the twenty marks into named must-hit points, state how many marks each is worth, and give a one-line note on how candidates typically get each point wrong. It should separate an A from a B answer by requiring the A answer to articulate the precise mechanism of instability—the updating of the probability that Death is in Damascus as the agent's credence in staying changes—and to say something substantive about the mixed-strategy response, whereas a B answer may correctly compute the two theories' verdicts and gesture at instability without tracing the mechanism or evaluating randomization.

Do not ask students for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and do not refer to any real author, title, or text in the question itself; the setup above is the only background needed. Write the exam question, the model answer, and the marking scheme.
