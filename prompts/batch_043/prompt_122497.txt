I run a small reading group where I work — mostly engineers, no one with formal philosophy training — and we take it in turns to pick a novel and write the one-page handout that goes out before we meet. My turn came round and I chose Vonnegut's *Slaughterhouse-Five*, which I have now read three times, and I have got myself into an argument I cannot settle, partly because I suspect I am using the vocabulary wrong.

Separately, and for my own amusement, I have been reading popular accounts of the arrow of time: the puzzle that the underlying dynamics seem indifferent to which way time runs while everything at our scale is not; the answer that the asymmetry is statistical and bottom-out in entropy, so that the direction is supposed to be inherited from a very low-entropy early state of the universe rather than from the laws themselves; and the further move that memory, records, and our sense of the past as fixed and the future as open get their direction from that gradient rather than supplying it themselves. I would like to bring the two together, and I would like the honest version of the result rather than the flattering one.

Four things, specifically.

First, our group is running together three claims I think are distinct: that time is reversed, that its order is scrambled, and that every moment is equally real. Billy's experience looks to me like a scramble, and a scramble is not a reversal, and neither of them is the same as the claim about equal reality — you can hold every moment to be equally real and still have a universe with an entropy gradient laid across it. I would like that distinction policed, and I would like to be told which of the three, if any, the novel actually commits itself to rather than merely toys with.

Second, my own attempt at a philosophical contribution, which I suspect is too quick and want taken apart at the exact step where it fails. Billy has, apparently, veridical memories of events that lie later in the entropy order than the remembering — his own death among them. If the standard story is that traces and records point in the direction of increasing entropy, then Billy carries records pointing both ways, and there is no fact of the matter about which direction his psychological arrow runs in; that would make him a local counterexample to reducing the psychological arrow to the thermodynamic one. Is the fault that the novel gives us no reason to treat those memories as veridical, or is the inference from having records to the arrow's being undecided already broken?

Third, the scene where Billy turns on the television alone and watches a war film running backwards: bombers flying backwards over Germany, bombs gathered back into the bays, the wounded and the dead restored, peacetime and boyhood arrived at in reverse. Read as physics it is nonsense; read as a wish it is devastating. Is the scene exploiting the same intuition that makes any reversed film look absurd, or is it quietly substituting a moral arrow for a thermodynamic one, where what needs undoing is death and guilt rather than heat?

Fourth, the fatalism. Billy does not warn anyone about Dresden, and the novel's refrain about what has happened having happened reads to several of us as a metaphysical thesis. I have a suspicion this is exactly the conflation people make — that if all times are equally real then nothing anyone does makes a difference — and I want to know whether the novel makes that inference, or hands it to Billy as a way of living with himself and lets us judge it.

And the part I expect a careful reader to insist on: Billy is an unreliable narrator with untreated trauma, the Tralfamadorians may be a delusion with a planet attached, and the frame is about a novelist unable to write about a firebombing. If the metaphysical reading is a category error and the novel's real subject is grief and the ethics of narrating it, say so — and say what, if anything, the arrow lens still buys me for the meeting.

Please give me no citations, reading lists, or pointers to papers: I have no library access and would rather have the argument than the bibliography. Correct my terms where they are wrong, but keep it in plain English. And do not reduce the novel to an illustration of a position — I want to know where the physics and the book pull against each other, and what, if anything, the book shows about time that the arguments do not.
