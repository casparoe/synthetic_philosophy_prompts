I am a retired cell biologist who, two years into auditing a graduate seminar in ethics, has been asked to circulate a single page stating where I have landed on the question of moral worth — on what it is for an action to be done for the right reasons — so that the others can attack it. I would like the set checked for consistency before it goes round. In the order I arrived at it:

(1) An action has moral worth only if the agent acted for the reasons that make it right; an action can be entirely right and still have no worth if what moved the agent was something else.

(2) The best case is the agent who acts well straight off, from settled character, without deliberating, and who would be spoiled by stopping to ask why. Worth does not require the reason to be before the mind at the time of action, and there are cases where its being there would ruin the action.

(3) Whether an action is right is wholly independent of what moved the agent. Rightness and worth are different properties, and I want no traffic between them.

(4) A person is answerable only for what is within her control, and I do not think what moves her at a given moment is: we shape our motives at best indirectly, over years, by habit and by what we attend to. I doubt too that we know them — a sincere avowal of what moved one is weak evidence.

(5) Praise and blame ought to track moral worth; that, I take it, is what they are for.

(6) A device of my own, which I flag as mine rather than as anything I have read: call a reason *operative* if it is what actually moved the agent, and *standing* if it did not move her but would have done so had the operative one been absent. I hold that worth is fixed by an agent's whole stock of standing reasons, not by the operative one alone.

The convenor tells me (1) is the Kantian-sounding item and (2) the Aristotelian one; I would rather not trade on either name. I also suspect, without being able to say why, that (1) and (2) are the pair most under threat. Two cases I expect to be pressed with: the man who returns a wallet he could perfectly well keep, moved only by an aversion to being someone who keeps other people's property and with no thought at all of the owner's loss; and the woman who gives a large sum chiefly for the deduction and the public credit, but who would have given as much, and from pity, had neither been on offer.

Which of these are in genuine tension and which only look as though they are? Rank the real tensions by severity rather than treating them all alike; say for each what would have to give and what the cheapest repair would cost me; and tell me which single revision would clean up the most. Please argue rather than cite: I cannot chase down references, and I would not trust a name I had no way of checking.
