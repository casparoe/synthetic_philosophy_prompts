I'm retired now — I spent most of my working life in geophysical fluid dynamics, and a couple of years ago I started sitting in on a philosophy reading group at the local university mostly for something to do on Tuesdays, and somehow I've ended up loosing sleep over the platonism-versus-nominalism business in the philosophy of mathematics. I've drifted into a position of my own that I've started calling the attractor view (the group rolls their eyes at the name, which I take as a sign I might be onto something). Roughly: the platonist is right that '2 + 3 = 5' and 'there are infinitely many primes' are not up to us, but the reason isn't that there's a realm of mind-independent objects out there that we somehow perceive. It's that these claims are what I think of as fixed points: any practice at all that gets rich enough to model its own syntax — to encode its own sentences, run something like a successor operation, and check its own inferences — will land on them again, and there's no coherent alternative for it to land on instead. So basic arithmetic is forced, and it's forced cheaply: you don't need a heaven of objects, you just need the observation that nobody can build a rival. But here's the part I actually care about, and that I think makes it a different view rather than platonism in a cheap coat: the forcing stops. Set theory past a modest core, the big-cardinal principles, and the continuum-problem-style questions that set theorists argue about are not fixed points — you can build coherent practices on either side of them — so about that region I'm a nominalist or a formalist or whatever the word is. My friend who was a real mathematician says this is just a gerrymandered halfway house and that I've drawn the line exactly wherever it was convenient.

So: what would count against it? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to flag which ones a defender of my own view should concede as genuine and which ones I'm entitled to shrug off. Four lines I've been chewing on and can't sort out myself. (1) If the 'unavoidable' core turned out to depend on background choices I'm quietly treating as neutral — classical versus intuitionistic logic, say, or which notion of finiteness you start from — then my line isn't a feature of the territory at all, it's a feature of my own starting kit, and the view either inflates into full-blown platonism or deflates into the nominalist camp. (2) The applicability worry: real physics runs on analysis, not on arithmetic, and analysis sits up in the part of mathematics I've declared optional, so am I committed to saying that the applicability of the calculus is a brute fact, and is that fatal or just embarrassing? (3) If someone produced a workable, empirically successful theory built on a mathematics that contradicts standard arithmetic — a universe with finitely many positions where addition just wraps around, used seriously in an actual research programme rather than as a formal toy — I take it that's the end of me; but I want to know whether it's really the end of me or whether I get to say 'not a genuine rival practice' and slither out of it. (4) And the opposite pressure: if some question in the optional region turned out to matter for a real measurement — some genuinely independent set-theoretic principle that shifts a predicted number — does that push my line upward, and how many such cases before I should admit there was never a line there?

What would move me most personally, I think, is (2) and (4) together. I came out of a science where the mathematics always felt like it was doing honest work, and if the forced/optional split can't explain why the optional half is the half that gets used, I'd rather hear that now than keep polishing the name. Please don't just hand me a reading list or tell me who to go look up — I can't get to the library easily and I've had enough of that from the reading group. And be honest if the view is insulated by construction: if I've built it so that any counterexample can be waved off as 'not a real practice', say so, and then tell me whether that's a disaster for a view like this or just what a view like this is supposed to look like.
