I am a high-school student in Year 12, and along with two friends I go to a philosophy club at my school. We were revising in the library last week when my older brother, who is studying Descartes for his A-levels, came home and started teasing me about our elderly Labrador, Max. He said that Descartes argued animals are really nothing more than very complicated machines, and that he could prove this from the fact that animals cannot use language. Max whines, wags his tail and nudges us when he wants food or company, but he cannot put together words or signs to express thoughts about any subject; according to my brother, Descartes held that this shows animals have no reason and no real inner life at all, and that what looks to us like sadness or joy is only the working of their bodily organs, like the movements of a clockwork toy. To make the point vividly, my brother has been building a robot dog in his engineering club; it can whimper, roll over and nuzzle when touched, and he says that, if Descartes is right, the robot and Max differ only in how they were made, not in whether anything is going on inside them.

The difficulty is that Max is now quite ill, and my parents are discussing whether to have him put to sleep and replace him with my brother's robot dog, which they think will be easier to look after. My brother says that, if Descartes is right, neither Max nor the robot would really suffer anyway, so the change is morally harmless; the robot will behave in all the same ways and no one who took the Cartesian view seriously should mind. I feel sure that this is wrong, or at least that we owe something to Max that we do not owe to a machine, but I cannot put my finger on why; whenever I try to object, my brother says my discomfort is mere sentiment and that Descartes would tell me to be consistent and treat the robot and the dog in exactly the same way. This has made me unsure even about smaller things, such as whether I should bother to sit with Max and comfort him when he whimpers, if he has no real experiences and my kindness makes no difference to him.

Could you advise me? Is my brother's application of Descartes fair, or is he leaving something important out? More concretely, should I accept that there is no important moral difference between Max and a well-programmed robot, or is there a reason—perhaps one Descartes himself might accept, or perhaps one that shows his argument is mistaken—to treat the living dog differently? I am not looking for general reassurance about being kind to pets or for any reading suggestions; I want to understand what the underlying philosophical disagreement is and how it bears on what I should do. Please set out the reasoning clearly and, so far as possible, tell me where the argument really bites. Please answer in British English.
