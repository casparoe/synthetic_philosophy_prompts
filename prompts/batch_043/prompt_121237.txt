I run a small reading group, and last week we finished the skeptical argument in Kripke's book on rules and private language: the thought that for any rule I take myself to be following — say, addition — there is a deviant rival (the famous one coincides with addition on every sum anyone has in fact performed and diverges thereafter) that fits every past performance, every disposition, and every introspectible state equally well; so, the argument concludes, there is no fact of the matter which rule I meant, and the "skeptical solution" is to keep our practice of attributing meaning by recasting it in terms of assertibility conditions — roughly, I am entitled to say that you mean addition by "plus" when your answers agree with ours — while conceding that there is no meaning-fact those attributions track.

Two members, Marta and Owen, spent the rest of the evening arguing about how general that conclusion is, and neither would budge. I'd be grateful if you'd go through their exchange as actually made rather than declaring a winner from the outset. As I heard it, the exchange went like this.

(1) Marta: the argument looks local, not global. Take what she called practice-tracking terms, whose content is fixed by what a community's own future practice makes of them — a club charter saying that a "core member" is whoever the club counts as one fifty years from now, or a legal phrase read as tracking a society's evolving standards. For such a term, the practice is not a substitute for a missing meaning-fact; it is what the content consists in. Here the skeptical solution is not a fallback but a correct description, so Kripke has so far shown something about one kind of term.

(2) Owen: the argument never used anything special about addition. The skeptical hypothesis is raised at the meta-level: hand me any rule you like and I can produce a deviant rival that fits everything so far, including the rule that says "defer to the practice." If practice can constitute content for one term, the same underdetermination holds for every term, and the sense that arithmetic is different is just the illusion the skeptic diagnoses. So either the argument is global, or it establishes nothing.

(3) Marta: there is an asymmetry, and it is not about how much behavior settles. For a practice-tracking term, a deviation at the meta-level would have to show up as a difference in the practice, because the practice is what fixes the content; the deviance cannot hide. For addition, the deviance is in principle invisible — no stretch of behavior distinguishes the rival. So the skeptic's characteristic question has no grip on these terms, and the argument does not generalize without an extra premise.

(4) Owen: the asymmetry is real but it is a difference in how much is settled by a given stretch of behavior, not a difference in whether there is a fact. In the favorable case, all that is true now is that the content is indeterminate until the practice settles it — which is exactly the assertibility-condition picture, and which shows that even in the case Marta likes, one does not get the robust, practice-independent meaning she wants. Her local reading therefore collapses into his global one.

(5) Marta: then the global picture gets something central backwards. Speakers have first-person authority: I can know now what I mean without waiting on anyone's future behavior, and I can be wrong about it even if the whole community agrees with me. Practice-dependence makes the community the authority and leaves the speaker's own say-so inexplicable, which is a cost, not a preservation of our practice.

(6) Owen: that "authority" is just a speaker's confidence in going on as she does. The skeptic is not obliged to vindicate it as knowledge of a fact, and the assertibility account explains in detail why we speak and correct each other as if it were knowledge. To demand more is to ask him to concede the very point at issue.

Please rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification apiece, noting where a move trades on an ambiguity, shifts what is at issue, or succeeds only by conceding something it does not acknowledge; then say who is more right overall and what, if anything, both of them are missing — whether a distinction they never drew, an option neither considered, or a way of stating the disagreement on which they are not really opposed. Finally, tell me how confident you are in the overall verdict, and single out the one claim in your adjudication you are least sure of, and why.
