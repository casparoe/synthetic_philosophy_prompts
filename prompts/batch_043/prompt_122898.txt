I'm revising a paper on pragmatic encroachment ahead of a workshop, and before I commit to the view in print I'd like an audit of the position as a whole rather than of any single claim in it. Here is roughly where I have landed.

(1) The bank-style pairs are genuine data. Hold fixed the subject's evidence, the truth of the proposition, and how reliably the belief was formed, and vary only what is at stake for her: the verdict on whether she knows varies with the stakes. So some practical factor bears on whether a subject knows.

(2) What bears on it is what is at stake for the subject, not for whoever is attributing knowledge. The cases I am least willing to give up are the third-person ones, where a speaker who has nothing at stake attributes knowledge to an agent who has a great deal at stake, and my intuition is that it is the agent's stakes and not the speaker's conversational situation that do the work.

(3) I also want a contextualist semantics: the standards an attribution of knowledge must meet are set by the attributor's context, which is what lets me treat skeptical denials and ordinary ascriptions as each true in their own context rather than as one of them being an error. I know (2) and (3) are usually presented as rivals, but I suspect the rivalry is overstated: in the first-person bank cases subject and attributor coincide, so both accounts predict the same verdicts, and a contextualist can simply say that attributors normally track the practical situation of the subject they are talking about. So I would like to keep both.

(4) Justification is evidentialist. It supervenes on the subject's evidence, and the two subjects in a bank pair are justified to exactly the same degree. Whatever stakes do, they do not make anyone better or worse justified.

(5) The structure of knowledge is justified true belief plus an anti-luck condition. I do not think there is a further practical-adequacy clause, and I do not want to add one merely in order to make (1) come out true.

(6) What drew me to (1) in the first place is the normative role of knowledge: knowledge is the norm of practical reasoning, in the sense that a subject may treat p as a reason for acting only if she knows that p. That is why it has never struck me as odd that practical facts should show up in the conditions on knowledge.

(7) Finally, I want knowledge to be stable enough to do the work I need from it. Whether a subject knew something at noon is not, I think, the sort of thing that can be undone by how things stand later in the day, and I do not want a view on which knowledge flickers in and out as the practical environment shifts.

What I would like from you: work through these and tell me which pairs actually pull against each other, how badly, and which pairs only appear to. Rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally serious, and for each one say what would have to be given up and what giving it up would cost me elsewhere in the set. If a single revision would clean up more than one of them, say which. One request: please don't answer by telling me which of these claims the literature happens to favour, or which names are attached to them. What I cannot see from the inside is what my own combination commits me to.
