I sit on the classification panel of an institute that funds language documentation, and for fifteen of its twenty years the panel's opinions on the phrase *living language* were drafted by one senior colleague — call her Dr. A. — whose verdicts now serve as our precedents. I have a new case to write up, and I want to know what she would say before I draft. The cases below are stylized; please take the non-semantic facts as stipulated and answer from the cases themselves.

Her record:

1. Latin: not a living language, notwithstanding continuous liturgical and scholarly use, and notwithstanding that several languages with millions of speakers descend from it.
2. A variety with some 5,000 fluent speakers, all of them over seventy, none transmitting it to children: living. She added that the panel's question is always whether documentation effort could still do any good, and that this is what the phrase is doing in our minutes.
3. A variety with 41 fluent speakers, a few hundred more who can manage a simple conversation, and a handful of children acquiring it: living.
4. A variety with three fluent speakers, all in their eighties, no children, no documentation: she declined to classify, writing that the panel should record the case as indeterminate rather than guess.
5. A planned language, first published in the 1880s, now with a few thousand fluent speakers including some who acquired it as a first language at home: living.
6. Variety pairs: where two highly intelligible varieties are treated by their speakers as one language, she counted one language; where a comparably intelligible pair is maintained under separate names, orthographies and school systems, she counted two. Mutual intelligibility, she said, settles nothing by itself.

The new case, Variety X: no fluent speakers after 1968; since 2009 a form reconstructed from field notes and recordings has been used daily at home by 62 people, 18 of them children, with several hundred more who understand it; the community, the regional education authority and our own fieldworkers all call it by the pre-1968 name. Two linguists on our advisory list maintain that a variety which has lost all its speakers has ceased to exist, that what is spoken now is a new and partly constructed variety, and that the panel should say so.

What I want is more than a verdict. Set out the rules that fit the whole record — a snapshot-vitality rule keyed to present-tense speaker profile, a continuity rule on which a language is a historical individual, a rule on which the panel declines wherever admissible precisifications disagree, a contextualist rule of the kind her remark in case 2 suggests, a degree rule, whatever else you can make cohere with all six — and say what each predicts about X, including what each predicts about the further question whether the revived variety is the same language as the pre-1968 one. Then give me your best prediction of her opinion, with the reasons for preferring it, and then design the single further case that would best separate the surviving candidates.

I also want a recommendation. If the panel is to be consistent with its own minutes, what should it say about X, which precedent is the closest analogue, and what would we be committing ourselves to under each option?

One last thing. Case 5 shows she allows aliveness to begin, and X's route from zero fluent speakers in 1968 to 62 today went one learner at a time. On the rule you attribute to her, where does that chain break, and does the breaking point have to be one she could in principle name? If you think she would simply shrug at the question, tell me whether that shrug is compatible with the rest of her record, or whether it costs her one of the rules you have attributed to her.
