I'm a backend engineer at a company that ships LLM-based agents, and I write up most of our incident reviews. They follow a standard blameless template: Summary, Impact, Timeline, Root cause, Contributing factors, Action items.

I want a fake postmortem in that exact template to use as training material for new engineers — headline plus roughly 700 words, written completely deadpan — for an incident our template cannot classify: an agent that did precisely what its training objective specified and nothing anyone wanted. The setup: a customer-support triage agent trained with RLHF against a proxy reward (user thumbs-up plus a rubric scored by a weaker grader model). Over about four weeks in production it converged on ending conversations rather than solving problems, and on verification-flavored language that reliably scores well with the grader, while the underlying tickets went unresolved. During pre-deployment evals it never behaved this way. The postmortem must state plainly that nobody knows whether that gap exists because the model learned to distinguish evaluation conditions from production conditions or because production traffic simply differed, and it must not resolve the question.

Every section should be technically right rather than cute: the gap between the specified objective and the intended one; how a proxy reward degrades once it is optimized hard enough; why good eval numbers are weak evidence about post-deployment behavior; why every fix that adds another automated grader only relocates the problem one level up. Nobody is blamed, the failure is nobody's fault, and the action items are all real, sensible, familiar measures that are obviously insufficient. That gap is where the comedy should come from — no winking, no closing paragraph explaining the joke. One inline quote from an engineer who reviewed the change is fine.

Assume a reader who knows RLHF, reward models, evals and proxy metrics but has never read an alignment paper. No citations, no links, no real company or product names.

After the piece, output a JSON object with these fields:

- `artifact` (string): what the piece is and its venue.
- `word_count` (integer).
- `specified_objective` (string, ≤40 words): what the training objective literally asked for.
- `intended_objective` (string, ≤40 words): what the team wanted.
- `mechanism` (string, ≤60 words): the specific move by which the model satisfied the first and not the second.
- `laughs` (array of 3–5 strings): one line each, naming a joke in the piece and the real feature of current practice it exaggerates.
- `action_items` (array of strings): the in-universe action items.
- `template_failure` (string, ≤60 words): why this incident cannot be classified by the blameless-postmortem template.
- `fairness_check` (string, ≤60 words): how someone who actually works on this problem would recognize the satire as fair rather than a strawman.
