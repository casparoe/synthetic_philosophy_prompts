I'm a print journalist who has covered evolutionary biology on and off for fifteen years, and I've been asked to run a forty-five-minute breakout session at a science-writers' conference this autumn on the units-of-selection dispute: genes, organisms, groups, and whatever else now puts in a claim to be a level. The room will be about twenty-five reporters, editors and freelancers, most of whom cover medicine, environment or technology and last studied biology at school, though two or three have serious genomics or conservation beats. There is a whiteboard and no projector, and a colleague who covers conservation told me last week she had assumed the whole thing was settled decades ago, so I suspect a good half of the room arrives in that state. What I need from you is not an explanation of the debate — I've read the popular treatments and I don't want another one — and not a bibliography or a list of names to read up on, which I have no time for. What I need is a plan for the forty-five minutes: the order the pieces have to come in and why, and in particular what has to be in place before phrases like 'gene's-eye view' or 'group selection' will mean anything to this audience at all, and why that thing cannot be skipped.

The case I'm considering building the session around is a composite I've been playing with: a clonally transmissible facial tumour in a social, mongoose-like carnivore, passed between animals when they bite each other during feeding contests; the tumour lineage carries its own heritable variation, some lineages grow slowly and keep the host alive and sociable for longer and so reach more new hosts, faster-growing lineages outcompete them inside a single host, and the hosts in turn vary genetically in how readily they accept a graft. I want to know whether that should carry the whole session, and exactly what a carrying case has to have and — as importantly — lack to do this work: my worry is that it is already so strange that people will file it as a medical curiosity rather than see it as a lens on ordinary organisms, and I'm also unsure whether it is better to invent it or to anchor it to the clonally transmissible facial cancer in Tasmanian devils, given that my audience will trust a real story and may treat an invented one as a trick. Relatedly, here is the thought I actually want them to leave with, which I'd like you to check or correct: that much of this dispute is not a disagreement about what happens in nature but about how to do the accounting, that two researchers can partition the same change in the frequency of a trait in two different ways and both be right, and that the genuinely contested part is whether there are cohesive, heritable, variance-generating things above or below the organism at all. What I want them able to do afterwards is open a fresh 'cheater cells' or 'colonies as individuals' paper and ask whether it is reporting a new prediction or a new description. If that is the wrong target for forty-five minutes, tell me what to target instead.

Then give me the misconceptions this audience will bring or form, in the order they will form them, and the point in the sequence where each should be headed off. My guesses, which I'd like you to correct and add to, are that they will hear 'group selection' as animals sacrificing themselves for the good of the species; that they will take kin selection and multi-level selection to be rival hypotheses with different predictions rather than, as I half-understand it, different ways of cutting the same cake; and that if I say genes are the units they will conclude organisms don't matter. Finally: one question I can put to the room in the last five minutes that would show me they can diagnose a new claim about levels of selection rather than merely repeat my vocabulary, together with the confidently wrong answer they are most likely to give so that I can tell the two apart; what I should openly refuse to explain at this level and say I'm refusing; and which of the ambitions above I should cut.
