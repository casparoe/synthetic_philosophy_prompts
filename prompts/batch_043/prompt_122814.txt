I've been reading about the philosophy of perception on my own for a while now — no classes, just whatever I can find that isn't paywalled, plus badgering a friend who's in a psych PhD program until she changes the subject — and I keep coming back to one experiment. You flash a red dot in one spot, then, maybe a tenth of a second later, a green dot a little ways over to the right, and people reliably report seeing a *single* dot that slides across and turns green somewhere in the middle of its journey. Which is strange, because nothing ever moved and nothing ever changed color, and the color change is reported as happening before the second dot was even presented. The way I've got it pinned in my head, the philosopher who made this famous (Dennett, I'm pretty sure, early nineties, the multiple-drafts guy) uses it to set up a fork: either the brain fills in the movement after the fact and quietly rewrites your memory of the earlier part, so you really did have the plain unedited experience first and then the record was doctored — the Stalinesque version — or the brain holds things back for a beat, does its stitching, and only then hands you a finished experience with a falsified timestamp on it, so there never was a moment when you had the unedited one at all — the Orwellian version. (I might have the two labels flipped; if I do, tell me in one line and then use the right ones.) And then the punchline, as I understand it, is that there's no fact of the matter which one happened: any experiment you can run comes out the same on either story, so the question of when the experience "really" occurred isn't a deep mystery, it's an empty one. I half believe that and half think it's a cop-out, which is where the argument with my friend always stalls out.

So here's the thing I've been daydreaming about, and I want you to write it as a story. Imagine a fictional finding — I'm obviously making this up, so don't go looking for it — that's supposed to break the tie. Call it draft leak: a rare condition (your pick how it happens, a lesion, a drug, some electrode thing) in which people get *both*. They report the raw version first — red dot flashes, sits there red, nothing moves — and then, a moment later, the smooth single dot gliding over and changing color mid-flight, and they describe the first one as having been overwritten or replaced. My first thought was that this settles it cleanly: there was an earlier, unedited conscious experience, and then it got revised, so it's the Stalinesque story and the question was empirical after all. But the more I turn it over the less sure I am, because the leaked draft is itself something the person experiences *later*, at the moment of the leak, and a later experience can represent an earlier one without being one. From the inside you maybe can't tell a recovered memory from a fabricated one, and maybe the leak is just one more thing the editor has to smooth over. I genuinely don't know which way this cuts and I'd like the story to find out.

Setting, so you've got something to work with: near future, a company that sells a consumer implant which puts a deliberate delay of something like a hundred milliseconds into your visual feed so it can clean it up before you get it — blink gaps removed, saccadic jumpiness smoothed, apparent motion stitched, noise scrubbed — and which employs human continuity editors on the night shift, people who see the raw frames and choose which polished version gets delivered to the customer. Your narrator is one of those editors, four years in, very good at not looking too closely at what the raw feed shows. One night a clip gets flagged for review: in the raw frames there's a figure standing in the road, and in the version that was actually delivered to the client driving that car, the road is empty. Meanwhile the client — or someone in the client's family, you choose — turns out to have draft leak, and keeps insisting that they saw the figure and also didn't. Give me 1000 to 1500 words. Rules of the road, because I've seen these go wrong: nobody in the story gives a lecture, and nobody says the words qualia or Cartesian theater out loud; the argument has to live in what happens and in what your narrator decides to do with the clip. No actual secret police, no dystopia cosplay, no character explaining that the past gets rewritten — the Orwell-and-Stalin business should be in the bones of the situation, not painted on it. It should be accurate enough that someone who really knows this debate could tell which of the two readings your story is quietly siding with and why, and couldn't catch you getting either reading backwards or the red-dot demo wrong. And please don't tie it off with a neat answer — end on whatever is still stuck open, even if that's just her hand hovering over the approve button.
