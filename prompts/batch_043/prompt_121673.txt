I came to philosophy late and by way of a different discipline. For most of my working life I was an experimental physical chemist, and two habits from that life have stayed with me. The first is that when a dispute survives a decade without either side visibly shifting, that is usually evidence that the two parties were not, despite appearances, talking about the same quantity — the disagreement was in the operationalization, or in the words, not in the world. The second is that a prediction is worth very little once the result is already in, so I prefer to see predictions committed to in advance and attributed to a named party.

My reading group has arrived at exactly the kind of standoff I mean. There are six of us, split fairly evenly between those drawn to the neutrality-based liberalism that descends from Rawls and those drawn to the communitarian critique of it associated with Sandel, MacIntyre and Taylor. After a year we can all recite the objections, and nobody has moved. I want the document we should have produced instead: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between a liberal and a communitarian, written so that both could put their names to it.

Please write it as follows.

**1. Agreed claims.** A numbered section of eight to ten claims both sides can sign. I do not want the sort of thing nobody would ever have denied — that community matters, that individuals matter, that we should be careful — because signing it costs nothing. Each claim should be one that at least one of the two sides would have baulked at before the collaboration: about what the self is like and how it comes to have the ends it has; about whether a conception of the good can be criticized from outside or only from within; about what the state is doing when it declines to justify itself by appeal to a conception of the good; about what liberal neutrality is a claim about (the content of policy, its justification, its predicted effects, or something else); about how much of the communitarian's target is really a description of actual liberal practice rather than of liberal theory; and about the limits both sides accept — in particular, what each concedes the other is right to insist on, and where each says plainly that its own view has a cost it is willing to pay.

**2. Disagreements.** A table. Columns: the disputed point in neutral wording; the same point in the words the liberal would sign; the same point in the words the communitarian would sign; whether the disagreement is substantive or, in your judgement, verbal; and which crux below it hangs on. Where you think a row is verbal, say what substantive question it should be replaced by, or say why it cannot be.

**3. Cruxes.** The sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. I have invented three cases; they are mine, not the literature's, so treat them as stipulations rather than as something to look up. For each, I want (a) why both parties would accept that the case bears on the crux, (b) what each predicts, written down as a prediction and not as a retrospective reading, and (c) which outcome each side would count as evidence against itself. If one of my cases is a bad test, say so and discard it rather than making do.

- *The matched towns.* Two towns are alike in every respect the social sciences can measure: same density and variety of local associations, same rates of participation and trust, same levels of provision for local public goods, same rates of entry and exit over a generation. They differ in one respect: in the first, the practices that constitute local membership are open to anyone who takes up residence; in the second, they are tied to long residence and to being recognized by those already inside, and newcomers are not admitted to them. The communitarian usually claims that the embedded, partly constitutive kind of membership is what does the work. So: if all the measurable outcomes match, is there anything left for the communitarian to point to — a difference in the kind of good realized rather than its quantity — and does the liberal have any remaining objection to the first town's arrangements?

- *The open door.* A community imposes no legal barrier to leaving and no material penalty; the state's writ runs in it, its members know their rights, and exit is formally costless. But leaving means the loss of every relationship and role that gave the leaver's life its shape, and the community's practices are such that this loss is near-total. Does an intact legal right of exit do the legitimating work the liberal assigns to it, and if not, does that tell against the liberal's test for coercion or only against its application?

- *The continuity subsidy.* A state uses general tax revenue to fund, on equal terms, any voluntary association that can show a specified degree of intergenerational continuity and internal density — a threshold stated without reference to what the association is for. The scheme's aim is content-neutral; its predictable effect is to favour some ways of life over others, because only some generate associations of that shape. Does the liberal object, and on what ground, given the aim is neutral? Does the communitarian welcome it, or does the communitarian's complaint turn out to have been about the content of particular goods after all rather than about embeddedness and continuity as such? I take this case to separate two things the communitarian literature often runs together, and I would like to know whether it really does.

Finally: somewhere in the statement there will be a crux that no observation settles, because the two sides differ in what they value and not in what they expect. Name it explicitly when you get there, and stop there rather than manufacturing a test that would only look like one. And tell me, in a short closing paragraph addressed to me rather than to the parties, whether anything in the document would actually change a mind, or whether the honest verdict is that the two sides should agree to disagree and get on with something else. About 1,200 words for the statement itself.
