I think I've accidentally argued myself into not trusting any long derivation at all, and I need someone to find the step where it goes wrong. Background: I run a reading group on the lottery/preface cluster and this is the argument I was going to present as a reductio of the standard moral. Numbers are placeholders, nothing hangs on them.

(1) Belief is just credence above a threshold: if my credence in p is above some high t (say .99), then I'm justified in believing p, and justified belief is the attitude that lets me treat p as settled when I go on to use it in reasoning or action.

(2) Fair lottery, a million tickets, exactly one winner, and I know all this. For each ticket i, my credence that i loses is .999999, above threshold. So for each ticket, I'm justified in believing that it loses.

(3) I'm also justified in believing that some ticket wins, since that's how the thing is set up and I was told so.

(4) So justified belief isn't aggregative: being justified in each of p1...pn doesn't get you justified in their conjunction, even when you can see that the list is exhaustive. Call this the standard moral of the lottery.

(5) Bridge to deduction: an argument with premises p1...pn and conclusion q hands me at most as much justification for q as I have for the conjunction p1 ∧ ... ∧ pn. So aggregativity is exactly what a derivation with many premises needs, and by (4) it doesn't have it.

(6) Everything real has many premises. A 300-step proof. A theory resting on a thousand measurements. A case built out of forty exhibits. Or a 'preface chain': I write a book with 500 claims, each about .99 reliable, so the book is already something like 99% likely to contain an error; then someone writes a commentary asserting that each of my claims is correct, at .99 per claim; then someone writes a survey of the commentary; and so on. Nothing in any of these is certain, and the items aren't perfectly correlated, so the justification I have for the conjunction is at best in the neighborhood of the product of the per-item figures, which collapses fast.

(7) So nobody is ever justified in believing the conclusion of a long proof, a wide-evidence theory, or a multi-exhibit case, on the basis of the derivation. And that's crazy — we plainly do get knowledge out of long derivations, and that's not something I'm willing to trade away.

(8) It also seems to eat itself: this argument has seven steps, so if (7) is right I'm not justified in believing (7) on the basis of it. I genuinely can't tell whether that's a reductio or just a cute observation.

What I'd most want to keep: (2), (3), and the pre-theoretic claim in (7). I'd give up (1) fairly happily if that's where the problem is. But I tried that and it doesn't seem to help: restate the whole thing in terms of credence instead of belief and step (6) still looks like it goes through, the pressure just shows up as 'your credence in the conclusion should be low' rather than 'you shouldn't believe it.' Or am I wrong that it restates?

One more thing that makes me suspicious of (4) specifically. Run the lottery lopsided instead of even: you're told the winner is one of the first ten tickets and nothing more. Now the 'ticket i loses' claims for tickets 11 through a million are certain, the ones for tickets 1 through 10 sit at .9 and probably fall below threshold, and no paradox appears at all. Same closure principle, same number of items, no problem. That makes me think (4) isn't really about closure and isn't really about how many items there are, but I can't say what it is about instead, and it doesn't obviously point at a bad step in (5) or (6).

So: which step do you reject? What was that step doing that made it look innocent? And what does rejecting it cost me elsewhere — in particular in the preface case, which I care about more than the lottery, and which I'd like to still be able to say something about?
