Setup: in my book there are two factions who've been sniping at each other for generations. The tranquilists hold that only two things fundamentally matter: that craving be absent (the restless wanting, the itch for things to be otherwise than they are) and that suffering be absent. Everything we normally call a good — achievement, love, knowledge, beauty, play — matters at most as a way of quieting craving or warding off suffering, and the feeling that these things matter in themselves is itself a by-product of the craving that makes them feel urgent. Call the other camp plenitude pluralists: they hold that some things matter in their own right even when no craving is involved and no suffering gets prevented — that someone actually understood something hard, that a friendship really had the depth it had, that the music got written.

Some facts about my world that both sides accept, so you can argue from them rather than about them. There's a safe, cheap, reversible procedure called the Hush: it removes the restless wanting but leaves sensory enjoyment, memory, skill, and the ability to act on reasons intact — people still finish their projects, they just do it without the tug. There's a forty-year volunteer register (the Maunder register): people on the Hush rate their lives as going very well, but roughly a third of them, once the Hush is reversed, say something was missing and can't say what; a few refuse reversal. And there's the case of the archivist Ilse, who spends thirty years copying the songs of a dying language no living person will ever hear; on the Hush she keeps the same hours and the same care and produces exactly the same archive.

What I want is the joint document that a committed tranquilist and a committed plenitude pluralist could actually both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration: my two characters are supposed to read it aloud to each other in chapter nine and neither of them can feel like they sold out. Cruxes I suspect matter: whether the "something was missing" reports are evidence of a lost good or of withdrawal from the wanting itself; whether there can be "dormant goods" that exist only while they're being pursued; whether the significance people feel is a good or a symptom; and whether "matters" is even one word here or two (worth promoting vs. worth admiring) — if you think some of the fight is only about words, say which part and why.
