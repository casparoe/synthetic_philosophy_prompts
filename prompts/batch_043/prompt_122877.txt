I want a careful comparison of two sceptical arguments that are, I think, too often treated as the same argument wearing different clothes. My suspicion is that they are not, and that the difference tells us something about what is really doing the work in rule-following scepticism.

Call the first the *speaker argument*. It is the sceptical argument about rule-following familiar from discussions of *Kripkenstein*, and I will set it up in the standard shape without needing it attributed to any text. Take addition. Everything about me that might be offered as constituting my meaning addition by the addition sign — my finite record of past applications, my dispositions to answer, my introspective sense of what I intend, the physical state of my brain and its causal history — is equally compatible with my meaning any of indefinitely many functions that coincide with addition everywhere I have actually gone and diverge somewhere I have not: a function that agrees with addition on all pairs of numbers below some threshold and returns a fixed value for every pair above it, say. If no candidate constituent of meaning singles out addition rather than one of these, then, the argument concludes, there is no fact of the matter about what I mean; the appearance of a determinate rule has to be accounted for instead by what my community would let me get away with saying — assertibility conditions rather than truth conditions. I know the standard realist replies (dispositional, primitivist, use-based, and their elaborations); I do not need them surveyed. I want to know how the comparison below bears on them.

Call the second the *artefact argument*. A machine learning system is trained on finitely many input-output pairs, all of which are consistent with addition. Indefinitely many distinct algorithms fit that training set exactly while diverging from addition, and from each other, on unseen inputs. So the training record does not determine which algorithm the system implements, and the claim that it learned addition looks underdetermined by the evidence in what appears to be just the way my meaning addition is. But the two cases come apart in ways I want weighed, not merely listed:

1. In the artefact case we are not restricted to behavioural evidence: we can inspect the system's weights and architecture, and we have a tolerably well-developed notion of what it is for a physical mechanism to implement one algorithm rather than another. Does the availability of a mechanism dissolve the scepticism, or only relocate it? Specifically, is the claim that these weights implement addition rather than threshold-addition itself a rule-following claim of exactly the kind the speaker argument calls into question? If relocating it is enough, why is it not equally enough in the speaker case, where we also have a brain?

2. The speaker worry is normative: not merely which output will I produce, but which answer would be *correct* for me. The artefact worry looks merely predictive: which output will it produce. Is that asymmetry real, and if so what generates it — intention, the practice of holding one another to account, the absence of a point of view in the artefact? And if the artefact's correct outputs are in the end fixed by what its designer intended, does the artefact argument simply smuggle the speaker argument back in?

3. What would the analogue of the sceptical solution be in the artefact case? The natural candidates for the role of the community are the training distribution, the loss function, and the population of users who would accept the system's outputs. Do any of these play the sceptical solution's role, or do they collapse into a straight realist answer about which function the system in fact minimizes error with respect to? If they collapse, does that collapse undermine the sceptical solution in the speaker case as well? And if one of them genuinely plays the role, consider a system trained exclusively on a community's verdicts about who has followed a rule correctly — trained, that is, on assertibility conditions themselves, with no other data about the rule. Does the sceptical solution have the resources to deny that such a system means something by its outputs, or does it entail that it does? If it entails that, is that a cost or a consequence the sceptic can live with?

The deliverable I want is this. First, say what you take the *core* of rule-following scepticism to be: underdetermination by finite evidence, the irreducibility of semantic normativity, a first-person versus third-person asymmetry, the regress of interpretation, or something else I have not named. Second, using that diagnosis, say whether the artefact argument contains the core or only a superficial resemblance to it. Third, say which of the two arguments is the harder one to answer and why: if the artefact argument is easier, I want to know what that tells us about what was carrying the weight in the speaker argument, and if it is just as hard, I want to know what has to be true of meaning for that to be so. Argue for the comparison rather than describing it, and tell me where you think the question is still open because it depends on a prior disagreement you are not going to settle here.
