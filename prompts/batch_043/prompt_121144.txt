I'm a third-year undergraduate currently enrolled in our department's seminar on combinatorial games, and I've been asked to lead one fifty-minute session of it next week on the strategy-stealing argument. My classmates can state what a strategy is (a function from histories to moves), have done backward induction on small game trees, and have seen the determinacy result for finite games of perfect information stated and applied but never proved; in an earlier session they brute-forced tic-tac-toe and a 3×3 Hex board by hand. A good third of them are computer science majors who will want to know whether the proof hands them an algorithm. I don't need the argument explained to me — I need a plan for explaining it, and for what has to be in place before it can even be stated so that the conclusion lands as surprising rather than as a restatement of the truism that moving first is better.

Specifically, I'd like:

1. The dependency ordering. Which components have to precede which — the reduction ad absurdum shape, the symmetry of the game under swapping the two players, the condition that an extra move is never a disadvantage, and determinacy plus the absence of draws as the pair of facts that converts "the second player has no winning strategy" into "the first player has one" — and what goes wrong if I present the conclusion before the side conditions, or the copycat manoeuvre before the notion of a complete strategy is fixed. Which pieces can I assume from the earlier sessions and which must I re-derive in the room?

2. The carrying example. One game the whole session hangs on, playable in pairs on paper in under ten minutes, which cannot end in a draw, in which the never-a-disadvantage condition is visible at a glance rather than asserted, and which the students cannot solve exhaustively in the time they have. Tell me what such an example must have and lack, and why not the obvious candidates — Hex on a small board, Chomp, tic-tac-toe, Gomoku — if not those.

3. The misconception map in the order the students will actually form them, each attached to the point in the sequence where it should be headed off. The ones I anticipate: that the argument identifies a good first move; that it establishes a first-player win rather than the absence of a second-player win, and so shows that White is better at chess; that the extra-move condition is a technical formality, or that it merely says no move is ever bad; and confusion about the stealing step itself — whether the first player is literally passing a turn, and whether the stolen strategy might want to make use of the extra stone. Which of these deserve class time up front and which should I let surface before correcting?

4. One task for the final ten minutes that would let me distinguish a student who has the argument from one who has only memorized its shape. My instinct is to hand them a game for which the correct answer is that the argument does not apply, together with the question of which condition fails, rather than one more game where it works — but tell me if that's the wrong instinct and why.

5. What I should deliberately omit at this level, and say out loud that I'm omitting. I'm inclined to list the proof of determinacy, the bridge from Hex to Brouwer's fixed-point theorem, and anything about computational complexity, but I'd rather you correct that list than confirm it.
