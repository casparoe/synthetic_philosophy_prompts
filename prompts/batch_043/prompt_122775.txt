A senior philosopher in my department — call her Professor V. — has for some years kept a private ledger of "transfer claims": arguments that export Gödel's incompleteness theorems to subject matters outside mathematics. Each entry states the argument in a paragraph and records her verdict, **transfers** or **does not transfer**, occasionally with a one-line note. I have to write up a new entry for her, and I would like to predict the verdict before I send it. I am less interested in the final word than in the rule she seems to be running, in the rules that fit her record equally well, and in which sub-case would tell those rules apart. Please reason from the cases themselves; I do not want citations, reading lists, or suggestions of literature.

Her ledger, in the order the entries were written:

1. **The Lucas–Penrose argument.** A human mathematician who can see that a formal system F is sound can also see the truth of F's Gödel sentence, which F cannot prove; so human mathematical knowledge outruns any fixed machine, and mechanism about the mind is false. Verdict: does not transfer. Note: even granting the premise, the conclusion is about what we are, not about what a system can derive.

2. **The "no final theory" argument.** Any finite, effectively stated physical theory, combined with ordinary mathematics, will leave arithmetical questions undecided — for instance whether some specified computation halts; so physics can never arrive at a complete theory of everything. Verdict: does not transfer. Note: the arithmetic here is ours, not the theory's; what the argument produces are not physical predictions, and each of them is decidable one level up.

3. **Self-trust.** A reasoner whose reasoning is capturable by a consistent, effectively axiomatized theory extending a modest amount of arithmetic cannot prove its own consistency, and — by Löb's theorem — can establish "if I prove φ, then φ" only in those cases where it can prove φ outright. So no such reasoner can hand itself a general certificate of its own reliability. Verdict: transfers. Note: this is the theorem itself in epistemic dress.

4. **The obstacle to self-certified action.** An agent that will act only on a proof that the action is safe cannot, in general, produce the conditional "if I prove this action safe, then it is safe," since by Löb's theorem it could produce that conditional only if it could prove the action safe outright. An insistence on fully self-certified action therefore stultifies itself. Verdict: transfers. Note: same structure, one level down.

5. **Legal indeterminacy.** Any legal order whose valid norms are effectively enumerable will face disputes the norms alone do not settle; therefore legitimate adjudication necessarily draws on standards outside the code. Verdict: does not transfer. Note: no code contains a sentence about its own derivability; vagueness and conflict of norms are not diagonalisation.

6. **Program verification.** No program can, in general, determine whether an arbitrary other program is correct; so the limits of formal methods are Gödelian. Verdict: does not transfer. Note: true, but that is Church and Turing; this ledger is about incompleteness.

Two things strike me about the record. Every entry she has accepted concerns a system's inability to certify *itself*, and every entry she has rejected fails for a reason that would stand no matter how the question of self-certification were settled. And she has never been asked about a system's relation to what it *produces* — its outputs, its rebuilt copies, its successors.

The new submission, drafted by a colleague who works on machine learning, is this. Let S be a self-modifying artificial agent whose reasoning is captured by a consistent, effectively axiomatized theory T extending enough arithmetic, and which builds a successor S′ only after proving that S′ will still pursue S's objectives. The claim is that Gödel transfers to this setting and that no self-contained assurance argument for recursive self-improvement exists. Four sub-cases are offered:

(a) S′ is a strict extension of S: it retains all of S's inference and adds strength — say, a reflection principle for S. S is given S′'s full specification. Claim: S cannot prove S′ safe.

(b) S′ is S exactly — identical specification, rebuilt on faster hardware. Claim: S cannot prove S′ safe.

(c) S′ is a restricted device whose entire behaviour S can verify by exhaustive simulation and inspection, e.g. a lookup table with a proven bound on its run time. Claim: S can prove S′ safe; the obstacle, where it exists, is an obstacle about strength.

(d) Take the lineage S = S₀, S₁, S₂, …, each successor slightly stronger than the last and each generated by S₀'s own rules. Claim: S₀ cannot certify the lineage as a whole, so the series can never be justified from where it starts — even though each individual step can be certified from the next system up.

What verdicts do you expect her to record on (a), (b), (c) and (d), and on the entry as a whole? State the rule she is most likely running, together with the other rules that fit entries 1–6 just as well, and say what each predicts about the new case. Which of the four sub-cases is load-bearing, and which is redundant given the others — note that (b) may simply restate entry 3 rather than test anything. Give your best guess with the reasons for preferring it, and be explicit where you think her precedents underdetermine the answer and what principle she would be committing herself to with each choice. Finally, draft the fifth sub-case I should send her: the one that best separates the rules that survive.
