I'm a data engineer with no philosophy background and I lurk on a longtermist-ish forum where people argue about whether the far future should dominate everything we care about. About a year ago someone posted that the simulation argument should calm everyone down: if we are probably inside a simulated history, then the future is probably short and small, because most such runs would be a few centuries of ancestors being simulated before someone shuts the thing down, and the astronomically huge number of future people that makes existential-risk work look dominant gets replaced by a much smaller number. Someone else answered that this changes nothing: even granting the simulation bit, the run could be duplicated a million times over, or the civilization outside could still have a giant future that what we do inside somehow tracks, or the simulators turning us off is itself an existential catastrophe, so the worry just gets relocated rather than defused. A third person said the simulation stuff is a pure sideshow, because fanaticism is a problem about whether you follow expected value when the probability is tiny and the payoff is enormous, and that problem is identical inside and outside a simulation. I've now read maybe six of these threads and I have never once seen anyone move, or even say what would move them.

My own hypothesis, which I want stress-tested rather than flattered, is that this is several arguments accidentally sharing a name and that the interesting one has nothing to do with simulation. Roughly: there's a disguised empirical question about the distribution of run lengths and about what simulations get used for; there's a separate formal question about how to add up value when the very same history might be lived many times over, which I've started calling the rerun question — count value per run or per distinct history — and it looks to me like you can be a fanatic or not on either answer; and then there's the real fanaticism fight about decision rules under tiny probabilities of huge payoffs, which the simulation framing neither helps nor hurts. I also suspect people are treating being in a simulation as an on/off switch when what would actually do any work is the shape of the distribution: a world of many short cheap sims and a world of one staggeringly long sim are both cases where we are in a simulation, and they seem to push in opposite directions.
