I'm a compiler engineer by day — my work is mostly on meta-circular evaluators, runtime reflection, cyclic object graphs, and garbage collection, so I'm fluent in the environment model of evaluation, closures, pointers, and self-reference in data structures. By night I sit in on a cognitive science reading group, where we recently went over Hofstadter's strange-loop account of the self: the idea that the self is not an object but a self-referential symbol, a pattern in the brain that refers to the very system that contains it, producing a tangled hierarchy in which the symbol is both a product of the system and one of the causes of what the system does, and that this loop is what we mean by 'I'. I've been playing with a variant of this picture that I haven't seen discussed anywhere, and I want to know whether it holds up. Call it the sharded self-symbol. Instead of a single 'I' that refers to the whole system, suppose the system maintains a family of partial self-symbols, I_1 through I_n, each bound to a different subsystem (perceptual, motor-executive, autobiographical, and so on). No single symbol refers to the system as a whole; each refers to its own subsystem and also carries a deferral pointer telling it which other partial symbol to consult when a question falls outside its subsystem's domain. Those deferral pointers form a directed graph, and in a toy simulator I wrote I can tune two things: the topology of that graph, and the refresh rate at which each partial symbol is re-bound to the current state of its subsystem. When the graph is strongly connected and the refresh rates are synchronized, the simulated agent produces something like a unified self-report; when I fragment the graph or desynchronize the refresh rates, it behaves less like one agent and more like a committee, or like the classic split-brain cases. A friend in control theory tells me my deferral graph is just a consensus protocol with extra steps, which I choose to take as a compliment. I'm confident about the code; what I'm not confident about is the metaphysics.

So here is what I want. First, restate Hofstadter's original single-symbol account, and then my sharded variant, as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use at work: bindings and environments, pointers and cyclic structures, meta-circular evaluation, message passing between subsystems, garbage collection, and reflection, meaning a program inspecting or modifying its own runtime state. Where the mapping is your own invention rather than something the original claim already contained, say so explicitly rather than quietly importing my framework's assumptions and calling it translation. Second, give me the residue report for each: which parts map cleanly onto that vocabulary, which survive only with distortion, and which cannot be expressed in it at all. The questions I most want answered are whether the sharded variant is still a strange loop in Hofstadter's sense, or whether distributing the self-reference over a graph destroys the tangled hierarchy that is supposed to do the work; and whether the leftover — the fact that 'I' seems to mean something to the system that has it, and seems to come as a single unified point of view — is a defect of my programming-languages vocabulary, or a sign that my sharded model, or Hofstadter's original, is less clear than it looks. Please don't cite anything or point me toward reading; I want your own worked translation and your own verdict on the residue.
