I keep a prediction journal. Three years now, a few hundred resolved questions, scored with the quadratic rule that forecasting people use. I have no academic background in any of this — I taught myself from blogs, a forum, and one or two books I only half understood — so please don't assume I know the vocabulary. If you use technical terms, say what they mean in a clause.

I have noticed something that I cannot get anyone to discuss with me, and I want to know whether it has a name.

First instance. Last year I wrote myself a cluster of questions about a single upcoming event: whether a particular company would release a product before the end of the year, and if it did, in which quarter. Asked one at a time — a single yes-or-no on ships this year, then, separately, conditional on shipping, which quarter — my numbers came out one way. Written instead as one question with five cells (does not ship; first quarter; second; third; fourth), the number I wanted to put on does not ship was clearly lower. Nothing about the world changed between the two exercises, and nothing about my information did either; I did them the same afternoon. I have since rerun the arithmetic both ways over the previous two years, and the gap is systematic, not noise.

Second instance, which I suspect is the same thing seen from the other side. My own record says that when I write 80 I am right something like 74 per cent of the time, and when I write 95 I am right something like 82 per cent of the time. So I now shade my high numbers down before writing them. Everyone tells me this is fine: my real opinion just is the shaded number, and the shading is me learning from evidence about myself. That does not match the phenomenology. I do not feel that I believe the shaded number. I feel that I believe the unshaded one and am writing a different one because of how I am going to be graded — and the whole selling point of the framework I picked this up from was that a proper rule never rewards you for writing anything other than your real opinion, and that the only thing that matters is being near the truth.

What the two cases have in common, as far as I can tell, is this: the number it is best for me to write depends on bookkeeping — how finely the outcomes are carved up, how many questions I am being asked at once, what my scoring history happens to look like — and not only on what I believe and on what is true. Both halves of the advertising slogan then seem to fail, in a way that has nothing to do with the truth of anything.

The only reply I get from people who know more than I do is that this is just how the rule works. That is a description of my example, not an answer.

So, three things I want, and I will not be satisfied with a label dropped on the table:

Whether this is one phenomenon or two. The carving-up-the-outcomes case and the shading case feel different in kind, and I would rather have two names with a clear relation between them than one name papered over a join.

Where the edges are relative to things it resembles but is not. I can think of four candidates and cannot sort them: deliberately gaming a score; my opinion simply not being a sharp number in the first place, so that any single number is a compression with losses; my track record being genuine evidence about my own reliability, which I am entitled to use; and whatever the property is called that is supposed to make a rule reward only honesty. Tell me which of these my case is, which it is not, and what the exact point of difference is in each instance.

The boundary case I cannot locate for myself. Sometimes moving a number after a re-description seems clearly right. I split does not ship into delayed indefinitely and cancelled outright, realised I had been folding two quite different failures with different likelihoods into one box, and my numbers moved a lot. That felt like noticing something, not like bookkeeping. And yet, on my own telling, the world and my information about it were unchanged there too — exactly as in the first instance, where the movement felt illegitimate. I want the test that separates them, stated so that I could apply it to a new case without already knowing which way it should come out.

One more constraint: I have read, paraphrasing badly, that different scoring rules are defended for different reasons and that there is disagreement about which is correct. If the trouble I am describing is an artefact of the quadratic rule specifically, say so, and say whether the logarithmic one has the same feature; I would like to know whether I have found something about accuracy or only something about my scoring convention.

I am not asking for citations, a bibliography, or a reading list — I cannot chase references and I would not trust a list I could not check. Use the established terms if you know them, to help me place things, but the answer I want is the carving, not the sources. Please do not open with the name if there is one; I would rather see the shape of the thing first.

Sections, in this order, with roughly these lengths:

What you think I am describing — about 120 words. Restate the phenomenon in your own terms before you name anything, so I can check we are talking about the same thing.

Names, if any — about 150 words. The established term or terms if there are any; if there are none, say plainly that there are none.

One thing or two — about 200 words. Which of my two instances share a structure, and on what principle you are dividing or unifying them.

Nearest neighbours, and why each is not quite it — about 250 words. The four candidates I listed, plus any I have missed; for each, the exact point of difference from my case.

The edge case — about 200 words. My delayed-versus-cancelled example, the general test you would apply, and at least one new case of your own on each side of the line.

Does the choice of rule matter — about 150 words.

What I should call it — about 100 words. Your recommended term, a one-sentence definition, and one sentence each on what would and would not count as an instance of it.
