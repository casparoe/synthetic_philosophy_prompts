I did a maths degree about ten years ago, then drifted into software, and this spring I started running a fortnightly reading group at my local bookshop on what we think we're doing when we do mathematics. We've had three sessions and it goes the same way every time: someone says they suppose they're a platonist, and then five minutes later they're arguing with the next person, who also just said they're a platonist. So for session four I want to throw out the labels and use something diagnostic instead — a decision tree that sorts people by what they'll say about cases, not by which word they use for themselves. The group is mostly ex-maths students plus one retired engineer who reads a lot of popular physics, so assume a decent maths background and no philosophy training at all.

What I'd like you to build: start everyone from the things basically all of us accept (that the twin prime conjecture and the continuum hypothesis are meaningful sentences, that proofs give us knowledge, that mathematics gets applied to physics with ridiculous success), and route them from there to a named position. Eight to ten branch questions, each phrased as a concrete case someone with a maths degree can answer without knowing where it leads — no question that just asks "do numbers exist?". Some cases I definitely want in there: the two children who are taught the natural numbers as different set-theoretic reductions, so that whether 2 is {∅,{∅}} or {{∅}} looks like it has no answer at all; the fact that the second-order Peano axioms pin the naturals down up to isomorphism while any first-order version has non-standard models; whether the continuum hypothesis has a determinate truth value given that it's independent of the standard axioms; whether a consistent bit of pure mathematics that nobody has ever applied to anything (some invented algebra with no physics attached) describes something real anyway; whether we could so much as refer to or know about objects that dont causally interact with us, and whether it helps to say such objects are "thin" rather than spooky; whether the truth of there being infinitely many twin primes could even in principle depend on what our best physical theory needs; whether a definition that quantifies over a totality containing the very thing being defined is legitimate; and whether there could be two non-isomorphic structures that equally deserve to be called the natural numbers.

The three fault lines I most care about, and which I want the tree to actually seperate rather than fudge: (1) objects versus structure — is arithmetic about individual objects with identities of their own, or about positions in a pattern, where nothing matters beyond the relations? (2) the epistemic one — how do we get knowledge of, or reference to, causally inert things, if we do at all? (3) uniqueness versus plenitude — is there one intended natural number structure and one universe of sets, or do all the consistent candidates exist side by side (non-standard models, a set-theoretic multiverse, that sort of thing)? Then give me the leaves: eight to ten of them, each with the name of the position — platonism in its sparse and plenitudinous varieties, ante rem and in re structuralism, fictionalism, neo-logicism, constructivism, formalism, finitism or predicativism, whatever you think belongs — or just "unnamed" if you're not confident a real view sits at that leaf, and one sentence per leaf on that view's most awkward commitment. Finally, a short note on (a) which combinations of answers your tree allows but nobody actually occupies, and whether that's because the combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored, and (b) any pair of leaves your questions can't really tell apart — I suspect platonism and ante rem structuralism are going to collapse into each other with questions like these, but be honest with me if that's what happens. No bibliography or reading list, we're just talking in a bookshop; the tree, the leaves, and the diagnostics is all I need.
