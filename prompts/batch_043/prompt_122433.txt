I'm a backend engineer with no philosophy training. I got here from the forecasting/ML side, where you score models with log loss and Brier score, and I've ended up believing the accuracy-first story about epistemic rationality. Roughly: there's nothing to being epistemically rational over and above having credences that score well for accuracy, expected accuracy is the thing you maximize, and the usual rules fall out of that instead of being laid down by fiat — your degrees of belief should hang together as probabilities, you should update by conditioning on what you learn, you should set your credences to the chances when you know them. What I like about it is that it turns "why *these* norms?" into an answerable question instead of a stare-down.

Two things are nagging at me though, both from work, and I want to know whether they're real defeaters or just me not getting it.

First: the score depends on the output space. Same forecaster, same day — "will it rain tomorrow" as a binary gets you one Brier score; splitting it into drizzle/light/heavy gets you another; ten millimetre buckets gets a third; there's no privileged one. The accuracy arguments all seem to presuppose a fixed space of possibilities and then prove things *inside* it, but nothing in the view says which space to use, and I can't see how you'd even compare accuracies across different carvings. If "maximize expected accuracy" has no determinate content until someone hands you a partition, and the theory can't say which one, that feels like it might be fatal rather than just awkward. Maybe there's a boring answer (use the finest partition available to you, or the one generated by the propositions you actually have attitudes toward) — but then the view looks committed to something I think is false: that a real agent should always be refining its hypothesis space as finely as it can, with no credit given for the compute and working memory that costs. An engineer hears "ideal agent is maximally fine-grained, accuracy is the only currency, cost is irrelevant" and hears a spec for something that would never ship.

Second, smaller and probably dumber: in ML we evaluate with top-1 accuracy all the time — did you put the most mass on the right answer, yes or no. Obviously that's not a proper scoring rule; optimizing it makes you report certainty on your single best guess no matter what you actually think. But it made me notice that the accuracy-first program gets its verdicts by restricting itself to strictly proper rules, and there are infinitely many of those that don't agree about everything. So part of me thinks the trick is being done in the choice of the family, not in the derivation: pick the measures that already encode "cohere and condition" and of course you derive "cohere and condition." Is that the famous "which measure?" objection, is it as strong as it feels to me, or am I confusing myself?

So: what would count against this view? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, I want the ones that have arguably already shown up, and I want to know which ones an actual card-carrying accuracy-first person would *concede* as hitting them versus brush off as a category error — not just what its critics say about it. And be straight with me about whether the framework has been hedged to the point where it no longer risks anything, and whether that matters for something making this kind of foundational claim.
