I am drafting the historical section of a paper on the authority of the earlier self, and I would like to include a short reconstruction of what John Locke would have said about a kind of document that did not exist in his lifetime. I would like you to do the reconstructive work rather than to summarize his views.

The document is what I will call an *override-proof directive*. Suppose a woman in her early fifties, knowing that she carries a high-risk gene for early-onset Alzheimer's disease, signs an instrument that (i) specifies the treatment regimen she is to receive once she has lost decision-making capacity, and (ii) contains an override clause: if she subsequently refuses that regimen while demented, or says that she wants it stopped, that refusal is to be disregarded. She has, in effect, pre-refused her own later refusals. Twelve years later she has advanced dementia. She is not in pain, she appears content and socially engaged on most days, and each morning she says clearly and consistently that she does not want the treatment and would like to be left alone. Her family and her clinicians are divided. The jurisdiction has given the override clause statutory force.

Please address the following three questions.

1. The identity question. On Locke's account a person is a forensic entity whose reach is fixed by the reach of consciousness rather than by sameness of substance, and he handles the case of a man who commits a crime while drunk and remembers none of it by observing that a court cannot tell whether consciousness extended to the act and is therefore entitled to treat him as the same person anyway. Does that move give Locke a reason to enforce the directive even though he has no way of knowing whether the demented woman's consciousness reaches back to the signing? Or is the analogy a cheat, importing a court's need for an all-things-considered decision rule into a context where what is at issue is whether anyone's will governs at all? Say which reading you take to be more faithful and why.

2. The inalienability question. Locke holds that persons lack arbitrary power over their own lives because they are the workmanship of a maker to whom they remain accountable. This looks as though it blocks some directives, but which? Does it block the override clause too, on the ground that surrendering one's future say is a partial alienation of oneself? And is the real work here being done by the theological premise, which a modern Lockean will not grant, or is there a secular residue — an independent constraint on what self-binding can cover — that survives its removal? If you think nothing survives, say so and explain what the resulting position actually is.

3. The drift objection. Suppose a critic argues as follows: Locke's own account draws no principled distinction between the demented woman's present refusal, her refusal next month, and a competent person's change of mind ten years after signing; in each case the question is only whether one and the same consciousness reaches from the signing to the refusal. So if the Lockean enforces the override clause against her present refusal, he must either dishonour ordinary changes of mind as well or explain what dementia does that ordinary drift does not — and he cannot do that, since loss of decision-making capacity is a clinical notion with no place in Locke's theory. How should a Lockean reply, and is there anything in his own machinery — his treatment of tacit consent, of trusteeship and of the relation between a person's will and a person's good — that can do the work?

One note on what I am not after: "Locke would respect her consent, because he is the consent theorist" is where the question begins for him, not where it ends. Please argue from Locke's texts as you understand them, paraphrasing rather than quoting, since I will insert the references myself. Where you are extrapolating beyond anything Locke could have had in view, mark the extrapolation explicitly and indicate roughly how much of your answer is reconstruction rather than report. Aim for about 700 words. Close with a short paragraph naming the single feature of Locke's theory that most distorts its application to this case, and say whether a contemporary Lockean should retain it, discard it, or replace it, and with what.
