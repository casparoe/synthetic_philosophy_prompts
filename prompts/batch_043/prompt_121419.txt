I am writing to ask for a piece of translation work that I expect to be difficult, and I would be grateful for an answer that is careful rather than quick. Please write in American English.

Let me tell you who I am so that you can aim the answer properly. I have no academic background in philosophy; what I know of it I have picked up on my own, reading slowly and usually a long way behind. What I do have is fifteen years of making maps as a serious amateur: topographic sheets and small-craft charts for a regional trail atlas, a lot of time spent on projections, datums, coordinate systems, and the trade-offs between preserving shape and preserving area. The vocabulary I actually think in is this one: the ground versus the chart; projections, datums, and coordinate grids; scale and generalization; conformal versus equal-area distortion; an atlas as a set of overlapping charts with transition rules between them; the difference between a feature of the ground and an artifact of how I drew it; survey data versus interpolation; and the standing worry about what counts as ground truth when every measurement is made through an instrument.

Recently I have been reading about the debate over structural realism in the philosophy of science, and I want it restated in that vocabulary. As I currently understand the landscape, there are two main positions. The moderate one says that our mature scientific theories get the mathematical structure of the world right, while the nature of the things supposed to occupy that structure is something we should stay agnostic about. The stronger one says that there is no object level at all: structure is what there is, and our talk of objects is a façon de parler derived from it. Two arguments seem to push people in this direction. First, unless the structure of a successful theory somehow matches the structure of the world, the theory's record of novel prediction would be an inexplicable coincidence. Second, the historical record: theory after theory was empirically triumphant while the unobservable stuff it posited — ethers, fluids of heat, and the like — later turned out not to exist, and yet the equations of the discredited theory reappear as a special case inside its successor. Two worries pull back the other way: the question of what the structure is a structure *of*, if there are no objects; and a worry I have half-understood, to the effect that a purely structural claim may be nearly vacuous, because given enough distinguishable things in the world you can always relabel them so that they instantiate whatever formal pattern you like, unless something further constrains which real relations the theoretical relations must be.

Here is what I would like from you.

First, take those four items in turn — the argument from predictive success, the argument from the historical record, the moderate structuralist position, and the strong one — and restate each in my vocabulary as faithfully as you can. Do not quietly improve the arguments while translating them; if a claim only fits once you have added something that was not in the original, say so explicitly at that point.

Second, two places where I especially want the residue report.

The vacuity worry. In my terms I hear it like this: saying that the ground has the same structure as my chart says almost nothing unless I pin down which marks on the chart have to stand for what on the ground, and require that correspondence to be systematic across the whole atlas. Please tell me whether that is a fair rendering, whether stating it in cartographic terms makes the worry look stronger or weaker than it looks in the philosophers' own terms, and whether my framing suggests a fix that their framing hides — or only a fix that works for maps and not for theories.

A distinction of my own, which I have been calling "datum versus distance." Some features of a chart are obviously artifacts of my choices: the grid, the projection, the datum, the scale at which I generalize. My suspicion is that the debate has been asking how much structure survives theory change, when the more useful question is which parts of the structure are projection and which are ground.
