You are a professional philosopher and an experienced examiner who is known for setting questions that cannot be answered by reproducing a summary of the reading; assume that role for this task and think carefully before you write. I am putting together the final for my third-year seminar on killing, letting die, and the shape of agency. Last year's cohort was a disaster in a specific way: every single paper rehearsed the same moves — that some people think the distinction between doing and allowing harm is morally significant while consequentialists deny it, that we can ask whether the agent intervenes in a course of events or merely fails to prevent it — and I could not tell the strong students from the weak ones, because the strong ones had nothing better to do with their time than decorate the same summary. So I need one essay question, answerable in ninety minutes, closed book, that forces a candidate to do actual philosophy: to take a position on what the distinction consists in, to test that position against cases that differ in only one respect, and to say what the distinction would have to be tracking in order to carry the moral weight people want it to carry. I was talking about this with a friend who works in clinical ethics, and she pointed out something that I think is the seed of the question: whether a clinician counts as killing or letting die often seems to turn on facts that look morally arbitrary — whether a treatment has already been started, or whether a machine happens to be wired so that it runs until stopped rather than stopping unless held. That observation is what I want the candidates to chew on.

Here is the raw material I want you to build the question out of; please feel free to sharpen it, rename the cases, or drop anything that does not earn its place. (1) The Standing Order: a mis-entered flag in a hospital's automated dosing system means that unless a nurse overrides it within ninety seconds, a patient receives a lethal dose. Nurse N sees the alert, knows the one-keystroke override, and does nothing. Now vary only one thing at a time: N herself entered the flag; a colleague entered it; the system generated it randomly. Does N kill or let die, and does the answer to that descriptive question shift across the three variants? (2) The Extubation: a ventilated patient for whom continued treatment is futile dies within minutes if the tube is withdrawn, and would have died in just the same way if the tube had never been inserted. Withdrawing is classified as letting die, and so is never starting — which seems to make the classification hostage to whether the intervention happens to have already begun. (3) The Screening: one hospital administrator declines to fund a screening programme, leaving a known 2 per cent mortality risk in place; another diverts money from a working programme and thereby raises mortality from 2 per cent to 2.1 per cent. If what we do and allow at the level of outcomes is what matters, the two look very different; if what matters is each patient's ex ante risk, the two look worryingly alike. And (4), the thing I could not answer myself: one of my students proposed what she called the Locked Baseline View — that whether an agent is doing harm or merely allowing it is settled not by what the agent does at the fatal moment but by the last earlier choice at which some agent had an unforced option to alter the course of events, so that in the Standing Order the relevant agent is whoever entered the flag, not the nurse who declines to override. She claimed this explains why we treat the nurse leniently. I suspect it makes the distinction hostage to arbitrary history — to who happened to act first, to whether the last unforced choice was made by a child or by someone with no duty at all, or by a machine whose default was set decades ago — but I could not give her a clean argument, and I want my students to try.

Please do the following. First, brainstorm two or three possible cruces for the question and pick the one that most resists a memorized answer; tell me briefly, in a line or two, what you picked and why. Then write the question itself, as it would appear on the paper: a short, clean stem, the cases in the form you think works best, and the sub-questions in the order that makes the reasoning compulsory rather than optional. Build in at least one instruction that blocks the obvious escape routes — for instance, a candidate should not be able to score by saying that the doctrine of double effect settles it (that is a claim about intention and foresight, not about what the agent does), nor by announcing that the distinction is real and then leaving it at that. After that, write a model answer of roughly 800 to 900 words, in the voice of a genuinely strong student rather than as an outline: it should state a criterion for the doing/allowing distinction, run it against the paired cases, take a stand on the Locked Baseline View, and offer a positive proposal about what the distinction would have to be tracking — the agent's authorship of a lethal sequence, or the victim's claim over the agent's agency, or what would have happened anyway, or something better — defended against one serious objection. It should not lean on any published author by name; I want something a candidate could argue from scratch with no reading. Finally, give me a marking scheme out of 20, organised as five must-hit points with the marks each is worth, where each point names the specific move an A answer must make, the move that would hold an answer at a B, and the mistake that should cap it at a C; add one line per point on how candidates typically get it wrong, since I want to be able to write useful comments in the margin. Please check, before you finish, that your model answer would actually earn full marks under your own scheme, and tell me if you had to adjust either to make them consistent.
