I'm a PhD student in philosophy, and I'm trying to decide whether to develop a short paper I sketched last term for a seminar on chance and probability, or to drop it and start over on something safer. The draft argues for something I've been calling "Prospective Deference": roughly, the idea that deference to objective chance shouldn't be hostage to which time you happen to be standing at, so that if you know you will be required to align your credence with the chances at some later time, that fact constrains you now. My advisor's verdict on the central argument was that it is "either trivial or broken, and possibly both," which I assume was mostly a joke, but I'd like an outside read before I invest more time. One thing I especially can't tell: whether this is genuinely unclaimed territory or whether I have rediscovered, in clumsier form, something that everyone in the area already knows. Please don't give me citations, titles, or a reading list — I have library access myself — just tell me in your own words whether the move is familiar and, if it is, what it usually goes by.

Here are the notes, more or less as they stand.

Background. Lewis's Principal Principle says, in the form I'm using, that where ch_t(A) is the objective chance at time t of A, and E is admissible evidence, a rational agent's credence in A, conditional on the proposition that ch_t(A) = x together with E, should be x. Admissible evidence is roughly evidence whose bearing on A goes entirely by way of its bearing on the chances of A. I want to rely on one ground-clearing assumption: since admissibility is essentially a matter of what a piece of evidence is about, any evidence whose content concerns nothing but the chances — a readout of the chances themselves, say — is automatically admissible.

The proposal. Call it Prospective Deference, or PD: for any later time t', an agent at t whose evidence E is admissible ought to set her credence in A, given E, equal to her own expectation at t of the value of ch_{t'}(A). If you will be required to align with the chances tomorrow, that requirement should reach backwards and constrain you today.

Why it may be trivial. Suppose chances at different times hang together in the following way: there is a single probability measure over histories, and the chance function at a time is just that measure conditionalized on the history up to that time. Then the chance functions line up so that the current chance of A is the expectation of any later chance of A. Given that, the number PD tells you to adopt just is the current chance, and PD collapses into the ordinary Principal Principle. I take this to be the "trivial" half of my advisor's complaint.

Why it may not be. The collapse leans on that unity assumption, and it also requires that the agent's later evidence be admissible. More interestingly, there seem to be cases where there is no current chance to defer to but there will be a later one. Imagine a system that passes at noon into a state that the chance laws of its world simply do not cover — the laws assign no chance at all to how long it lingers there or to what it does next — and that by one o'clock it will have settled into one of two covered states, U and D, in which the chance of a later result R is respectively 0.9 and 0.1. At noon nothing has a chance of R; the chance is gappy. The Principal Principle says nothing. But if my confidence that the system will settle into U rather than D is 0.5, PD tells me to set my credence in R at 0.5 times 0.9 plus 0.5 times 0.1, which is 0.5. So PD appears to extend chance-deference into a region where the Principal Principle has no application at all.

Worries I can already see. First, in the gappy case the output is not dictated by the chances alone: had my confidence in U been 0.3, PD would have returned 0.34, so it isn't obvious that PD is a deference principle rather than an instruction to mix chance-deference with ordinary subjective uncertainty. Second, there is the undermining problem: if some of what I will learn later is itself evidence about the chances, admissibility may fail, and then PD seems to tell me to defer prospectively to evidence that would be inadmissible if I had it now, which looks incoherent. Third, I don't know how widely the unity assumption is accepted, or whether some accounts of chance deliberately reject it, in which case my collapse argument has no force and the whole thing turns on a contested piece of metaphysics.

What I'd like from you is exactly five numbered comments, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no conclusion — one comment on each of the following, in this order: (1) whether my statement of the Principal Principle and of admissibility is accurate, including the ground-clearing assumption about chance-focused evidence; (2) whether the collapse argument is sound as stated, or whether it quietly assumes something it shouldn't; (3) whether the gappy case is coherent and whether PD really does yield a determinate answer there; (4) whether this idea is already familiar under some other description, and if so what the standard way of drawing the distinction would be — again, no references or bibliography; (5) whether some version of it is worth a semester of work, and if so what single change you would make to the proposal to get it there.
