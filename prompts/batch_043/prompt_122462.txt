The question I set was:

"On Monday a potter takes a single lump of clay, C, and moulds it into a statue, S, which stands on a plinth. On Tuesday a curator knocks it over and stamps it flat into a shapeless slab. On Wednesday the potter takes the very same matter and moulds it back into a form indistinguishable from Monday's; call the Wednesday object S*. (1) Set out the standard argument from Leibniz's Law for the conclusion that S is not identical with C, and say how each of the following three responses handles that argument: the constitution-without-identity view, the sortal-relativity view, and the eliminativist view that there is no statue but only matter arranged statue-wise. (2) Now consider four claims: (a) C exists on Monday, Tuesday and Wednesday; (b) S exists on Monday, ceases to exist on Tuesday, and does not exist on Wednesday; (c) S* is numerically identical with S; (d) at no time are there two material objects occupying exactly the same region of space. For each of the three responses, say which of (a)–(d) it must deny, and say where you think the costs are lowest, with reasons. (3) Finally, in one paragraph: does anything in the argument of part (1) turn on the matter's being clay rather than, say, bronze cast into a mould?"

The answer, numbered for convenience but otherwise as submitted:

1. The argument I'll call the standard argument has three steps. First: on Monday, S and C occupy exactly the same region of space and are made of exactly the same matter — every bit of matter in the one is in the other. Second: S and C differ in their properties. C could have survived being stamped flat on Tuesday, since a shapeless slab of clay is still the same clay, whereas S could not have survived that, since a statue cannot survive the loss of its form. And C has a temporal property that S lacks: it existed on Sunday, before there was any statue. Third: by Leibniz's Law, if S were identical with C, then whatever is true of the one would be true of the other; something is true of C that is not true of S; so S is not identical with C. One caveat about the second step: it is no use appealing to the property of being a statue, because the defender of identity will simply say that she is describing a single thing twice over. The disagreement has to be located in modal or temporal properties, not in the sortals we happen to reach for — which is why the argument is standardly run as a modal argument.

2. There is a worry about what explains the difference in step two. At any given moment on Monday, S and C appear to differ in no microphysical respect: same particles, same arrangement, same region. So the constitution theorist owes us an account of what grounds the difference in persistence conditions, and the answer has to be that the difference is a difference in the kinds the two objects belong to: to be a statue is to carry a different persistence condition from that carried by being a lump of clay. That is not a difference in what there is at a time, but in what would have had to happen for the thing to go on existing. I take it that a satisfying version of the view would have to say more about how kind-membership grounds persistence conditions, and that this is the weakest joint in it.

3. The three responses divide as follows. Constitution-without-identity accepts the argument and its conclusion: on Monday there really are two material objects in that region, spatially coincident, S constituted by C but not identical with it. Sortal-relativity blocks the argument at the third step, on the grounds that an identity statement is only complete when a sortal is supplied: S is the same clay as C without being the same statue as C, so Leibniz's Law cannot get a grip on the bare claim that S is C. The eliminativist refuses the first step, since on her view there is no second object for the clay to be compared with — there is clay arranged statue-wise and that is all. (And having given up statues, she should for consistency give up the clay as well, since if there is no statue there is nothing for the clay to be the matter of.)

4. As for the four claims: (a) is given up only by a view more extreme than any of the three, since a lump of clay plainly survives flattening and re-forming, and both the constitution theorist and the sortal-relativist can say that C is there on all three days. (b) the constitution theorist accepts happily — on Monday there is a statue, on Tuesday there is not — while the eliminativist will reject it as false rather than as costly, there being no such object to begin with. (c) is the difficult one, and I think it is true. What makes S* the same statue as S is that the same matter has been put back into the same form, and if constitution is a matter of the clay's being arranged statue-wise rather than a matter of brute spatio-temporal continuity, then restoring the arrangement is enough to restore the object: the gap on Tuesday is a gap in the statue's career, not the end of it. So the constitution theorist should accept (c), and the eliminativist will deny it along with (b).

5. (d) is where the constitution view is most exposed, since it says that on Monday there are two things in the same place at the same time. But I do not think this is as bad as it is made to sound: the statue and the clay are not two unrelated objects competing for the same space; they are one and the same portion of reality picked out under two descriptions, and 'constitution' is just the name we give to that relation. Read as the claim that there are not two independently existing things in the region, the constitution theorist can accept (d) as well, and the sortal-relativist accepts it trivially.

6. On part (3), I do not think clay matters. What the argument needs is a matter-term and an artifact-term that both pick out something in the same region at the same time, and a bronze cast together with the bronze that makes it up will serve just as well; so will the case of a person and her body, where the same question about differing persistence conditions arises in a different form. The clay case is only easier because English gives us a mass term, 'clay', which lets us talk about the matter without committing ourselves to a countable object, so that the coincidence claim sounds less like double counting than it does with 'bronze'. The real moral is that the argument is about persistence conditions and not about pottery, and the eliminativist still owes an account of why we talk as if there were statues.
