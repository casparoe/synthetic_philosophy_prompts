ok so, context: I'm not an academic. I got into this stuff through a podcast, then some encyclopedia articles, then arguing with people in a Discord reading group where most everyone is further along than me. They've asked me to write my position on mental causation down in one place so they can take it apart, and before I post it I want to know whether it even hangs together. Sorry this is long.

Here's roughly where I've landed:

1. The physical world is causally closed. Any physical event that has a cause at all has a physical cause that's enough, given the way things were already set up, to bring it about. Nothing nonphysical pushes neurons around.

2. Mental properties are not physical properties. The multiple realizability thing convinces me: an octopus, or an alien, or something built out of silicon could be in pain without having our neurons. I'll admit I've never been clear whether this commits me about the individual event too — whether my pain right now is literally one and the same event as some neural event of mine — or only about properties in general.

3. Mental things really do cause physical things, in the ordinary full-strength sense. My decision to raise my arm caused my arm to go up. My nerves about the interview caused me to fumble it. Not "caused" in some watered-down sense.

4. I definately don't want to say every intentional action is overdetermined — two separate sufficient causes, a mental one and a physical one, lined up every single time. That seems like saying the gunshot and the heart attack kill him by coincidence over and over again.

5. I also don't like the move where people say the mental is "causally relevant" or "explanatorily relevant" but doesn't actually cause anything. As far as I can tell that's epiphenomenalism with better PR, and if it doesn't cause, I think we've lost the thing we wanted.

6. What I do like — and this part is my own idea, I don't know if it's a real position, I call it the two-ledger view — is this: causing is difference-making, and what makes a difference is relative to how finely you're describing things. At the person-level ledger, "she decided to wave, so she waved" is just true, fully true, not a shadow of anything. At the neural-level ledger, "these neurons fired, so those muscles contracted" is also just true. Neither one is the real story behind the other.

7. But also: causation is one relation. I don't want to end up saying there are two kinds of causing, physical causing and mental causing. One relation, described at different grains. That's supposed to be the whole point of the two ledgers.

8. The pain case, which is the one I care most about: when I touch a hot pan, I want to say it's the hurting itself — the actual nasty feel of it, not whatever physical state realizes it — that makes me pull my hand back and say ouch. The feel is what does the work.

9. A case verdict: even if I would have waved anyway — say I also had a twitch that would have produced the exact same wave a moment later — I still want to say my decision caused that wave.

10. Methodological: if an argument's conclusion is that nobody's decisions have ever caused anything, I treat that as a reason to throw out a premise rather than as a discovery. I'm not accepting that.

So: does this hang together? Go through it and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and how badly — and this matters just as much — which ones only look like they clash but are actually fine together, because I genuinely can't tell the difference anymore and I've wasted a lot of evenings on the wrong ones. For each real tension, say what I'd have to give up and what that costs me. Rank them by how much trouble I'm actually in instead of listing them all as equally bad, and if there's one revision that cleans up the most, say which.

Please don't just hand me the standard menu of replies to the exclusion argument (I've read those and they all sound fine in the abstract); I want to know where my particular combination breaks, and especially whether the two-ledger thing in 6 and 7 actually does any work or is just a restatement of the problem. Write it in plain American English, and don't go easy on it — I'd much rather be told I'm wrong then be told I'm doing great.
