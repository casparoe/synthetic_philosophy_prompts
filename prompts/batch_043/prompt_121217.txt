I chair the clinical ethics committee at a children's hospital with a level IV neonatal service and a long-term ventilation unit. We are consulted between thirty and forty times a year, and in roughly half of those consultations the question we are really being asked is not what should be done but whether somebody's judgement is a considered value or a bias: a clinician's recommendation not to escalate care for a child with profound cognitive impairment, where the family alleges bias against disability; a team's settled description of a mother as drug-seeking or non-compliant; a parent's refusal of an intervention the team regards as plainly in the child's interest; once or twice a year, one of our own standing practices. We answer within about forty-eight hours, in a note that goes in the chart and that a family can request, addressed to an attending physician who is free to ignore us. The committee is eight people — two physicians, a nurse, a social worker, a chaplain, a lawyer, a parent representative and me — who meet for ninety minutes and none of whom, except me, has studied philosophy. So I need a procedure rather than a theory: something that gives roughly the same answer in different hands, runnable by people who have never thought about this, and honest about the places where it cannot substitute for judgement.

Here is what I have cobbled together, which I suspect contains fewer distinct tests than it appears to and at least one that is actively misleading. One: the swap test — do we reach the same judgement about a relevantly similar patient who lacks the suspect feature? Two: the stated-reason test — can the holder give a reason that mentions neither the suspect feature nor anything that tracks it, with the obvious regress, since every reason can be re-described as a proxy, so that I also need a stopping rule for proxy-chasing. Three: the reversal test — would the holder accept the judgement from the position of the person it burdens? Four: the counter-evidence test — can the holder say in advance what would count against the judgement, rather than naming it after the fact? Five: endorsement under reflection — does the judgement survive unhurried, informed reconsideration, which I think is close to useless as I have stated it, because it insulates reflective people from the charge and convicts people mainly for being inarticulate. Six: convergence among judges differently situated, especially judges who share the suspect feature. Seven: a role test, since the same psychological state seems to me a permissible preference in a parent and a disqualifying partiality in a clinician or in us. Eight: something I have been calling exposure asymmetry — a judgement is more suspect when the cost of its being wrong falls entirely on people who had no say in it while the holder is insulated from that cost — though I cannot tell whether this is a test for bias or merely a reason to be careful.

The difficulties I most want handled are these. Every test seems to presuppose some view about which features are genuine reasons, so that calling a preference a bias threatens to mean only that the committee dislikes it; I want the procedure to say explicitly whose normative baseline it uses — the hospital's stated commitments, the duties of the role occupied, the law, the affected parties — and how it avoids being a rubber stamp for our own considered preferences. I want the line between process and content kept straight: an unflattering causal history does not by itself make a judgement wrong, a sound judgement and a distorted one can be the same judgement, a true generalisation can still be a bias when it is applied to an individual in place of evidence about that individual, and a base rate can itself be the residue of earlier unfairness rather than an independent justification. And I want to know what bias even means when the subject is a rule rather than a person, since a policy has no psychology.

I do not want a survey of theories of bias and I cannot use citations: my committee has no library and I have no way to check them. What I want is a decision procedure, in the order I should run it, with what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where it terminates in a clear yes or no, and where it has to stop and hand the question to a person because it is a question of values rather than facts. Tell me which of my eight moves to keep, which to drop, and which are secretly the same move; give me the stopping rule for proxies; and tell me how the procedure weights the two errors it can make, calling a genuine value a bias and letting a bias through in the costume of a value, given that it cannot minimise both and that we cannot ask the family to decide that question for us.

Then run it on three cases, without softening the results — I would rather have one case come out against the party the committee is temperamentally inclined to favour than three tidy ones.

The first: a four-year-old, ventilator-dependent after a hypoxic brain injury. The ICU team recommends against tracheostomy and chronic ventilation, giving as their reason that she will never recognise us and that this is not a life. The parents allege bias against cognitive disability, and it is a matter of record that the same team recommended exactly this escalation three years ago for a child with comparable physical dependence and intact cognition. The parents' own position rests on a religious conviction about the sanctity of life; the team's rests on a view about quality of life. Both look like preferences.

The second: parents of a nine-year-old with a progressive neuromuscular disease insist on a gastrostomy tube and full resuscitation against unanimous team advice. The team's private view is that the parents are performing for a large online audience that sends them money, and are partly motivated by guilt over an earlier decision concerning an older sibling. The parents' stated reason is, again, a religious conviction. The committee is temperamentally on the side of families.

The third: our own unit's rule that home ventilation is offered only where a family can name two trained caregivers and lives within an hour of the hospital. It is justified by readmission statistics — statistics collected under a system in which home-nursing hours were themselves allocated by a distance rule. In practice it excludes single-parent households, families in crowded housing, and rural families.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: which of these questions it will answer badly no matter how carefully we run it, and what we should say in the chart when we reach that point.
