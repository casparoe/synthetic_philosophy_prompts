I've been working my way through the back catalogue of a podcast where a moral philosopher takes listener letters about whether someone really wants the thing they say they want, or whether it's internalized something-or-other. Her verdicts so far, on the episodes I've got to:

- a woman married at 16 who doesn't want her own daughter educated past primary school: bias, don't defer
- an adult refusing a life-saving blood transfusion on religious grounds: respect it
- a 19-year-old with untreated severe depression who says he wants to be left alone: bias
- a Deaf adult, native signer, active in the Deaf community, declining a cochlear implant: respect it. When a later letter asked about parents choosing for a Deaf toddler she said that's a different question and wouldn't rule
- a 62-year-old man who's done the same factory job since he left school and says he never wanted anything else: respect it, and she got visibly annoyed when her co-host muttered something about false consciousness
- a devout Catholic woman who says she doesn't want to be ordained and feels no sense of loss: bias

Next week's letter is the one I care about. N., 26, grew up in a tight-knit religious minority, left at 18 for a degree in a big city, dropped out after a year, went home, married at 21, and now says she wants a big family and never to work outside the home. There's no family pressure on her any more — they'd support her if she left — but she has no income of her own. Her old university friends tell her she's just internalized her community's norms, and she wants to know wether they're right to discount what she says she wants.

I think she's running some version of: a stated preference deserves deference only if the alternative was a live option for her when the preference formed, not merely legally permitted and socially unthinkable. My partner thinks she's just deferring to whatever anyone says about their own life and only overriding when there's an identifiable distorting mechanism like depression or fear. Those come apart on N., which is definately why I'm asking.

So: which way does she go, and how confident are you? Set out the rules that fit her verdicts about as well as each other, say what each one predicts about N., and tell me which further letter would actually separate them. If some fact I've left out is the one that would decide it, say what I should be asking N. instead. And if neither rule really fits and her verdicts are just a mess, or if the whole does-she-really-want-it framing is itself the problem, say that plainly rather than fitting a rule to it.
