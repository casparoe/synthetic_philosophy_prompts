I'm the editor of my school newspaper, and I'm trying to write something about a fight in the lunch line three weeks ago — or rather about the argument the fight set off afterward, which I think is the more interesting story. I was sitting right there for the argument and deliberately said almost nothing, both because I want to represent both sides fairly and because I genuinely couldn't tell who was winning. That's why I'm asking you.

The background: one sophomore shoved another over something he'd said about his sister. The kid who got shoved reported it to the dean instead of shoving back, and the dean suspended both of them under our zero-tolerance rule. Most of the school thinks reporting was obviously the right call; a noticeable number of kids privately think it was pathetic. My two friends — I'll keep their names out of it — spent about forty minutes going at each other over exactly this. They both referred to the honor / dignity / victimhood way of talking about cultures of norms, and they spelled that part out between them at some length, so please don't give me a summary of it: I have that. What I need is a verdict on the moves they actually made, which I've tried to reconstruct as faithfully as I can:

(1) Elena opened by saying that "handle it yourself" is the thing that makes school violence self-perpetuating. Someone gets shoved over an insult, feels he has to answer it or lose his standing, and now two people are suspended instead of one. A norm that converts every insult into a possible fight isn't just different from a norm that routes disputes through an authority — it's worse, and she said she was willing to say that out loud rather than calling it a matter of taste.

(2) Tobias replied that she was counting the failures and ignoring the function. The point of the code, he said, isn't the fight, it's the insults that never get said. A reputation for retaliating is insurance bought in advance, and it only works if you're actually willing to pay out when the moment comes — which means you can't evaluate it by looking at the fights that happened. That's like judging fire insurance by the fires and ignoring the houses that didn't burn.

(3) Elena said the insurance is only available to people who can credibly be dangerous. If you're small, or you're a girl, or you have more to lose than the other guy, the same code tells you to swallow it quietly. So it isn't a general institution for keeping peace; it works best for the people least likely to need it, and it offloads the entire cost of order onto whoever got hit.

(4) Tobias said that was the best point in the exchange and that he'd grant it as a criticism — but that it's a criticism about who pays, not about whether the code is justified where it actually shows up, namely places where the authorities can't help you or will penalize you for asking. Our dean suspended both kids. From the kid who got shoved, the official channel wasn't neutral, and he said he didn't see why a norm that hands disputes to a biased authority is morally better than one that settles them locally.

(5) Elena said that move makes his position unfalsifiable: if the code is rational when institutions are weak and irrational when they're strong, he isn't defending honor norms anymore, he's saying "do whatever works," which quietly refuses to rank the cultures at all. She then said she did want to rank at least one of them: she thinks the victimhood version is worse than both, because it keeps the third-party machinery of the dignity version but drops the part where you're supposed to win on the merits, and the goal becomes being seen as the injured party rather than settling anything.

(6) Tobias said that was too harsh and too tidy at the same time. Appealing to a public audience, he said, is what you do when the official referee is captured; the online version isn't a corruption of the dignity norm, it's what that norm degenerates into without a trustworthy authority. And he added that the three categories are too clean for real life: our school runs all three at once — one set of norms in the hallway, another in the classroom, a third one on Instagram — and nobody in the building actually lives inside just one of them.

(7) Elena said that if he's right about that, then "which culture is better?" was the wrong question all along, and the real question is which norm belongs in which setting. But she also said she'd arrived at that by conceding his point, and she wasn't sure she should have — it felt like she'd lost something important by agreeing that the dean's bias counted as a defense.

What I'd like from you is a move-by-move adjudication: for each of the seven, say whether the claim being made is right, right but doing less work than its author thinks, or genuinely weak, and why. Four things I especially want sorted out:

(a) Does the insurance/deterrence reply in (2) actually answer (1), or does it just relocate the question to "how much violence is the deterrence buying, and is it worth it"?

(b) Is the biased-authority reply in (4) a defense of honor norms, or is it an admission that they're only conditionally justified — and if it's the latter, does that count as answering Elena's charge that he won't rank the cultures?

(c) Each of these cultures seems to have a built-in vice-word for the others' central virtue: the person who reports is a coward, the person who fights back is a thug, the person who publicizes is a manipulator. Is that symmetry a real obstacle to comparing them morally, or just a rhetorical problem that a better description could get past?

(d) Is "the right norm depends on the setting" a genuine answer to the question of whether any of them is morally superior, or is it a way of changing the subject?

Two things I'd also like you to bring in, because neither of them touched them. First: what happens to a reputation-based system when leaving is cheap — muting, blocking, transferring, just moving away? My guess is that cheap exit dissolves the whole deterrent logic, but I can't tell whether that's a point against honor norms or just a fact about modern life that tells us nothing about their merits. Second, and this is my own guess at what both of them missed: they compared the cultures by their worst outputs — the fight, the snitching, the pile-on — but maybe the more revealing comparison is what each one demands from bystanders, the people not in the dispute: to stay out of it, to serve as a witness or an authority, or to publicly take a side. I'd like to know whether you think anything turns on that, or whether I'm just inventing a distinction because I want an ending for my article.

Two final requests. Please keep everything to argument — I can't look anything up and I don't want titles, authors, or a reading list, just your reasoning about which claims are true. And please attach a confidence level to each of your verdicts, then single out the one thing in your answer that you think a specialist would most likely push back on, and say why it's the weak spot.
