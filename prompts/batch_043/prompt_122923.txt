I've got 20 minutes plus maybe 15 of questions at an internal ethics review at the lab I work at, and I'm presenting a half-baked policy principle about when our assistant should refuse. I want to be ready for the room.

The view I'm defending, short version: refusals should be governed by what I've been calling the fallback test — refuse a request only when, conditional on our refusing, the user's expected next-best route to the same end is better than what we'd produce by helping. Sktech of the argument: (1) refusing almost never deletes an option, it reallocates it — to another model, to a search engine, to a forum, to the user doing it themselves badly; (2) so the choice we're actually making isn't help vs. no-harm but us vs. someone worse, or occasionally someone better; (3) so the thing that should drive refusal is the size of that gap — call it the degradation gradient — not the badness of the downstream act taken on its own; (4) and it matters what kind of refusal it is: gatekeeping refusals, where we withhold information or capability the user can get elsewhere, collapse into a race to the bottom, whereas authoring refusals, where we'd be co-writing something that doesn't exist yet, and role refusals, where the assistant declines to occupy a role at all, do different work and shouldn't all be run through the same test. I've got a 2x2 on a slide for that last bit and it is definately not doing the work I want it to.

Who's in the room: two alignment-flavored safety people who think in tail risk and are allergic to expected-value framings, and who will say the gradient is unmeasurable and that I've built a machine for talking yourself into helping; a trust-and-safety lead who has actually handled self-harm and abuse-material escalations, is legally risk-averse, and thinks my principle is a clever way to make the duty of care conditional on what some third party might do; a Kantian-ish philosopher from our advisory board who cares about autonomy and about the assistant being drawn into the wrong kind of relationship with a user, and who won't be moved by "someone worse would have done it anyway"; a product lead who mainly wants to know whether this makes us look arbitrary, i.e. whether two users asking the same thing get different answers and someone screenshotted it; and a red-teamer who will point out that whatever we decide, the capability is in the weights, so the gap I'm leaning on is something an adversary can close on purpose.
