I am writing a one-page self-assessment for genetic counselling trainees, to be completed before a seminar on embryo selection and prenatal testing, and I want it to sort trainees by what they say about cases rather than by which label they would claim. Please build it as a decision tree: four or five branch nodes, each phrased as a concrete question a trainee can answer without knowing where it leads, ending in six to nine named leaves, or "unnamed" where no one defends the combination.

Construct the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of views: begin with the questions and the answer combinations they permit, then locate in that space the views I need covered — procreative beneficence (a moral reason to select the child expected to have the best life), satisficing (a reason only to have a good-enough child), a "negative-only" view (reasons to avoid serious disease but none to maximise wellbeing), parental discretion or procreative liberty, the disability-rights objection that certain selections express a condemnatory message about existing people, and the impersonal no-difference view on which no one is harmed by being selected over — and then diagnose one or two unoccupied combinations: is nobody there because the combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored?

Two distinctions matter for my purposes and each should get a node. First, identity-affecting selection (which embryo to transfer) versus identity-preserving intervention (editing or treating the embryo that will become this very child). Second, what I call cohort expansion: choosing among the embryos one happens to have versus generating or screening more embryos in order to widen the choice set — I want to know whether any of these views makes expansion obligatory, and whether the tree can distinguish "select the best of those available" from "make sure the best available is good enough."

Use at least these three cases: a small projected difference in a polygenic score; declining to transfer an embryo carrying a late-onset condition that the parent also lives with; and selecting against deafness where a deaf couple would welcome a deaf child. Give each leaf one sentence on its most awkward commitment, flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate, and close by telling me which branch question you are least confident is doing real diagnostic work and what argument or case would lead you to redraw the tree.
