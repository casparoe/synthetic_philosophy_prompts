THE QUESTION

Background. On the Friday before lecture week the teacher announces to the class:

"There will be exactly one exam during next week's classes (Monday to Friday), and on the morning of the day of the exam you will not be able to deduce, from this announcement together with the calendar and the fact that no exam has taken place on any earlier day of that week, that the exam is on that day."

Call this announcement A. Throughout, "deduce" means "derive by classical logical consequence", and a set of premises that is inconsistent has everything as a consequence. Assume the students are competent in that sense and that they believe the teacher; do not assume either that they know A or that they do not — that is part of what is at issue.

(a) [6 marks] Set out as carefully as you can the backward argument which concludes that no exam can satisfy A, so that the teacher cannot keep the announcement. Isolate the assumption about the students' reasoning that the Friday step requires, and explain why that step needs it.

(b) [4 marks] Is A satisfiable — is there a day on which the teacher can hold the exam and keep the announcement? Answer this for the one-day version of the announcement (a week containing only Monday), the two-day version (Monday to Tuesday), and the five-day version, and explain what the pattern tells you about the argument in (a).

(c) [6 marks] Let A* be the result of replacing, inside A, the words "from this announcement together with the calendar and the fact that no exam has taken place on any earlier day of that week" with the words "from everything you will know on that morning". Does the argument in (a) go through for A*? Is A* satisfiable, and does the answer depend on what the students have worked out? Say what, if anything, the contrast between A and A* shows about where the difficulty is really located.

(d) [4 marks] Assess the following response: "There is no real paradox. The announcement is simply true on Wednesday, and everything else is a fact about the students' psychology — about what they expect."

THE ANSWER

(a) A says two things: that there is exactly one exam next week, and that on the morning of the exam the date is not a consequence of the evidence base consisting of A itself, the calendar, and the days of that week which have already passed without an exam. Now take Friday. Suppose the exam has not occurred by Thursday evening. Then on Friday morning the students' evidence is A, the calendar, and the fact that Monday to Thursday have passed without an exam; those premises entail that the exam is on Friday, so an exam on Friday would not be a surprise, and Friday is excluded. The step for Friday needs two assumptions: that the students are willing to use A as a premiss (they need it to infer that there is an exam at all, and hence, with Monday to Thursday elapsed, that it is on Friday), and that they are competent, in the sense that anything entailed by their evidence is deducible by them. Given both, Friday goes. Now Thursday: by Wednesday evening the students know that Monday to Wednesday have passed without an exam, and, by the previous step, that Friday is impossible; so an exam on Thursday would be deducible on Thursday morning, and Thursday goes. The same reasoning removes Wednesday, then Tuesday, then Monday. No day survives. The announcement is therefore self-refuting: taking it as a premiss, the students can derive that no exam can satisfy it, which is to say that it is false.

(b) There is nonetheless a model: Wednesday. On Wednesday morning the students' evidence, as A itself fixes it, is A plus the calendar plus the fact that Monday and Tuesday have passed without an exam, and that is compatible with Wednesday, Thursday and Friday, so the date does not follow and they are surprised. The one-day announcement has no model, since with only Monday in the window the date follows from A alone. The two-day announcement does have one: on Monday morning no day of that week has yet elapsed, so the evidence leaves both Monday and Tuesday open. The pattern is that a model exists whenever the window has more than one day. This is the important point: run on the two-day announcement, the backward argument concludes that neither day is possible, yet Monday plainly is possible. So the argument fails, and it fails at the inductive step rather than at the base step: for Thursday to be excluded the students must know that Friday is impossible, and "Friday is impossible" is not part of the evidence base over which A's surprise clause quantifies.

(c) A* is not really a stronger announcement. What bears on the date of the exam is the calendar and the days that have already gone by without an exam; everything else the students will know on that morning — what they had for breakfast, what they think of the teacher — is irrelevant. So the extra words add nothing, the argument in (a) goes through exactly as before, and its conclusion is wrong for exactly the same reason: Wednesday is still a model, since on Wednesday morning the students' evidence still leaves Wednesday, Thursday and Friday open. A and A* stand or fall together.

(d) The response is right. A is true on Wednesday, the students' argument against it fails, and once we see where it fails there is nothing left except the empirical fact that people who have talked themselves into expecting no exam are surprised when one arrives. The puzzle is about expectations rather than about knowledge, and it is no deeper than a conjuring trick. What made it look like a paradox is only that the announcement talks about what the students can work out, which made people think there was something epistemological going on.
