Bit of a random one. I'm in this reading group that's half people from my uni's early American history seminar and half from a self-improvement Discord I got talked into joining, and we're working through Franklin's Autobiography at the moment — the bit where he lays out his project for moral perfection: a list of virtues, each one given its turn for a week, a little ruled book with a column for every day and a black mark whenever he slips, the whole cycle run through a few times a year. My flatmate, who does growth analytics for a living, says the table is just a badly designed experiment and that if Franklin had been serious he'd have built it completely differently: no control condition, no blinding, the person running the intervention is also the person scoring the outcome, a dozen-odd overlapping traits rotated weekly so nothing can be attributed to anything, and the whole thing hopelessly confounded with whatever else was happening to him that year. Someone else in the group replied that he's missing the point, that the self-surveillance *is* the mechanism, and that an arrangement which "worked" by some external measure would be worse for Franklin's purposes. I want to know what Franklin himself would have said to the analytics objection: would he have cared about causal attribution at all, and is there anything in the way he actually set the scheme up — the order he takes the virtues in, the fact that he only ever works on one at a time, the way he talks about cleaning the pages rather than about his character — that suggests he was trying to measure something rather than change something? Also, as far as I remember he more or less concedes near the end that he never arrived at the perfection he aimed at and only thought the attempt made him better and happier, and there's that joke about how even if he had conquered pride he'd probably have been proud of his humility; does that resignation make him more or less likely to take the analytics guy seriously?

The bit I'm more interested in, though, is the modern version. Say you could hand Franklin three things and explain them properly, and he'd got his head round them: (a) streak apps with commitment contracts, where you put money down and it goes to a cause you hate if you break an unbroken daily run; (b) frictionless self-design — deleting the apps, putting the gym kit by the door, locking the biscuits in the shed, arranging your life so the temptation never actually arrives and you never have to exercise self-command at all; (c) an agreeable AI coach that checks in each morning, remembers everything you've told it, never shames you, and gently reframes whatever you did, plus the option of a prescription — stimulants, or one of the new appetite drugs — for the traits he'd have filed under industry and temperance. For each one: would he count it as work on virtue, as cheating, or as something he simply has no vocabulary for? Rank the three, and tell me which is closest to the little book in its mechanism even though it looks nothing like it. I'm definately least sure about (b): on one reading the entire point of his project was to become the sort of person who doesn't need the props, so removing the temptation is removing the exercise; on another reading he was exactly the pragmatic tinkerer who'd rearrange his environment in a heartbeat and think anyone who didn't was being precious about it.

Be explicit about how confident you are in each call, and flag which parts you're reconstructing from what he actually did versus what he'd probably say if you asked him. For the call you're least confident about, say what would change your mind — which consideration, or which detail of his practice, would flip it. And tell me where this whole exercise is most likely to go wrong: what's the failure mode of asking a man who died in 1790 what he'd think about push notifications, and are there questions in the three cases above that his framework just can't register an answer to?
