I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on rationality and the self, and I am writing one of the final exam questions. I want an essay question on transformative experience that a student who has only memorized the standard summary of L. A. Paul's argument cannot answer well. The question should turn on a case I have designed but not seen discussed, and it should force students to locate exactly where the alleged problem sits, rather than restate it.

Here is the case. An agent must choose whether to undergo a personally transformative change, of the kind that on the standard account both teaches her for the first time what a certain experience is like and alters the very preferences she would need in order to evaluate it. Before choosing, she may consult a Predictor: a perfectly reliable device that tells her, without giving her any phenomenal acquaintance with the experience, everything about the outcome, including what her preferences will be afterwards, what she will then judge her own well-being to be, and what her post-transformation self will say about the choice she made. Two variants. In the first, the Predictor reports that she will prefer the outcome and will also endorse having become the person who prefers it. In the second, it reports that she will prefer the outcome but will not endorse having become the person who prefers it: she will regard the change as made by someone whose aims she no longer shares.

Please produce the following.

1. The exam question as it would appear on the paper, with the case, any sub-questions, and a stated limit of about 1,000 words for a 90-minute exam.

2. A model answer of roughly 700 words, which says whether the Predictor dissolves the problem in either variant, defends that verdict against the strongest reply, and uses the contrast between the variants to say what the problem, if there is one, is really about: a deficit of evidence, a failure of phenomenal imagination, the indeterminacy of comparisons across successive selves, or something about the authority of a future self's preferences.

3. A marking scheme out of 20, built around five moves a strong answer must make, with the marks each is worth and a one-line note on the error candidates typically make on each. Then two or three sentences on the difference between A-range and B-range work: the B answer should be the one that reconstructs the standard argument competently but treats the case as merely an information problem, or merely a phenomenal-knowledge problem, and never asks whose preferences are supposed to have authority over the choice.
