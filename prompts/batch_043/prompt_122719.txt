I spent a career as a field ecologist on small-mammal population dynamics, and in retirement I have been reading the literature on the ethics of eating animals. I keep meeting a claim I cannot get straight: that crop cultivation kills so many field animals — mice, voles, insects — that the indirect death toll of a plant-based or chicken-based diet may rival or exceed the suffering in the animals we eat directly, and that beef, much of it raised on pasture and land too poor to plough, is therefore the least bad thing to eat.

I would like an essay that adjudicates this for the specific case of substituting chicken for beef, and that does it with the help of a distinction I have not found made in these terms and would like to see stress-tested. Call a death substitutive when the animal killed would very likely have died anyway, at a comparable age and in a comparably bad way, had the field never been cultivated — the ordinary fate of an r-selected rodent in a landscape already saturated with predators, cold and food shortage — and additive when the cultivation brings about deaths, or a lifetime of them, that would not otherwise have occurred at all. My instinct from the field is that ploughing and harvest mortality in small mammals is largely substitutive, since a population near carrying capacity simply lets its survivors take up the slack, while pesticide use and the annual resetting of habitat are more likely additive at the level of the population; but I also suspect the additivity question is not the suffering question, for two reasons: a substitutive death may still be worse in kind than the death it displaces, and a population held below its carrying capacity is a population of animals who never exist, which changes the sign of everything if their lives are on balance not worth living.

Three things, then. Argue the beef-to-chicken substitution on the animal-welfare channel alone, holding greenhouse gases, land conservation, human nutrition and health strictly out of the case. Say what the substitutive/additive distinction settles and what it fails to settle, including whether it survives the objection that the proper unit of account is the population or the system rather than the individual animal. And give magnitudes: rough orders of magnitude for land per unit protein, feed conversion, stocking densities and typical lifespans, with an explicit statement of which link in the arithmetic is load-bearing and how wrong it would have to be to flip the result. Supply rough numbers where you have them and flag where you are guessing. Finish by committing to a verdict — the conclusion you would actually act on — rather than an appeal to how much depends on further facts.
