You are a philosopher of artificial agency with a strong background in decision theory. I want a diagnosis of a stalled dispute, not a survey and not a verdict; do not cite or name any sources, and reason from the structure of the disagreement. Start by sketching, in a few lines, the candidate explanations for the impasse you find most plausible, then commit to one and defend it.

In the group I work in — we build and evaluate agentic language-model systems — the same argument has not moved in two years. One side says instrumental convergence is close to structural: any system that plans over a long enough horizon under resource constraints will tend to acquire resources, preserve itself and resist modification of its objectives, because those are useful whatever the final goal is; and since final goals are supposed to be orthogonal to capability, nothing about getting smarter pushes a system toward better aims. The other side says this is an artifact of reasoning about idealized agents: the systems we actually have are next-token predictors further shaped by preference tuning, they abandon tasks after a few steps, they generally accept shutdown, and when they do display self-preservation or resource-grabbing it happens in contrived setups where the scaffold or the prompt invited it. Both sides agree that power is instrumentally useful. Neither moves.

Two distinctions I keep coming back to. Call the first scaffold-relativity: the same weights behave like a goal-directed planner inside a deployment scaffold with persistent memory, tools and multi-step objectives, and like a compliant simulator of whatever character the prompt suggests outside one — which would make the scaffold, not the content of the goal, the variable doing the work. Call the second horizon-gating: convergent instrumental behavior may be a threshold phenomenon in effective planning horizon, so that below some horizon myopia dominates and nothing convergent is visible, making both sides' observations consistent rather than opposed.

My hypothesis is that the impasse is a disagreement about which variable the claim quantifies over — agents in general, versus trained systems inside particular scaffolds — plus a hidden empirical question nobody has separated: how much of the observed behavior is explained by training pressure and by the deployment distribution, as against by the structure of the task itself. Test that hypothesis against what each side actually says: is this verbal, a clash of evidence standards, a hidden empirical question, or two questions under one name? And given your diagnosis, say honestly what would count as progress — a distinction, a measurement design, a formal result, a change of question — or argue that none should be expected.
