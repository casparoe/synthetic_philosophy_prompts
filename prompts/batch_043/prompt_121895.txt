I'm not an academic — I work in insurance claims, and I've been chewing on something since an argument at a board-game night that I don't think either of us won. A friend posed what I later learned is called Kavka's toxin puzzle, though he posed it with an extra gadget I haven't seen discussed anywhere: the money is wired the instant a perfectly reliable intention-detector — a brain scanner, say — registers that you have formed a genuine intention to drink, at noon tomorrow, a dose of toxin that will make you miserably ill for twenty-four hours and do nothing else, good or bad, to you. You never have to drink it. The moment you form the intention you are paid and the intention has done all the work it will ever do; at noon tomorrow you will have no reason whatsoever to drink, and you know that now. The gadget, as I see it, is there to block the cheap answers: you can't collect by pretending, by acting as if, or by working yourself into a state you half believe in, because the scanner reads the real state and not its behavioural shadow. My credence that a normally reflective adult can collect the money in that setup is around 0.4; my friend's is around 0.85; and I've started to suspect we aren't disagreeing about the same thing.

So give me your credence that the money can be collected — where collected means the scanner registers a state that really is an intention to drink the toxin, as opposed to a fantasy, a pretense, a piece of self-deception, or a plan already earmarked as one you will abandon — and, more importantly, take it apart. I want a number and one line of justification for at least these: whether there is any conceptual bar to intending what you fully expect not to do (does intending to do something require believing you will do it, or a settledness that partly consists in surviving later deliberation, or does intention come apart from belief and from behavioural disposition cleanly enough for a pure, non-executive intending to be coherent at all); whether, granting that it is coherent, a human being can actually produce such a state at will, given that this case offers no hook to hang it on — no self-deception to exploit, no emotion to whip up, no commitment device to install, since installing one would give you a fresh reason to drink tomorrow and would therefore change the case rather than solve it; whether an intention adopted purely because someone is paying you to have it is still the thing the offer asks for, or whether intending for that kind of reason is as degenerate as believing for pragmatic reasons; and whether an intention formed at nine in the morning with full knowledge that you will rescind it at one minute to noon the next day is an intention at all, rather than something whose time horizon is too short to count. Then combine your terms into the headline number, tell me which single term the number is most sensitive to, and tell me which of the two of us is most likely getting a sub-question wrong rather than weighing them differently.

One more thing, because I think this is where the real action is. Consider the sustained version: same setup, except the scanner checks again at noon and the money is wired only if the intention is still present at that moment — you needn't drink, but you must still mean it. Give me a second number for that version and explain the size of the gap between the two. If the second comes out much lower than the first, what does that tell us about whether the difficulty in this puzzle is really about forming an intention at will, or about keeping one alight when you have already decided to let it go?
