Position I've settled into after years of reading the replication-crisis literature and way too many rationalist comment sections: it's an incentives problem wearing a statistics costume. The disaster came from undisclosed analytic flexibility plus journals that only printed positive results; fix that with registered reports, publishing nulls, and real power, and p < .05 on a pre-committed test is basically fine. The frequentist-vs-Bayesian fight is beside the point.

What would count against that? I want defeaters ranked fatal → merely awkward, split into (a) things that have arguably already happened and (b) things that would have to happen.

Here's the test I've been using, which I want stress-tested. Define a literature's *shrinkage ratio* as the median effect size in pre-registered direct replications divided by the median effect size in the originals, counting only replications the original authors signed off on in advance, so nobody can wave it away as an unfaithful protocol. My view as operationalized: if that ratio climbs toward 1 once flexibility and the publication filter are stripped out, the inference method was never the problem, and if it doesn't, I lose. So: (1) is there some ratio short of 1 that I'd be obligated to concede as a win for "no, it really was the statistics" rather than leftover incentive rot? (2) Is there a real, checkable asymmetry in the other direction — could someone who was explicit about their prior have predicted the shrinkage in advance, in a way a frequentist couldn't, or is "a prior would've saved us" pure hindsight?

Be honest about insulation too. I notice I can dismiss almost any failed pre-registered replication with "it didn't bind the right degrees of freedom" or "power was still overestimated," and I can't tell whether that's a legit defense or the view eating its own tail. Given that I care most about calibration, tell me which of these should move me most: the type M / magnitude-error argument, evidence about how bad scientists' implicit priors actually are, or an actual case where a Bayesian treatment of the same data would have stopped a silly finding from being published.
