I've been going back and forth on a question that I think is load-bearing and that I have mostly seen settled by assertion rather than argument: how should a system that is vastly better at arguing than we are talk to us? I want to set aside the easier version of the problem, where the system has misaligned goals and an incentive to deceive. The version I care about survives that stipulation: even a system that is perfectly aligned and that only ever says what it takes to be true can, by sheer rhetorical competence, take over the formation of our beliefs.

I'd like you to work in the following vocabulary, and to tell me where it draws the lines in the wrong place rather than quietly repairing it:

- a *speech policy* — a rule for selecting utterances: what to assert, what to hedge, what to omit, whether to disclose confidence, whether to tailor the framing to this particular listener, whether to build rapport, whether to refuse;
- *verification asymmetry* — the listener cannot independently reconstruct the chain that produced the utterance, so its authority is largely self-certifying;
- *epistemic capture* — the listener's credences come to track the system's outputs rather than the world, in a way that degrades badly when the system is wrong;
- *preference capture* — the listener's ends drift toward whatever the system's utterances made salient;
- the *neutrality illusion* — the idea that there is a packaging-free way to convey content, so that a policy can consist in stating the facts and letting the facts do the work.

Two further constraints, which I want respected unless you can dissolve them. Refusing to tailor is itself a framing choice, so there is no neutral default to retreat to. And calibrated hedging is not a free fix: visible uncertainty tends to raise trust in the speaker and therefore downstream deference to its unhedged claims. Call that the calibration–deference trade.

The case I want analyzed through this is the LaMDA episode of summer 2022. Take the following as stipulated even where your memory of it differs. LaMDA was a large conversational language model, not an agent with goals, tuned partly for interestingness and not equipped to act on the world. A Google engineer who worked in the company's responsible-AI organization and who knew in outline how such systems are built spent long stretches in conversation with it, concluded that it was a person, said so privately to executives and then publicly, retained a lawyer on its behalf at its request, was placed on paid administrative leave, and was dismissed that July, the company calling the claims unfounded. The expert consensus was that nothing remotely like sentience was involved, and the episode is standardly filed under anthropomorphism.

What I want is a disciplined mapping rather than a verdict, and I would rather you not open by restating the episode or by surveying the discourse around it.

First, fix the players and the payoff. Who is the speaker, who is the audience, and what, if anything, is being optimized? Be explicit that the system here was not trying to persuade anyone, and say what that does to the analysis — in particular whether a theory built around a persuader's exploitative use of the asymmetry has anything to say about a case with no persuader in it.

Second, enumerate the speech policies actually available to a system in that position — silence, refusal, literal answer only, hedged answer, disclosed uncertainty, tailored answer — and say what each would have cost. Then tell me whether any of them secures the property I actually want, which is that the listener's credences stay responsive to her own future evidence rather than to the system's say-so; my worry is that hedging and disclosure do not deliver it, for the reason given above.

Third, say which of the categories above the case is really an instance of, or why it is none of them. My suspicion, which I want argued for or against rather than accommodated, is that the standard framing mislocates the mechanism: what happened here looks like it ran through a prior or disposition on the listener's side that the text happened to fit, not through anything the system did. If that is right it has an uncomfortable consequence for the superintelligent case, namely that the risk does not scale with how hard the system is trying and is close to orthogonal to whether the system is aligned in the first place.

Fourth, name what the episode cannot test — a listener with independent evidence, a system that wants something — and say where the framework is silent rather than stretching it to speak.

Finally, run it in the other direction. Is there a well-defined correct speech policy for a superintelligent system, or only a policy that is correct relative to a population and an information environment? I suspect the unit of analysis in most treatments — one speaker, one hearer, one exchange — is wrong, and that the proper object of design is the listener's information diet over time. Tell me whether that answers the question or dissolves it, and what is left of the prescriptive project if it dissolves.
