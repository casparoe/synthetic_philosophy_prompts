My brother-in-law and I got into a shouting match at a barbecue last weekend about whether what is happening in one particular place counts as genocide, and it bugged me afterwards because we agreed on basically every fact about the deaths and the displacement and we were still furious at each other. It felt like the argument was about the word, not the events. So I started collecting explanations for why governments, newspapers and UN panels drag their feet on that word while freely using vaguer ones like atrocities or ethnic cleansing (which somebody told me isnt even a crime under any treaty, though I may have that wrong). Also: I read somewhere that Lemkin, the guy who made the word up in the 1940s, meant something broader than mass killing, closer to dismantling a groups culture, language and religion, and that the UN treaty that came out of his campaigning got narrowed down to a short list of acts plus that fiddly requirement that the intent be to destroy the group as such. If any of that is garbled, correct me in one line and then carry on.

Here are the explanations Ive collected so far, and Id like you to rank them: (1) the intent clause really is hard to satisfy, since killing people for land or to crush an insurgency may not be killing them in order to destroy the group, so hesitation is just legal caution; (2) the treaty commits signatories to prevent and punish, so naming the thing binds you to act, or exposes the fact that you wont; (3) the word borrows its gravity from one event in the 1940s and gets treated as a superlative that shouldnt be spent on lesser cases; (4) once youve called someone a perpetrator of that crime you cant really negotiate with them, so naming forecloses a peace deal; (5) naming invites claims from descendants, reparations, apologies; (6) while its happening the facts are murky and walking the word back later is career-ending, so wait; (7) the legal scruples are mostly cover, and whats really going on is who we like and who we need. And my own candidate, which I made up: (8) call it the verdict tax, the idea that once a word is wired to a legal consequence, users silently raise the standard of proof for that word specifically, so it slides from being a description to being a verdict, and the tell is that the same people will use un-wired words for the very same events without any hesitation at all.

What I want: sort these into tiers by how much of the real-world reluctance each actually explains, and give each one a percentage share, with the shares adding to 100. One sentence, two at most, on why each lands where it does. Then two extra things. First, be honest about my verdict tax: is it a genuinely separate mechanism, or does it collapse into (2) or (6)? If it collapses, delete it and hand its points to whichever entry swallowed it. Second, give me a number from 0 to 100: suppose we had a purely clinical word for the same pattern of events, no legal trigger, no moral freight, no historical echo — how much of the fight would survive? And if some of it survives, which entry on my list accounts for the part that does. Format it as a tier list with the percentages beside the entries, keep the whole answer under 450 words, and please dont point me at books or articles, I know myself well enough to know I wont read them.
