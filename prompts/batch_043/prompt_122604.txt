I am a graduate student in philosophy, currently taking a seminar on rational choice, and I help run my department's small interview series, in which we talk to people who defend a specific, contested position. Next session our guest is a philosopher-economist who defends the time-average (or ergodic) resolution of the St. Petersburg paradox: the claim that the paradox is an artefact of evaluating a multiplicatively growing quantity by the expectation of its ensemble average, when what an individual agent actually experiences is the growth rate of its own wealth over time; once maximization of expected payoff is replaced by maximization of expected logarithmic growth, a finite, wealth-dependent entry price falls out, and, on her view, this also explains why the standard responses feel unsatisfying.

She is a practiced speaker, and she has three stock moves she deploys very smoothly: that the one-shot case is not really one-shot, since any individual decision belongs to a longer sequence of decisions; that logarithmic utility is here derived rather than stipulated, so her view is not simply the classic Bernoulli response in new clothes; and that the deflationary answer, according to which the paradox dissolves once you notice that any real house has a finite bankroll, explains the wrong phenomenon.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions, ordered as a sequence, that force her to apply her view rather than restate it, and that make those three moves progressively harder to fall back on. For each question, give the answer she is most likely to give, in one line, and then a follow-up that presses on whatever the weakest point of that answer is. In particular, the sequence should cover:

1. The one-shot worry, in its strongest form: the game is offered once, to someone who will never encounter anything relevantly like it again, and who knows this. Anticipate the move to sequences, and design a follow-up that makes it costly.
2. Whether the view is genuinely more than the classic logarithmic-utility response, and whether the derivation of log utility from growth-rate maximization simply relocates the normative choice: why is maximizing the growth rate the rational objective rather than one admissible preference among others, and if it is, has anything been solved rather than restated?
3. The wealth-dependence of the resulting fair price. Press on whether it is a virtue of the view that the same prospect is worth more to a richer player, or a defect that the value of the prospect depends on something extrinsic to it.
4. A comparison with the finite-bankroll response on its own turf. If the house can pay out at most 2^N dollars, the expected payoff of the truncated game is about N dollars, so the deflationary answer makes the price grow additively in the house's cap, whereas her answer makes it grow with the logarithm of the player's wealth. Ask which behaviour is the right one for realistic values, and then a follow-up in which the cap is removed altogether because the counterparty can create the payoff instrument at will, so that only her answer is left standing.
5. A variant in which the player may buy any number m of independent tickets at the same price each, i.e. may realize the ensemble average synchronically, by holding many copies at once, rather than successively over time. Does her criterion return a finite price here, and does that price approach the divergent expected value as m grows? If she answers that the per-trial growth rate is finite and so nothing changes, the follow-up should ask what the optimal m is and whether allowing m to be chosen alters the price she recommends.
6. A second variant, which I would like her to work through rather than characterize: a coin is tossed until it lands heads; if the first heads occurs on toss n, the player receives 2^n/n dollars when n is odd and forfeits 2^n/n dollars when n is even. Here the expectation is the alternating harmonic series, so it converges only conditionally and its value depends on the order of summation, and there is also an unbounded downside tail that can push wealth to zero or below. Her criterion needs a well-defined expectation to be non-ergodic about and needs wealth to remain positive for logarithms to exist, so ask how it extends to this case, whether the extension still returns a determinate finite price, and, if she introduces a bankruptcy floor or absorbing state, whether the view has then quietly become a version of the finite-bankroll answer it was supposed to replace.
7. Whether the view solves the normative question or changes the subject: is there a residual, well-posed question of the form what is the most a one-shot player ought to pay that her criterion declines to answer, and if not, why not.
8. A closing question about what would count against the view: what observation about prudent actual behaviour, or what structural argument, would make her give up the growth-rate criterion, given that it is often criticized for recommending the rejection of ordinary favourable gambles when the stake is a sizeable fraction of the agent's wealth.

Nothing in the sequence should require her to recall a citation, a name, or a bibliographic detail; every question should be answerable from the view itself plus the variant games as I have described them. Assume a listener who has taken one graduate decision-theory course: keep the technical apparatus explicit but do not spend the questions on exposition.
