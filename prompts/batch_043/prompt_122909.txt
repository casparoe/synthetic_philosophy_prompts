I am a backend engineer with no formal philosophy background. I maintain the control software for a large automated insect-rearing facility. Every managed thing in the codebase lives in one config table: a row with an id, a kind tag, a state vector, and a setpoint. The table currently holds climate controllers, humidity controllers, feeding actuators, and the insect colonies themselves. I have been asked to add a welfare budget module: something that decides, when two managed things have competing interests, whose interests win and by how much. My instinct was to make this a number rather than a flag. I wrote a design doc arguing that sentience — and therefore moral standing — comes in degrees, that a colony is not a thermostat, and that we should assign every row a real-valued welfare weight and aggregate.

A colleague read the doc and replied with an argument against me. As far as I can tell every step is one I endorsed, so I want to know which one to take back.

First, the ladder he uses, given in my own terms. Call the depth of a system's counterfactual engagement with its own states its D-score, and imagine a sequence of managed things where each one differs from the last by exactly one small increment of D:

L0: purely reactive. Current output is a function of current input. A bimetallic strip in a thermostat; a line-following robot.

L1: retentive. An internal state persists across time and biases later outputs, so the response to the same input depends on history. Satiety, fatigue, a primed state.

L2: integrating. Aversive and attractive inputs are converted into a single decision variable, so the system will accept a small cost now to avoid a larger aversive state later, or take a longer route to avoid a bad region of state space.

L3: self-referential. The system acts not only on the world but on its own dispositions: it can learn to stop approaching something, can be trained out of a response, and its behaviour changes if you block the change in disposition rather than the stimulus.

L4: diachronic. The system carries a model of itself over time, has preferences about its own future preferences, and its current states are evaluated against a continuing self.

L5: us. An adult human, at the top.

Now his argument:

(1) Gradualism. Moral standing is a function of degree of sentience, and that function is continuous and strictly increasing: no threshold, no jump. Any system with nonzero sentience has nonzero moral weight, and small differences in sentience make for small differences in moral weight.

(2) Tolerance. Take any two systems whose D-scores differ by a small enough amount. Then no moral theory may treat them differently in kind: their moral status is the same, in the sense that whatever protections, tier, or kind of consideration one gets, the other gets too.

(3) Fine grain. The ladder above is such a sequence: consecutive rungs differ by less than the small amount in (2). Justification: each rung adds one increment of counterfactual depth, and no single increment is where anything morally dramatic could be happening — each one is a natural, local extension of the previous one, of exactly the sort that already happens inside a control loop.

(4) By (2) and (3), applied rung by rung from L0 to L5, the thermostat has the same moral status as the adult human. In particular, grading is not merely continuous but flat: no tiering, no thresholds of protection, no distinction between switching off a climate controller and killing a person.

(5) That is absurd, and my design doc needs tiering to do anything at all: the whole point of the module is to say that some rows get protections that other rows do not.

(6) So gradualism (1) is false. Moral status is discontinuous. There is a real threshold somewhere on the ladder.

(7) But (6) does not get me out: wherever I put the threshold, (2) and (3) still tell me that the two rungs it separates differ negligibly, so my placement of it is arbitrary — which is the exact arbitrariness that drove me to gradualism in the first place. And if the best I can do is an arbitrary cutoff, it is hard to see how tiering is tracking anything real rather than being a decision I made.

So: which step do you reject, and why did that step look fine when I wrote it down? Is the argument valid with a false premise, or is there something wrong in the inference from (2) and (3) to (4)? Please price the alternatives honestly — especially what it costs to keep (1), since that is the claim I most want to keep, and what it costs to keep (2), since some kind of tolerance principle seems to be doing all the real work against arbitrary thresholds.

Two further things would make the answer directly useful to me rather than merely interesting:

Say whether the argument is trading on that, and whether the two jobs need separate machinery.

Tell me whether the reasoning above is about how sentience is distributed in the world or about what I can know about it, because those give me very different engineering instructions, and a threshold chosen under uncertainty is a different object from a threshold in nature. I would like, at the end, a concrete recommendation about what my config file should actually contain.
