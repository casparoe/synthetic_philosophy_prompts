I sit on a body that reviews sentencing in a jurisdiction that has recently abolished life without parole, created a general offence of corporate manslaughter, and is now deciding whether criminal liability can attach to autonomous software systems. The work has forced me to notice that my verdicts on individual cases are far more confident than any account of punishment I can state, so I would like the account extracted from the verdicts rather than the reverse.

Here is the principle I have been testing, so that you can break it: the culpability of the agent at the time of the act fixes a ceiling on what may be imposed; expressive, deterrent and reparative considerations may move a sentence within that ceiling but never above it; and where there is no agent who deserves anything, the state may still impose hard treatment, but it must then be justified as what it really is — a tax, a quarantine, a compensation scheme — and judged by the standards that govern those, not by the rhetoric of punishment.

My verdicts:

1. Two drivers, same blood alcohol, same road, same night; one is stopped at a checkpoint, the other kills a child. I want them sentenced identically. The death adds nothing.

2. A warehouse manager skips a required safety inspection once, an omission I regard as mild negligence. There is a fire; eleven workers die. I want a sentence substantially longer than for the same omission with no fire, and a sentence of a few weeks strikes me as an insult to the dead. I will not give this up.

3. An offender suffers a brain injury after the act and before trial and cannot grasp that he is being punished. No deterrent, reformative or communicative gain is available. I want the case to proceed and the sentence reduced only modestly, because prison will in fact be harder on him than on others.

4. A company is convicted of a fraud committed sixteen years ago. Every officer and every shareholder has turned over; the burden now falls on current employees and on a teachers' pension fund. I want a large fine, though smaller than what would have been imposed at the time, and no liability of any kind for any individual now there. I count the losses to innocent shareholders as a real reason to reduce, but not a decisive one.

5. An embezzler confesses before any investigation begins, repays everything, and in the process loses his marriage, his career and his savings. I want a nominal sentence — but nominal because of the confession and the repayment. I want zero weight given to the ruin he has already undergone and zero weight given to the fact that his guilt torments him.

6. An official administered, forty years ago, a programme that was lawful at the time, judicially approved, and required of him professionally, and that we now regard as a grave injustice. I want a conviction and a fine, no imprisonment, and I want the proceeding to be chiefly about the public record rather than about him.

Two of these look hard to reconcile even to me. In the first I refuse to let outcome luck count, and in the second I insist on it. In the third I punish where nothing forward-looking is achieved yet make a small allowance for how much the punishment will hurt, while in the fifth I refuse to make any allowance at all for suffering that has already arrived. If a single principle cannot hold both members of a pair, say which verdict you would overturn and what you would put in its place.

What I want from you: a principle, or at most two or three candidates, stated precisely enough to apply, with the case that motivates each clause; an explicit statement of which of my verdicts each candidate cannot accommodate; and verdicts on three cases I have not settled. First, sanctions directed at an autonomous software system whose unanticipated decision caused a death: permanent deletion, suspension from deployment, and a levy on its operator's revenue — which of these, if any, is punishment on your account, and which is justified? Second, posthumous conviction: a perpetrator of a historical atrocity dies unprosecuted; does your principle permit condemning him, and does it matter whether there are living beneficiaries of his estate who might forfeit? Third, a case of your own construction, chosen because it discriminates between your candidates.

Finally, tell me whether the ceiling clause is worth keeping. It is the part of my view I am least sure of, and case two is the reason.
