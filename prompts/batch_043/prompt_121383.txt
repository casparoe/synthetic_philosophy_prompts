The question:

**3. Probability in the Everett interpretation.** Writers distinguish the *incoherence problem* for probability — roughly, how there can be anything for probability to be about when every outcome occurs — from the *quantitative problem* — why the weights should be the Born-rule weights rather than, say, equal weights across branches.

(a) Explain why a solution to the incoherence problem does not by itself settle the quantitative problem. [8]

(b) Call someone who holds that, before a measurement on a qubit in the state α|↑⟩ + β|↓⟩, you should divide your credence equally between the two outcomes a 'branch egalitarian': each outcome-branch contains a successor of you, and no branch is privileged over any other. Now suppose that after the measurement, but before you look at the result, an experimenter couples an extra decohering system to the up-branch only, so that there are then two up-branches (of amplitude α/√2 each) and one down-branch (of amplitude β). Nothing about the measured system, or about your situation, changes. Give the strongest objection to branch egalitarianism that this device suggests. [7]

(c) The Deutsch–Wallace approach argues that a rational agent in a branching universe must weight branches by squared amplitude. Does this argument rest on any substantive assumption about the physics of branching, or does the Born rule come out of rationality alone? And is Zurek's envariance derivation simply the same argument in other notation? [10]

The student's answer, verbatim:

(a) In Everett every outcome of a measurement actually occurs, so it looks as though there is nothing left for probability to be about: probability seems to presuppose that exactly one of the alternatives will in fact obtain. The standard reply is that the uncertainty is self-locating. Before the measurement I do not know which of my successors I will become, even though I know that successors of mine will see each outcome; and uncertainty about one's location within the branching structure is enough both to make probability talk coherent and to explain why I should care about the weights at all. That settles the incoherence problem. But it does not settle the quantitative problem, which is a further, independent question: granting that my credences concern which branch I will find myself on, what should the numbers be? Nothing in the self-locating-uncertainty move tells me that the up-outcome gets |α|² rather than, say, a half. So the quantitative problem needs its own argument.

(b) The device described is, I think, decisive against branch egalitarianism. Egalitarianism makes credence a function of how many branches there are; but the number of branches is not fixed by the physics. Coupling an idle decohering system to the up-branch turns one branch into two, and could just as well turn it into a thousand, while changing nothing about the measured system, about the amplitudes, or about what anyone will observe. Egalitarianism would then have my credence in 'up' jump from a half to two-thirds at the press of a button, which is absurd: a rule for setting credences should not be sensitive to how finely the environment is sliced up. The general moral is that branch number has no invariant physical meaning, since branching is a matter of degree and the decomposition into branches is only approximately selected by decoherence, whereas squared amplitude is additive and unaffected by such fine-graining. That is why the Born weights are the only candidate left once branch counting is off the table.

(c) The Deutsch–Wallace argument is a representation theorem. Consider an agent choosing between acts whose outcomes are branch-dependent — bets, in effect, on which branch she will end up on. If her preferences over such acts satisfy the usual rationality constraints (that they are ordered, continuous, and independent in the way expected-utility theory requires), then they can be represented as maximising expected utility with respect to some weighting over branches; and the only weighting that does this is the squared-amplitude one. Because those constraints are simply the axioms of rational choice, nothing specifically quantum — and in particular nothing about the physics of branching — has to be assumed: the Born rule falls out of rationality alone. That is the appeal of the approach, since the Everettian can then say that a Born-rule agent just is a rational agent. Zurek's envariance derivation reaches the same result from the side of the formalism, and I take the two to be the same argument in different notation: an entangled state whose Schmidt coefficients are equal is left invariant by certain swaps of the environment together with compensating counter-swaps on the system, and since swapping environment states cannot alter the probabilities of system states, equal coefficients must receive equal probabilities, with a limit argument extending this to unequal coefficients. The envariance route also does something the decision-theoretic one does not: it fixes the basis in which probabilities are defined, since the entanglement structure picks out a unique Schmidt decomposition. So the Everettian does not after all need decoherence to solve the preferred-basis problem.
