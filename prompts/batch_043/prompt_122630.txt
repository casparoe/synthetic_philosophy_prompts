I'm writing a 6,000-word term paper for a graduate seminar on explanation in the historical and social sciences, and I want to spend it on a question my supervisor dismisses as merely methodological and that I think is substantive: whether the question why the Industrial Revolution happened in Britain and not in China is a well-posed contrastive question at all, or whether it is several different questions — some well posed, some not — bundled into one grammatical form. My half-formed idea is a test I've been calling the shared-factor criterion: decompose the contrastive question into the conjuncts why Britain industrialized and why China did not, and ask whether the best available answers to the two conjuncts share any common factor. If they share nothing — if the first is answered by coal location, colonial extraction, and high wages encouraging labor-saving machinery, and the second by demographics, state fiscal policy, or the structure of the agrarian economy — then the phrase and not is doing no explanatory work, the question is really two separate questions that happen to have been juxtaposed, and the honest answer to it is that there is no single thing to explain. If they do share a factor, then the contrast is genuine, and the paper should say which factor and how we would tell. I have two further suspicions I want worked into the framing rather than left as asides. The first is a unit-matching or synchronization problem: Britain and China are contrastands of very different sizes and with very different internal tempos, so the content of the question shifts depending on what we pair with what — a small island polity against a continent-scale empire, or against one commercially advanced river delta within it — and on where in time we freeze the comparison, since by the middle of the nineteenth century we may already be looking at the consequence rather than the cause of the thing being explained. The second is a denominator worry: if the set of regions that could plausibly have been the site was large, then any winning site paired with any losing one generates an equally urgent-sounding contrastive question, and the apparent depth of the puzzle may be an artifact of having selected the foil after seeing the outcome. I suspect a good deal of the argument in this area is really disagreement about the pairing and about the candidate set, conducted as if it were disagreement about causes.

What I want from you is a proposal, not the paper. Give me a precise statement of the question such that it has a determinate answer — I am aware that is well posed is itself vague, so a large part of the value you can add is in saying what would make a contrastive historical question well posed and what would make it degenerate. Then say why the positions one might occupy here leave it open: the one that treats this as a straightforward causal question to be settled by finding the relevant factor, the one that treats the contrast as an artifact of framing that dissolves once you compare like with like at the regional level, and the one that says the question is fine but the expected answer is a conjunction of independent causes. Then the method, as a section-by-section plan rather than a bibliography: what I would actually do in each part of the paper, including how I would apply the shared-factor criterion without having to settle the substantive historical disputes, and why this approach rather than the obvious alternatives of just surveying the causal hypotheses or just doing conceptual analysis of contrastive explanation. Lay out the possible conclusions and what would decide between them — including the possibility that the criterion is satisfied trivially by every contrast, or is so demanding that almost no historical contrast passes, either of which should count as the test being the wrong test.

Two constraints on scope and format. On scope: this is one seminar paper, I work from secondary literature in translation, and I am not going to do archival work or learn to read primary sources, so the plan has to make progress on conceptual and argumentative resources alone, and I want an honest paragraph on which parts of the question would be too big for the paper and should be explicitly set aside rather than gestured at. On format: I will add the citations myself, so refer to positions, arguments, and kinds of evidence by description rather than by author and date, and don't build any step of the method around tracking down a source. I would also like a short closing section on what the deliverable would be for someone who is not a philosopher — a historian colleague asked me, reasonably, what they would be able to do differently if my paper succeeded, and I did not have a good answer, so give me one: something like a repair recipe for restating a contrastive question, or a checklist for when to distrust one.
