I'm writing a term paper for a graduate seminar on formal epistemology, and I'd like your comments on the notes before I turn them into a draft. My supervisor's position, which he stated in about a sentence and which I take to be the received view, is that the preface paradox is just the lottery paradox with the number of tickets unknown, and that anything which solves one thereby solves the other. My paper argues that this is wrong, and that it looks right only because the standard toy examples for the two paradoxes share a hidden assumption which, once relaxed, pushes the cases in opposite directions. I'd like to know whether the argument I've sketched actually supports that conclusion, whether the probabilistic machinery is sound, and, perhaps most usefully, whether the case against the unification survives the strongest version of my supervisor's claim rather than the throwaway version he actually gave me.

The core observation concerns the association between error-events. In the lottery, take the error-event for my belief that ticket i will lose to be the proposition that ticket i wins; these events are mutually exclusive, so they cannot co-occur, which is about as far from independence as one can get in the negative direction, and the consequence is that the conjunction of my beliefs has probability 0 even though each conjunct has probability 0.99. In the preface, my error-events — the proposition that claim j is false — typically can co-occur, and where the author's errors have common causes (a corrupted dataset, an inference schema used throughout one chapter, a stretch of work done while exhausted) they positively co-occur: one error raises the probability of another. I try to make this vivid with two examples that share their naive arithmetic. A hundred tickets: each ticket loses with probability 0.99, so if the conjuncts were independent the probability that all hundred lose would be about 0.37, whereas in fact it is 0. A hundred claims in a book, each with a 1% marginal chance of being in error: if the error-events were independent, the probability of an error-free book would again be about 0.37; but if errors cluster — suppose that on nine occasions in ten the manuscript is entirely clean and on the tenth a corrupted chapter throws off around ten errors together, which preserves the 1% marginal rate — then the probability of an error-free book is about 0.9. So the independence assumption that is doing all the work in the standard presentation errs downward in one case and upward in the other, which is what I mean by saying that it conceals the difference rather than exposing a shared structure. Two further asymmetries are supposed to follow, and I want to lean on both. First, the lottery's defeater is sharp and structural: I know there is exactly one winner among exactly n tickets. The preface's defeater is vague and empirical: I have a base-rate reason to expect at least one error somewhere, with no bound on how many. Second, and this is the punchline I am least confident about, clustering raises the probability that the book is error-free while making any discovered error a near-certain sign of a common cause, so the calibrated preface author ought to trust her book more than the standard treatment permits before the first erratum arrives and far less afterwards: under independence, learning that one claim is wrong leaves the probability that any other given claim is wrong at roughly 0.01, whereas under clustering it should collapse almost to the probability that the corrupted-mode hypothesis is true.

I would be grateful for comments on four things in particular. (1) Is the probabilistic reasoning correct as stated, including the clustering calculation and the claim that the marginal per-claim error rate can be held fixed at 1% while the probability of the conjunction moves from about 0.37 to about 0.9? (2) Is the association parameter doing real independent work, or is it merely a redescription of a difference my supervisor could happily absorb by saying that the preface is the lottery with unknown n and unknown dependence structure, and that no solution needs to care which? (3) My constructive proposal, only sketched so far, is a closure rule indexed to common-cause risk: one may aggregate justified beliefs into a justified conjunctive belief only when one has positive reason to treat the error-events as independent or negatively associated, and otherwise one's confidence in the conjunction should be governed by the probability that the common cause is absent rather than by the product of the marginals. Is that a genuine alternative to the familiar menu of responses — denying closure, adjusting the threshold, distinguishing acceptance from belief, contextualizing justification — or does it collapse into one of them under pressure? (4) Is the before-the-erratum/after-the-erratum punchline really a consequence of the view, or have I smuggled in an extra assumption about how the author comes to learn of the error, given that a randomly found error and a deliberately hunted-for one may not be evidentially equivalent? Please don't spend space on reading suggestions or on reconstructing the literature for me; I am working offline this week and cannot chase references, so I would much rather have your own assessment of where the argument holds and where it breaks, including in the parts you take to be uninteresting.
