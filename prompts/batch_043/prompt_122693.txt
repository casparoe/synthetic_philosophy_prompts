I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy of religion, and I'm revising a paper on Molinism for a graduate workshop in a few weeks. The core of the paper is a case I've been calling the "recalcitrant agent." Say that a counterfactual of freedom has the form: were creature S in complete circumstance C, S would freely do A. Now let C* be just like an ordinary circumstance C except that, shortly before the decision, S is reliably and truly informed by a messenger she has every reason to trust that God's middle knowledge contains the counterfactual "were S in C\*, S would freely do A\*." Call S recalcitrant if, for every action A open to her, it is true that were she in C\* and were she informed that she would freely do A, she would freely do something other than A. My thought is that for such a creature no counterfactual with antecedent C\* can be true: the truth of any candidate is itself part of what constitutes C\*, via the content of the revelation, so each candidate's truth is self-undermining and the set of counterfactuals about S in C\* has no fixed point.

What I can't tell is how damaging this is. As I understand the view, middle knowledge is God's knowledge of what any possible free creature would freely do in any circumstance in which it might find itself, and it is precisely this knowledge that gives God foreknowledge of the free acts of the creatures He actually creates — that is why it has to sit logically between His knowledge of mere possibilities and His post-decree knowledge of what He has willed, i.e. prior to creation itself: if God had to wait for the decree to know what I will do, then either His foreknowledge is a guess or it is secured by His own determination of my act, and freedom is lost either way. But if my case is coherent, then for at least some creature–circumstance pairs there are no true counterfactuals of freedom at all, and a God who lacks truths about what a creature would do can have no foreknowledge of what that creature will do. That looks to me like open theism arrived at by the back door. My supervisor's response is that Molinists should simply say that worlds containing the revelational circumstance C\* are infeasible — not among the worlds God can actualize, because of how the counterfactuals turn out. But that strikes me as no reply: infeasibility is supposed to be a limitation on what God can get creatures to do, not a gap in what there is for God to know, and the gap is the whole problem.

I don't need references or a reading list — I have the literature to hand. What I want is the conceptual diagnosis: is the recalcitrant-agent case coherent, does it really deprive Molinism of any true counterfactual, and if it does, does the collapse I describe follow? Please also tell me plainly if I have misdescribed what middle knowledge is for, since my supervisor hinted at that without explaining. Conclude your reply with a final line of exactly the form Verdict: YES if my argument succeeds and Molinism is pushed toward open theism or something equally unstable, or Verdict: NO if it does not.
