I'm in the fourth year of a PhD and applying for a one-year dissertation fellowship. I need the proposal document itself: about two pages, single-spaced, for one chapter of a dissertation on the coherence arguments for expected utility maximization. Please don't include a bibliography or a literature review keyed to particular papers and years — I'll add those myself. Refer to positions and argument-types by name (Dutch book arguments, money-pump arguments, representation theorems, the sure-thing principle, consequentialism in sequential choice, the sophisticated/resolute distinction) rather than to individual authors and dates.

Here is the half-formed idea, which is the part I most need sharpened — and if you think it's misguided, say so in the proposal and tell me what the better version of the question is.

Money-pump arguments are usually presented as a single family: if your preferences violate axiom A, then someone can arrange a sequence of offers you'll each accept and end up extracting resources from you, so A is a requirement of rationality. But the arguments I can construct seem to differ along a dimension I haven't seen catalogued. Some of them work on a single, unchanging preference ordering and give the agent no new information between the steps: a cyclic-preference pump, for instance, just walks you around the cycle and collects a fee at each trade. Others — in particular everything I can build for independence, which is the axiom doing the specifically expected-utility work as opposed to mere coherence — seem to require that a chance event resolve somewhere in the middle of the sequence, so that the agent makes the later choice with information she did not have when she made the earlier one. If that is right, then the second kind of argument cannot be reconstructed as a pure "you paid for something you could have had for free" argument. The later self is choosing under different information, and connecting the two stages seems to require a norm about planning agency — something like: don't adopt a plan you know in advance you will abandon, or don't adopt a plan whose execution you can foresee will leave you worse off than an alternative plan available to you — rather than a norm about consistency among attitudes held at one time. So the hypothesis would be that the coherence arguments are not all of a piece: the ones that take you all the way to expected utility maximization, as opposed to mere coherence of preference, rest on a substantive and contestable norm of diachronic agency, and "maximize expected utility" is therefore not supported by coherence considerations alone.

What I need in the proposal:

1. The question stated precisely enough that it has a determinate answer, including a statement of what would have to be true for the hypothesis to hold and what would refute it.
2. Why the existing positions leave the question open — in particular, why a reader who thinks the representation theorems already settle everything is missing something, and why a reader who thinks the money-pump arguments are merely heuristic is also missing something.
3. The method: what I would actually do, month by month, across a single year, given that this is an armchair project with no archival or empirical component. Include how I would go about establishing the taxonomy claim (that the pumps fall into two classes along the dimension I describe) in a way that isn't just a list of examples I happened to think of.
4. The range of possible conclusions, and which considerations would decide between them.
5. What would count as the project failing, stated honestly — including the outcome where the taxonomy turns out not to be exhaustive or the diachronic norm turns out to be derivable from synchronic considerations after all.
6. An honest note on scope: what makes this too big for a year, and what you would cut first.

Finally, add a short closing section separating claims in the framing that are settled in the way structural results about representation theorems are settled from claims that are genuinely contested among people who work on this — whether dynamic arguments can underwrite consequentialism, whether exploitability is itself a normative notion, whether planning norms are independent of coherence norms, and anything else you find contested.
