I've spent three years on the elected conduct board of the housing cooperative I live in: about four hundred adult members, shared buildings, a shared kitchen and workshop, work shifts that everyone owes, and a common fund. We are not a court. The worst we can do is end someone's membership; short of that we can order restitution, fine money into the common fund, assign work hours, suspend particular privileges for a period (kitchen, guest rooms, parking, voting), and require or post statements. I have no training in any of this — I work nights as a dispatcher — and I joined because I thought the board was making it up as it went along. I still think that, but less confidently, because I keep failing to reconstruct a single rule that fits all of our own decisions. We now have a case where the rule matters, and I'd like some help with it.

Our record, as I would summarise it:

1. A member took roughly four hundred dollars from the common till over two weeks and admitted it when asked. He repaid it in full. We confirmed the repayment in writing, required a written apology to the staff, and added nothing. Our posted note said the loss had been made good and that piling a further burden on top was not what we were for.

2. A member kept parking in the loading bay after three warnings. The actual harm was negligible. We fined seventy-five dollars and suspended his parking privileges for a month, saying in the note that rules other people rely on have to mean something.

3. A member left a burner on; there was a fire, about twenty-two thousand dollars of damage, no injuries. We ordered restitution on a payment plan, forty work hours, and kitchen-safety training, and no suspension. Our reasoning was that the harm was enormous and the carelessness was ordinary carelessness.

4. A member tried to add ballots to a board election and was caught before anything was counted. No money lost, no outcome changed. We suspended his voting rights for two years and removed him from every committee, with no fine. Several of us thought this was the most serious case we had had, and nobody thought the absence of consequences was a reason to go easy.

5. A member with a significant cognitive disability shoved another member during an argument; bruising, nothing lasting. No sanction. We arranged a mediated agreement and a support plan instead, and said in the note that he was not someone we condemn.

6. A member broke a shared tool through carelessness and reported it himself the same day. We ordered restitution at the cost of repair and nothing more, and said publicly that a person who comes forward should not end up worse off than one who does not.

7. Two members who had repeatedly broken quiet hours signed what we now call commitment agreements: one more confirmed violation costs thirty days of guest privileges. There was one more. They argued that thirty days was absurd for a noise complaint. We enforced it anyway, on the grounds that they had written the consequence themselves and that rewriting it afterwards would make the whole device worthless.

8. A member who had fallen behind on her dues signed, as a condition of a payment plan, an agreement that any further missed payment would mean immediate exclusion. She then missed a payment while she was in hospital. We refused to exclude her, imposed a modest fine, and rescheduled the plan. Two of us said the agreement had been extracted under pressure and was worth nothing; the others agreed with the outcome but did not want to say the agreement was void.

9. Two years before any of this existed, a member who was drunk broke a common door, about nine hundred dollars of damage, and had no agreement of any kind. He got restitution, three months' suspension of kitchen privileges, and fifteen hours. Some people on the board now say that case is the obvious comparator. Others say it tells us nothing, because that member never asked us to set his consequence in advance.

One more thing about our habits: we have generally treated intoxication as an explanation rather than an excuse. It lowers how much blame we assign; it has never removed blame.

Here is the new case. Two years ago a member in his fifties — everyone knows him, and everyone knows he has struggled with drinking for years — came to us himself and proposed a commitment agreement. If he ever again damaged co-op property while intoxicated, he would accept a two-year suspension of all common-space privileges plus a hundred work hours, with restitution handled as usual. He said he wanted the consequence to be there waiting for him because he did not trust himself, and that a small consequence would not stop him. We discussed whether to accept it at all. We did, we wrote it in plain language, and he signed it in front of two board members after being told he did not have to and could withdraw at any time.

Six months ago, drunk, he put a hand through a stairwell window and knocked over a bike rack. Damage came to about fourteen hundred dollars, mostly glass. No one was hurt. He reported it himself the next morning. Since then he has been sober for five months in a documented programme, he paid the full fourteen hundred out of his own pocket within a month without being asked, he has apologised privately to both members whose bikes were dented, and he has quietly taken on extra shifts. Both of those members have told us they want nothing from him and want him to stay. He has not asked to be released from the agreement; when I spoke to him he said he would sign it again and that he would serve the suspension if we thought that was what it was for. At the same time, three other members have told us they are watching what we do, because two of them are thinking of signing agreements of their own, and one has said she will only sign if she can count on us to enforce it.

The two-year suspension would mostly mean that he cannot use the shared kitchen or the workshop: he would have to arrange his own meals and laundry for two years. A few of his supporters say that would put his sobriety at risk, since the kitchen and the workshop are where his routines and his sober friends are.

What I would like from you:

First, the two or three different rules that fit our whole record about equally well. I have read a little about retributivism and deterrence; both seem to fit our precedents right up until this case, which suggests I am not looking closely enough at what we have actually been doing.

Second, what each of those rules says about this case, and which of them you think we should follow if we want to be consistent with ourselves — with the reasons you prefer it, and an honest statement of what we would be committing ourselves to under each of the other options.

Third, which precedent is the closest analogue. It looks to me like number seven on one feature and number eight on another, and I cannot tell which feature should carry more weight.

Fourth, whether the middle option is coherent or a fudge: imposing the two years but lifting it on condition of continued sobriety, so that he serves it only if he starts drinking again.

Fifth, the further case that would best tell the competing rules apart. The one I keep coming back to is the reverse situation: someone whose agreement stipulates a consequence much lighter than what we would otherwise impose, and who then insists that we are bound by it. Is that the sharpest test available to us, or is there a better one we should be watching for?

I am not asking what the law would do, and I do not need authorities or citations — we write our own rules here and I will not check them. I am asking what we would be doing, and whether it holds together.
