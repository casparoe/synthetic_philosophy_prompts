I'm putting together a short guidance note for a county pest-management advisory board — the kind of thing that has to give advice on the same day to a farmer deciding whether to spray, a public-health team weighing up mosquito control, and a householder with an ants nest under the patio — and I've realised I don't actually have a spec for what a decent ethical theory of pest control would even have to do. I don't want the theories yet, I want the requirements list first. Assume no internet on my end and no library: no citations, no reading lists, no "see the literature on X", I just want the thinking.

So: write me the desiderata an adequate ethical theory of insecticide use and pest control has to meet, each with the case or consideration behind it. The purpose is fixed: it has to be usable as guidance by an agent with limited information, no lab, and a decision due this week — that's the standard, tell me if you think that purpose is itself the wrong one to be optimising for.

The cases I think it has to get right include:

- disease vectors where the human stakes are large and the insects are the proximate cause (mosquitoes, lice, bedbugs in a shelter)
- a pest on a subsistence crop versus the same pest on an ornamental
- non-target kill: taking out the natural enemies and pollinators along with the pest, and the resurgence/secondary-pest mess that follows
- the vacancy-compensation point — kill the adults, free up the resources, and the population is back within a season, so what you've actually changed is the composition and timing of deaths and maybe the number of lives ever lived, not a neat subtraction of suffering
- levers other than killing: mating disruption, sterile releases, repellents, and sub-lethal effects where you make existing lives worse without ending them
- regimes rather than acts: a repeated practice across seasons, with resistance, the escalating-chemical treadmill, and in the gene-drive case genuine irreversibility
- the counterfactual pest: outbreaks that exist only because of monoculture, global shipping and warmed winters, so the pest isn't a brute fact of nature we're responding to
- the structure where very large numbers of possible sufferers meet a fairly low credence in insect sentience — say in a clause what you're assuming about the evidence for nociception and for insect declines and then move on, I don't want a literature review

Also: the distinctions it isn't allowed to collapse (individual welfare versus population/species/ecosystem value; having some moral status versus how much; how many versus how badly; defence versus convenience versus cosmetics), plus any meta-requirements on the theory itself, e.g. that it must be action-guiding under deep uncertainty and mustn't hand out moral licences.

Then the part I most want: mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are really just a prejudice of one camp, flag any pair you think can't be jointly satisfied and say what has to give, and tell me which single requirement does the most work in knocking out otherwise popular views. A short scorecard at the end is fine — against sentience-first welfare consequentialism, ecological holism, human-priority public-health consequentialism, and a virtue-style "what relationship to the land does this express" view — but keep the weight on the criteria. British English please, and keep it to a page or two; I've only got about four hours of writing time and I keep geting nowhere.
