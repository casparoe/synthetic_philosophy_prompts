I have no academic training in philosophy and have been working through this one question on my own for about a year, ever since an argument with a friend that neither of us could bring to a close. The friend holds that a reason is necessarily somebody's reason: if there is no one whose reason it is, then there is no reason, and sentences that appear to say otherwise are either false or are saying something else in disguise. I found myself wanting to deny that, but I could not say what a reason would have to be for it to come free of any owner.

The proposition I want your credence on is this: reasons can exist unattached, meaning that it can be true that there is a reason for some outcome to obtain, or for someone to act in some way, in a case where no agent has that reason, no agent will ever have it, and no agent could ever have acted on it.

To make the question less slippery I have been sorting the cases that pull me in the friend's direction or mine, and I would like you to use roughly this sorting:

1. The sealed-off case. A being suffers in a region that nothing outside it can reach or even detect. There is, I want to say, a reason for its suffering to stop, yet there is nobody who has that reason and nobody who could act on it.
2. The temporal case. Long before there were any agents, was there already a reason for things to go one way rather than another, or did such reasons begin only once there were agents to be their bearers? The same question arises for a world where agency dies out permanently.
3. The uncomprehending agent case. Someone exists, and could physically perform the action, but has no capacity at all to register the consideration in favour of it. Is the reason theirs, or does it belong to no one while still being a reason?
4. The residue case. Perhaps what I am calling a free-floating reason is not a reason at all but a fact about what ought to be, or about how it would be better for the world to be, left over once every agent is removed.

My own best attempt at a theory was to say that a free-floating reason is really a counterfactual claim: that any agent who were appropriately placed would have that reason. My friend replies that this is just an ordinary reason with the agent slot left open and quantified over, and so it is not free-floating in the sense at issue, and I am not sure I have an answer. I would like you to take a position on whether that counterfactual move rescues the idea or quietly gives it away.

Please give me a single number for the proposition, in the 0 to 1 range, and then the decomposition that produces it: the sub-questions your number depends on, a rough probability for each, one line of justification per number, and how you are combining them. The sub-questions I can see are these, and you should add any you think I have missed:

- whether a reason must be a reason for somebody, or whether that is just a habit of our sentences about reasons;
- whether a reason requires that acting on it be possible, for someone, at some time;
- whether a reason requires that some agent be able to grasp it, or whether guidance is incidental to being a reason;
- whether the counterfactual reading of the sealed-off and temporal cases is the best explanation of the pull those cases have, or a way of changing the subject;
- whether a reason that explains nothing, guides nothing, and could never be responded to would be an idle posit even if it existed;
- whether anything practical actually rides on the answer, for instance whether it makes sense to think we can owe something to beings no one can ever help, or to think the world would be better arranged in certain ways even if agency were to vanish.

My own credence in the proposition is around 0.6 and my friend's is around 0.15, so please also say which of these sub-questions two people at those numbers are most likely disagreeing about, and what it is about that sub-question that does the work.

Finally, tell me which of your numbers you are least confident in, and describe what would move it by the largest amount: a new argument, a way of handling one of the four cases, or a clarification of what we are asking when we ask whether reasons exist at all.
