You're a moral philosopher who also reads the cross-cultural psychology literature seriously. Take your time, brainstorm a pile of candidate defeaters before you pick any, and please don't just run through the standard objection-list you'd get in a survey article.

The position I've ended up at: the variation that WEIRD-samples work keeps turning up gives us very little reason to give up on moral objectivity, because what varies is the parameter settings and not the machinery. Every culture seems to run some version of a few core routines — don't harm an insider for no reason, don't cheat on reciprocal arrangements, defer to certain superiors or sacred things — and what differs is how wide the circle of protected beings is drawn (ancestors, animals, fetuses, outgroupers), how much weight purity and loyalty get when they collide with harm and fairness, and whether the domain is handled mostly by rules or mostly by relationships. The disagreement is downstream of shared structure, the way languages differ in whether they mark evidentiality while sharing a grammar.

What would count against that? I want defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to separate the ones a defender of my view should *concede* as counting against it from the ones they'd be right to shrug off as already consistent with it. Two things I'd particularly like you to chew on:

1. Grain. Am I just describing norms at whatever level of generality makes them match? If "don't harm an insider gratuitously" is universal only because "insider" and "gratuitously" can be filled in locally, is there any content left, or does my view collapse into the formal claim that every culture has some norms it treats as non-optional?

2. A study I keep imagining. Suppose you run the moral/conventional probes in a lot of small-scale societies, but you add three relativizing questions: would it still be wrong in a village where everyone did it, would it still be wrong if the ancestors or gods commanded it, and would it still be wrong if no one ever found out. Suppose the result is that a small core survives all three everywhere, but the core is *tiny* and its membership shifts a lot between samples, and in a couple of places nothing survives the "the ancestors command it" question at all.

Does that kill the parameter-setting story or just shrink it? Also: what does my view predict that its rivals don't, and has it been qualified to the point where no finding could touch it — and if it has, is that actually a problem for a claim of this kind, or is insulation fine here? For what its worth, what would move me most is evidence about the *unit* of explanation: if the variation tracks things like market integration or residential mobility rather than "culture" in any thick sense, my story has the wrong explanatory target even if the shared-structure part is true.
