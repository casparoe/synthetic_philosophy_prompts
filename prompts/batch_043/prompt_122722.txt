Last night I got into one of those arguments I've been having with the same friend for about fifteen years — this time in his kitchen, long after everyone else had gone to bed — about what you're allowed to say flatly. His position, once I made him state it precisely, was that the only rule governing assertion is truth: if what you say is true you have done nothing wrong in saying it, and if it is false you have broken the rule, however blameless you are. I couldn't accept it, but every time I tried to state my own view it came out as a pile of clauses, so on the way home and again this morning I wrote down my gut verdicts on a dozen-odd cases, and I want to know whether they add up to a principle or whether some of them have to go. Meeting at three, which I saw on the invitation and double-checked: fine. Train delayed, where I read it off the departures board, the board turns out to have been frozen since yesterday, and the train is in fact delayed for an unrelated reason: not fine, even though true and even though my friend insists it's a Gettier case and therefore irrelevant — I still think I shouldn't have said it. The last bus has gone, said to a stranger at a stop, when I'm about eighty percent sure from the timetable: not fine. That café does good coffee, said to a friend when I'm about equally sure: fine. A neighbour's insurance claim will be denied, when all I know is that ninety-two percent of such claims are denied: not fine. Ninety-two percent of such claims are denied, on exactly the same evidence, to the same neighbour: fine. I'm not certain, but I think the deadline is Friday: fine, even though I don't know. The same hedge, printed in six-point type at the bottom of an email I know nobody will read, above a flat claim about the deadline: not fine, or at least a lot worse. Something I don't know, said out loud into an empty room while checking my own voicemail: still not fine, even though there's no hearer to mislead. Our curriculum committee's official statement that the new major doesn't satisfy the College requirement, when no individual member knows this — each of us knows one part, and the conclusion only becomes visible once we pool what we know: fine. The vase would have cracked if you'd dropped it: fine, though nobody can know that. Our GP telling a parent this isn't meningitis on the basis of a gestalt she can't articulate: fine. My uncle asserting, with total conviction and on genuinely worthless evidence, that the council is covering something up: not fine. Meeting at three, said to a colleague who I know will infer from it that we're in the big room when we're not, and I stay silent about the room: the assertion itself is fine, but I owe him more. The lab result is negative, said after I read it myself but was also told by a senior colleague that the assay batch was faulty: not fine.

What I came up with, and what I most want tested rather than admired, is this: assert that p only if you are in a position to know that p, where being in a position to know means that your evidence discriminates p from the alternatives that are live in the context, and liveness is partly fixed by what is at stake for whoever is relying on you — unless either you flag the shortfall in a way your audience will actually take in, or nobody is in a position to know p and your evidence is the best available. I'd like a principle, or two or three candidates, stated precisely enough that I can go on testing it: one clause at a time, each tied to the case that motivates it, with an honest account of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which of them I should therefore give up. I'd also like to see how it handles the cases I deliberately left out because I genuinely go back and forth on them: asserting a well-supported but unproved mathematical conjecture to a class; asserting something to a hearer who has strong misleading evidence and won't believe you anyway; reading out, as a spokesperson, a statement written by other people whose truth you have no grasp of; asserting a borderline vague claim like this heap is large; saying the same sentence at a party and then again under oath. And I'd like to know whether the pair that worries me most — the unheard hedge, where the audience's take-up seems to matter, and the empty-room assertion, where it seems not to — can both be kept, or whether one of the two has to be overturned and which.
