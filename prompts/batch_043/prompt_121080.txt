I'm about two thirds of the way through a very readable popular book on evolution that a lab mate lent me, and there's a passage in it about what it means to say a trait has a function that I can't tell is fair or not. I'm doing a PhD in enzyme engineering, so I spend all day talking about the function of variants that have never existed anywhere in the natural world, and this matters to me more than it probably should. Here's the passage, more or less as written:

"Philosophers used to treat talk of what a trait is 'for' as just shorthand for how it evolved, but there are now two rival accounts, and the argument between them is pretty much the whole of the modern debate about function.

The first, the historical or 'selected-effects' account — Millikan, Neander and co. — says that a trait's function is the effect it had that explains why natural selection favoured it, and hence why it is still around today. The heart's function is to pump blood because pumping blood is what hearts were selected for.

The second, the 'causal-role' account, associated with Cummins, says that to call something a function is simply to say what it contributes to some larger capacity of the system it sits inside. On this view the heart's function is to pump blood only because we happen to take an interest in blood circulation; carve the system up differently and that same heart equally has the function of making a thumping noise, or of keeping cardiologists in business.

The causal-role account wins, and wins easily. It covers everything that has no evolutionary history at all — the very first heart, an enzyme a lab breeds in a fortnight, the parts of a robot — whereas the historical account is stuck saying that none of these have functions, which nobody seriously believes. It is also the account that medicine already assumes whenever it defines disease as the failure of a normal function, so it is the one that actually matters outside philosophy.

The historical account does win one round: malfunction. If a trait's function is fixed by its past, then a heart that cannot pump still has pumping as its function and merely fails at it — it is broken, not functionless. On the causal-role view, a heart that does not pump simply does not have that function any more, so malfunction collapses into not having a function at all, and you lose the ability to call it diseased."

What I'd like is a sanity check on how much of that I should believe. Could you go through it and tell me which bits a philosopher of biology would happily sign off on, which are the sort of simplification a populariser can't avoid, and which are actually wrong? I'm suspicious of at least two things: the claim that the "carve the system up differently" point is a neutral feature rather than a problem for the causal-role view, and the bit at the end where he says that view can't call a broken heart diseased, since a couple of paragraphs earlier he said it's the view doctors already use — that looks like wanting it both ways, but I may be reading it wrong, and I definately don't know where else he might be sliding.

My supervisor, who did some philosophy as an undergrad, read the passage and says the book has it backwards: the historical view is the one that fits lab talk better, because what we do in directed evolution is selection, just in a tube rather than a field, so a variant we have selected for a reaction does have a selection history and the no-history objection doesn't touch it. That sounded too convenient to be true. Is he right, or is he cheating?

There's a related case from our own work that I'd like to know what each account says about. We had a variant that definitely catalyses the reaction we bred it for, but we worked out later on that it's only sticking around in our cultures because it sits on the same plasmid as the resistance marker we select on — strip the marker away and the catalytic activity by itself wouldn't keep it in the population. Does either account get to say that this thing's function is the catalysis, and does it make any difference that we didn't know about the hitchhiking at the time?

Anyway — settled view, defensible simplification, or howler, and in particular whether his conclusion that the causal-role view wins easily survives whatever else you have to correct.
