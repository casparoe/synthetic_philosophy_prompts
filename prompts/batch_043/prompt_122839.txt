I am a retired experimental physicist — solid state, some forty years of it — who has lately been sitting in on a philosophy of physics reading group at the local university, and I have constructed an argument about Bell's theorem whose conclusion I find unacceptable. I would be grateful for a diagnosis: which step goes wrong, why it looked sound, and what each of the ways out costs.

First, three things I want kept apart, since I suspect an equivocation is hiding in the neighbourhood:

(i) *Light-cone locality*: no causal influence is propagated outside the light cones.
(ii) *Screening locality* — what I take Bell to have meant by local causality: a complete specification λ of the beables in the overlap of the two backward light cones renders Alice's outcome probabilistically independent of Bob's setting, and conversely.
(iii) *Interventional locality*: no manipulation available at one wing shifts the outcome distribution at the other. This is the no-signalling condition, and the no-signalling theorem secures it in quantum mechanics; relatedly, the violation is standardly located in outcome dependence rather than parameter dependence.

The usual reading, as I understand it, is that the Bell experiments refute (ii), that (iii) survives untouched, and that what people mean when they say Bell refuted locality is (i) — even though what the formalism directly violates is (ii).

Now the argument. I assume throughout that we will not help ourselves to a denial of measurement independence unless we are forced to.

1. In (ii) the screening variables are confined to the *backward* light cones. So local causality is not locality simpliciter; it is the conjunction of locality in the light-cone sense with the thesis that the complete causes of an event lie in its past.
2. Light-cone locality itself is neutral about temporal direction. An influence running from an event into its past light cone stays inside the light cones exactly as much as one running into its future.
3. Accordingly there are continuous paths between spacelike-separated events whose every segment is timelike or null: from one event back to a point in the overlap of the two past light cones and forward to the other, or forward to a point in the overlap of the two future light cones and back down to the other. Call these round trips. Each segment is light-cone-respecting; the construction requires only that the events share something in their past or in their future, which in a spacetime like ours they generically do.
4. So the singlet correlations can in principle be modelled without any influence outside the light cones at all: a setting on one wing propagates along a round trip to the distant outcome, and the correlations are screened off locally at every step. This, broadly, is what the time-symmetric approaches to quantum mechanics do.
5. Hence Bell's theorem does not force us to accept action at a distance. It forces a choice: either some influences travel outside the light cones, or some influences travel backwards. The canonical slogan — Bell refuted locality — conflates locality with the arrow of causation.
6. Now the step I distrust. Nothing in the construction of a round trip uses anything specific to Bell correlations. For essentially any two events with a shared region in their past or future light cones — that is, for essentially any two events — the same resources are available. So if a round trip is an admissible explanation of the singlet correlations, it is equally an admissible explanation of any correlation between any two events.
7. Take then any correlation on which a causal inference is ordinarily based — a correlation between smoking and carcinoma, or between a barometer's reading and a storm, or between the setting of an experimental apparatus and a later record. In each case one may say that no forward causal link is needed: the correlation is produced by an influence going forward into the overlap and back again, or back into the overlap and forward again. The manoeuvre is available for the correlation between an intervention and its apparent effect as well, since the intervention is itself an event with a light cone.
8. It follows that no correlation is ever evidence of a forward causal connection, and that the manipulationist account of causation — which the reading group treats as common ground — rests on nothing more than a prejudice about the direction of time that the Bell case has revealed to be optional.
9. That is absurd. Whatever the correct thing to say about Bell correlations is, it cannot be something that renders the practice of experimental science unintelligible.
10. So at least one of steps 1 to 6 is false.

Where I would like to end up: I want to keep steps 1 and 2. The observation that Bell's formulation builds in a causal arrow, and the observation that light cones are direction-neutral, both seem to me correct and worth having. I want to keep 9 above all. I am willing to surrender any of 3 to 6, and I would rather surrender one of them than give up 1 or 2 — but I want to be told what the surrender buys and what it costs.

Specifically, I would like you to consider three diagnoses and tell me which you favour:

- Is the flaw in the generalisation of step 6, on the grounds that the admissibility of a round-trip explanation can be constrained by something the Bell case satisfies and the smoking case does not — a no-bilking requirement, a ban on fine-tuning, the requirement that an intervention be an independent cause of its effect, or the thermodynamic boundary condition — and, if so, does that constraint simply reinstate, under another name, the very temporal asymmetry that steps 1 and 2 were supposed to have shown to be optional?
- Or is the flaw in steps 2 and 5, on the grounds that the locality worth wanting just *is* past-screened locality, so that the retrocausalist has preserved a word and lost the phenomenon? If that is your answer, I would like to know what becomes of the claim that Bell's theorem is a theorem about locality rather than about the arrow of time.
- Or is my colleague right that the round trip is superdeterminism run in reverse, and that the real moral of the argument is that measurement independence, not locality, is bearing all the weight in Bell's theorem — in which case I would like to know why the reversed version is thought to be less conspiratorial than the forward one.

Two further things, if you can fit them in. First, can time-symmetric models secure no-signalling without the sort of fine-tuning that gets superdeterminism called conspiratorial; and if that is where the argument really turns, say so plainly and say why. Second, where does the restored locality sit with respect to the parameter-dependence/outcome-dependence decomposition — is the retrocausalist restoring outcome independence, parameter independence, or something the decomposition does not carve at the joints?

Write for a reader who knows the physics but is new to the philosophy: define the terms of art as you go, and do not rest anything on a reference I should have to look up. I would also value a closing sentence or two on whether the argument, once repaired, leaves us having learned anything about Bell correlations that the standard presentation omits.
