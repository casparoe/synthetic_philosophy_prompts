Hi — I run the "pilot pipeline" for a mid-sized city (around 200k people). Basically every new idea in this city now arrives as a request for a six-month pilot: try it in one district, measure it, then decide. We get about forty of these a year, three of us review them, we have maybe two hours each, none of us has stats training beyond a spreadsheet, and anything we clear goes to a council committee that meets monthly and can be yanked by the mayor's office whenever they feel like it. What actually happens is that most pilots turn into either a press release or a quiet permanent extension, and the measurement mostly just decorates whatever people already wanted to do.

What I want from you is a procedure my team can run on a proposal, grounded in Dewey's experimentalism about inquiry and democracy — the version where inquiry starts from a genuinely indeterminate situation rather than from a proposition to be verified, where a hypothesis is a plan of action and its test is what happens when you act on it, where warrant is a matter of degree and always revisable so nothing "proves" anything, and where the democratic bit isn't a vote at the end but the requirement that the people who will bear the consequences get a real hand in forming and judging the hypothesis, not just a chance to react to it afterward.

Don't write me an essay about Dewey. Write me something I can put on one page and hand to a new hire. Concretely:

- The questions, in order, and what counts as a sufficient answer to each. I need to be able to tell "answered" from "sounded plausible in the meeting."
- Where the procedure stops with a NO — this isn't a pilot, don't run it.
- Where it stops and hands the decision to a person or to the committee because the question is one of values rather than facts. Say explicitly which questions those are, because that's what I have to defend when a council member asks why we rejected their thing.
- A one-page output — call it a pilot charter — that records what the situation was, what the hypothesis is as an action, who bears what, and what would count as having learned something.
- How to handle the hard cases we actually get, including: (a) interventions that can't be undone, so there's no going back to a reconstructed situation — demolishing things, disbanding a team, closing a school; (b) pilots that change the very thing they measure, so the result won't travel; (c) pilots where the metric is easy to game and gaming looks identical to success; (d) pilots where the benefits land on one group and the costs on a group that has no seat in the process; (e) pilots nobody can opt out of — a school-wide or district-wide thing where the "subjects" are just people who live there; (f) null results, which is most of them, and which currently just get ignored; (g) pilots that quietly become permanent because nobody scheduled the decision.

Two things I'd like built in that I haven't seen anywhere. First, a "counterfactual who" step: name the specific person or role who is worse off if this succeeds, and if the proposing department can't name one, that itself is the finding. Second, a "what has to stay still" step: list what must remain in place for a result in the pilot district to mean anything about next year city-wide, and then check whether the pilot itself changes anything on that list. My suspicion is that most of our proposals fail right there and we've been pretending otherwise.

Then run it on three real-shaped ones:

1. A four-day week for municipal office staff — same hours compressed into four days — proposed by HR, expected to cut sick leave and help recruitment.
2. Predictive targeting of code-enforcement inspectors using a model trained on historical 311 complaint data, so we inspect blocks the model flags rather than responding to complaints.
3. Unarmed mental-health crisis response teams in two districts. I should say up front that I publicly backed this one and the mayor already announced it, so I'm not neutral, and if the procedure says something inconvenient I'd rather hear it now than in front of the committee.

For each, show me the gates it passes and fails, the charter line, and whether it's a NO, a REFRAME (run it, but the claim you're entitled to make afterward is much smaller — say what the smaller claim is), or a ROUTE (this isn't an experiment, it's a decision about what kind of city we want, so take it to full deliberation). I expect at least one of these to come out as an answer I don't like, and I want you to say plainly what my options are in that case.

Finally, tell me what the procedure can't do. I have to present this to a committee that will absolutely over-trust it the moment it looks official, so I need the honest failure modes in writing — where the checklist gives false confidence, where it's a vocabulary for decisions we'd have made anyway, and what it can't tell us at all.
