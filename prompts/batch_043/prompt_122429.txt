CONSERVATION OF GAMING (draft)

1. Anyone who works near a target knows the slogan version: a measure stops being a good measure once it gets used as a target. My complaint is that the usual way of telling this understates how stubborn the thing is. The usual story is that a proxy is fine until someone optimises it, at which point it decouples from whatever it stood for, and the remedy is a better proxy. I think the remedy doesn't behave anything like that, and the reason deserves its own name.

2. First separate three things that normally get run together.

(a) Tail decay. The proxy tracks the target across the range of cases you've actually observed. Optimisation pushes behaviour outside that range, and the relationship just doesn't extrapolate. Nothing here is anybody's fault and nobody has to be gaming anything — it's a straight statistical failure.

(b) Channel capture. The proxy is something agents can act on fairly directly — a tick-box, a timestamp, a count. Now the decoupling is the agents' doing, and it can be total: the number improves while the underlying quantity gets worse.

(c) Blindness. This is the one I never see pulled out on its own. Once (b) is running, the number that would have told you (b) was running is the very number being manipulated. So the monitoring system reports calm — often suspiciously good calm — exactly where behaviour has drifted furthest. Call it the flat-line problem: heavily gamed indicators tend to look healthier than lightly gamed ones, because gaming is easiest where the target is most rigid, and gaming suppresses variance as well as shifting the mean. An organisation that ranks its own indicators by how settled they look will therefore rank its most corrupted ones highest.

3. The conjecture. Reforming a gamed metric tends to relocate the gaming rather than reduce it, and the relocation is systematically away from the visible. Take a composite example, not a real one: Mardenbridge social services is measured on days from referral to first face-to-face contact. Staff learn to log a doorstep knock as the contact. The metric is patched — contacts must now last twenty minutes and be logged by a qualified worker — and doorstep knocks become twenty-minute doorstep knocks. Meanwhile the thing that actually made cases go well, an experienced worker phoning the family first to find out what was going on, has no metric attached to it, so it quietly stops happening. Note what the patch did: it closed the one channel anybody was watching, which means the channels that remain are, by construction, the uninstrumented ones. Corollary — call it the scarring rule — the more often a metric has been patched, the less you should trust it, including when it currently looks spotless.

4. The objection I can't shake. Some reform changes the payoff structure rather than the channel: random audits with real penalties, or moving the metric onto something the agent can't touch, or changing who the agent is. My reply is to distinguish channel-substituting reforms from incentive-changing ones, and to say conservation applies to the first sort. But then the content looks dangerously close to: reforms that leave the incentive to game unchanged leave the gaming unchanged. That's close to a tautology. Is there a non-trivial version, or am I just decorating one?

5. A wrinkle that might cut against the whole thing. Every exploit is also a demonstration — of a capability, and of a gap between your proxy and what you actually wanted. In machine learning the outputs of reward hacking get recycled as training data; the gaming is information. So a purely anti-gaming posture destroys the cheapest map of your own blind spots you're ever going to get. Institutional analogue: exploit registries, where every discovered workaround has to be logged and circulated internally, on the grounds that the log is a picture of where your proxies are thin. But if gaming generates information, maybe it's partly self-limiting — each exploit teaches the principal something, so maybe conservation only holds where the agents' rate of learning exceeds the principal's, which is an empirical claim I have no way of defending.
