You are a political theorist who also designs deliberative simulations: rule-governed role-plays and games for seminars, policy workshops and city-council trainings, where the point is that participants run into a problem by having to act inside it rather than being told about it. Treat what follows as a design problem, not as a topic to expound. Before you produce the activity, spend a few hundred words on diagnosis: what exactly is the thing participants need to *feel* rather than be told, which feature of the mechanics is supposed to produce that feeling, and what the most likely way for the design to fail is. If you think my framing of the target is wrong or that the idea I sketch below is a bad mechanism, say so and replace it.

Setting. Upper-level undergraduate seminar in political theory, 18 students, one 75-minute session. It is the third meeting of a unit on liberalism and its communitarian critics; students have done the readings and can recite the standard moves, which is the problem — they can recite them without any of them biting. One instructor, no co-facilitator, no money, no props beyond printed cards, index cards, poker chips or scrabble tiles, a whiteboard and a timer.

What participants should come away seeing. Not another tour of the two positions. I want them to feel, in their own decisions, that the familiar dispute about whether the self is a chooser of its ends or is partly constituted by attachments it did not choose shows up in politics as a question about exit versus voice: about whether a member's ability to leave is what disciplines a collective, or what quietly destroys it. Specifically, I want three recognitions to arrive from experience: (i) that making exit cheap does not simply protect freedom but changes what a complaint can do, because a grievance backed by no willingness to stay is worth little; (ii) that abolishing exit does not produce solidarity but redistributes power toward whoever can most credibly threaten to leave, so that the people with the deepest attachments and the fewest options end up with the least influence; and (iii) that the real design space is not "voluntary association or community" but the institutions that make loyalty possible at all — vesting, rights of return, exemption-with-membership, cooling-off periods, and so on. If those three land, the metaphysical quarrel about the self should afterwards look less like the whole debate and more like one contested input to an institutional question.

Constraints on the design. No lecture up front; the mechanics must carry the idea, and participants should not hear the labels (liberal, communitarian, voluntarist, encumbered self, priority of the right) until the debrief. Participants must make real choices with real, if token, stakes — contributions, side payments, proposals, seconds, walkouts — and the rounds must be short enough that at least one collective project visibly succeeds and at least one visibly collapses within the session. The activity must be playable by someone who has never run a game before: unambiguous rules, a one-page facilitator sheet, and a stated fallback if a phase runs long.

My half-formed mechanic, to develop or discard. Each participant holds two cards. The first is public: a constitutive attachment of the kind that is not a preference to be traded off but a limit on what they can plausibly agree to — the only caregiver for a relative and therefore unable to attend evening meetings; a family that has worked the same plot for four generations; a recent arrival whom everyone expects to move on; someone whose religious community forbids swearing oaths; someone whose livelihood depends on a practice the assembly is considering banning. The second card is secret: that person's real exit cost, which need not match the attachment they display. So a person can be publicly deeply rooted and privately one phone call away from a job elsewhere, or publicly footloose and privately unable to leave. Members may raise a grievance (voice) at a cost, and it succeeds only if others back it; or they may leave, or buy their way out of the common scheme while staying nominally a member. My hope is that the gap between displayed attachment and private exit cost does the philosophical work: participants cannot tell genuine loyalty from a bluff, and that uncertainty is where the liberal and communitarian pictures actually diverge. If you have a cleaner mechanism for making the same structure felt, use it.

Deliverables. A complete, runnable activity: setup and materials; the cards (write them out); rules and a payoff or scoring structure that needs no real money; a minute-by-minute timeline for 75 minutes with the phases and what the facilitator says at each transition, and nothing more; the intended debrief, mapping specific moments in what actually happens onto the parts of the debate — the conception of the person, the neutrality of the state, the priority of the right over the good, the question of which community and who counts as a member; an honest list of what will predictably go wrong the first time it is run, including the ways students will try to dissolve the game by being reasonable, and how to handle each; and a frank note on which feature of the disagreement the activity cannot reproduce and which the facilitator will simply have to supply in words — I would rather know this in advance than discover it in the room.

Epistemic requirement. In the debrief notes, separate explicitly what is settled and uncontroversial among people who know this literature from what is your own reading, a live controversy, or a simplification you have built into the rules for the sake of playability. Flag every point at which the mechanics stack the deck toward one side, and tell me what a sympathetic liberal and a sympathetic communitarian would each say the game leaves out or misrepresents. Where you are uncertain whether a claim is standard or contested, say that it is contested rather than presenting it as the consensus. Do not cite or name-check texts, authors or editions: I will supply the reading list and the attributions myself, and I do not want the activity to depend on anything students would have to look up.
