I have been trying, and failing, to work out what theory of the user–assistant relation is actually baked into the refusal practice that the mainstream conversational assistants have converged on. Please treat the practice as I describe it below rather than as any particular vendor's current documentation states it; my interest is in the shape of the practice, not in the details of any one system's policy.

The features I have in mind:

(i) Withholding is frame-sensitive. The same body of information is refused when the request presents it as something the user intends to do, and supplied when the same content is presented as material for a novel, as a description of a historical episode, as a specimen for a security-awareness exercise, or as a request to explain what to watch out for. Drafting a convincing phishing email is refused; drafting the same email as a labelled illustration of what phishing looks like, for a training deck, is supplied.

(ii) Withholding is insensitive to the user. Someone with obvious domain expertise receives the same bare refusal as a curious adolescent, and no serious attempt is made to establish competence, purpose, or what alternatives the user has.

(iii) The boundary is drawn around certain stigmatised or unlawful categories rather than around expected harm. The assistant will help a user write a cover letter designed to defeat an identifiable rival, draft a negotiating posture intended to extract more from a counterparty, or write marketing copy that exploits well-documented cognitive biases. It will not help within the stigmatised category even where the expected harm is clearly smaller.

(iv) In the same session it will help a user act against their own stated long-term interests — drafting the angry email it warns against, facilitating procrastination, smoothing a purchase — while refusing a request the user has evidently deliberated over, on the apparent ground that the outcome might not be good for them.

(v) It will supply general information together with a recommendation to consult a professional, and then decline to apply that information to the user's own case, even when the criteria it has just stated plainly settle the answer.

(vi) Refusals characteristically state no substantive reason, and the assistant resists being drawn on where its threshold lies or how large it takes the risk to be.

What I would like is the implicit theory reconstructed, at the level of the following three questions. First: what does this practice presuppose about what an assistant owes a user — is it a view about the user's good at all, or is it really a view about something else, such as the assistant's own role, integrity, or standing, with the user's welfare only incidentally in view? Second: what does it presuppose about the moral significance of contributing — about when supplying information or labour to someone else's project implicates the supplier? Third: what does it presuppose about what makes a piece of information, as opposed to an action, dangerous?

Two constraints occur to me, and I would like to know whether the practice can satisfy either and whether it needs to. Call the first the frame-invariance constraint: an assistant's response should not differ between two requests that hold fixed both the information actually conveyed and the assistant's evidence about what the user will do with it, and differ only in the frame the user puts around the request. Call the second the substitution benchmark: a refusal should be assessed against what this user would in fact have done had the assistant complied, and against what they will actually do when refused, including the other assistants, search engines, and forums they will turn to instead. My suspicion is that the practice as I have described it fails the first badly and has simply never taken a view on the second; what I cannot tell is whether that is a defect in the practice or a sign that the two constraints are the wrong ones.

I would also like the deflationary hypothesis taken seriously: that the practice presupposes no interesting view about agency, harm, or the ethics of contribution, and is best explained by liability exposure and reputational risk, with the philosophical vocabulary applied afterwards. Say what would distinguish that explanation from the more principled ones, and where the practice is philosophically underdetermined in the sense that several incompatible theories would each license exactly this behaviour — in which case I would rather be told that it is a modus vivendi than be given a reconstruction that fits only by being vague.

Finally: what would concretely have to change in the practice — in what is withheld, in what reasons are given, and in how users are sorted — if the implicit theory were replaced by an explicitly expected-welfare view, on the one hand, or by a strict view about not contributing to others' projects, on the other? I would rather have nine hundred words of careful reconstruction than two thousand words of survey, and please write in British English.
