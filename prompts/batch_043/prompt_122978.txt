i'm a novelist and I've let myself be talked into giving the opening talk at a weekend literary festival this spring — twenty minutes, saturday morning, after coffee and before anyone's awake. The festival theme is "the common reader," and I want to actually do something with that rather than say it a lot.

Here's the thing I want the talk to be about. Hofstadter's Person Paper move — take a word everybody insists is generic and show that it's secretly specific by running the marking backwards — is something I've been chewing on for a novel I'm finishing. In the book there's a language where the ordinary all-purpose word for a person descends from the word for a householder; nobody in the book notices, beacuse the etymology went dead centuries earlier, and a fair amount of the plot turns on who does and doesn't get counted by it. I've started calling that a hollow generic: a word that sounds like it covers everyone and quietly doesn't, and whose hollowness is invisible precisely because the history has gone.

But I don't want twenty minutes about my book, and I really don't want twenty minutes of -man and -person examples, which every single person in the room has already sat through. Two problems with the room: maybe a third of it is linguists, translators and copy editors who read that essay years ago and will actually groan if I explain it; the rest are readers who've never heard of it and will drift the second I get technical. And I've got a personal stake that makes the clean version of the satire unavailable to me: I publish under my initials, reviewers have read me as a man across three books, and I've never corrected it because it's been good for me. So the tidy story — swap the word, fix the default — is one I can't honestly stand up and tell, since in my case the default latched onto a form that was already neutral and re-marked it anyway.

Can you write the talk? What I need from it:

- The point gets made by demonstration, not explanation. One thing the room does or pictures in the first two minutes that they can't un-picture, and that the talk keeps coming back to.
- Hofstadter gets named exactly once, in a sentence that works whether or not you've read him.
- No -man words as examples. Fresh material, and at least one example from the invented-language business in my book.
- It can't land as "language is hopeless," and it can't land as a scold aimed at the editors in the room who've spent careers on this. It also can't let me off the hook for the initials — I want that admission in there somewhere without it turning into a confession bit.
- It has to end somewhere the general readers can walk out with, without the linguists feeling I sold the point out to get there.
