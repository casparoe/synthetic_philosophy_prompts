hey — I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I'm stuck on about forty pages of my manuscript where three characters argue about whether you can know a book is good without having read it. All three of them are professionally in the business of telling other people what's good: one ghostwrites prize citations for books she hasn't opened, one writes blurbs, one runs a bookshop and has read everything. Right now they sound like they're reciting positions at each other, and I want them to sound like they actually disagree about something. So I need a sorting instrument, not an essay.

What I'm after: a decision tree, six or seven branch questions, each one a concrete case with two or three answers I could actually imagine a person giving, landing on seven or eight leaves. Ground rules:

1. Every node has to be a question someone can answer without knowing which position it leads to. No "are aesthetic judgments truth-apt?" — give me a case and let me answer it. If a character can tell where a question is heading, the question is broken.
2. Build it from the logical space, not from a list of names: start from the questions and the combinations of answers they allow, then drop the actual views into the cells. Where no one lives, say so and tell me why in one line — incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.
3. Leaves named descriptively, by what the view says. No bibliography, no footnotes, no "as so-and-so argues in such-and-such." I'm writing in a place with no library and no wifi and I really don't need to know who got there first.
4. Flag any pair of leaves your questions can't actually pull apart. I'd rather have a note saying "these two come out the same on every case I can think of" than a fake node invented to separate them.

Cases I need you to use (you can add one or two, but these go in):

- The citation: a reader whose taste matches yours near-perfectly tells you a novel is a masterpiece. Neither of you has read it; she read a detailed report from someone who did.
- The burnt manuscript: a novel exists in one copy behind glass, in a language nobody alive reads fluently, and a scholar you trust completely says it's the best thing she's ever read. Nobody will ever be able to check. This one is load-bearing for my plot, so I need it to do real diagnostic work rather than just make everyone sad.
- The prediction: your friend hasn't read the book either, but knows you cold, and says "you'll love it." Her claim seems to be about you, not about the book.
- The trained ear: a critic hears structural stuff you structurally cannot hear — counterpoint, whatever — and reports it. You could read the same pages and hear nothing.
- Ten thousand strangers' aggregate rating versus one person who knows your taste.
- The cheap-access case: you could read the first page yourself in ten seconds and haven't; the person telling you has. Does "you could just look" change anything about whether you now know?
- The priming case: someone tells you the last chapter quietly quotes an earlier scene, and once told you genuinely perceive it differently when you do read. Is that testimony substituting for first-hand experience, or improving it? I suspect it's a fourth thing rather than a leaf, but you decide where it goes.
- The insensitive case: a character who has no sensitivity in the domain at all — no ear, no feel for prose rhythm, nothing. Can she get anything at all out of being told?

There's a distinction I've been chewing on that I can't place, and I want you to either use it or kill it: whether the judgment in question is supposed to guide what the judger does next (read it, buy it, vote for it) or to place the work in an order of merit whether or not anyone ever experiences it. If that's a real branch, put it in the tree. If it's a distinction doing no work, say so plainly and drop it.

Leaves that must show up, described by what they claim rather than by any label I'd have to look up:

- The hard one: nothing comes across at all. No knowledge, not even a justified belief.
- The one where you end up with something real but it still isn't knowledge.
- The one where it works, but only because you independently have reason to think this testifier's sensibility tracks yours.
- The one where testimony is just testimony and there's nothing special about the aesthetic case.
- The "sure you can know, but you shouldn't" one — and if there are two genuinely different reasons hiding inside it, split it. My guess is there are: one about owning your own taste rather than borrowing someone's, one about what aesthetic engagement is even for.
- The one that says there's no aesthetic knowledge to transmit in the first place, so the whole debate is misframed.
- The one that says you aren't learning about the book at all, you're learning about your own likely future response to it.

Plus at least two unoccupied cells with a one-line diagnosis each, and a mark on anything the tree can't separate.

Last thing, and I mean it for the whole map and not just the leaves: finish with a short note on how confident you are in this as a picture of the debate, and single out the one node or leaf you're least sure about, with a sentence on whether it's shaky because the distinction does no work or because people genuinely haven't settled it. Also point to anywhere you had to invent a position to fill a hole in the tree — those are exactly the spots I'll put a character.

Six novelists in my writing group are going to take this thing, none of them with any philosophy training and all of them extremely good at arguing, so keep the cases concrete and the node language plain. Jargon at the leaves if you must, nowhere else. And please, no reading lists — I definately don't need to know who published what in 1973.
