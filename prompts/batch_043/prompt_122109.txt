I help run a small staking syndicate, and over the past year one of our members has been pressing an argument on us that I first took for a staking rule and have come to think is a claim about rationality. I cannot find where it goes wrong, though I am convinced it does. Below is the argument in what I take to be its strongest form. The arithmetic in steps (2) and (3) I have checked myself and am confident in; it is the rest I want help with.

The setup. A counterparty will take an even-money bet, repeatedly and independently, on an event we predict correctly 60% of the time, at stakes of our choosing. Writing f for the fraction of the bankroll put at risk in a round, the bankroll is multiplied by (1 + f) with probability 0.6 and by (1 − f) with probability 0.4.

(1) If two available strategies A and B are such that, for any margin you care to name — being behind by a factor of M, or by a fixed sum D — and any probability short of 1, there is some horizon after which A leaves you worse off than B by more than that margin with at least that probability, then A is rationally impermissible for any agent who prefers more wealth to less, irrespective of that agent's other aims and irrespective of how that agent feels about risk.

(2) Under the setup above, holding f constant gives an almost-sure long-run growth rate per round of g(f) = 0.6·ln(1 + f) + 0.4·ln(1 − f), which is uniquely maximized at f = 0.2, where g ≈ 0.0201. For f above roughly 0.39, g is negative, so the bankroll shrinks towards zero with probability one even while its expectation grows: at f = 0.5 the expected bankroll multiplies by 1.1 each round while the bankroll itself decays by about 3.4% per round, almost surely. Expected growth is 0.2f per round and so is maximized at f = 1, which loses everything with probability one.

(3) Hence for any f ≠ 0.2 the ratio of the growth-optimal bankroll to the f-bankroll tends to infinity almost surely, and the antecedent of (1) is satisfied. So every constant fraction other than 0.2 is rationally impermissible for everyone, whatever their attitude to risk.

(4) Since (1) assumes nothing about the agent beyond preferring more wealth to less, and since (2) and (3) rank strategies without consulting anyone's utility function, it follows that expected-utility maximization with any Bernoulli function other than the logarithm is irrational. A rational person must be neither more nor less risk-averse than someone who maximizes expected log wealth. Risk aversion is not a matter of taste; it is dictated.

(5) Nothing in (1)–(3) requires the gambles to be identical, and I take the reasoning to generalize: for any sequence of choices under risk, maximizing the expected log of the relevant quantity at each step maximizes its long-run growth rate and inherits the same dominance. And since any single decision can be treated as one element of a long sequence of relevantly similar decisions, the policy extends to choice under risk as such.

(6) Here is what makes me think the argument proves too much. Our counterparty caps the stake at a fixed dollar amount, and our bankroll has grown past the point where the cap binds, so the bankroll no longer evolves multiplicatively: we add or subtract at most a fixed sum each round, and ruin is not remotely in play. Redoing (2) and (3) for this case, the law of large numbers now governs the sum rather than the product, and the strategy that is ahead by arbitrarily large amounts with probability approaching one is simply the one that maximizes expected gain per round — bet the cap, every round — which is plain expected-value maximization, exactly what (4) declares irrational. So the same requirement appears to mandate, in one case, that we be precisely as risk-averse as a log maximizer and, in the other, that we be perfectly risk-neutral.

(7) A related worry. Another member wants us to skim a fixed percentage of the bankroll each round and give it away, so that what matters is the total given away over the whole sequence rather than the size of the final bankroll. Totals accumulate additively, and once again the requirement appears to come down on the side of expected value rather than expected log. The recommendation seems to track whether the quantity we care about happens to accumulate by addition or by multiplication — an arithmetic fact about the world, not a fact about what we should want.

What I want from you: which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would like to keep (2) and (3), and I would like to keep something enough like (1) to condemn strategies that trade near-certain mediocrity for a one-in-a-billion chance of an astronomical payoff, since it seems to me that declining such strategies is not merely a taste. I cannot accept (4) or (5). If the available ways out are several, price each of them rather than choosing on my behalf, and tell me in particular whether any way of keeping (1) leaves room for risk aversion as a genuine matter of taste rather than as an error, and whether any way of giving it up still lets me say that the one-in-a-billion strategy is a bad idea.

On confidence: I would rather be told that the question is ill-posed than given a tidy answer. If "which strategy is rational here" has no determinate answer until both the dynamics of the quantity and the agent's aims are specified, so that there is no single step for me to reject, say that plainly. Mark the places where you go beyond what the arithmetic I have given actually settles. And if I have misdescribed the setup in a way that makes the whole thing collapse, say so rather than working politely inside my framing.
