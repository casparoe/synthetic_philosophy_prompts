Me and my friend got into a massive argument last night about naturalizing mental content and I'm still kind of annoyed about it, so I want to know who is actually right here.

He was defending the biological version of teleosemantics — the Millikan-ish proper function thing (I think that's the standard name for it), where the content of a representation is supposed to be fixed by what the mechanism that uses it was selected for, back in the species' history. I said the whole thing collapses, and here's why. Natural selection doesnt give a damn whether a creature's inner states are true. It only cares whether they tend to produce behaviour that pays off. So when the theory says content is determined by what the state was selected for, what its really saying is: content is whatever the state does that happens to be useful. And then there's no room left for a state getting things wrong. Take the frog case — a frog's fly-detector fires at a bit of grit, the frog snaps at nothing. On the theory, there's no fact of the matter that the frog misrepresented anything, because the only specification of content on offer was in terms of success. He kept saying no, that's what the business about normal conditions is for, which just sounds like a dodge to me, like smuggling in the very thing you were supposed to explain.

Then I tried to make it worse with a case of my own, the two lakes thing. Two populations of the same frog species, physically identical, one on each side of a ridge. West of the ridge, for thousands of generations, the only small dark moving things around were flies — well, flies plus the occasional bit of grit, which frogs snap at, spit out, and which never mattered either way. East of the ridge, small dark moving things were about half flies and half a calorie-rich seed that drops off a certain tree, and the frogs that snapped at both got more food and out-reproduced the picky ones. Now fast forward: someone transplants a load of east frogs to the west lake and vice versa, so both groups are sitting in the same environment, same flies and grit and seeds in front of them, same mechanisms, same everything you could measure. My claim is that it definately cannot be true that the west frog misrepresents when it snaps at grit while the transplanted east frog — same body, same input, same behaviour — doesn't, purely because of what happened to its ancestors. Whatever content is, it can't be that historical. He says that's just an intuition dressed up as an argument and I said fine, give me the argument then, and we went in circles for another forty minutes.

So: is the normal conditions move an actual answer or is it the dodge I think it is? Is my collapse argument attacking a strawman version of the theory? And does the theory, if it survives, end up committed to something insane like the east and west frogs literally representing different things while being molecule for molecule identical — because if it does, isn't that just game over?
