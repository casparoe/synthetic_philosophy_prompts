I write narrative content for memorial museums and historic sites, and over the better part of fifteen years I have noticed a pattern in which defeats become usable to the people who inherit them. I have never found a word for it. I would like to know whether there is an established concept for what I am describing, and — this matters to me more than the label — how exactly it should be carved and where its edges fall.

What I keep running into is this. Of the defeats a community could remember, the ones that become the emotional centre of its memory are the ones that hand later generations a loss with nothing attached to it: the losers are frozen at the moment of destruction, having acted but, as it seems, not having chosen. The 1940 evacuation from Dunkirk is inherited as small boats and rescue; the armistice that followed it, and the years of government under the occupier after that, are inherited as something else entirely — a subject that took decades to become discussable at all. The Confederate commemoration that took hold in the decades after 1865 is assembled out of the charge at Cemetery Ridge, the dead, and the generals; the actual programme of the government those men fought for, and its record of conscripting, impressing and taxing, was progressively dropped, and oddly the commemoration seems to have grown stronger as the original stake became less sayable. The Kosovo battlefield is inherited as a single afternoon and a refusal, not as the centuries of accommodation that came afterwards. And the last holdout at Masada became an emblem of defiance, while the far larger catastrophe of the Temple's destruction was inherited as grief and self-reproach rather than as a founding story.

My working explanation is that a defeat is myth-ready to the extent that inheriting it does not require inheriting the losers' exercise of power, either before or after the loss — so that what gets commemorated is the losing itself rather than what was lost. This is not just the familiar "moral victory" move; that is something a community does with a defeat, whereas what I am describing looks like a property of the defeat, since it predicts which defeats the move will work on. But two cases make me doubt the explanation. The Charge of the Light Brigade is a defeat with a documented blunder and an argument about whose fault it was that has never ended, and it is commemorated anyway. And victories get turned into emblems too, which makes me unsure whether defeat has anything essential to do with it.

So: is there an established term for this property — the thing that makes some defeats inheritable as emblems and others only as embarrassments — or does the relevant literature have concepts near it that my description only partly fits? If the latter, tell me which are nearest, how my description differs from each, and which fits best. If there is nothing established, coin a name and define it, and fix its boundaries so that the two cases above fall inside or outside for a stated reason, rather than leaving them as loose ends. I am not writing an academic piece, so please do not spend your answer on a bibliography, further reading, or citations: I want the concept, its edges, and the argument for why you drew the boundary where you did. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble and no restatement of what I have asked.
