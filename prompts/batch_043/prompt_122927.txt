I am writing a chapter that has to anticipate how a senior historian will rule on a case she has not yet ruled on publicly, and I would rather get the prediction wrong now than after the chapter is in print. Everything you need is below; please work from the record I give you rather than from anything you know about the actual scholarship on Troy, Arthur, or the Exodus, because what I am after is the rule her verdicts are tracking.

Her three rulings on the famous cases, which I take as given:

Troy. She accepts a historical destruction of a fortified city at the site on the Aegean coast, and accepts that Greek-speaking fighters were involved in fighting there, but denies the abduction, the wooden horse, and the divine machinery. History at the level of the event, legend at the level of the account.

Arthur. She denies that there was such a king. The name does not appear until centuries after the battles it is attached to; the battles are either unlocatable or are attested elsewhere without him; nothing in the fifth- and sixth-century record needs him to be explained.

The Exodus. She denies the exodus as narrated — no Egyptian record of it, no assemblage of a large population moving through the Sinai — but she allows that a small group with Egyptian names and a tradition of servitude entered the highland settlements, on the ground that servitude is not an origin anyone would invent for themselves and there is no reason for later writers to have supplied it.

Three rulings from her own monograph, on cases I will name neutrally:

The battle at the ford. A saga composed around 1180 describes a battle fought in 1036 at a river crossing, in which the ancestor of the saga's patron fled the field. A mass grave of some fifty men with weapon trauma has been excavated two and a half kilometres from that crossing and dated to the middle decades of the eleventh century, and the crossing appears in the great survey of 1086 under a name meaning the red ford, that is, in a document written a century before the saga. The saga also says the river ran red and a saint's staff floated upstream. She rules: history for the battle and for the flight, legend for the portents.

The law-giver's clauses. A law-code written down around 1240 is prefaced with an attribution of three specific clauses to a king who died in 990. The same three clauses, which restrict the crown rather than extend it, appear in two provincial law-books compiled independently of one another and of the code, in different scriptoria, in a region where one province was normally hostile to the other's kings. No material evidence could in principle exist. She rules: history.

The martyr at the spring. A passio written around 1130 says a virgin named Regula was martyred in 287 beside a spring, and that a church was built on the spot. Excavation beneath the church found a Roman-period spring with a third-century coin deposit and, a generation earlier than the alleged martyrdom, the burial of a woman whose skull injury had healed. She rules: legend, on the ground that the cult came first and the story was written to explain it — the holy place produced the martyr rather than the martyr the holy place.

I have been trying to sort these, and I have four rough instruments that I do not fully trust. First, a level distinction: she may be answering different questions when she says history and legend — whether an event of that kind occurred at all, whether the outline is right as to date and scale and agent, and whether the surviving account is a reliable report of it. Second, an asymmetry I call the cult-first inversion: a material correlate can be downstream of a tradition, produced by it in the form of a shrine, a place-name, or a commemorative monument, rather than upstream of it, so that citing the correlate as corroboration reverses the causal arrow. Third, the problem of self-sealing content: some traditions say, as part of their content, that the evidence which would test them has been destroyed, which is exactly the feature that makes them hard to falsify. Fourth, a genre-matching test: compare what the tradition claims with the kinds of record that actually survive from the gap, since a lost battle leaves no administrative trace while a lost estate does.

The new case, which she has agreed to rule on in a lecture next spring. A chronicle of an abbey, surviving in a fifteenth-century copy but composed, on internal evidence, around 1340, says that a storm surge in 1287 destroyed the abbey's whole coastal estate with its twelve dependent hamlets and drowned four hundred people, that the abbey's title deeds for that coast were lost in the same water, which is why the abbey later lost a long tithe dispute with a neighbouring lord, and that a bell from the drowned church was found intact three miles up the beach and rehung as a warning. In the chronicle's account the abbey's cellarer drowned going back for a cask of wine rather than for the reliquary. The material record: a dated marine clay layer along that coast consistent with inundation progressive across the years 1250 to 1330, one abrupt sand sheet within that window which cannot be dated to a year, and a commemorative cross erected at the traditional site of the drowned church, which is where the excavations were begun. Five of the twelve hamlets can be identified on later maps, and two of those five were still rendering rent in 1327. The documentary record, which for this region and period survives in an unusually good series of rentals and court rolls from 1270 onward, shows a sharp fall in the abbey's coastal rents between 1290 and 1327 and the note lands lost to the sea entered against several holdings; one specialist reads the hand as fourteenth-century, another as a later archivist's gloss, and the manuscript will not settle it. The abbey's cartulary is missing precisely the folios covering that coast, a physical gap in the binding rather than a silent omission, and an abbey inventing the story could more cheaply have forged replacement charters than left the gap.
