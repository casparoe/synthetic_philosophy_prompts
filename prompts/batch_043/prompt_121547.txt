I sit on the ethics consultation committee at a mid-sized community hospital, and we've slowly accumulated a track record on advance directives without ever agreeing on what we actually think we're doing, so I want to use our own precedents rather than general principles. Assume for the sake of the question that our state's advance directive statute doesn't settle any of these cases and that nobody is suing anybody: I want to know what we'd be committing to, not what the law requires. Please answer in American English.

Here are our rulings so far, more or less as they were recorded. (1) Mr. Alvarez, diagnosed with early Alzheimer's, signed a directive while he still had documented capacity saying no antibiotics once he no longer recognized his family; two years later, at that point, cheerful and seemingly content, he got pneumonia and we honored it and he died. (2) Ms. Byrne, severe anorexia, no written directive but three years of telling her therapist she'd never accept a feeding tube, all of it said while she was dangerously underweight; we judged her to lack capacity for the decision and fed her over her objection, and she later thanked us. (3) Mr. Okonkwo signed a written directive refusing blood products as a devout believer; after a stroke at 70 he developed apathy, drifted away from his church, and when asked about the directive sort of shrugged and said he didn't care anymore; we treated that as no revocation at all and honored the document. (4) Ms. Lindqvist, bipolar, wrote a Ulysses-style directive while well saying that if she became manic she should be admitted and medicated even over her protest; when a manic episode arrived she was euphoric, hugely productive, and reasoning perfectly well about everything except her own need for treatment, and we honored it anyway. (5) Mr. Rasmussen signed a formal directive to stop dialysis during a severe depressive episode while technically retaining capacity; after ECT he said he wanted to live and was grateful to still be here, and we kept dialyzing him, on the grounds that the document was a product of the depression. I can think of at least two rules that fit all five — something like "a written directive binds unless there's a later competent statement aimed squarely at it," and something like "the self whose judgment about this wasn't distorted by the relevant condition gets to speak, whenever that self existed" — and I'm sure there are others, which is exactly my problem.

The new case is what set us off. Ms. Iversen is 71 with moderate Alzheimer's, no statutory advance directive and no named proxy. Eighteen months ago, while fully competent, she took part in a research study at our hospital in which she answered a few hundred hypothetical treatment scenarios; from her answers the team built an algorithmic "values profile," went through it with her, and she signed a one-page statement saying that if she can't decide for herself the team should use the profile — informal, not on the state form, no proxy named. She's now fractured her hip. The choice is surgery plus months of painful rehab versus palliative management. She currently says, cheerfully, that she wants the operation and then she'll go home, and geriatrics says she probably does pass the standard capacity test for this decision: she understands, retains, weighs, communicates. The profile, built from her competent answers, confidently predicts she'd refuse surgery in exactly this scenario, because while competent she was extremely averse to dependence and institutional care and had said she'd rather die than end up in a nursing home; the surgery is roughly a coin flip between independent living and permanent nursing home placement. Her daughter wants palliation, her son wants surgery, and one of our members points out that people answer hypotheticals differently than they decide in the moment, so the profile may just capture a hypothetical version of her. I was arguing about this with a friend who does estate planning and she thought the answer was obvious, which is how I know it isn't.

So: what rules are actually consistent with our five prior rulings, what does each of them say about Ms. Iversen, and what's your best guess at what we should decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves? Which of the five is the closest analog and why? What principle would we be adopting with each option, stated plainly enough that we could see whether we want to own it next time? And which further case — not just a harder version of this one — would actually tell the competing rules apart? One more thing: our geriatrician keeps framing the whole thing as a fight between what she calls precedent autonomy and the welfare of the person who is definately in the bed right now, but I have a suspicion that's not the real axis and that we may be asking two different questions under one heading, one about whose values count and one about who is best placed to know them. Tell me if that's right, and please write your answer in American English.
