I sit on the program committee of a private foundation that is about to commit roughly six million dollars over four years to a small research program on the Fermi paradox: six to eight projects, mostly postdoctoral fellowships and workshops, no new observational infrastructure. The committee's mandate is explicitly not to solve the paradox. It is to fund work whose conclusions would change what some identifiable actor does — whether to expand searches and in which modalities, whether to treat so-called late-filter scenarios as live inputs to long-term assessments of humanity's own prospects, or whether to conclude that the silence is largely an artifact of how little has been looked at and that the puzzle is premature.

Before we can rank the proposals in front of us, I think we need a ranking of questions, and I would like yours. Please engage with three stipulations rather than restating the standard debate.

First, disaggregate the silence. The single datum is really a bundle of non-detections: no artifacts or probes in the solar system, no unambiguous extragalactic waste-heat or megastructure signatures, null results in narrowband radio and optical technosignature surveys, and so on. Each strand has a coverage fraction — how much of the relevant parameter space has actually been surveyed at a sensitivity that would detect the signature in question — and a translation sensitivity — how much a null result really constrains, conditional on assumptions about what a technosphere emits, in which bands, and for how long. The familiar argument that a mature civilization spreading at even a small fraction of light speed would need only a cosmically brief interval to traverse the Galaxy draws its force from treating this bundle as one overwhelmingly strong observation. I want your prioritization to say which strands are load-bearing and which are decorative, and why.

Second, separate two payoffs that can come apart: the other-directed payoff of learning something about extraterrestrial abundance, longevity, or typical trajectories, and the self-directed payoff of generating and stress-testing hypotheses about what might curtail humanity's future. A question can score highly on one and near zero on the other. Tell me which this program should be optimizing, and show how the ordering changes under the alternative.

Third, fix the criteria yourself. The committee default is importance, tractability, neglectedness. I suspect that misses two things: how upstream a question is of other questions, and how much a decision would actually differ across the possible answers (a question can be important, tractable, and neglected and still be decision-irrelevant if every answer recommends the same action). Propose the criterion set you would defend to a skeptical committee, and be explicit about the trade-offs between criteria rather than aggregating them into a single score.

Given all that: what are the four to six questions worth four years of someone's career here, in what order, and why? For each, say what it is upstream of, why it is tractable in that timeframe by people who theorize and model rather than observe, and what result over the next four years — from instruments already built or surveys already funded — would move it up or down the list. Then tell me which currently fashionable lines of work you would decline to fund and what the failure mode is that makes them attractive but sterile. Finally, if your honest view is that a dedicated program is the wrong use of six million dollars, say so and say what you would fund instead under the same mandate.

Argue from the structure of the problem, not from who has said what about it; I do not need citations or a map of the literature. A sharp memo of roughly twelve hundred to fifteen hundred words is worth more to me than an exhaustive survey.
