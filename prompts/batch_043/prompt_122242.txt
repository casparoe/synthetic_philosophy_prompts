I help run a fortnightly "madness and medicine" reading group — half first-year med students, two psych residents, one philosophy undergrad who's read Szasz and won't shut up about him, and me. Next session is on whether mental illness is a myth, and instead of another hour of everyone asserting things at each other I want to hand out a sorting tree: answer eight or nine questions about concrete cases, end up at a named position. Its going to be a one-page photocopy, so: a flowchart with a question in each box, seven or eight leaves at the bottom, plus a page of notes.

The main rule: no node may name a position or ask someone which view they like. Every branch has to be a case or a claim about a case, phrased so a student who has never heard of Szasz can answer it.

The branch questions I'd like the tree built around, in whatever order makes it work (add or drop as you think best, but say why):
- the autopsy test: would a pathologist who knew nothing about this person's life find a bodily lesion?
- the design test: is some mechanism failing to do what it is there for — and say what you're using for "there for", selection history, statistical typicality, normal functioning for the age, whatever — even when nothing shows up at autopsy?
- the exit test: does the thing dissolve if you change the person's situation, poverty, grief, a violent household, a trade that no longer exists?
- the coercion test: does having the label license anyone to do something to this person against their will?

Cases you have to use. I made most of them up on purpose so people can't pattern-match from memory; keep them roughly as described:
1. Degreaser's dementia: a factory worker in his forties, years of exposure to an industrial solvent, now apathetic, tremulous, can't hold a plan in mind; white-matter damage visible on imaging and at autopsy.
2. The old asylum patients whose grandiosity and personality change turned out to be late-stage syphilis.
3. Six months after her husband dies, a woman can't sleep, can't work, and hears his voice at the door; the big diagnostic manuals have spent the last few years arguing about whether this counts as a disorder.
4. In a port town that lost its container terminal, dozens of longshoremen in their fifties turn up with a fixed conviction that their bodies are only fit for loading work. Not in pain, not low in mood, no measurable deficit on any cognitive task — they just can't picture another life and won't retrain.
5. Teenagers who can't sit through a lesson but will play one strategy game for nine hours straight and do fine in an apprenticeship.
6. A syndrome that spreads by being described: the symptoms track whatever the online community currently says the symptoms are. Real distress, no lesion, no measurable dysfunction, and a diagnosis gets you housing and a payment.
7. A man who planned and carried out a violent assault, feels no guilt, was abused as a child; blunted stress response and reduced amygdala volume on average in people like him, but nothing you can point to on his own scan.
8. A woman starving herself to death, entirely articulate about why, not depressed, says she would rather die, refuses treatment.

Leaves I want at the bottom: hard Szaszian (disease requires a demonstrable lesion, everything else is problems in living); harmful-dysfunction naturalist; the "it'll all turn out to be neurology eventually" eliminativist; the labelling/deviance leaf; the meaningful-suffering leaf (intelligible given the life, so not an illness) and, if the tree can reach it separately, intelligible-and-still-an-illness; the useful-fiction leaf (not a disease, keep the category anyway because it unlocks care, money and excuses); and a pluralist leaf for whoever answers like a different theory on every case.

Two things I care about more than the diagram itself:
- The coercion test has to be in there somewhere, and the tree has to be able to seperate someone who is Szaszian about the metaphysics but happy to section people, from someone who thinks there are real brain diseases but that involuntary commitment is never justified. If your tree can't tell them apart, say so and tell me what question to add.
- Mark the spots in the logical space where nobody actually lives — I'd guess something like "no lesion, no dysfunction, no distress, no social disvalue, and still a disease", or "we found the lesion, and it's a reason to punish him harder" — and give me one line on each: incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Also flag any two of my leaves that these eight cases can't actually pull apart.
