So I served on a jury last spring — nothing dramatic, a small-time fraud case — and I've been chewing on it since, because the thing we do in a courtroom is genuinely weird when you write it down. We deliberately pick twelve people who know nothing about the case and then forbid them from doing their own research or bringing in anything they already know. We make each side put its case through witnesses who had first-hand knowledge and are under oath. We mostly refuse to let anyone tell the jury what some absent person said, even when it's probably true. The lawyer calling a witness can't basically feed them their answers, but the other side can do exactly that on cross. The decider picks between two packaged stories rather than going out and investigating. And if the decider can't tell, there's a burden of proof and a hung jury to absorb the failure instead of forcing a guess. At the same time we make both sides hand over what they found in discovery, we forbid the advocate from arguing what they know to be false, and the judge can go get a neutral expert of their own.

The reason it's come back to me: my team ships a document-QA thing and we grade it with an LLM judge, and someone proposed replacing the single grader with a debate setup — two models take opposite answers, take turns making short statements, a weaker model picks the winner, and we train on that verdict. The pitch is that this scales to questions the judge couldn't answer itself. But some of us have read the stuff about obfuscated arguments: if the winning move is to bury one bad step in the middle of a long chain of steps that are each individually true and checkable, a judge with bounded attention may just reward whoever sounds more confident, and training on that verdict could make the models better at looking right rather than being right.

So what I actually want: treat the courtroom thing as the practice and tell me what it presupposes. What view of testimony, of expertise, or of what an argumentative clash is even for, does it only make sense under? Does it presuppose that adversarial testing converges on the truth, or is it doing something weaker — allocating the risk of error, or forcing each side to expose its case to someone with an interest in breaking it — and does that weaker thing survive being turned into a scoring rule for models? Please be careful about the difference between what the practice strictly presupposes and what it merely sits well with. I suspect those rules are pulling in different directions: the enforced ignorance of the jury, the hearsay ban, the discovery obligations, the rule that the advocate doesn't testify, the burden of proof. Which of them are load-bearing for the truth story, and which are really about fairness, or about not scaring witnesses off, or about letting losers accept the outcome?

Then the part I care most about. Map it onto the model setup and say which of the courtroom's presuppositions the debate protocol actually inherits and which it quietly drops. My guesses, tell me if I'm wrong: the decider in court is judging people who lose something if they're caught lying and who are under a professional duty not to mislead, while the debaters have no stakes and no duty; the courtroom gives the decider documents and generous time and, crucially, the option of not deciding; and the courtroom's evidence is anchored to first-hand witnesses rather than to whatever either side chooses to assert. For each of those: is it essential to the practice's claim to get at the truth, and can you rebuild it in the AI version cheaply, or does rebuilding it cost you the thing that made debate attractive in the first place? We're also toying with two hacks and I want to know what each presupposes. One: every debater must file, before seeing the other side's argument, the single check that would prove them wrong — call it a falsification deposit — and the judge may run only those checks. Two: when the judge can't tell, it abstains and the system falls back on a pre-committed default answer instead of a coin flip, the way the burden of proof does. Do those actually import the courtroom idea or just its vocabulary?

If you think part of the fight between the debate people and the obfuscation people is verbal, say which part and why. And if you had to design the eval so it doesnt just train fluent liars, what would you change — I don't mean use a better judge, I mean which feature of the procedure is carrying the weight, and what would we have to give up to get it. Sorry this is long, I've definately been overthinking it.
