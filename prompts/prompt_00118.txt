I've been developing a graduate seminar on bargaining and decision theory for agents with access to commitment devices, and I keep getting tripped up on the "commitment races" problem as it's been articulated in the rationalist/decision-theory literature (e.g., Kokotajlo's writeup and related discussions of logical time and threat-resistant bargaining). The rough idea, as I understand it, is that if agents can benefit from precommitting to strategies (including punitive ones) before their counterparts do, this creates pressure to commit as early as possible in "logical time," which seems to generate a regress or race condition rather than a stable equilibrium. What I'm struggling to articulate clearly for my students is why earlier commitment isn't simply, unambiguously advantageous in the way that first-mover advantage is in ordinary game theory — and how the introduction of updateless decision theories is supposed to help, given that updatelessness itself seems to just push the race further back rather than dissolving it.

Could you give me an intuitive, mechanism-level explanation of why the commitment race doesn't have an obvious terminus, and what specifically breaks down when naive updatelessness is proposed as a solution? I'd like to understand the core unresolved tension well enough to motivate it for students without leaning on hand-wavy appeals to "logical time is confusing" — ideally something that clarifies what would even count as a resolution, if one is possible.
