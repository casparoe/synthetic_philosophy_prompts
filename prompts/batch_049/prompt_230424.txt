I am a novelist, and in the book I am drafting a statutory body, the Biotic Classification Commission, rules case by case on whether particular entities count as alive. I need its ruling in a new case to be consistent with the precedents I have already written. The Commission has held: (1) a laboratory RNA molecule that copies itself with occasional errors in a cell-free medium is alive, because the capacity for Darwinian evolution was decisive and neither a cell membrane nor a metabolism was required; (2) a desiccated bacterial endospore is alive, as a dormant phase of a metabolizing, reproducing lineage; and (3) a sterile mule is alive, because active self-maintenance, not reproductive capacity, is what matters for an individual organism. The new case is a synthetic protocell that imports nutrients, repairs its own membrane, and keeps its internal composition stable against its surroundings — it has a genuine, continuous metabolism and homeostasis — but it cannot copy itself or evolve. What should the Commission decide, which of its earlier rulings is the closest analog, and if the precedents genuinely underdetermine the answer, what principle would it be adopting under each possible ruling — and what further case would reveal which principle it had actually adopted?
