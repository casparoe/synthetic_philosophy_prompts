I run a small volunteer anti-corruption group in a mid-sized city — mostly retired teachers, shop owners, a couple of city employees who keep a low profile. We spend our monthly meetings arguing about cases people bring in, and I've started to think we're not even arguing about the same concept. Some members say corruption is only when someone breaks an actual rule or law; if a mayor gives his nephew a job and the hiring manual doesn't forbid it, they call it bad judgment, not corruption. Others say no, it's corruption whenever a public official puts private loyalties ahead of the public, legal or not. Then there's a third group that pushes back on all of us, saying gift-giving and helping your people are how communities hold together, and calling every cash envelope at a funeral 'corruption' is just imposing an outside standard. The same three cases keep splitting us: a procurement officer steering a small contract to a cousin, a nurse taking a gift from a family after a surgery, and a councilor fast-tracking a permit for someone who donated to their campaign.

Id like to make a 'which view of corruption do you actually hold?' quiz for our next workshop, so we can sort people by how they answer cases rather than which label they've learned to defend. Please write eight to ten questions, each a short concrete scenario with three answer options that reflect genuinely different judgments; then give a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto at least four positions: the rule-based/legalist position (corruption equals breaking formal rules), the public-interest position (it's about predictable harm to the public, whatever the rules say), the relational position (a genuine personal relationship can turn the same act into a gift, not a bribe), and the universalist position (even fully legal favor-swapping is corruption if it entrenches inequality). If a fifth position falls out of the questions naturally, add it. No question should just ask 'do you think clientelism is wrong?' — each one should be a case where the positions come apart, like the same amount of money given before versus after a permit is issued, or a favor done for a stranger versus a close relative. At the end, tell me which pairs of positions the quiz can't fully separate, if any, and which answer patterns have no real defenders.
