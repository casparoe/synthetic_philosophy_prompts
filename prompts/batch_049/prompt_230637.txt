I teach social and political philosophy, and I've somehow ended up chairing our college's study-abroad advising committee. The questions students keep bringing in are exactly the ones the philosophy of conventions and social norms is about, but our advisors have no systematic way to turn that into usable advice. The recurring question is some version of: 'Do I actually have to tip here? queue this formally? not cross against the light? or am I just doing it to avoid weird looks?' Advisors see several students a day in peak season, have maybe fifteen minutes each, and whatever guidance they give ends up in the handbook I have to sign off on, so I need something defensible and reusable, not a one-off opinion. Get it wrong and a student either offends their hosts or follows some local practice they find demeaning out of misplaced scrupulousness.

So: you're an expert on social norms, conventions, and the line between the conventional and the moral. Please draft a one-page decision procedure — a checklist or flowchart in words — that a non-philosopher advisor can run through with a student to determine whether the student is morally required, merely expected, or free to skip a local convention. Draw on whatever philosophical work on conventions and norms you actually know, but do not cite anything; I want the reasoning compressed, not a reading list. Think carefully, and maybe start by listing the different reasons a norm can bind a visitor before you collapse them into steps. The procedure should distinguish (at least) between: (a) norms whose violation would impose real costs on someone who reasonably relied on you, like a tipped worker whose employer can legally pay them a couple dollars an hour; (b) harmless coordination devices like a queue; and (c) norms that work mostly as ways of showing respect or recognition, like forms of address and greetings. It should also handle the asymmetry where a local breaking their own norm can look like critique or reform, while a visitor doing the same thing reads as contempt. It should say what to do when compliance is cheap but the student objects on principle, and it should mark the steps where the procedure can't settle things and has to hand the decision back to a person because it's a value conflict rather than an application of a norm.

Then run the procedure on three cases. First: a student going to a U.S. state where servers earn a tipped minimum wage and depend on tips, who believes tipping is a pernicious custom that lets employers underpay workers and wants to refuse to tip as a protest; I suspect the procedure will tell them to tip anyway, and I want that made honest and uncomfortable rather than softened. Second: a student in a city where crossing against the light is technically a petty offense but totally unenforced, and the host family crosses against the light constantly. Third: a student told they must wait in a long, formal queue at a market where, as far as they can tell, nobody would be harmed by stepping ahead. Lastly, tell me plainly what the procedure cannot do — which cases its likely to misclassify, and where the biggest judgment calls are hidden inside steps that look mechanical.
