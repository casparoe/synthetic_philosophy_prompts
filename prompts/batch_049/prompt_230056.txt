I am drafting a statement of my normative views on strikes and the right to organize, and before I circulate it I would like a consistency check. I find myself committed to all of the following, but I have not worked out how they fit together.

1. The right to strike is a collective right held by workers as an organized group, not merely the sum of individual rights to quit or withhold labor. A lawful strike is not a mass resignation; it is a collective act whose authority belongs to the workers as a body.
2. Workers who cross a picket line during a properly called strike may be morally criticized for undermining their coworkers' collective action, but the law should not compel them to join the strike. The individual choice to work must remain legally protected.
3. Procedure is not a side issue for me. The normative authority of a strike comes from the collective decision of the workers it binds: it should normally be authorized through the union, after a fair and informed vote in which the workers understand the likely costs and risks. A strike called by an unaccountable faction without such authorization is presumptively illegitimate.
4. Yet there are cases in which the employer's conduct is itself a serious and ongoing violation of workers' basic rights — dangerous working conditions, systematic non-payment of wages, coercive management practices. In those cases, even a minority of the affected workers may walk out immediately, without majority authorization. The employer's wrong, not the procedure, does the justificatory work.
5. Strikers may pressure their own employer, but they may not blockade, picket, or otherwise coerce a different, neutral employer merely because it does business with the struck firm. Pressuring parties who are not involved in the dispute is wrong.
6. Sympathy strikes, however, are legitimate. If the employees of a second firm decide for themselves to stop work in support of strikers at the first firm, that is a permissible exercise of their own collective rights, even though their own employer is not party to the original dispute.
7. The mere fact that a strike raises prices, delays shipments, cancels services, or inconveniences the public does not count against its legitimacy. Consumers have no entitlement to uninterrupted access to the goods and services that others produce.
8. Nevertheless, I think strikes by emergency-room nurses, firefighters, and air-traffic controllers should be legally prohibited or tightly restricted, because their withdrawal of labor endangers the lives and basic security of third parties.

My question is whether these commitments form a coherent package. Go through them pairwise where relevant: which pairs are genuinely in tension, and for each, how severe is the tension and what would have to give? Which pairs only seem to be in tension, and why does the appearance dissolve under a more precise reading? Finally, rank the genuine tensions by severity and tell me which single revision would do the most to make the set coherent.
