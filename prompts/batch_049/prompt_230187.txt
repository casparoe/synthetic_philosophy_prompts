I am moderating a Q&A session with a philosopher who defends the self-indication assumption (SIA) across anthropic reasoning: the principle that, other things equal, one should treat one's own existence as evidence favoring hypotheses on which many observers exist, since one is more likely to exist if the universe contains many observers. She is an invented defender of a real, recognizable view, not a real person, so the questions should engage only the position. Her standing reply to the presumptuous philosopher is that the case is not a reductio of SIA but a straightforward consequence of correct Bayesian updating, and her standing objection to the self-sampling assumption is that it must choose a reference class and any choice begs the question. Write a sequence of questions that makes this defensive line progressively harder to run. Start by asking whether SIA itself can fix the notion of "observer" without covertly adopting a reference class, then confront her with the presumptuous philosopher scenario—two otherwise empirically equivalent cosmological theories differing only in how many observers they entail—and after her expected response that she is merely conditionalizing, press the follow-up of whether any possible evidence could count against the more-populous theory. Conclude with a question about what empirical or conceptual finding would lead her to abandon SIA. Throughout, each main question should anticipate the standard reply and include the follow-up that would expose what that reply presupposes.
