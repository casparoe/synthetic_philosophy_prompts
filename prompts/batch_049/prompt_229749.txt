I am preparing a recorded colloquium interview with a political philosopher and legal scholar whose work defends the conditional-amnesty position in transitional justice. The topic has recently become personal for me: a member of my family was disappeared during Argentina's last military dictatorship, and my relatives obtained the relevant case records only within the past year, which has forced me to think hard about what disclosure can and cannot do when prosecution is unavailable. I am not interested in questions that merely invite her to restate the view, nor in biographical or "what drew you to this field" questions.

Her position, as I understand it, is this. A state emerging from mass atrocity may legitimately offer individual perpetrators amnesty on condition of full public disclosure, when (a) pressing prosecutions would realistically endanger a negotiated transition rather than merely being politically costly, (b) the required disclosure is full, specific, and subject to independent verification, and (c) the amnesty is granted case by case rather than as a blanket shield. She makes two further claims: that truth obtained in this way has intrinsic reparative value for victims and survivors, and that South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission amnesty process, for all its documented failures, remains the best available model for such bargains. - the impunity objection: that any amnesty, even conditional and individualized, entrenches a norm that power can negotiate away accountability, with long-term costs to the rule of law in the successor state;
