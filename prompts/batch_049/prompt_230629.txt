I keep noticing a pattern in how historians explain big events, and I don't have a word for it. Everyone agrees the First World War had many causes — the alliance system, the naval arms race, the assassination at Sarajevo — but everyone also agrees that if you removed any single one of them, the war would probably still have come about anyway. Yet nobody treats that as a reason to stop calling any of them a cause. So historical explanation seems full of causes that fail the "but for" test, and that's treated as fine rather than as a defect. Is there a name for this? I know "overdetermination" is somewhere nearby, but the standard cases (two rocks hitting a window at the same moment) have each cause sufficient on its own, whereas here no single cause looks close to sufficient. I don't just want a label, though: I want to know what exactly makes these cases hang together, where the boundary is between a but-for-failing cause that still genuinely explains and one that shouldn't count, and whether the historians' tolerance here is better understood as a different notion of causation or a different notion of explanation. Could you also seperate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, no preamble, and don't restate my question.
