I have been trying to articulate the principle behind my judgments that some hypotheses are simpler than others, and I want to know whether a single coherent principle can be extracted from them, or whether I am helping myself to a notion of simplicity that no formal account can underwrite. I would like a definite answer: the principle you would defend, stated precisely, not a survey of options.

My working principle is something like this: when two hypotheses fit the available data, the simpler one deserves more prior probability, and simplicity is shortness of description—roughly, the length of the shortest program that generates the hypothesis's predictions. This should cash out as a prior of the Solomonoff sort, where probability decreases with Kolmogorov complexity.

Here are the verdicts the principle has to capture.

(1) I judge a linear rule—y increases by one whenever x increases by one—to be simpler than a wiggly 50-parameter function that fits the same points, even when the linear rule fits slightly worse, and I think the linear rule is the better inductive bet.

(2) I judge the hypothesis that all emeralds are green to be simpler than the hypothesis that all emeralds are grue, even though the two fit the same observations so far, and I do not think introducing a time-indexed predicate is a legitimate way to flip the simplicity ordering.

(3) I judge the hypothesis that a sequence is just 01 repeated a million times to be simpler than the hypothesis that it is this particular random-looking string, even if the second hypothesis can be stated briefly in a language that happens to contain a primitive name for that very string.

(4) Yet I also accept that for any data at all, there is some programming language in which the data has a very short description—add a primitive that outputs exactly those data—and I feel that simplicity judgments should not be at the mercy of whoever designed the language. I want the difference between (3) and the rigged language in (4) to be principled, not just a feeling.

(5) I accept that two universal machines can disagree about which of two hypotheses is shorter; but I still believe there is a language-independent fact of the matter that a smooth curve is simpler than a lookup table, and I do not see how a Solomonoff-style prior, defined relative to a machine, can deliver that fact.

(6) I think a hypothesis encoded by a long, random-looking program should receive less prior probability than a short, elegant one even when both fit the data; but if the relevant prior is not computable, I do not see how it can be a norm governing the beliefs of a finite agent like me.

(7) I suspect the language-dependence worry in (5) is overstated because any two universal machines agree on simplicity up to an additive constant; but for two fixed hypotheses, the constant may exceed the difference in length, so the invariance theorem seems to leave the particular case unsettled.

Is there a principle—Solomonoff induction, Kolmogorov complexity, minimum description length, or something else—that captures what is right in these verdicts and says clearly which of them I should drop? Give me the principle you would defend, stated precisely enough that I can see how it handles (1) through (7), and tell me what it says about a further case I have not settled: two hypotheses that are equally short, but one of which is astronomically more expensive to compute with. And be explicit about which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, rather than saving all of them with epicycles.
