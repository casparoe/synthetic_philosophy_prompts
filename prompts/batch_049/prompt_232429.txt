I run an upper-level undergraduate seminar on institutional design with about twenty students and a single ninety-minute session to devote to this. The course has been working through the idea of futarchy: roughly, the proposal that a polity should democratically choose a metric of success, while prediction markets, rather than majority votes, decide which policies are actually adopted, by forecasting that metric conditional on each candidate policy. I want the students to feel, in one session, both the force of the claim that markets can aggregate dispersed private information better than open deliberation or voting, and the force of the standard doubts: thin or one-sided markets, manipulation, unequal access to information, the gap between what a person bets and what a person wants, and the problem of choosing and gaming the success metric. The activity should be runnable without any opening lecture on the idea: the procedure should simply be introduced to them as "how today's decision will be made," and the conceptual vocabulary should be held back until the debrief.

Please design a prediction-market governance game in which the class actually governs some small real decision using something like the futarchic procedure: first a values vote on what outcome dimension should count as success (for example, what would make the decision good for the class), then a conditional prediction market on that dimension under two candidate policies, and finally a decision rule that mechanically follows the market's judgment rather than the initial vote. The decision should be real and consequential enough that preferences are authentic—a scheduling choice, an allocation of workload, a grade-weight adjustment within fixed bounds—but contained enough to be settled in the room. Give me complete rules, a trading mechanism using paper tokens or play money, a payoff scheme that does not require real money, a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the exercise onto the parts of the futarchic idea: the separation of values from beliefs, the hope for information aggregation, and the failure points the students actually encountered.

Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it—collusion, markets too thin to price anything, students trading to express preferences rather than to convey information, one student holding a dominant informational position, or the class strategically gaming the metric it just chose—and how I should handle each failure when it happens. Be frank about which feature of futarchy a single classroom exercise cannot reproduce, such as the difference between a one-off, small-stakes decision and repeated, high-stakes governance where the metric itself becomes a target of strategic behavior.
