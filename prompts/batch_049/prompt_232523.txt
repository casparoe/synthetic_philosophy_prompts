I spent thirty years in a spectroscopy lab and I'm new to philosophy, so I'll ask a maybe-dumb question. I've been reading about what markets are for, and the laws that govern trading and betting keep looking to me like they have a very specific but possibly incoherent theory of information.

Here's the practice as I understand it. If an executive at a drug company learns the trial failed and sells his shares before the announcement, he goes to prison: insider trading. But if a quant fund buys the same stock because it hires clever people, or pays for a faster feed, or uses satellite photos of factory parking lots to get a jump on the next quarterly report, that's legal, and a lot of people would call it exactly what markets are supposed to do. Both cases involve one party making money off information the other side hasn't put together yet. The difference isn't confidence or accuracy, and it isn't that one is more likely to be right. The difference seems to be something about whether the information was obtained in the right way.

Then there's a third case. Regulators in the U.S. have generally been comfortable with a casino taking bets on a horse race or a football game, but much less comfortable with a platform taking bets on which party will control Congress — even though the second one would at least distill public information into a number people could use, even if its not obvious why that should be less respectable than a bet on a horse.

While I was turning this over, I came across a no-trade theorem, or at least a gloss of one. If I understood it right: under some strict assumptions, if the only reason two parties would trade is a difference in information, then a rational person should notice the other side's willingness to trade, take it as evidence, update, and the trade should unravel. So purely informational differences should not generate trade at all.

So here's what I want to know. What theory of information — or of what a market is even for — does this pattern of practices presuppose? Is there a coherent line that lets you forbid insider trading, praise the quant fund, and block election betting, or is the practice trying to serve fairness, property rights, and price discovery at the same time without noticing they pull apart? I'd like you to separate what the rules strictly presuppose from what they merely fit well with. And tell me what would have to change if we actually ran markets on the view that their job is to aggregate information; how would the no-trade theorem fit into that picture?
