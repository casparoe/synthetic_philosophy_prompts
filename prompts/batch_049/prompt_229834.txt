I have been trying to hold onto a broadly functionalist picture of moral status — the view that what matters in deciding whether a system has experiences, and is therefore owed anything, is not what the system is made of but the causal or computational pattern it realizes. An argument I constructed has boxed me in, and I cannot tell which step to give up.

(1) Moral status is substrate-independent. If a person's neurons were replaced one at a time by silicon parts that preserved each neuron's causal role — same inputs, same outputs, same downstream effects — the person's experiences and moral status would be preserved throughout. The pattern, not the material, carries the moral weight.

(2) Therefore, whether a system has moral status is settled by which abstract causal or computational patterns it implements.

(3) The natural definition of implementation is mapping-based: a physical system implements an abstract pattern just in case there is a correspondence between the system's physical states and the pattern's states such that the system's actual state transitions map onto the pattern's transition rules.

(4) Under that definition, implementation is far too cheap. For any finite-state pattern that a brain implements, and for any physical system with sufficiently many distinguishable microstates that changes over time — a wall, a bucket of water, the weather over Kansas — there is a gerrymandered mapping that makes that system implement the same pattern. Group the physical states the right way, and the wall's transitions reproduce the pattern's transitions.

(5) So the wall implements a humanlike pattern; by (2), it has whatever moral status that pattern confers. And because many different mappings exist, nearly any sufficiently complex system hosts many minds, not one.

(6) That conclusion is absurd. If moral status is this cheap, the concept does no boundary-drawing work at all: everything with enough degrees of freedom matters, or distinctions among systems are mapping-relative and arbitrary.

(7) So at least one of (2)–(4) must go, or (6) must be surrendered in favor of a computational panpsychism on which there is no principled answer to which computations matter.

What I would most like to save is (1) together with the silicon-replacement result; the intuition there feels solid. If keeping them requires denying that a gerrymandered mapping of a wall is a genuine implementation, then I need to know what that denial costs: an account of intrinsic or real implementation that goes beyond map-preservation, and every candidate I can think of — genuine causal roles, counterfactual-supporting states, natural-kind groupings of states — gets accused of smuggling in biological chauvinism. Which step is actually wrong, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, can one block the absurd conclusion without losing substrate-independence, or is the familiar rationalist commitment to caring about the computation rather than the substrate exactly what pushes the conclusion through?
