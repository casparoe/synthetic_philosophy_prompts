I retired a few years ago after a career in chemistry—mostly kinetics and spectroscopy—and I've been filling the time with political philosophy, read in a pretty haphazard way. Recently I came across an essay by Simone Weil (I can't recall the title) where she argued, if I understood her correctly, that political parties are not just occasionally corrupt but corrupting by their very structure: they manufacture collective passions, and they put pressure on members to stop judging things for themselves. The idea has stuck with me more than I expected.

It's stuck because of a local argument I'm in the middle of. I've spent years in a neighborhood group that started out completely practical—we fixed a flooding problem, got a bus route restored, that sort of thing. We deliberately kept it nonpartisan, and it has worked partly because people with very different politics leave their party hats at the door. Now a younger faction is pushing to turn the group into a proper political party: register candidates, adopt a platform, impose voting discipline. Their argument is that we're being naive, and that without a party label and its discipline we will never have real influence. My old scientific instinct says they're probably right about the mechanics of power. But I've also noticed that over the last few months the tone has shifted—people have started talking about loyalty and "our side" in a way they didn't before.

So here's what I'd like help with: should Weil's worry about the party form itself bear on this decision, or is it a purist objection that can't survive contact with actual politics? More concretely, is there reason to think that becoming a party would change how the group thinks and acts, in a way that loosing the label later wouldn't fix?
