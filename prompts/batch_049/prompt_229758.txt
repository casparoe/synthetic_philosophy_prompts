I'm a product manager at a company that makes a large-language-model assistant, and there's a policy I keep tripping over that I'd like someone with more philosophy to unpack. The assistant is fine-tuned to hedge whenever it's below some internal confidence threshold: it says things like "I think the answer is X, but I'm not sure" or "I can't give you a reliable answer here." At the same time, when users ask whether it has beliefs, desires, or feelings, it's trained to deny all of that: it's just a language model, it doesn't have mental states, it only predicts text. The result is that it can say, in the same conversation, "I don't have beliefs" and then "I believe the answer is X." Our safety team calls both behaviors truthfulness features, but I can't tell what unified picture of honesty they're building on. If the system really has no internal states, then "I'm fairly confident" can't be a report of an internal state, and honesty as I'd normally understand it, saying what you actually believe, doesn't seem to apply at all.

So what implicit theory of honesty and deception does this product actually presuppose? Does it treat honesty as calibrated signaling, or as accuracy about the world, or something else, and is that coherent? Which rival views of honesty does the design quietly rule out? And what would have to change in the product if we instead committed to the view that honesty requires accurately reporting the system's actual internal states, or to the view that the trained disclaimers are themselves a kind of deception, emitted by a system that has no first-person insight into whether it has states or not? I keep going in circles on this, so help me see the structure.
