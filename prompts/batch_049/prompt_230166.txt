I recently read a popular book on the philosophy of probability that gives a compact summary of Bertrand's paradox and its consequences for the principle of indifference. The book says, in substance, the following. Bertrand asked for the probability that a chord drawn at random in a circle is longer than a side of the inscribed equilateral triangle. There are at least three natural ways to interpret "at random," and they yield probabilities of one-half, one-third, and one-fourth. The principle of indifference says that when no evidence favors one possible case over another, equal probabilities should be assigned; since all three drawing methods look like fair applications of it, the principle gives contradictory answers about a single well-defined event. The book's conclusion is that Bertrand's paradox therefore exposes the principle of indifference as incoherent, and that by choosing a convenient description of the possibilities one can defend almost any probability assignment whatever.

I suspect the phrase "contradictory answers about a single well-defined event" and the book's conclusion are doing more work than the mathematics justifies, but I don't have the technical background to separate the fair parts from the overstatement. Please audit this summary for me: state which parts a careful probabilist or philosopher of probability would accept, which are subtly mistaken, and what a defender of the principle of indifference would say in reply.
