I'm a retired physicist and I keep trying to figure out what makes me call one claim about the past pseudohistory and another claim legitimate speculation. My gut verdicts don't line up, and I want to know if there's a principle behind them.

Atlantis. As literal history I'd call it pseudohistory — the island that sank in a day and a night, the war with Athens, all of it. But I also think Plato was probably garbling something real, maybe the Thera eruption and the Minoan collapse. So I hold both: literal Atlantis is crankery, but "Plato half-remembered a real catastrophe" is fine. That already feels like it should bother me more than it does.

Troy. Before Schliemann, using Homer as a source for a real city looked like the same move — trusting myth against the professionals. But a Bronze Age city at Hisarlik really was destroyed more than once. So rejecting expert consensus can't be what makes something pseudohistory. Yet I'd still put Fomenko's New Chronology — the claim that ancient and medieval history is a vast forgery — in a totally different, bottomless category. The difference can't just be that Troy turned out right and Fomenko is absurd, because that would make pseudohistory a matter of hindsight.

Ancient aliens. Von Däniken's claim that the pyramids or the Nazca lines needed extraterrestrial help is pseudohistory, obviously. But the weird part is that some of the puzzles he points to are real engineering questions, and his explanations are at least phrased in a physical-sounding vocabulary. I want to say the problem isn't that his hypotheses are extraordinary — extraordinary things do happen — but something about how he treats evidence: he dosen't seem to care whether it could count against him. Compare Wegener: continental drift was mocked for decades and is now orthodoxy. So "the establishment rejected it" works on neither side as a criterion.

Menzies and pre-Columbian contact. I think Gavin Menzies' 1421 thesis — that Chinese fleets reached the Americas before Columbus — is pseudohistory, even though pre-Columbian contact is a perfectly respectable question and we now know the Norse reached Newfoundland (L'Anse aux Meadows). Menzies probably has more "evidence" than the Norse case did early on. So it can't just be amount of evidence either.

Is there one principle of demarcation that gets these verdicts, or most of them? What would it say about a case I keep going back and forth on: the Kensington Runestone, a carved stone found in Minnesota in 1898 with an inscription claiming Norse explorers reached the area in 1362, which most specialists think is a modern hoax but which keeps getting re-litigated on rune forms and weathering? And which of my verdicts does the principle force me to give up?
