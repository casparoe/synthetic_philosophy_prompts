Question: What is inference to the best explanation, and how seriously should we take the bad lot objection?

Student response: Inference to the best explanation is the form of reasoning in which one infers, from a body of evidence, that the hypothesis that best explains the evidence is true. The quality of an explanation is assessed through virtues such as scope, precision, simplicity, and fit with background knowledge. For example, if a friend is unusually curt, I may infer that she is upset because that hypothesis would make sense of several features of her behavior at once. Lipton distinguishes between what he calls the loveliest explanation and the likeliest explanation, but this is mostly a terminological nicety: the loveliest explanation is simply the one that scores highest on the explanatory virtues, and since those virtues are precisely what make a hypothesis probable, the loveliest explanation is by definition the likeliest one. The bad lot objection, introduced by van Fraassen, observes that inference to the best explanation can only choose the best hypothesis from among the candidates actually considered. If the true hypothesis is not in that set, the procedure will still select the best of a bad lot, and the selected hypothesis will be false. This shows that in any real case where we cannot prove that our candidate list is exhaustive, inference to the best explanation gives us no reason at all to prefer its output over a randomly chosen hypothesis. Since we can never prove exhaustiveness in advance, the only way to make the inference rational would be to guarantee that the truth is already among our candidates, which is impossible. Inference to the best explanation is therefore rationally indefensible in practice.
