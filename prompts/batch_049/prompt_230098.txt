Here's a thing I've been turning over. The Confucian role-ethics reading of the Analects says that there's no self standing behind or beneath the roles you play: you are constitued by being a daughter or son, a friend, a teacher, a minister, and the work of a life is to get good at those roles—to play them with the right attention, timing, and sensitivity to ritual—rather than to uncover some inner essence that was waiting there all along. Now think about the modern ideal of authenticity, the idea that's all over therapy talk and career advice that you have a true inner self and the main job is to make your outer life match it: quit the job that looks good on paper, set firm boundaries with family, refuse to keep performing a role that doesn't feel like the real you. What do you think Confucius would have made of that? I don't want a generic "he'd say people should be sincere" answer. I want you to actually work it out from role ethics. Can the role view even state the ideal of authenticity, or does it have to diagnose the whole "real you" talk as a confusion? Or would Confucius say the search for a real self points at a genuine problem—say, people playing their roles mechanically or dishonestly—but badly misdescribes it? And push the other way too: does the role view have a hard time explaining why someone stuck in a role that is genuinely deforming her should leave it, rather than just learn to play the role more deftly? Give me your best reconstruction of what the position would be, and say where you think it creaks.
