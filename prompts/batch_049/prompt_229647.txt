I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I keep running into a line that gets used to shut down arguments about misinformation: "you can't reason someone out of a position they didn't reason themselves into." The latest sighting was a media scholar on a panel about whether fact-checking on social media does anything; he deployed it to dismiss the whole enterprise — corrective posts, prebunking videos, all of it — on the grounds that people inside epistemic bubbles never arrive at their views by weighing evidence, so presenting better evidence can't dislodge them. It sounded right when he said it, and then to true: the sentence seemed to be doing six different jobs at once depending on what counts as "reasoning" and what it is to "reason yourself into" a belief.

I'd like you to unpack it for me. Lay out the distinct precise claims that slogan could be expressing — about how beliefs are caused, what role evidence can play after a belief is formed, identity-protective cognition, social trust, motivated reasoning, the difference between an echo chamber and an epistemic bubble, whatever else you find — and give me a verdict on each reading: trivial, substantive and defensible, or just false. I suspect at least one reading is just a truism wearing work clothes and at least one is probably false, but I can't say which. Then tell me which of these readings the "fact-checking is useless" argument actually needs for its conclusion to follow, and which one the panelist (and people like him) probably mean when they use the line to end the argument.
