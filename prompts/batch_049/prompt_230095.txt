I teach a third-year philosophy of mind seminar and I’m drafting the final exam. I want one 90-minute essay question on the Orwellian/Stalinesque debate about the timing of conscious experience, built around the color-phi phenomenon. You are to play the role of a philosopher of mind who has examined on this material before and is comfortable with the Multiple Drafts / Cartesian Theater framing. Think carefully before you commit: first brainstorm the three or four most common student mistakes on this topic, then write the exam question, then the model answer, then the marking scheme.

The exam question must not simply ask students to define Orwellian and Stalinesque revision or to summarize the color-phi display. It should present a concrete, tempting empirical test and force the student to explain why that test cannot settle the dispute even in principle. Here is the target. A student proposes that subjects viewing the color-phi display — two differently colored spots flashed in alternation, which they report as a single moving spot that changes color partway along its apparent path — press a button the instant they first become aware of the moving spot or its color change. The student’s thought is that a Stalinesque delay between stimulus and consciousness would show up as a longer reaction time, while Orwellian revision after consciousness would leave reaction time fast but corrupt the retrospective report. The exam question should ask students to assess this proposal and diagnose the underlying assumption about consciousness that it relies on.

Deliverables:

1. The exam question, self-contained, not requiring authors or citations, with a final sentence telling students they may use the button-press proposal plus one other illustrative proposal as a way into the issue.

2. A model answer of roughly 750 words. It should: (a) state the anomaly the color-phi display poses; (b) state the Orwellian and Stalinesque models without caricature; (c) show precisely why the button-press latencies cannot discriminate between them; (d) identify the deeper assumption — a single moment or place at which contents become conscious — that generates the appearance of a real empirical dispute; and (e) explain what a genuinely deciding experiment would need. The final paragraph should say what would change the conclusion: which independent criterion or differently structured theory would make the debate empirically tractable, and where the no-fact-of-the-matter argument is most vulnerable.

3. A marking scheme worth 25 marks. It must separate an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific moves each must contain. For each of the common mistakes you brainstormed, say which mark it loses and why, phrasing each penalty as a misunderstanding — for example, assuming that a delay before consciousness adds linearly to reaction time, or treating Orwellian revision as deliberate lying.
