I am a philosopher of mind, and next month I am giving a talk at our university's Center for Mind and Brain defending the following methodological thesis: the science of consciousness should adopt heterophenomenology as its default stance. By heterophenomenology I mean the practice of treating a subject's first-person reports—verbal descriptions, button presses, confidence ratings—as data about what the subject believes, or is disposed to say, is going on in her own mind, rather than as direct evidence about the experiences themselves. My positive claim is that this stance is not eliminativist: it is a neutral method that lets researchers study the contents of mental life without first committing to any theory of what consciousness is, and it is the only way to keep the empirical study of consciousness from quietly smuggling in a metaphysics.

The audience is not a philosophy department. It is a consciousness research group of roughly thirty cognitive psychologists and neuroscientists who use first-person reports as outcome measures in paradigms such as binocular rivalry, visual masking, and change blindness, and who treat a report like I saw the face as evidence that the face was consciously seen. Several of them have recently adopted no-report paradigms, in which consciousness is inferred from behavior, eye movements, or neural decoding rather than from subjective reports, specifically to avoid the worry that the report step contaminates the measure. Two philosophers also attend: one is a practitioner of micro-phenomenological interviewing, who thinks subjects can be trained to give much richer and more reliable descriptions of lived experience than ordinary spontaneous reports allow; the other is an illusionist, who holds that phenomenal consciousness has no properties over and above those posited by a deflationary theory and that the appearances are a useful fiction. The scientists are not much moved by metaphysics; they will object to anything that seems to force them to redo their experiments or to stop treating well-designed subjective reports as data about consciousness itself.

I know the standard philosophical objections—that heterophenomenology is not really neutral, that it ignores the hard problem, that its subject is a theorist's fiction. I need the objections this audience will actually raise. My argument, for targeting purposes, runs roughly as follows: reports are our only entry point into another subject's conscious life, and any report can be false, biased, or confabulated, so the honest procedure is to record what subjects say they experience and then explain why they say it; treating the reports as direct evidence for experience is itself a substantive empirical assumption that would have to be justified, and researchers who make that assumption are already doing philosophy of mind, only without noticing it.

Please prepare my Q&A prep sheet: the six to eight objections I am most likely to get from this group, ordered by how much damage I should expect each to do, taking both likelihood and force into account, phrased in the way these scientists and those two philosophers would actually phrase them, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is weakest. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks.
