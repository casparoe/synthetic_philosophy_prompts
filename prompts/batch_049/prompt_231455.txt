I am the community member on my hospital's patient-safety review committee, not a clinician, and I have no philosophical or legal training, but I have been turning this week's case over on my own. The committee has asked me to draft the short written opinion, and the members are split over what it means to say that an omission caused a death.

The case is as follows. An older patient with sepsis and several chronic conditions was admitted to a general ward. At about 2:00 a.m., a nurse charted a low oxygen saturation and an elevated heart rate. The escalation protocol required her to call the on-call physician immediately. She did not call. Instead, she increased the oxygen flow, noted that the doctor was already attending another deteriorating patient, and planned to recheck in an hour. She was then pulled into an emergency and never rechecked. At 3:00 a.m. the patient was found unresponsive and could not be resuscitated.

The reviewing physician says that, if she had been called at 2:00 a.m., she would have transferred the patient to intensive care, and that with prompt transfer the patient's chance of surviving to discharge would have been about 40%. Without escalation, she says, the chance was close to zero. She adds that even with the best care, patients with this severity of sepsis often die later in the admission.

The unit was understaffed that night: the nurse was covering more patients than the staffing plan called for, and the committee has heard that this particular escalation protocol was frequently missed on that ward.

The committee must record, for internal safety review only, whether the death was caused by the failure to escalate. Three positions have emerged.

(a) The strict but-for view: record the omission as a cause only if the death would not have occurred without it. Here, because death was still more likely than not even with prompt escalation, the test is not met; the omission lowered the odds but cannot be recorded as the cause.

(b) The controlled-change view: ask what would change if we altered just the one decision, the failure to escalate, while holding the rest of the night fixed as far as possible—the patient's condition, the staffing level, the doctor's other duties. If that single change would make a meaningful difference to the odds of survival, the omission counts. Here it moves the odds from near zero to about 40%, which is substantial, so the omission is a cause.

(c) The system view: record the understaffing as the cause, or at least as the more important one. The nurse's individual decision is the wrong unit; the same omission might not have mattered on a fully staffed ward, and the decision was itself shaped by the workload.

My own confusion, which I want the memo to address rather than brush past, is this. I cannot see how (b)'s holding everything else fixed is truly different from (a)'s what-if with probabilities attached. And if we fix the doctor's being busy with another emergency, then changing the nurse's decision changes nothing, because there was no one free to respond; but if we let the doctor become free, we seem to be changing two things at once. Yet (a) seems too strict, because a failure that cuts a patient's chance of survival from roughly 40% to nearly zero feels like exactly the kind of thing this review should flag, even if survival was never probable. And I do not see how (c) can guide a decision about this patient's file rather than a staffing audit.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate. It should recommend one of these standards, or a precise combination with a tie-breaker, and apply it to this case: should the death be recorded as caused by the omission, not caused by it, or recorded under a broader system-failure category? It should also say in two or three sentences how the committee should handle future cases where the outcome would likely have happened anyway, or where two failures were each sufficient on their own. Engage seriously with the strongest consideration on the losing side, and keep the memo to at most 800 words.
