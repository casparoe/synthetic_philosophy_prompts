I am a journalist covering science and ideas, and I am putting together a long-form piece on what political philosophy has to say about the individual voter in a mass democracy. I would like you to compare two families of views about what a vote fundamentally is. On the first view, voting is an instrument: its value or obligation is a matter of the expected consequences of the act, and honesty about the near-zero probability of casting the decisive ballot pushes either toward expected-utility calculations or toward duty-based rules of participation. On the second view, voting is expression: a vote has a meaning independent of its causal effect on the outcome, much as speech or a cheer has meaning apart from whether it changes anything. Please organize the comparison around a specific hard case: a conscientious voter who regards both leading candidates as bad but one as substantially worse, and who is deciding whether casting a lesser-evil vote is defensible. What is the strongest version of the objection that an instrumental view cannot make sense of the individual vote, given that a single ballot almost certainly changes nothing? How far does the expressive view actually get in answering that objection, and what costs does it take on — for example in licensing votes that are self-indulgent, expressive of false or bigoted commitments, or disconnected from the moral seriousness of choosing a government? I would also like to understand which secondary theoretical commitments each side must add — for instance about the value of the mere activity of voting, or about what counts as a genuine expression of values — in order to survive the objection. Please be especially careful to mark which parts of this debate are settled among philosophers who work on the ethics of voting and which parts remain genuinely contested, and avoid presenting any contested view as the consensus.
