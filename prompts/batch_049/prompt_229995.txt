I chair the quarterly expert elicitation panel at a mid-sized engineering consultancy. Every quarter, six to ten senior engineers and cost modelers independently turn the same evidence package into probability distributions over three quantities for each major bid: capital cost overrun, schedule slip, and recordable safety incidents. The board's risk committee treats our consensus distributions as inputs to a go/no-go decision on bids worth up to nine figures, and I have to defend the process if the numbers later prove wrong. I have about one week between the evidence package going out and the consensus meeting. None of the panelists has formal training in decision theory, and there is no room to run a full Bayesian elicitation exercise from scratch each quarter. Our current method is for each panelist to present and defend their numbers orally, after which we take the median; this silently treats every panelist's numbers as equally credible and gives nobody a diagnosis of why people still disagree after reading the same package.

I want to replace this with a reusable disagreement-audit procedure built as honestly as possible on the common prior assumption as I understand it: the idea that agents who start from the same prior and condition on the same evidence should end with the same posterior, so any remaining divergence traces to evidence that was not actually pooled or to someone not having updated correctly. I know that this is a substantive assumption rather than a theorem about all rational agents everywhere, and that the formal result depends delicately on assumptions like common knowledge of posteriors, which a panel of humans probably never satisfies exactly.

Give me a procedure I can run each quarter. It should include a sequence of tests, in order, for a set of panel responses; a checklist of the distinct ways responses can diverge even under a common prior; and decision rules for what to do when a test fails, up to and including reporting that the process has not converged. Mark each step where the procedure is really a judgment call presented as a mechanical step, and specify which formal distinctions must survive the simplification (for example, common prior versus common knowledge of posteriors versus common evidence) and which can be dropped. Work the procedure through three concrete applications: one where it traces the disagreement to information that was never pooled; one where it traces the disagreement to a substantive difference in priors or in how evidence is weighted, and gives an answer I will not like; and one where it cannot distinguish those explanations and has to say so. End by stating what the procedure cannot do, in particular whether it can ever establish that the panel actually shares a prior rather than merely failing to find evidence that they do not.
