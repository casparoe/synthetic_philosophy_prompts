Next month I am giving a department colloquium defending the following thesis: influence counts as manipulative when, and only when, it works by reducing the target's deliberative access to the considerations that bear on her decision — by directing attention away from them, by framing them so they lose salience, or by exploiting a predictable failure to retrieve them. What matters is how the influence engages her capacity to act on reasons, not whether it is deceptive, covert, or irrational. From this I draw two conclusions the room will resist. First, some fully disclosed, welfare-improving choice-architecture interventions — a cafeteria that announces its shelf-placement strategy, a pension plan that discloses its auto-enrollment default — remain manipulative, because disclosure does not restore the considerations the intervention keeps out of the target's active weighing. Second, some covert or emotionally charged appeals are not manipulative, because they leave the target's grip on the relevant reasons intact. My argument has three steps: paired cases designed to pull disclosure apart from manipulation; a diagnosis of the underlying wrong as relating to the target as an obstacle to be steered rather than a reasoner to be engaged; and the claim that this account, unlike motive- or outcome-based accounts, explains why manipulation and paternalism can come apart.

The audience is a philosophy colloquium of roughly twenty people: mostly moral philosophers with strong Kantian sympathies, two empirically minded ethicists who work with behavioral-science findings and are friendly to nudges, one philosopher of action who works on attention and akrasia, and a senior consequentialist who habitually asks whether any proposed distinction earns its keep in downstream normative judgments. Give me the objections in roughly the order I should expect them from this room, phrased the way these people would phrase them, and for each objection state who is most likely to raise it, how likely and how damaging it is, what I should concede, what I should hold, a two-minute reply, and the specific step of my argument it puts under the most pressure. End with the question I should most hope nobody asks, and explain why it is dangerous.
