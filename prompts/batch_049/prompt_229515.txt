I run the grantmaking side of a mid-sized family foundation. A surprising share of our commitments never reach a signed grant agreement: a program officer tells an applicant on a site visit that "we'll fund this next cycle," someone sends a one-line email saying we are "on board," a board member tells an organization at a dinner that the foundation "will stand behind them." Later the project goes sideways, or our priorities shift, or the organization turns out to have problems, and I have to decide which of these soft commitments we are morally bound to honor. These cases come up roughly monthly, the sums are small to midsize but large for the recipients, we usually have only a day or two to decide, and I cannot pull in counsel or a philosopher for each one. The people who would use the procedure are program officers without legal or philosophical training.

From what I have read, philosophers treat promising as a distinctive kind of obligation and disagree about why it binds: some locate the wrong in breaking the act of assurance itself, some in the expectations the promisor knowingly invited, and some mainly in the reliance or harm that follows. I want that theory turned into a reusable decision procedure, not an essay. The procedure should be a checklist to run in order, with guidance on what counts as a sufficiently good answer to move to the next step. It should keep three questions strictly separate: (a) did we make a promise-like commitment at all, as opposed to a prediction, a statement of current intention, a hope, or fundraising politeness; (b) does that commitment bind us in the particular case; and (c) if it binds, may we still break or revise it, and what do we then owe the counterparty. It should also say clearly which steps are judgment calls that no checklist can eliminate, and where the procedure should stop and hand the decision to a person because it is a question of values rather than facts.

Then apply the procedure to three cases: a senior program officer's verbal "we'll fund you next cycle" to a first-time applicant she met on a site visit; a board member's unauthorized dinner-table assurance that the foundation "will stand behind" an organization, after which the organization signs a lease in reliance; and a conditional pledge — "if we launch the fellowships program, your group will be first in line" — where we decide not to launch the program but the group spent months preparing an application because of the pledge. Make sure at least one case yields an answer I will not like. Please begin with the procedure itself, with no preface or commentary, then run the cases through it, and close with what the procedure cannot decide for us.
