I run a small reading group on decision theory, and a colleague recently gave us an argument that has unsettled me more than anything we have read for a long time. We had been considering why anyone would take one box in Newcomb's problem, and he claimed that the standard evidential reasoning behind one-boxing commits you, by steps that are hard to refuse, to a conclusion about choice in large universes that seems plainly absurd. I cannot find the step at which it overreaches, and I would like to know which one to give up, and what giving it up costs.

The argument runs as follows. (1) One-boxing is rational because your choice is evidence about the predictor's earlier prediction: conditional on your one-boxing, the opaque box is probably full, and the fact that your act does not causally produce the prediction is beside the point. (2) If that is the right principle of rational choice, then an act's rationality can turn entirely on what it indicates about the choices of other agents, even when it cannot causally influence them. (3) Now consider a Twin Prisoner's Dilemma. You and a perfect psychological duplicate of yourself sit in causally isolated rooms, each choosing whether to cooperate with or defect from the other, with your payoff determined by both choices. Since you are duplicates running the same deliberation, your choices are perfectly correlated: conditional on your cooperating, your twin cooperates; conditional on your defecting, your twin defects. Cooperation therefore indicates that your twin cooperates, which is better for you, so by (2) you should cooperate. (4) Once common-cause correlations of this kind are admitted as decision-relevant, there can be no principled line between the perfect correlation of twins and the weaker correlations shared by any two sufficiently similar reasoners; if anything, the difference is merely one of degree. (5) In a sufficiently large universe, almost any act you consider will be correlated, however weakly, with the choices of a vast population of agents who share some of your history, your genes, your culture or your reasoning situation. The accumulated news-value of the act — what it would tell you about all these agents were you to perform it — will routinely swamp its ordinary causal consequences. (6) The principle that vindicates one-boxing therefore also implies that, in a large universe, the rational act is simply the act that would be the best news about the largest correlated population, however remote, unidentifiable and causally unreachable that population is. (7) That cannot be a correct account of individual rational choice: it turns private deliberation into an invisible plebiscite among unknown agents, and lets the mundane causal consequences of one's actions become almost irrelevant.

So either one-boxing is wrong, or one of the later premises is, or the conclusion really does follow. I am inclined to keep (1) and reject (7), but I cannot see how to do that without cutting the argument at (4) or (5), and I do not know whether the cut can be made without also losing the original one-boxing verdict. Where does the argument go wrong — if it does — and what exactly does rejecting that step commit one to?
