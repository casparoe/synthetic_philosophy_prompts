I teach a graduate seminar in the philosophy of mathematics, and next week we are working on the axiom of choice. The students have seen the standard material before: Zermelo's well-ordering theorem, Zorn's lemma, the Banach–Tarski theorem, the Vitali construction, and the usual textbook story that choice is indispensable across analysis, algebra, and topology and that its strange consequences are simply the price of that power. I want to move them past the cartoon version of the debate, and I have found that the quickest way to do that is to give them a genuinely even-handed dialogue to read aloud and then pick apart. My own attempts at writing it always end up with one side as a strawman, so I would like you to do it.

Write a dialogue of no more than 1,000 words between two equally competent philosophers of mathematics who disagree deeply about full choice. One should defend the axiom of choice as a legitimate principle of classical mathematics and as more than a mere convenience, arguing that the supposed paradoxes are either misdescribed or an acceptable cost; the other should reject full choice, or insist that it be replaced by weaker or definable forms, and should press on what it means to say that a choice function or a well-ordering exists when no one can specify it. They should respond to one another's actual points, make concessions when a point tells against them, and then move to the next difficulty. In the course of the exchange the two should engage with at least three specific cases or principles, drawn from the Vitali construction, the Banach–Tarski decomposition, countable choice, dependent choice, or the claim that many uses of choice in analysis can be recovered by definable choice on Borel or analytic sets. I am not looking for a resolution; I want the dialogue to display the divergent standards of existence, proof, and method that keep the disagreement alive. The register should be that of a serious but collegial argument in a seminar, and the technicalities should be compressed enough that a philosophy student without a full degree in mathematics can still follow.
