Exam question: Explain Pascal’s account of the relation between justice and force, and then discuss how his claim that custom is the whole of equity bears on that account. In particular, does Pascal end up reducing justice to whatever happens to be commanded by effective power, or does he preserve some independent normative standard?

Student answer: Pascal starts from the observation that people can never agree about what justice requires, whereas force is publicly recognisable and not subject to the same disputes. From this he infers that justice, left to itself, is too controversial to secure social order. It can be recognised by reason, but it cannot be enforced under the ordinary conditions of civil life. Force, by contrast, is an unambiguous social fact: we can see who can compel. So far this is straightforward. Pascal then argues that force without justice is tyrannical, because it imposes commands with no claim on conscience and is just violence. By the same token, justice without force is merely ineffective. Legitimate authority therefore has to unite the two: justice must be made effective through force, and force must be made just. For Pascal, the institution that does this is custom. Since bare reason provides no agreed practical criterion of justice, custom supplies the content of public justice, and it acquires its authority simply by being accepted. Force then backs up this socially recognised custom. That is why Pascal says custom is the whole of equity. Crucially, this is not a reduction of justice to power, because Pascal preserves a distinction between the just and the useful: the underlying moral law remains accessible to reason, and custom is only a remedy for our fallen inability to coordinate on that law, not the source of the law itself.
