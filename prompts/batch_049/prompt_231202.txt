For the past several years I have been following a dispute in the philosophy of history and the historical social sciences that has, by any honest accounting, stalled. The question is what causal work the small, incidental details of a historical moment can do. One side holds that history is shot through with genuine hinge points: outcomes of vast scale turning on causes strikingly small relative to their effects, such as a message that arrived late, an unexpected death, or a missed turn on a road. On this view, large-scale trajectories are often highly sensitive to small perturbations, and serious historiography should be built around counterfactuals that take such hinges seriously. The other side treats the hinge-point picture as a narrative artifact. It argues that small causes only operate within channels established by slowly moving structures, such as geography, demography, fiscal and military institutions, and transport and communication technology, so that the outcomes the first side calls contingent were in fact overdetermined or robust: remove the famous nail and another nail, or a different but functionally similar path, would have delivered roughly the same result.

What puzzles me is not that the two sides disagree, but that the disagreement has proven so immobile. They seem to accept the same archival record and the same settled facts about the episodes in dispute. They also do not, so far as I can see, differ about the metaphysics of causation in general. Each side's best arguments and examples have been on the table for decades, and restating them with new cases has not shifted anyone. My working hypothesis is that the real disagreement is methodological or conceptual rather than empirical: a difference about what a historical counterfactual must establish to count as evidence, about whether the impossibility of rerunning the past is a principled limit or a practical one, or about whether the two camps are simply using the word "contingent" for different things, such as token-level causal sensitivity versus the absence of a structural explanation. But I can also see a case that a hidden question is buried in it, namely whether large social systems have the right dynamical properties for small perturbations to cascade, and whether that question is even answerable with historical data.

I want a diagnosis of the structure of this impasse, not a verdict for one side. Specifically: is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one, a difference about standards of evidence, two groups answering different questions under one label, or is the question itself ill-posed? Support the diagnosis with what each side actually argues, and then say what would count as progress given the diagnosis, such as a distinction, a formal or computational result, a methodological innovation, or a better-posed question, or, if you conclude that no progress should be expected, argue for that conclusion.
