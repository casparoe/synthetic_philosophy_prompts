I've spent a fair amount of time reading LessWrong and the neighboring rationalist blogs, and one thing I've inherited from that corner of the internet is a dislike of using one word for two different jobs. Lately I've been noticing a distinction I don't have a clean label for, and I'd like you to help me pin it down precisely rather than just naming it.

Take the following cases.

An enormously powerful being credibly threatens to torture me unless I genuinely admire it. It seems plain that there is a consideration counting in favor of my admiring it, and even that, in some practical sense, I ought to bring it about that I admire it. But the threat does nothing to make the being admirable. If you ask whether the being is the sort of thing that is worth admiring, or whether admiration would be a correct response to it, the threat is beside the point.

Someone offers me a large sum of money to be afraid of a completely harmless spider. There is again something to be said in favor of being afraid of it: the money. It may be sensible to bring it about that I fear the spider. But the spider is not dangerous, and the fear is not, as a response to the spider, appropriate or correct.

I notice the same structure in less artificial cases too. A consideration can count in favor of being in some state—fearing, admiring, wanting, liking—entirely because of what happens as a result of being in that state, while being irrelevant to whether the state is an accurate or appropriate response to its object. The same ambiguity seems to show up when people talk about having a reason to believe something on grounds of comfort or usefulness rather than evidence.

I want to know whether this exact class of considerations has an established technical name, and I want the carving as much as the label. Where is the boundary between considerations of this kind and considerations that do bear on whether an attitude fits or is correct toward its object? Is the boundary the same across attitudes, or does belief require special treatment because it has its own standard of correctness that is independent of the attitude's usefulness? Are there cases that sit on the boundary, or plausible arguments that the two categories overlap more than the examples suggest? If there is an established name, say what the nearest neighbouring concepts are and how each fails to match what I have described; if there is no established name, propose one, define it, and specify its edges.

End your reply with a single machine-readable line of the form \answer{...} containing the established label if there is one, or your proposed name if there is not.
