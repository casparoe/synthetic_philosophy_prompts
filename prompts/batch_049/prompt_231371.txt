In preparing a graduate unit on the nonidentity problem, I presented the following argument in seminar. It seemed to prove too much and none of us could immediately see where it failed, so before I teach the material again I want a precise diagnosis.

(1) A person is wronged by an act or policy only if that act or policy makes the person worse off than they would have been in its absence.

(2) A person is not made worse off by an act or policy that is a necessary causal condition of that person's ever having existed, provided the life that person lives is worth living. The only alternative is never existing, and a life worth living is not worse for the person who lives it than never having existed.

(3) For every person now alive who is descended from people enslaved in the transatlantic slave trade, the slave trade is a necessary causal condition of that person's existence. Had it not occurred, the specific chain of migrations, meetings, unions, and conceptions that produced that particular person would not have occurred, and that person would never have existed.

(4) Therefore, by (2), the slave trade made no present descendant of those it enslaved worse off than that descendant would otherwise have been.

(5) Therefore, by (1), the slave trade wronged no present descendant of those it enslaved.

(6) A person now alive has a claim of justice grounded in an act only if that act wronged them.

(7) Therefore, no present descendant of those enslaved has a claim of justice grounded in the slave trade.

(8) By exactly parallel reasoning, no future person is wronged by any present policy that is a necessary causal condition of that person's existence. Large-scale choices—a high-emissions energy policy, delayed decarbonization, the depletion of a finite resource base—alter who meets whom, who stays, and who is born; under a different policy, different people would eventually be conceived. So long as the resulting lives remain worth living, none of those future people is made worse off by our present choice, and by (1) none is wronged by it.

(9) These conclusions cannot be right. It cannot be that present descendants of enslaved people have no claim of justice grounded in what was done to their ancestors; and it cannot be that a policy knowingly bequeathing future people lives that are only barely worth living—lives shaped by avoidable climate damage—wrongs no one.

So either (1), (2), or (6) is false, or at least one inference in the argument is invalid. Where does the argument go wrong? If a premise is false, identify it and explain what rejecting it commits us to. If a step is invalid, locate the precise step and state the distinction it overlooks. I would prefer to keep the judgments in (9), but I do not want to pay for them by quietly surrendering every person-affecting constraint on moral evaluation; tell me what the least costly revision is and what it commits me to.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning; no preamble and no restating of the question.
