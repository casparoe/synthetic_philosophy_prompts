Longtime rationalist-blog reader here, kicking around something from the cross-cultural philosophy-of-mind debates and hoping someone can put a name on it, or tell me there isn't one.

The pattern: someone claims a philosophical intuition is universal — not a theorem, just one of those "everyone finds X" claims. Then a study shows that when you translate the standard vignette into another language and run it on, say, undergraduates somewhere else, their answers split differently. And the person who wanted universality doesn't update; instead they say the translation didn't preserve the relevant nuance, or the participants weren't answering the question as a question about the concept rather than as a practical matter, or they lacked the training to use the terms in the stipulative way. So the claim starts out as "the folk concept is universal" and ends up as "the concept is universal among competent speakers," where competent speaker is defined after the fact by whatever group gives the original answer.

Concretely: in the classic justification-by-luck case — a man has very good evidence for a belief, the belief is true, but only because of a lucky coincidence — people in some non-Western samples are apparently much more likely to say he "doesn't know" than American students are. The universalist's first move is usually that the translated word for "justified" carries a stronger or weaker connotation. Fair enough, sometimes. But it would definetly be suspicious even if no translation problem could be found, wouldn't it? The same move shows up with the name-thief case: a man steals a discovery and publishes it under his own name, and when you ask who the name refers to, speakers of some languages disagree with the standard Western answer. The reply is that the question doesn't track "reference" in the techical sense, or that respondents are answering about credit, not naming.

Is there an established term for this exact move — the post-hoc restriction of the population whose intuitions count, used to protect a universality claim from cross-cultural evidence? It's adjacent to No True Scotsman and a bit to moving the goalposts, but the tricky part is that the restriction is sometimes legitimate: some translations really are bad, and some survey questions really do confound what's being tested. So I'm not just after a label; I want the carve. Under what conditions is this a legitimate competence objection, and under what conditions is it just immunizing the claim against evidence? If there's no standard term, give me one with a definition and boundary cases.
