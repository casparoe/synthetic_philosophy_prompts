I spent this evening arguing with a friend about the wrong kind of reasons problem, and I came away unsure whether the package of views I defended actually hangs together. I have written the package down below. Before I defend it again, I want an audit.

Here are the commitments I seem to hold.

1. I hold a buck-passing account of value. What it is for a thing to be valuable in some attitude-relevant way — admirable, desirable, enviable — is for it to have features that provide sufficient reason of the right kind for the corresponding attitude. The order of explanation runs from reasons to value, not from value to reasons.

2. I take the demon case at face value. If a powerful demon credibly threatens to harm me unless I admire it, I have a genuine reason to admire the demon, and this is a reason for admiration itself, not merely a reason to bring it about that I admire the demon. Yet the demon does not thereby become admirable. So wrong-kind reasons for attitudes are real.

3. I accept that the right-kind/wrong-kind distinction tracks the object-given/state-given distinction. Right-kind reasons are supplied by features of the object of the attitude — what it is like, what it did. Wrong-kind reasons are supplied by features of having the attitude, paradigmatically the consequences or costs of being in that state.

4. However, I do not think the object-given/state-given distinction can be drawn with enough independence to carry the explanation, because where one draws the line between a feature of the object and a feature of the state seems to shift with exactly the evaluative facts the distinction is meant to deliver. I therefore regard it as an extensional marker at best, not as a solution.

5. I hold that the underlying explanation must appeal to a primitive standard of fittingness for each attitude type. Admiration, for instance, has a standard of correctness: an object is admirable just when admiring it is fitting. A right-kind reason for an attitude is a fact in virtue of which the attitude meets that standard. I regard this as still a buck-passing view, because value is explained via the normative status of attitudes and the reasons that track it, rather than via a free-standing realm of value properties.

6. I think my view handles the doxastic case without strain. Evidence is a right-kind reason for belief because belief's standard is truth, and evidence bears on whether the believed proposition is true. A Pascalian incentive to believe is a wrong-kind reason because it bears on the benefits of believing rather than on the truth of what is believed.

Now: which pairs of these are in genuine tension, and which only appear to conflict? Rank the genuine tensions by severity, and say what criterion of severity you are using. For each genuine tension, specify the least costly revision — which commitment would have to be weakened or given up — and what philosophical price that revision incurs. Please keep the audit under 900 words.
