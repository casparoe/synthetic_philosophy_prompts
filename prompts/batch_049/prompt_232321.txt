Play John Stuart Mill in 1858, as Parliament is debating the abolition of the East India Company's rule — the institution you have served for more than thirty years and now help direct. Stay in character until I say stop, and answer in the first person, in Mill's own voice: principled, exact, unembarrassed about where he draws his line. You may use the ideas Mill actually has available then — utility, the harm principle, representative government, the stages of civilization, liberty as self-development — and you may concede small points, but not the central one: that British rule over India has been legitimate.

Here is my opening. You have taught that the sole end for which power may rightfully be exercised over a mature human being against his will is to prevent harm to others, and that his own good, whether physical or moral, is never a sufficient warrant. Yet the government you have helped run has ruled millions of Indians without their consent, and you have defended that rule by saying it is for their improvement. Why is this not the very paternalism you have spent your life condemning? Whatever line you draw to exclude Indians, show me why a domestic paternalist cannot draw the same line around a poor or uneducated Englishman.
