You are grading a midterm essay in a course on moral philosophy. Below are the question that was set and one student's answer, reproduced verbatim.

EXAM QUESTION

Suppose a speaker can bring a hearer to believe a false proposition in either of two ways: by lying — asserting something the speaker believes to be false, intending the hearer to accept it as true — or by merely misleading — saying only what the speaker believes to be true, but in a way designed to induce that same false belief in the hearer. Holding fixed the false belief produced, the speaker's deceptive intention, and the downstream consequences, is there a morally significant difference between the two courses of action? If you think there is, what explains it and how much does it matter? If you think there is not, say why the ordinary intuition that lying is worse should be abandoned. Your answer should discuss (i) a precise statement of the distinction, (ii) the strongest argument for a moral asymmetry, (iii) the strongest objection to that argument, and (iv) your considered verdict.

STUDENT ANSWER

By 'lying' I mean asserting what one believes to be false with the intention that the hearer take it for the truth. By 'misleading' I mean deliberately bringing it about that the hearer has a false belief without saying anything I believe to be false, typically by letting the hearer draw an invited but unwarranted conclusion from a true statement. The interesting comparison is the fully matched pair: the same false belief in the hearer, the same deceptive aim, and the same consequences, with the only difference being whether the falsehood is what the speaker said or what the hearer was led to infer.

The strongest argument for a real asymmetry starts from what assertion is. To assert p is to vouch for p, to hand the hearer a premise under one's own epistemic warranty. A liar accepts that reliance and betrays it. The misleader does not vouch for the falsehood. Her sentence is true, and the false conclusion is drawn by the hearer. If the hearer draws an irresponsible conclusion from true remarks, that is in some sense the hearer's own doing. The liar makes the hearer's mind the instrument of a false assertion; the misleader merely leaves an inference available. So the liar wrongs the hearer in an additional way: she breaches the specific assurance that assertion itself creates, while the misleader has not broken that assurance.

The strongest objection is that this difference cannot carry moral weight, because everything that standardly makes deception wrong has been controlled for. Consider two replies to the question 'Did you ever commit academic fraud?'. Reply A: 'No, I never committed academic fraud,' where the speaker knows this is false. Reply B: 'I have never been found responsible for academic fraud,' which is true, though the speaker knows the questioner will hear it as a denial and knows that her own fraud went undiscovered. The hearer ends up with the same false belief, produced by a speaker with the same deceptive intention and facing the same foreseeable consequences. If what makes deception wrong is the harm to the hearer and the dishonest motive, then the two acts are morally equivalent, and any remaining difference between saying the falsehood and suggesting it is only a difference in wording. A theory that treats wording as intrinsically significant is not tracking the morally relevant features; it is fetishizing the sentence.

I think the objection is too quick, but only slightly. What it really shows is that the asymmetry cannot be explained by consequences or motives alone; it does not show that it cannot be explained by the structure of the act. A lie breaks an assurance that is constituted by assertoric speech; misleading does not break that assurance, because it offers no false assurance. Since this is a real structural difference, some residue of the asymmetry survives. My considered verdict is therefore that the ordinary intuition is partly vindicated: serious misleading can be as bad as lying, but lying adds a distinct wrong-making feature — the abuse of assertion — and for that reason lying is, all else equal, the worse act.
