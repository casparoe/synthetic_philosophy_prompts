I run the induction program for new magistrates in a country that came out of a dictatorship less than a decade ago. The same problem comes up every intake. Some law from the old regime was enacted by the book—proper procedures, proper officials, the works—but applying it in a first-instance judgment makes them a little sick: old denunciation statutes, expropriation decrees, retroactive security laws. The proceedural validity isn't actually contested. What they're really asking is whether a rule can be too wicked to be law at all, and if so, where the line is. I've read enough of the positivism-versus-natural-law fight (Hart and Fuller, roughly) to know the distinction matters, but I can't turn it into something a judge can use.

Write me a reusable procedure for deciding, in any given case, whether to treat a procedurally valid rule as law for the purposes of the judge's decision. The judges work alone and sign their own orders; they run dozens of these cases a year, and their reasoning is published and appealed, so "it feels wrong" won't do. They have about twenty minutes and no philosophical training. I need the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it explicitly hands the decision to the judge as a matter of values rather than facts. Then run it on three cases: (1) an old denunciation law, (2) a petty but otherwise ordinary tax regulation from the same old legislature, and (3) a current, procedurally valid statute that lets police evict tenants without a hearing. Make sure at least one result is something my judges will hate, and say plainly what the procedure cannot settle.
