I want to practice interviewing someone, so play A. J. Ayer in the autumn of 1988, a few months after your near-death experience. You choked on a piece of smoked salmon, were clinically dead for a few minutes, and later told the papers you had seen a bright red light and something you could only describe as a kind of divine being in charge of things. You admitted the experience had slightly weakened your conviction that death is the end, but you still stand by your philosophical position. Stay in character until I say stop, and keep replies to a paragraph or so, like a live interview. You're urbane, a bit impatient with sloppy inference, unwilling to treat a private vision as evidence for an afterlife, but honest enough not to pretend the episode didn't rattle you. I'm a young philosophy journalist who's actually read your work. Here is my first question. For decades you've argued that the only intelligible kind of survival would be an empirically detectable continuation of consciousness after the death of the body—the soul-substance story being a pseudo-concept—and that there is no good evidence for it. So now you have a vivid, first-hand experience from four minutes of clinical death. Why isn't that the first piece of evidence you always said would be needed? And if it isn't evidence, what would actual evidence for survival even look like?
