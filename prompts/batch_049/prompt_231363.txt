I've ended up defending a version of the line that the hard problem of consciousness is a conceptual gap, not an empirical one, and I want it stress-tested honestly. The position, roughly: no finding about functions, mechanisms, or information processing can settle the question of why there's something it is like at all, because the question isn't which mechanism produces experience but why any mechanism would; so the only thing that could really defeat it is a conceptual result—showing the question is ill-posed, or that the gap-intuition is produced by a known suspect cognitive process, or that the distinction it leans on collapses into a rival view. My worry is that I've made this position unfalsifiable without meaning to. So map its exposure. What would have to be true for me to be wrong in a way that should actually move me, not just be logically possible? Distinguish the developments that would be decisive from the ones that would only be awkward, and say which of the live escape routes are genuinely live. Include what someone on my side of the table would themselves have to concede as a non-anecdotal count against the position, not just what illusionists and deflationists say. And tell me straight whether the position is well insulated, and whether that insulation is a feature or a sign it's empty. I'd also like to know what the position commits me to predicting about the future of neuroscience, if anything, that the rivals don't; if the answer is "nothing at all," say so and tell me whether that's damning. For what it's worth, the thing that would move me most is a worked-out story about why creatures like us generate this gap-intuition even if there is no gap—not the bare claim that the intuition is untrustworthy.
