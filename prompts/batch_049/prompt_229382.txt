I write about science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm working on a piece about what makes consent valid — across sex, medicine, data, the whole mess. I've been writing down my gut intutions on made-up cases, and now I want to know whether one principle gets them all, or close, and which of my verdicts has to go. I don't need references or the literature; I need the reasoning.

Here are the calls. Someone says "sleep with me or I send these photos to your family": not valid. Someone says "sleep with me or I'm leaving you": valid, but it feels rancid. A broke neighbor agrees to sex for $300 that covers their rent: I keep calling that valid, and hating myself a little. Someone takes off the condom mid-sex after the other person consented to sex with a condom: not valid. A woman says she's on the pill and isn't: not valid. A guy says "I love you" just to get sex, with no intention of seeing her again: this is the one I flip on — it's clearly wrong, but I can't decide if it's a consent problem or just ordinary jerk behavior. Then there's the pair that really bugs me. A person in labor, clutching the bed and in serious pain, says yes to an epidural: valid. That same person, at that same moment, says yes to enrolling in a study comparing pain drugs: I don't trust it. Pain seems to wreck one consent and not the other. On data: an app tucks "we will sell your health data" into a 47-page terms-of-service with a pre-ticked box: not valid. Same app, different flow, a one-line popup that says "we sell your location data to ad companies, continue?" and the person clicks yes: valid, even if impulsive. Tipsy but chatty and making jokes: valid. Slurring and can't stand up: not valid. An adult with a mild cognitive disability who can restate the surgery and its main risk in her own words: valid.

My working rule has been: consent is valid iff the person could understand the specific thing and its main risks, actually knew the material facts, and wasn't threatened. Test that rule against the list. I can already see trouble — the buried clause and the popup differ only in presentation, not in what a careful reader could find out, and the "I love you" lie seems to fall out invalid on the material-facts clause even though I'm not sure that's what I think. Also: what makes the pill lie different from the love lie? Please give me a precise principle (or a small set of candidates), tell me which of my verdicts it can't accommodate, say what it predicts for a case I didn't list, and use it to settle the I-love-you case if you can.
