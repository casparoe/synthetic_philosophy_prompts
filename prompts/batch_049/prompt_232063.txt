I'm putting together a little Q&A for a Meetup/seminar thing at work — data people mostly, but we're doing a mini "philosophy of social science" series, mostly for fun. Next session I'm talking to a philosopher who defends explanatory holism in social science. His whole thing is that good social-science explanations sometimes have to appeal to group-level stuff (institutions, networks, norms, class structure, whatever) and that you can't always translate those explanations into a story about individual beliefs, preferences, and choices without losing something important. I think he calls it non-reductive holism or something close.

I don't want the usual podcast fluff. I want questions that actually put pressure on his position, the way people in the field do. I know he gets the obvious objection — "but only individuals have minds, so how can a group-level fact do anything?" — and he's got a canned reply about how that confuses causal relevance with causal machinery, or something like that. I'd like the sequence to make that move harder for him to reuse, so each question should block a different escape route.

Can you draft maybe eight to ten questions, with the sequence getting progressively harder? In particular I'd like at least one question each on:

- the microfoundations demand (why shouldn't a macro explanation be penalized just because it skips the individual-level mechanism?),
- multiple realizability (if the same group-level property is realized by lots of different individual-level configurations, why is the group-level description doing the explaining rather than just labeling?),
- supervenience and causal redundancy (if all the social facts are fully fixed by facts about individuals, what causal work is left for the social facts to do?),
- and how social norms or roles get "into" individuals in a non-circular way (it seems like you need the group to explain the individual, but then you need individuals to explain the group).

For each question, also write the follow-up you'd ask if he gives the answer you'd expect him to give. Like, if he says group-level explanations are fine because they're more general, ask him why that generality isn't just a feature of our classification scheme rather than a feature of the world. I want the last question in the sequence to land on what would actually make him change his mind or revise the view — not evidence in a vaccuum, but something an opponent could in principle go out and show.

Also I'm coming at this as an engineer, so if any of the questions hinge on a distinction a smart non-philosopher might not know, can you put a one-line explanation in brackets right before that question?
