The Common Infrastructure Compact is a federation of roughly forty small, member-governed online services — cooperative social media, code hosting, mail — and for the past year I have been the only software engineer on its Supervisory Council; the rest are lawyers, historians, and administrators, and I usually get called in when a service goes down rather than when someone needs a theory of federation. I have no philosophical training, so the drafting history comes to me secondhand: a colleague tells me the compact's founders adapted two of its provisions from Kant's essay on perpetual peace. One is a non-interference provision: no member may alter the internal constitution or government of another by force, service disruption, defederation, or any other coercive means. The other is a governance precondition: membership presupposes that each member maintains a republican internal constitution, which our founding documents gloss as the people who use and operate the service having regular, effective means of removing the people who govern it. For years the two clauses were never tested against each other. Now they are.

The case: one mid-sized member, which I will call Arcadia, spent eighteen months under coordinated attacks on its infrastructure, and in response its elected user council delegated its powers to an unelected stability committee under a state-of-emergency exception. Last month that committee announced the arrangement is permanent and that it will renew its own mandate going forward. One bloc on the council reads the non-interference clause as absolute: whatever we think of Arcadia's internal change, coercing it back toward elections would be precisely the interference the compact forbids. The other bloc reads the governance precondition as non-negotiable: a service that stops being participatory has in substance left the compact, and a federation of participatory services is entitled to suspend cooperation, withdraw services, or expel a member that abandoned the condition on which it was admitted; otherwise the clause is decorative. They add that the emergency exception is now self-renewing, extended by the very committee it was meant to make temporary.

I have the task of submitting a decision memorandum ahead of the council's next sitting, and I need a short opinion of the kind fifteen busy non-philosophers can act on, not an essay. It should commit to a concrete course of action with a time frame — a conditioned suspension, a deadline for restoring elected governance, a fact-finding pause, or whatever is most defensible — and it should spend a paragraph on the strongest consideration that cuts against the recommendation rather than reporting both sides and abstaining. Put the bottom line first.
