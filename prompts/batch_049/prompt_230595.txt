I sit on my university's academic affairs committee, and we have to decide whether to scrap a rule that's been on the books since 1931: every graduating senior has to pass an oral exam on "the general field of the major," given by two faculty from outside the student's department. The exam has no rubric left, it's applied so inconsistently that the same student could pass or fail depending on who draws the short straw, and departments are furious about the weeks of faculty time it eats every spring. Most of the committee thinks it's dead weight. But a minority keeps insisting this is exactly a Chesterton's fence situation: if we can't say why the rule was put there, we have no business tearing it down, because it might be doing quiet work we've stopped noticing (cross-department accountability, making students synthesize under pressure, whatever). Draft the short decision memo I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend one concrete course of action — keep it, abolish it, or amend it with conditions and a deadline — and it should honestly engage the strongest argument on the loosing side rather than just restating the procedural history.
