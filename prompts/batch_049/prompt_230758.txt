I'm in a philosophy of psychiatry seminar and we keep going in circles about whether diagnoses like ADHD or borderline personality disorder are 'real.' The constructivists in the room say the categories are just social artifacts; the psychiatrists say they track real divisions in nature, or at least useful ones. Ive started to think the problem is that nobody has laid out what an adequate theory of psychiatric classification would even have to do before they argue about which theory is true.

So: write me the desiderata. What does a theory of psychiatric categories have to get right to count as adequate — which cases it has to handle (chronic vs episodic conditions, culturally variable presentations, fuzzy boundary cases, one person fitting many diagnoses at once), which distinctions it cannot collapse (like the difference between a category's being useful and its being real, or between explaining a condition and validating it), and which empirical findings it cannot contradict? For each requirement, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, and mark which ones are non-negotiable and which are just one camp's preference. One thing I especially want worked out: the looping idea (what some call a looping effect), where putting people into a category changes how they think and act, which then changes what the category seems to pick out. Does an adequate theory have to model that dynamic, or could a perfectly good theory treat the categories as fixed and still be fine? The point of the list is to arbitrate between people who want to tear down the diagnostic manuals and people who think the categories just need tweaking. And if you think two requirements cant be satisfied together, say which pair and why. Then briefly — no reading list, no citations — how the main families of views (natural-kind realism, constructivism, pragmatic/instrumentalist accounts) fare against the list, and tell me which single requirement does the most work in knocking out the usual candidates.
