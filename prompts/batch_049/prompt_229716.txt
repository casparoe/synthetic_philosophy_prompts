For a combined literature and bioethics assignment, I am writing an essay on Brave New World. My teacher introduced the hedonistic imperative as a serious philosophical position: the view that we should use biotechnology not merely to treat pain but to phase out suffering itself, altering the biological systems that produce felt distress so that severe suffering no longer occurs, eventually in nonhuman animals as well as humans. The class has mostly read the novel as a warning against engineered happiness, but I suspect that reading is too quick. The World State does not actually abolish suffering; it still contains a great deal of misery, from the deprivation of the lower castes to the Savage Reservation, the dying Linda, and John's torment at the end. What the state does is manage suffering through conditioning, distraction, soma, rigid social hierarchy, and exile. So the novel may be attacking one particular means of pursuing a goal like the hedonistic imperative rather than the goal itself. I would like a reading of the novel through this lens that is faithful to both the text and the philosophical position. Where does Huxley give genuine reasons to think that abolishing suffering, even by humane means, would be a loss because certain goods depend on vulnerability, struggle, or grief? Where does the novel instead give us reasons only to mistrust a specific policy, such as the shallowing of love, the replacement of grief with a drug, or the use of pleasure as social control? And where does it simply assume, without argument, that a world without suffering would have to be a world of cheap satisfactions? If the novel cannot actually refute the strongest version of the hedonistic imperative, I want the reading to say so plainly. Please anchor the argument in specific episodes where the distinction between the goal and the means does real work.
