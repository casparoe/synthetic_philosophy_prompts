Consider the following case, which is meant to sharpen a live dispute about the extended mind thesis. Amara has a severe autobiographical memory impairment. For years she has relied on a paper notebook in which she records appointments, decisions, and events, consulting it so fluently that retrieval feels to her almost like ordinary recall. She has recently switched to a wearable camera that automatically captures images of her surroundings every thirty seconds and uploads them to a searchable archive. A face- and scene-recognition system tags the images; when Amara wants to know what happened on a given afternoon, she queries the archive, and the system often surfaces events she had not thought to search for. Most of the storage, indexing, and salience-ranking is done by the device rather than by Amara herself.

Write an essay on whether the extended mind thesis—roughly, the view that a cognitive process or mental state can be partly constituted by equipment outside the head when that equipment is reliably coupled to the agent and plays a role that would count as cognitive if it were performed inside the skull—commits us to treating Amara's camera-archive as literally part of her memory. Engage seriously with both sides. On one side, press the objection that the device does too much of the work autonomously to count as Amara's cognition, and that treating every useful external store as part of the mind invites a "cognitive bloat" that drains the thesis of explanatory value. On the other side, press the worry that any principled line between the notebook and the camera may be impossible to draw without an unjustified bias toward what happens inside the skull. Do not end non-committally: defend a specific verdict, and state as precisely as you can the criterion that yields it.
