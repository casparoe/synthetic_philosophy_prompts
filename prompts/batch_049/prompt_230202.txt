I have been working through population ethics and keep encountering the same pattern: a principle is introduced, it handles two or three of the canonical cases, and then an opponent shows that it implies something most people find unacceptable—the Repugnant Conclusion (that a sufficiently enormous population of lives barely worth living would be better than a smaller population of excellent lives), the claim that adding lives of negative welfare could count as an improvement, or some other verdict that overturns a considered judgment about who is made better or worse. Before I read any more proposals, I want the full specification that an adequate theory of population axiology would have to meet. A theory in this area ranks states of affairs as better or worse when both the number of people who exist and their levels of well-being can differ.

Write it as a list of desiderata, with the motivating case or consideration behind each. Cover at least the following: the fixed-population cases any adequate theory must get right; the non-identity cases in which different policies bring about different people; the distinctions it cannot collapse (for example, between making a state better for the particular people who exist in it and making a state better by bringing new worthwhile lives into existence); the implications it must avoid, with a judgment as to which are genuine adequacy conditions and which are merely disliked implications we should probably learn to accept; and the formal constraints, such as transitivity, that the better-than relation should satisfy. For each item, say whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp.

For context, my purpose is to adjudicate between rival axiological views—total welfare, average welfare, person-affecting accounts that restrict value to what is better or worse for particular people, critical-level views that set a minimum welfare threshold below which adding a life does not improve an outcome, and views that make sufficiently good lives lexically prior to merely worth-living ones—not to derive immediate policy, so do not treat this as a list of policy constraints. The part I most want to see is where the list of requirements itself becomes the result. I suspect that some highly attractive desiderata cannot be jointly satisfied; in particular, avoiding the Repugnant Conclusion while holding on to mere addition—the principle that adding lives worth living without affecting existing people does not make an outcome worse—may conflict with transitivity and with some plausible egalitarian or dominance constraint. Flag any such incompatibility explicitly, state which requirement should be the first to be relaxed and why, and identify which requirement, if kept, does the most to eliminate otherwise serious theories. Then, briefly, indicate which families of views your list would count as adequate, but keep the weight on the criteria themselves rather than on a scorecard.
