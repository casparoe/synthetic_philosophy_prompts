I'm the science editor for a general-interest weekly's health-and-social-science section, and a longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums. I'm not looking for a primer: I already accept the points that heritability is a sample- and environment-specific partition of variance; that it is not a fixed property of a trait, not a statement about any individual, and not a measure of immutability; and that within-group heritability licenses no inference about between-group differences. What I need is to turn those points into a reusable editorial procedure.

Every week my desk has to decide, from press releases and abstracts, whether to run stories on new human-genetics findings — twin studies, SNP-based estimates, family studies, polygenic-score results. A decision takes about fifteen minutes, I have final sign-off, and the stakes are real because our readership is general and a bad headline survives any correction. The decision is: run, run with heavy caveats, or kill; and if we run, exactly how the headline and the opening paragraphs may phrase the finding, with the one or two non-negotiable caveats.

Please write me a fact-checking checklist, in the order the steps should be applied, that a literate but non-specialist colleague could follow. For each step, state the concrete error it catches and the shortest defensible wording for copy. Tell me which distinctions from quantitative genetics must survive the translation and which can safely be dropped for a lay readership. Flag openly which steps require numbers I often won't have and what to do then, and which steps are judgement calls dressed up as procedure.

Then apply the checklist to three cases:
1. A large twin study reports that divorce is 40 per cent heritable, and the writer wants to headline it with a formulation about divorce being 'in your genes'.
2. A SNP-based (GCTA) study reports that years of education is 12 per cent heritable, while older twin estimates put it around 40 per cent; the writer wants to say the difference means 'the environment is 88 per cent of the story'.
3. A study reports a high heritability for body weight within one affluent country, and the editorial team wants to use it to argue that population-level public-health interventions against obesity can have only limited effects.

For each case, say what the procedure should conclude, which step does the work, and what the final published wording would be. Make sure at least one of your applications concludes that I should kill a story the desk wants to run, and say why. Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot guarantee even when a study passes every step.

I want a committed answer: choose a definite order, definite editorial rules, and definite wording rather than laying out options and leaving me to select. Write the answer in British English.
