I've been trying to get clear on what coherence in my credences and preferences actually amounts to, and I keep coming back to Frank Ramsey's argument in "Truth and Probability." As I remember it, he says that if your degrees of belief violate the probability rules, a bookie can make a book against you—a set of bets you'll accept each on its own but that lose no matter what happens. The upshot I take is that being coherent just is not being susceptible to that kind of sure loss. But I'm not sure that principle gets my gut verdicts on concrete cases.

Here are my verdicts. (1) I'm 70% confident it'll rain tomorrow and 30% confident it won't: fine. (2) I'm 60% confident it'll rain and 60% confident it won't: not fine. (3) I'd pay $6 for a ticket that pays $10 if a fair coin lands heads: fine. (4) I'd pay $6 for a ticket that pays $10 if the same coin lands tails: fine. (5) I'd pay $12 for a ticket that pays $10 no matter what: fine. (6) But if I'm offered both tickets from (3) and (4) at those prices, I can see I'd be paying $12 for exactly the same thing as the guarenteed ticket in (5), which I said was worth $12. That feels off in a way I can't articulate. (7) I'd rather go to the Ethiopian place than the Thai place, and the Thai place than the Mexican place, but I'd rather go to the Mexican place than the Ethiopian place: not fine. (8) I think a fair coin is 50% likely to land heads, but if you ask me to bet at even odds on heads, I'd need better odds because I hate losing: fine.

Is there one principle that captures all of those, or close? If Ramsey's sure-loss principle is the right test, which of my verdicts does it overturn and why? And what does the principle say about the case I keep going back and forth on—being 60% confident it'll rain and 40% confident it won't, while declining to bet on rain at any odds because the stakes are high and I'm risk-averse? Please answer as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
