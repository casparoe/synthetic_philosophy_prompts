I co-run a long-standing reading group that includes condensed-matter physicists, philosophers of biology, and a few neuroscientists, and one debate has been running for years with almost no movement. The question is whether the higher-level sciences — thermodynamics, cell biology, cognitive psychology — are in principle reducible to physics. The reductionist side holds that special-science laws are compressed descriptions that a sufficiently patient derivation, microphysical laws plus bridge statements linking macroscopic quantities to microstates, could in principle recover; on this view, high-level explanation is a concession to human cognitive and computational limitations. The emergentist side concedes that every system is composed of microphysical degrees of freedom, that nothing violates the underlying dynamics, and that there is no special vital or mental stuff. But it insists that many explanatorily central features — a phase transition's universality class, the error-correcting properties of the genetic code, the content of a representational state — are either invisible or arbitrary at the microphysical level: the lower-level description lacks the resources to express the relevant kind, or it delivers a derivation so sensitive to initial conditions and boundary details that it stops being an explanation while remaining a calculation. Everyone agrees on the equations, the data, and the rejection of dualism and vitalism; the same examples get recycled each semester and nobody moves.

I am not asking you to adjudicate which side is right, only to diagnose the disagreement itself. My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not really about the world but about what an explanation has to do: reductionists treat explanation as subsumption under the most complete dynamical description available, while emergentists treat it as the identification of stable, coarse-grained dependency relations that remain invariant across wildly different microphysical realizers. But I suspect that diagnosis may be too neat. Could the stalemate instead be a hidden semantic disagreement about words like "level," "cause," or "nothing over and above"? Could it be a factual disagreement about what can actually be derived, in practice or in principle, from current physics — for example, about whether thermodynamic singularities genuinely survive the infinite-volume limit or are artifacts of idealization? Or are the two sides answering different questions under one heading, one about the structure of the world and one about the pragmatics of scientific understanding? Tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is and, given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a formal result about inter-level derivability, a well-chosen worked example, a distinction that dissolves the apparent conflict, a change of question — or an honest argument that no progress should be expected and why.
