Two of my roommates, Priya and Ben, got into it the other night about a video game, and the argument felt like it was about something bigger than the game. Ben had bought this notoriously grisly survival-horror game, and the only live options were: nobody plays it that weekend, Priya plays it, or Ben plays it. They've actually written their preference rankings on the fridge. Priya's is: nobody plays it > she plays it > Ben plays it. Her reasoning: she'd rather nobody touch it; but if someone has to play it, she'd rather it be her, because she thinks Ben will spiral and stop sleeping. Ben's is: Priya plays it > he plays it > nobody plays it. His reasoning: he thinks Priya is sheltered and would genuinely get something out of it, and he'd rather see her do that than play it himself, but he'd definitely rather play than have nobody play.

Their house rule has always been that each person gets a veto over whether they themselves play a given game. So Priya opened with: "I'm not playing it, and you're free to play it. That settles it."

Ben said: "Wait. Both of us would honestly rather have you play it than me. You just said so, in so many words. So the outcome 'Ben plays' is one we both rank below 'Priya plays.' How can that be a good outcome, let alone the one that respects our choices?"

Priya said: "Because my first choice is that nobody plays. The fact that I'd rather it be me than you if someone has to play doesn't mean I'm agreeing to play. I get final say over what I do with my own time."

Ben said: "I'm not saying you agree. I'm saying a decision rule that picks an outcome we both rank below another available one is broken. If everyone involved prefers X to Y, you shouldn't pick Y. That's the least you can ask."

Priya said: "If 'everyone prefers it' could override my no, then any time a group of people all agreed I'd be better off doing something, I could be made to do it. That's a terrible principle."

Ben said: "But nobody's overriding you by force. We're just noticing that your principle and mine, applied together, leave us with me playing — which we both think is worse than you playing. That's a reductio of your rule, not a defense of it."

Priya said: "Why is it a reductio of my rule and not of the principle that unanimous preference trumps an individual's veto? Maybe the lesson is that 'we both prefer it' doesn't give us a claim over you, even when it's true."

Ben said: "Then we're stuck with a procedure that systematically picks outcomes everyone prefers less. I don't see how you call that rationality; I call it a stalemate with extra steps."

They went around like this for another hour, and I honestly could not figure out who was right about what. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who's right on each point? Then step back: is there any way to hold both of their principles at once — (i) each person gets a veto over what they themselves play, and (ii) if everyone involved prefers X to Y, you shouldn't pick Y — or does their little scenario show that those two principles can't be consistently combined? If they can't both be kept, which one gives way, and what's the fix?
