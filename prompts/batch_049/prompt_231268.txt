I've spent the past year arguing about welfare in community meetings and online forums, and I keep noticing that people who use the same slogans—benefits should go to the deserving, nobody should be better off on welfare than in work, the system shouldn't humiliate people—turn out to hold very different views once a concrete case is put in front of them. I want to commission a classification instrument of the kind a serious magazine might publish: a which-position-do-you-actually-hold quiz that a person can take without knowing the labels in advance. It should be aimed at ordinary adults with no training in philosophy or policy, should run six to ten questions, and should lead to five to seven final positions, each described in plain language rather than by any textbook name. I am less interested in separating people who reject conditionality altogether from everyone else, and more interested in separating the large group who accept some conditionality but disagree sharply about what it is for.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of received positions. Start by working out the diagnostic cases and the claims they test, then see which combinations of answers actually have positions standing on them, and locate the leaves there. I want three fault lines to run through the whole thing: whether a person explains a given failure primarily as individual choice or as situation and structure; whether entitlement rests on desert, on need, or on reciprocity; and whether the point of a condition is to catch the undeserving, to support people who want to comply, or to discipline people into compliance. At least four leaves should appear in plain terms: help should be unconditional because need alone settles the question; help must be earned through demonstrated effort; conditions are acceptable only when they function as support rather than as punishment or surveillance; and the binding conditions belong on employers and institutions rather than on claimants. Unoccupied combinations should be marked as empty and briefly diagnosed as incoherent, unattractive, or simply unexplored rather than skipped over.

The tree must use these four cases: someone who commits deliberate and repeated benefit fraud while able to work; someone who misses a work-search appointment because of mental health problems they have not disclosed; a single parent offered a job during school hours with no available childcare; and a disability claimant whose condition improves but not enough to support steady work. Each case should force a real division rather than stand in for a label. Please also provide the scoring key, phrase every node so someone can answer it without knowing where it leads, and flag any pairs of leaves that the questions cannot actually separate, rather than pretending the tree cuts cleaner than it does.
