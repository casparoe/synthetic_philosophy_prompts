Question. In anthropic reasoning, the Self-Sampling Assumption (SSA) and the Self-Indication Assumption (SIA) give rival rules for updating on evidence about one's own existence. (i) State each assumption. (ii) Explain how they differ in their treatment of hypotheses that posit different numbers of observers. (iii) Present the Presumptuous Philosopher thought experiment, and explain which assumption it is standardly used to challenge and why. (iv) Say which assumption you think handles the case better, with reasons.

Student answer. SSA says one should reason as if one were randomly selected from the reference class of all observers who actually exist. SIA says one should reason as if one were randomly selected from the class of all possible observers. The key difference is that on SIA, the fact that one is an actualized observer raises the probability of a hypothesis roughly in proportion to the number of observers the hypothesis says exist, because if more observers are actualized it is more likely that the sampled possible observer turns out to be real. SSA, by contrast, remains neutral between hypotheses that posit different numbers of observers, since it samples only from observers who actually exist once a hypothesis is already assumed. In the Presumptuous Philosopher case, two otherwise equivalent theories with equal prior probability differ only in how many observers they postulate: T1 says there are one trillion observers, and T2 says one billion. On SIA, the philosopher's own existence is then about a trillion times more likely under T1 than under T2, so he becomes overwhelmingly confident in T1 even though nothing else distinguishes the theories. The objection is that this confidence rests on the bare fact of one's own existence, which should not discriminate between theories that make the same observational predictions. SSA avoids the result, leaving posterior odds equal to prior odds, so the case counts strongly against SIA and favors SSA.
