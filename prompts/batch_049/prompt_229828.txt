I am a philosophy professor whose research sits between formal epistemology and the philosophy of probability. I have repeatedly tried to explain Van Fraassen's Reflection principle to colleagues in statistics and forecasting, and the conversations keep stalling on vocabulary: the home literature talks of doxastic commitments, diachronic coherence, and of binding one's future self, while my interlocutors think in martingales, filtrations, calibration, and scoring rules. I want a systematic translation into their vocabulary, and my real interest is in what fails to survive the translation.

Restate the Reflection principle — the thesis that my present credence in a proposition A, conditional on my future self assigning a specific credence r to A, should equal r — together with the dialectic around it: the diachronic Dutch book or coherence argument offered in its favor, and the standard counterexamples involving anticipated forgetting, chemically or pathologically induced credence, and expected future irrationality. Do the whole translation in the vocabulary of probabilistic forecasting: conditional expectations with respect to an increasing filtration, the martingale property of conditional probabilities, the calibration of a sequence of probabilistic forecasts, and proper scoring rules. I am fluent in the measure-theoretic versions of these notions, so do not soften the mathematics.

Then give me, point by point, what translates cleanly, what translates only under distortion, and what has no counterpart in the target vocabulary at all. I want a committed verdict about the residue: is the untranslated remainder a defect of the forecasting vocabulary, or is it evidence that the principle was doing two jobs, only one of which is a coherence condition on forecasts? I have a specific hypothesis I want you to test rather than assume. I suspect Reflection is best decomposed into (i) a comparatively weak condition on an ideal agent — roughly, that conditional credences for a fixed proposition form a martingale when the agent's future credences come from conditionalizing on a filtration that refines the present information — and (ii) a much stronger and distinctively deferential condition, that one's future credence be treated, from the present point of view, as a perfectly calibrated forecaster about every proposition; and I suspect the standard counterexamples derive their force by targeting (ii) in cases where the future self is already known to fall short of the idealization in (i). Tell me whether the translation supports that decomposition, forces it to be revised, or dissolves it.

Keep the translation honest: do not quietly build the forecasting framework's assumptions into the restatement and then declare the philosophical dispute settled; flag every point at which the mapping between the two vocabularies is forced. But do be committed. Take a definite position on which part of the principle is the genuine rational requirement and which part is overreach, and argue for it rather than laying out the options. Lead with your bottom line about the residue in the first sentence, then give the translation and the reasoning for your verdict, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
