I am leading a two-hour seminar for a reading group of quantitative master's students in computer science and statistics. They are comfortable with probability, conditioning, expected utility, and basic causal graphs, but they have never worked through decision theory beyond Newcomb's problem, and most of them treat Newcomb's problem as a parlour trick rather than as a diagnostic instrument. I want the session to get them to see what counterfactual mugging and XOR blackmail jointly establish: that the genuine choice point in such cases is between selecting an act and selecting a policy or decision procedure, and that once that distinction is in place, updatelessness — acting on branches of reality one knows one is not in — stops looking like superstition and begins to look like a stable stopping point.

For reference, the two scenarios are, in compressed form. In counterfactual mugging, a perfect predictor flips a fair coin; if the coin lands tails it asks you for $100, and if it lands heads it pays you $10,000 just in case it predicted you would have paid had it landed tails. You are asked, so you know you are in the tails branch. In XOR blackmail, the predictor sends you a message just in case exactly one of the following holds: your house is infested with termites, or you will pay it $1,000; an infestation would cost you $1,000,000.

I do not want you to write the explanation itself. I want a teaching plan: the order in which components must be introduced and why each depends on the previous one, the single worked example the session should return to, the misconceptions this audience will predictably form and where in the sequence each should be headed off, the closing check that would show the idea has landed rather than merely been tolerated, and what should be left out and openly flagged as omitted.

Specifically, give me:

1. The prerequisite idea that must be secured before either problem is intelligible — the thing which, if it is not in place, will make the students experience both cases as conjuring tricks rather than arguments.

2. An ordering for the session. Should counterfactual mugging come before XOR blackmail, or the reverse? In what precise sense should the first case stage a problem for reasoning about acts, and the second a different problem for reasoning about evidence and correlation? Which intermediate notions (for example, pre-commitment as legitimate but unavailable in the posted choice; the difference between the probability of an act and the probability of a policy; prediction as correlation with the decision procedure rather than with the surface action) need to be built between the two cases, and why?

3. One carrying example. Decide which of the two scenarios should serve as the spine of the session, and state what features it must have and must lack for the pedagogical point to survive — for instance, a fair coin rather than a logical fact, perfect prediction rather than merely statistical prediction, a one-shot choice rather than a repeated game, and a mixed-upside structure in which the benefit lands in the branch the agent knows it is not in. If the cleanest spine is a simplified common core of both scenarios rather than either standard version, specify that core.

4. A misconception map. For each of the following predictable moves — and for any others you judge as pressing — say at which point in the sequence it should be addressed and how to address it without dismissing it: 'the predictor is impossible, so the problem floats on a false presupposition'; 'this is just Newcomb's problem with the labels changed'; 'I would pay only because it buys me a reputation as a cooperator'; 'XOR blackmail is simply correlation rather than causation, so the causal decision theorist is obviously right and that closes the matter'; 'the two problems are the same problem described twice'. Be specific about whether each misconception is best pre-empted by an example, by a modification of the carrying example, or by pausing the sequence and extending the current case.

5. A closing check of understanding. Design a short exercise or question, answerable in five minutes without new machinery, that would distinguish a student who has genuinely grasped why the two problems push in opposite directions from a student who has merely learned to say 'choose the policy, not the act'. Say what a correct-but-shallow answer looks like and what a correct-and-deep answer adds.

6. A conscious omission list. State which neighbouring topics — logical counterfactuals, proof-based decision theory, the formal differences among updateless, timeless, and functional decision theories, reflective oracles, or anything similar — you would explicitly decline to cover, and explain how drawing that boundary can itself reinforce the main point rather than deflate the session.

Do not provide a reading list or citations; the plan should be built from the two scenarios themselves.
