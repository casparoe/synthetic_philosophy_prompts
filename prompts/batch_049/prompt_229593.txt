I am a student in a graduate seminar on risk, rationality, and formal epistemology, and my term paper defends a version of the bounded-utility response to expected-value fanaticism. For the paper's opening section I need a decision problem built to order, and I would like you to construct it and then demonstrate that it meets the specification. I want the scenario engineered so that expected-utility maximization with an unbounded utility function that is linear in lives saved prescribes an action a sensible reader would judge fanatical, while expected-utility maximization with a bounded utility function prescribes the unremarkable alternative. Constraints: the case must be realistic, drawn from choices a person could actually face about money, career, or philanthropy; no Pascal's muggers, no perfect predictors, no exotic physics, and no one-off miracles. The pivotal probability must be generated by an ordinary causal chain, such as a research program succeeding and its recommendations being adopted, and must be no smaller than one in ten thousand; the pivotal payoff must be no larger than five million lives saved. The point of those bounds is that the fanatical verdict must survive without the absurd numbers that make a Pascal's mugging easy to dismiss. The two utility functions must be calibrated to agree across a wide range of ordinary life-saving choices, so that the disagreement in the case is traceable specifically to the difference between an unbounded and a bounded valuation of lives, rather than to general risk aversion. After laying out the acts, states, probabilities, and utility assignments, work through what each theory selects and why, and then tell me which structural feature of the case is doing the work. Finish by saying whether deleting that feature would collapse the case back into a standard Pascal's mugging, and how a defender of bounded utility would attack the unbounded calculation, and whether that attack rebuts the calculation or only changes the subject.
