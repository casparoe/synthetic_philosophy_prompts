I am a high-school junior, and for an independent study project on decision-making under uncertainty I have been recording choices my AP Psychology teacher makes in a continuing series of two-urn demonstrations. I would like help using that record to predict her next answer.

The setup is always the same. There are two urns, each containing 100 balls. Urn K contains exactly 50 red balls and 50 black balls. Urn U also contains 100 balls, each either red or black, but nobody has told her the number of red balls in Urn U; it could be any number from 0 to 100. In each game a single ball is drawn from one urn. A bet pays the stated amount of points if the named color is drawn, and pays nothing otherwise. The points are converted into a small prize at the end of the term, and for this exercise you should treat her as caring only about expected points, so diminishing marginal utility of money is not the issue.

So far I have recorded these choices. In every row, the uncertain bet is $10 on the named color from Urn U, and the known bet is on the same named color from Urn K, for the amount shown.

- $10 on red from U vs. $10 on red from K: she takes the K bet.
- $10 on black from U vs. $10 on black from K: she takes the K bet.
- $10 on red from U vs. $6 on red from K: she takes the U bet.
- $10 on red from U vs. $7 on red from K: she takes the U bet.
- $10 on red from U vs. $8 on red from K: she takes the K bet.
- $10 on red from U vs. $9 on red from K: she takes the K bet.

When the last four comparisons were rerun with black in place of red, she chose the same way each time.

Next week I plan to ask her about one new choice at a larger scale: $20 on red from Urn U versus $16 on red from Urn K. I know this looks like the familiar Ellsberg two-urn situation, and that is deliberate; I want to reconstruct her particular rule rather than just attach a label.

Please act as an expert in decision theory and the psychology of ambiguity. Think slowly, and begin by brainstorming at least three candidate rules that could reproduce her record. State each rule precisely enough that its prediction for the next case is just arithmetic. Be especially careful about any rule that assigns a single fixed probability to red in Urn U, and check it against the equal-payoff black choice before you trust it. Then tell me (a) what each surviving rule predicts for the $20-versus-$16 case, (b) your best prediction for what she will actually choose and the reasons you prefer the rule behind that prediction, and (c) one further choice involving the same two urns that would most decisively settle which rule she is actually using.
