I coordinate a peer-education program about sexual consent at a university, and after a recent training session in which the case of a man lying about being separated from his wife split the room, I want to develop a case-based module that forces our trainees to reckon with why deception matters rather than relying on slogans. I am not asking for your view about that actual case, and I do not want a bibliography. Instead, please construct a thought experiment to my specifications.

Design a sexual-consent scenario with these constraints. The person giving consent is an adult, sober, fully competent, and not under any threat, pressure, or arrangement that a normal person would classify as coercion. The person seeking consent tells one deliberate lie. The lie is not about the seeker's identity, is not about any communicable disease or contraception, and does not misrepresent the physical acts to be performed. Yet the scenario should be one in which all three of the following families of view would agree that the recipient did not have valid consent: autonomy-based views, views on which consent is invalid when it would wrong the consent-giver, and views on which consent transfers a permission through a communicated mental act. Then construct a minimally different scenario in which the same lie, told in the same way, does not vitiate consent, and show that what does the work is a structural feature of the two situations rather than a change in the lie's content or the seriousness of the stakes.

After the two scenarios, walk through the reasoning each family of view would give in the first and in the second, name the structural feature that separates them, say what happens to each theory's verdict if that feature is removed, and give the strongest objection you can from someone who holds that deception can never vitiate consent because consent is just a mental act. Keep the scenarios ordinary and realistic; no exotic psychology or law-enforcement hypotheticals.
