Before I read another account of free will, I want the full specification for a theory of the variety of free will that is worth wanting — not a semantic account of the ordinary phrase and not merely a solution to the compatibility problem. Spell out what such a theory must get right: the cases it must handle (manipulation, coercion, addiction, weakness of will, Frankfurt-style overdetermination, the willing addict, the self-endorsed but predictable chooser), the distinctions it cannot collapse (freedom of action versus freedom of the will; the capacity for moral responsibility versus the freedom it might still be reasonable to want even if blame and credit were downgraded; local choice versus the shape of a life across time), and the empirical or theoretical results it cannot contradict, with the consideration behind each requirement. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are parochial demands of a particular camp — leeway libertarians, source libertarians, reasons-responsiveness compatibilists, hierarchical compatibilists. Then say whether the demand for the ability to do otherwise and the demand to be an ultimate source of one's actions can be jointly satisfied in a naturalistic picture, and identify the single requirement that excludes the most popular accounts. Keep the scoring of named views brief; the criteria are what I am after.
