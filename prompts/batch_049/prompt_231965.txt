Write a mock appellate-court opinion of about 900 words deciding the dispute between Achilles and the Tortoise in Lewis Carroll's "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles." The voice should be deadpan and judicially formal throughout, with a case caption, a brief statement of facts, and an analysis section. The target of the satire is the idea that an inference loses, and can be given back, its force by writing the rule of inference down as an additional premise — and, more broadly, the demand that every inferential step be licensed by an explicit premise the reasoner has already accepted. The humor must come from the philosophy rather than from caricaturing the characters: someone working in the philosophy of logic should recognize that the writer understands the distinction between a rule of inference and a premise, why the Tortoise's request generates an infinite regress, and the difference between accepting a conditional and accepting a rule that licenses detaching a conclusion. A lay reader should still be able to follow it, because the statement of facts should retell the original gambit in paraphrase, without quoting Carroll: the Tortoise accepts A, B, and also the conditional If A and B then Z, yet declines to accept Z, and keeps inviting Achilles to write down further and further premises.

Treat the Tortoise's position fairly and precisely. The court should acknowledge that she has not contradicted herself and that no finite pile of premises compels her. The opinion should then dispose of the case in a way that does not secretly hand her one more premise to ask about — it should draw the rule/premise distinction in its own terms, while conceding what the episode really shows about the difference between an argument's being valid and an argument's rationally compelling someone who declines to draw conclusions at all. End with a short appended note — a clerk's note, footnote, or settled order — that is itself quietly caught in the same infinite regress, as a final joke.
