I teach an undergraduate seminar on creativity and technology, and I want to build a class around Jorge Luis Borges's story Pierre Menard, Author of the Quixote as the literary centerpiece of our unit on AI-generated art. For the sake of our discussion, here is the premise I want students to hold in view: Menard undertakes to produce, not to copy or transcribe, several passages of Don Quixote word for word — arriving at them as Pierre Menard in the twentieth century, through his own experiences and aesthetic program, rather than by reconstructing Cervantes in himself. The narrator then reads the two verbally identical texts side by side and judges Menard's version subtler and richer, different in meaning because of who wrote it, when, and by what method.

I want a reading of the story as an intuition pump in the AI-art debate that also refuses to let the story do the philosophical work by itself. Start by articulating the strongest claim the story seems to support: is it that a work is not identical with its text or image, but with the text plus its history of production; that creative value depends on authorship, intention, context, or effort rather than on the perceptible artifact alone? State precisely which version of that view the story earns, and point to the specific moments that carry the argument — the narrator's paired comparison of identical passages, the two methods Menard considers, the catalog of his visible works.

Then turn the story against itself. A formalist or anti-intentionalist would say the two texts are one text, and that the narrator's comparative reading is a dazzling conceit about historical atmosphere rather than a discovery in the object. Is there anything in the story that answers that objection, or is Borges partly satirizing the very contextualist lesson readers often take from it? Now bring the story to the present: if a diffusion model or a language model produces an apparently novel image or passage, is the model best described as a Menard (a distinct productive history yielding a distinct work), a pen (an instrument whose outputs derive their meaning from the human user who prompts and selects them), or a plagiarist (reproducing patterns from its training data without genuine authorship)? Say where the analogy holds and where it breaks, especially around intention, experience, and self-criticism, which Menard has and the model does not.

Conclude by telling me what the story adds to the AI-art debate that the arguments alone do not, and where an honest critic should concede that the story is supplying vivid rhetoric rather than a proof. I want my students to leave able to state the disagreement between those who locate creativity in the artifact and those who locate it in the act, not with a pat verdict.
