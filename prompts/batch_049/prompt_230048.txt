A friend of mine is deep into housing politics and his one-sentence take is that zoning is basically OPEC for apartments. The way he tells it, homeowners vote to cap the number of units that can exist in a city the same way OPEC countries vote to cap how many barrels of crude hit the market; the point of either cap is to keep the good scarce so the price stays high; and the fix follows straight from that — break the cartel, let supply respond to price, upzone, and rents fall. I can't shake the sense that the analogy is doing too much work, but I can't say exactly where it gives out.

I'd like you to audit it the way someone would audit any loaded analogy that's being used to license a policy conclusion. Make the mapping explicit — homeowners to cartel members, units to barrels, rents to crude prices, land to oil fields, upzoning to an OPEC breakup, whatever else belongs. Then seperate the disanalogies that actually matter for his conclusion from the ones that are just side differences. What does the cartel picture quietly import that a city mostly lacks — a homogeneous, shippable good; a handful of big coordinated sellers; a world price; profits that flow back to the members who did the restricting; that sort of thing — and which of those imports would actually be doing the argument's work if we didn't notice them? Then give the bottom line: does his conclusion (upzone enough and rents fall enough that housing stops being the central crisis) survive the disanalogies intact, survive only in a weaker form, or mostly not? Last, if the analogy is broken at a load-bearing joint, suggest a better one. I keep wonderin whether taxi medallions — or old guild entry restrictions — are closer than OPEC; check that too.

End with a single line that begins "Verdict:" and is a one-sentence summary of the whole audit.
