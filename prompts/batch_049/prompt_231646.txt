I'm trying to get straight about how much work the money pump argument can actually do, and I keep going back and forth. Take someone whose preferences over three prospects cycle: they prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A. The standard story is that a bookie who starts them off with C can trade them up to B, then A, then back to C, charging a small fee at each step, and return them to exactly where they started but poorer, with each trade rationalized by one of their pairwise preferences. People often slide from that story to the conclusion that cyclic preferences are simply irrational — that having those preferences is itself a rational defect, not just a way to lose money. (Let's take "irrational" throughout to mean "violates a requirement of instrumental rationality.") My own credence in that conclusion is around 0.45; a colleague of mine is at 0.9. I want your number, but more importantly I want the reasoning decomposed. Split the claim into the sub-questions your credence depends on — for instance: whether the money pump actually follows from the preferences alone; whether it's legitimate to assume the agent will accept each pairwise trade one at a time, or whether that smuggles in a pairwise way of choosing they might reject; whether being exploitable by another agent is the kind of failing that constitutes irrationality rather than just a cost or a risk; whether the argument still goes through once you notice that preferences may resynchronize after each trade, so the later trades may no longer be ones the agent would endorse; and whether the whole thing looks different if you don't think of preferences as rankings that automatically license pairwise trades. Assign rough credences to the sub-questions, show how they combine into your overall number, and tell me which sub-question separates the 0.45 person from the 0.9 person. And don't just lay out the options: pick a definite number, put it up front, and defend it.
