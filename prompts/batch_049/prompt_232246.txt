I help run a small reading group on distributive ethics that meets every other week, and next term we are taking up the leveling-down objection and the dispute between those who think equality itself matters and those who think that only people's levels of well-being matter, with special concern for the worse off. I want to make a handout that lets members discover which position in this debate they actually hold, but I want to sort them by their verdicts on concrete distributional choices rather than by asking which label they like; in this group, people tend to arrive with a label and then rationalize backwards, so I want the labels to come out as outputs, not go in as inputs.

Please design a decision tree or sorting quiz for this purpose. Build it from the logical space rather than from a list of standard positions: first the diagnostic questions and the combinations of answers they allow, then the named positions located in that space, and then a note on any combinations that are unoccupied, with a brief diagnosis of whether each empty region is empty because the position is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Each branch node should be a concrete choice between two policies or allocations, phrased so that someone can answer it without already knowing which position it routes to. At minimum, the following cases should appear as nodes: a leveling-down case in which some people are made worse off and nobody is made better off but the distribution becomes equal; a pure transfer that leaves total well-being unchanged but narrows the gap between better and worse off; a choice between a very large benefit to many moderately well-off people and a tiny benefit to a single worst-off person; and a case that varies whether the beneficiary is above or below a sufficiency threshold. Add further nodes if the logical space requires them.

The leaves I care about are utilitarianism, prioritarianism, leximin, sufficientarianism, telic egalitarianism, and relational or pluralist egalitarianism; include the difference principle only if you can place it honestly, and otherwise say why it sits outside the tree. For each leaf, give one sentence on the view's most awkward commitment. Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate. In particular, I would like you to address whether telic egalitarianism and relational egalitarianism can be separated by distributional cases at all, and, if not, what non-distributional question would separate them; and whether prioritarianism is genuinely separable from utilitarianism with a concave transformation applied to individuals' well-being levels. Since the handout will be circulated to a UK audience, please write the final questions and leaf descriptions in British English.
