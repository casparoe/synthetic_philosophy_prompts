I work on the infrastructure team at a company that runs a small internal prediction market. Employees trade play-money contracts on operational questions, such as whether a service migration will land by a target date. A month ago a market opened on whether our checkout service rewrite will be in production by the first day of next quarter. The price settled near 70 after the first afternoon — meaning the market was putting roughly a 70 percent probability on shipping on time — and has barely moved since, even though the usual project news kept arriving: a flaky integration suite, a changed API shape, an unchanged launch date.

Two coworkers, Maya and Dan, disagree about what the quiet means.

Maya says the quiet is a good sign. She points to an economics result called the no-trade theorem. Her summary: when everyone shares the same prior and is rational, people should not trade purely on differences in information, because the willingness to trade would itself leak the information and remove the profit. So on her view the market has done its job; the remaining disagreement has been priced away, and the 70 really does aggregate what the informed people know. She wants that number in our planning document.

Dan says this is backwards. He argues the theorem depends on assumptions our market plainly violates — common priors, common knowledge of rationality, no transaction costs, and a real profit motive. He says the market is quiet because the stakes are tiny, there is no market maker, and a handful of early trades from people who may not have known much are enough to anchor the price. On his view, silence is evidence about incentives and liquidity, not evidence that the price is informative.

Maya pushed back that Dan would still grant the whole point of the market: employees hold different private information, having seen different code, tests, and reviews. The theorem, she says, is precisely about people with different information; it says they still should not trade once the willingness to trade is common knowledge. So our messy information environment doesn't dodge the theorem. The only question is whether the frictions are large enough, and trading on our platform is cheap.

Dan answered that the trade is cheap, but the expected profit from correcting a play-money price isn't worth the time it takes to reason about the contract. A quiet market is therefore equivocal: it can mean the price has absorbed the information, or it can mean the price is wrong but no one is motivated to correct it. From silence alone, he says, you cannot tell which.

Maya's next move was that Dan is then committed to something odd. If quiet does not mean informative, the only way the market can become informative is for someone to trade against the prevailing price. But any such trade is a bet that the other side is wrong. So Dan is committed to saying informational markets work only by exploiting someone's mistake.

Dan replied that this isn't an embarrassment; it is a known live puzzle. Rational people with common priors should not do much speculative trading, yet real markets trade constantly. The usual escapes are different priors, hedging and liquidity needs, overconfidence, or frictions. Our quiet market, he says, is most plausibly the friction case: nobody bothers, so the price can sit away from the truth even though some people have better information.

Maya's last move was that Dan is then committed to treating our market prices as noise, which doesn't fit the fact that these prices have historically tracked real outcomes. Dan said he never claimed they were noise; he claimed only that the absence of trading doesn't license her inference. A quiet market could be quiet because the early traders were well informed, because everyone shares the same well-informed view, or because no one is sufficiently motivated to correct a stale price — and the observed silence cannot distinguish those.

I'd like you to adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each substantive claim, say who is right, who is overclaiming, and what the precise issue is. Then say what both of them are missing about the relationship between trading, price informativeness, and the no-trade theorem, and who is more right overall.
