I am a retired chemist who has come to philosophy late, and I now convene a monthly group that pairs films with problems in metaphysics. Next month we are watching Christopher Nolan's The Prestige (2006), and I want to use it to work through the identity of indiscernibles and Max Black's two-sphere thought experiment. The film seems to me to stage both halves of the issue: Borden's act rests on two men who live as one public person, while Angier's Tesla machine produces a duplicate each night and leaves him unable to say whether he will be the man in the tank or the man on the balcony. I would like a close reading of the film through this lens, and just as much, a sense of where the film resists it.

Specifically: where does The Prestige actually dramatize the principle that no two distinct things can share all their properties, and where does it slide into neighbouring questions — personal identity across time, social or legal identity, or the dramatic need to give twins different loves, temperaments, and fates so the audience can tell them apart? Black argued that a universe containing only two exactly similar spheres is a coherent, though symmetrical, state of affairs, so the spheres would be two while being qualitatively indiscernible. Does the film come out closer to Leibniz or to Black? Does it show that a bare numerical difference is enough, or does it show that human beings, unlike spheres, cannot stay indiscernible once they have histories? And is there anything the film adds to the thought experiment that the thought experiment cannot show on its own — for instance, about our refusal to count as two what presents no observable difference, or about why the difference between one and two matters so much when nothing qualitative seems to hang on it?

I would especially value places where the honest reading must admit the movie changes the subject. Do the duplicates and twins in the film ever meet the strict condition of being indiscernible, or does the plot quietly smuggle in differences in position, memory, and motive? If they never meet the condition, is the film still a useful way into the principle, or does it trade on a confusion? I am preparing discussion notes, so I would appreciate a reading ordered roughly by the film's major reveals: the birdcage trick, the emergence of Fallon, the first use of the Tesla machine, and the final gallery of drowned copies.
