I'm a journalist who covers markets and the people who build models of them, and I've been kicking around an essay on the epistemology of markets that would force me to actually write down what I believe about how much we can learn from prices. Over the years I've settled into a set of commitments, but I've started to suspect they can't all be true at once, and I honestly can't locate the fault line. I'd like an audit rather than a lecture.

The set is roughly this: (1) When there's a liquid market or a prediction market on a question, I treat the price as the best public summary of dispersed information and update toward it; I don't believe I can outguess it. (2) At the same time, I have a Groucho-Marx-ish reflex: if someone cold-emails me a trade, a tip, or a sweetheart deal, my first thought is that the offer itself is the signal, and the signal is bad. Nobody is trying to hand me money out of charity, so the fact that they selected me as a counterparty is itself evidence against the trade; I almost never act on individual proposals. (3) I know the rough no-trade story behind this — under common priors and common knowledge of rationality, people shouldn't trade on information differences alone, because each side's willingness to trade at a price leaks what they know — but I also think the common-prior assumption is false in real markets. People trade because their priors, models, and confidence differ, not because one side secretly knows better, so I dont think the theorem is a good description of actual liquidity and I dont want to lean on it too hard. (4) And yet I have no clean account of how much observed volume is information-driven rather than churn — fees, boredom, attention, forced trades. I suspect a lot of it is noise, which leaves me less sure that the price in (1) is as wise as I treat it as being. (5) The line I use to reconcile (1) and (2) is that they're the same inference at different grains: an individual offer is a selected, adversarial signal, while the market price is the average of thousands of such signals, and the averaging cancels the selection problem. I take that to be why the two halves of me coexist.

So: go through these and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension and which are only apparently in tension. Rank the trouble spots by how severe they are, say what would have to give in each case, and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most and what it would cost.
