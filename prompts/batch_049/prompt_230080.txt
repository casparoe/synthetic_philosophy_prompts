I want a reading of Everything Everywhere All at Once through the debate between counterpart theory and transworld identity. Here's the thing I keep flip-flopping on. The movie is full of alternate Evelyns — rock Evelyn, kung-fu Evelyn, hot-dog-fingers Evelyn — and verse-jumping lets the "main" Evelyn reach into their skills and memories. On a Lewisian counterpart theory, those other Evelyns aren't literally the same person: each Evelyn is world-bound, and the alternates are her counterparts, distinct individuals in other concrete worlds who resemble her in the right ways. On a Kripke/Plantinga-style view, by contrast, there's one Evelyn with many possible lives, and when the movie shows us those other lives it's tracking a single individual across worlds (or stipulating worlds in which she chose differently). Which picture does the film actually need, and which does it complicate? I'd like you to work through some specific beats: the rapid montage of lives she could have led, Jobu Tupaki experiencing every universe at once, the moment the line about wanting to just do laundry and taxes with Waymond in another life lands, and the way Evelyn saves her enemies by seeing the wounded versions of them. Does verse-jumping make better sense as counterpart-travel or as making transworld identity vivid? Where does the movie resist the philosophical frame — for instance, where its emotional stakes seem to demand a connection across versions that Lewis explicitly denies, or where the reciprocal verse stuff gets too tangled for either theory to handle cleanly? And be fair to the philosophy too: dont flatten Lewis into "everyone's sealed off from everyone else," and dont pretend the Kripkean view just asserts identity without explaining why that isn't a problem. End with something the film does to this debate that argument alone doesn't.
