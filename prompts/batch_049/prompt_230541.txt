I'm teaching an undergraduate module on cultural evolution and want a hands-on session for the week on memes and Darwinian approaches to culture. The class is around 16 second- and third-years and I've got 80 minutes. I'd like a game or simulation that makes them feel the core idea — that culture can change through variation, copying, and differential survival, and that some cultural items spread because they're good at getting themselves copied rather than because they're true or useful. I specifically want them to see that the spread of a cultural item is explained by differential replication: how many copies it leaves, how faithfully they are copied, and how long they last — not by anyone standing back and judging the variants as better. I once ran a plain chain-whisper exercise and it fell flat, because there was only one variant and nothing for it to be selected among, so the students just concluded that people are bad at repeating things. I want to avoid that this time. Ideally the rules should set up several competing variants that differ in catchiness, emotional punch, length, or ease of reproduction, with small copying errors introduced each generation, and a scoring system that doesn't need real money but still gives everyone a reason to pass items on faithfully.

Please design the full activity for me, including the rules and roles, how to organise the 80 minutes, what I'd need to print or prepare beforehand, and a debrief that moves from the students' own results to the Darwinian concepts of variation, heredity, differential fitness, and cumulative change — without me lecturing the point first. I'd also like a paragraph on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and an honest note on what the game cannot capture about real cultural evolution and I'll have to supply in discussion. The students should not hear the label meme until the debrief if possible, and the payoff structure should make the selection logic emerge from a tally at the end rather than from me explaining it.

Oh, and if you could write the answer, and any handout or slide text, in American English. My own notes use British spelling, so that's fine for the explanation you give me, but anything the students see should use American spelling and punctuation.
