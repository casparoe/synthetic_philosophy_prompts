I am the standards director for a seafood certification body whose higher-welfare label covers farmed finfish and, newly, farmed decapod crustaceans — mainly shrimp and prawns, with a few crab producers. Last night I spent a long evening arguing with a friend about whether shrimp can suffer. She kept insisting that without proof we should treat them as if they feel nothing and confine the label to finfish; I kept insisting that uncertainty is not a licence, and that our credibility depends on taking the possibility of crustacean suffering seriously. The argument ended with her saying, in effect: if you think that, write me the rule that treats a shrimp's possible pain with the right seriousness without shutting down every shrimp farm. I realized I could not produce such a rule, and that our current welfare requirements for crustacean slaughter are more a mood than a standard.

I want you to write that rule as a decision procedure our auditors can actually run. You are a philosopher who works on animal consciousness and practical ethics and who has experience turning philosophical precautions into operational frameworks for non-philosophers. Work through the problem in stages before presenting anything final: brainstorm the distinct difficulties the procedure must handle; identify the threshold questions and the credible answers an auditor might give to each; draft the rules; test them against the cases below; then produce one polished procedure.

The procedure is for a single auditor with no philosophical training, on a farm visit of at most half a day, deciding whether the operation's handling and slaughter practices for farmed fish and decapod crustaceans may be certified under the label. The auditor must return one of: certify; certify with corrective actions; do not certify. We run roughly 150 audits a year, a substantial share of them turning on slaughter methods, and the label's value to retailers depends on consistency across auditors; borderline cases can be escalated to me and a standards committee, but the procedure should make that the exception rather than the norm.

The inputs the auditor can reliably obtain are: the species and what is known about its nociception and pain-like states; the specific handling and slaughter methods; observed failure rates where the method is mechanical; the number of animals affected per cycle; and the availability, cost, and operator reliability of the best alternative method. The procedure must not require the auditor to settle whether shrimp, prawns, or crabs feel pain, and it must not silently assume they do or don't. It should discipline the precautionary principle rather than wave it around: say how much and what kind of evidence of possible sentience triggers protective measures, how the severity and duration of pain-if-it-exists enter, how numbers of animals do or do not aggregate across the operation's throughput, and where the procedure stops and hands the question to a values committee because the residual choice is about how much a possible discomfort in a huge number of animals weighs against an avoidable severe pain in a few, or against the survival of a producer.

Please apply the finished procedure to these cases, giving the decision and the decisive step in each:

(i) a high-volume whiteleg shrimp farm that chills live shrimp in an ice slurry and then mechanically splits them, with no pre-stunning, and argues that alternatives are unproven and expensive;

(ii) a salmon farm using automated percussive stunning with continuous monitoring that shows a 3% failure rate, after which fish proceed to bleeding while possibly still conscious;

(iii) a small crab producer that boils crabs alive and argues that rapid killing is safer for workers than machinery they do not trust.

At least one of these should be a case where the procedure, honestly applied, returns an answer I personally do not want to sign — either certifying something I find repellent or failing a producer I respect. Tell me explicitly which case that is and why the procedure gives that answer.

A few constraints. Give me a committed procedure: where a default is needed, choose it and defend it, rather than presenting a menu of defensible defaults. Only leave genuinely value-laden decisions to the values committee, and keep that list as short as you can; say plainly what the procedure cannot do — what it will not settle about crustacean sentience, and what no procedure can remove from the certifying body's judgment. Do not pad the document with citations or a literature review; I will supply the evidence base. And do not let the procedure collapse into either a pure precautionary veto that fails every practice not proved safe, or a pure cost-benefit test that permits any amount of suffering if the throughput is high enough.
