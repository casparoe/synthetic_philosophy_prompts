I am assembling a teaching file on the integrity objection, specifically on when an agent counts as having one thought too many before acting, and I would like a careful extrapolation from my colleague's recorded verdicts rather than a literature review.

Over the years, my colleague has committed herself to these six verdicts:

1. A man sees his wife and a stranger both drowning. He saves his wife, but only after asking himself whether he is permitted to give her priority. Verdict: one thought too many.
2. An emergency physician, deciding which patient to treat first, pauses to work through the hospital's triage protocol. Verdict: not one thought too many.
3. On her way to visit a close friend in hospital, a woman asks herself whether she is obligated to make the visit; she concludes that she is, and goes. Verdict: one thought too many.
4. Before writing a strong reference for a former doctoral student, a professor checks the university's conflict-of-interest rules and confirms that none apply. Verdict: not one thought too many.
5. An off-duty lifeguard sees his teenage neighbour struggling in a rip current. Before entering the water he asks himself whether he has a moral duty to go in even though he is off the clock; he then goes in and saves the neighbour. Verdict: one thought too many.
6. Two weeks before making his annual charitable gifts, a donor spends an hour comparing charities' cost-effectiveness. Verdict: not one thought too many.

The new case I want predicted: A man has decided to donate a kidney to his sister. Two weeks before the surgery, on a calm evening at home, he asks himself whether the donation is morally obligatory. He concludes that it is not obligatory, but proceeds with the donation exactly as planned.

Please do the following, using these headings in this order:

The precedents (150–200 words): restate each verdict and identify which feature of the case seems to be doing the work.

Candidate principles (350–450 words): articulate at least two general principles, each of which fits all six recorded verdicts without ad hoc amendments; state what each principle predicts about the kidney case; and make explicit the point on which they diverge.

Closest analog (100–150 words): identify which earlier case is the closest analog to the kidney case and why, and note whether this judgment changes depending on which candidate principle one adopts.

Prediction (250–300 words): give your best prediction of my colleague's verdict, with reasons for preferring one principle over its rivals; be explicit about which feature of the case—for instance, the content of the question, its timing, its distance from the point of action, or the presence of a partial relationship—carries the most weight for you.

Tie-breaker case (150–200 words): design the single best further case that would tell apart whichever principles remain live after your prediction, state what each predicts for it, and explain why it isolates the difference cleanly.
