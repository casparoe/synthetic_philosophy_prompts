Two members of my philosophy reading group have been arguing since September across two positions that never quite meet. One is a Quinean confirmation holist: every attempt to draw a boundary between analytic and synthetic sentences either presupposes the notion it is meant to explain or collapses into a surrogate such as entrenchment in a holistic web, and no sentence is in principle immune to revision. The other is a neo-Carnapian: the Quinean critique damages only an epistemically loaded, rationalist conception of truths knowable independent of experience, and it leaves a framework-relative notion of analyticity intact — once a language is set up with formation rules, meaning postulates, and inference rules, some sentences hold in virtue of the framework and can be recognized without consulting the course of experience. Each party believes the other keeps shifting between senses of the key terms. The Quinean suspects the neo-Carnapian of oscillating between an arbitrary conventional stipulation, which is real but uninteresting, and an epistemic privilege, which is interesting but unearned. The neo-Carnapian suspects the Quinean of banning any distinction that is not behavioral or epistemic while quietly relying on a graded notion of centrality among beliefs that is doing the same work as analyticity under another name.

Draft the joint statement these two should have produced after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Open with a numbered section of claims both can sign, about confirmation holism, revisability, meaning postulates, and what the original Quinean critique did and did not establish; the wording must be precise enough that neither side will later repudiate it as vacuous or slanted. Then write a disagreements table: for each residual issue, give the Quinean and neo-Carnapian formulations, each phrased so its own side would accept it as its considered view, and identify the crux on which the row actually turns. Next, address the cruxes directly. The statement should make genuine progress on at least three questions. First, when a recalcitrant observation leads a community to stop asserting a sentence, is the difference between changing a belief and changing the meaning of one of the terms a factual difference or a difference of description? Second, does an explicitly stipulated definition, or an inference rule that introduces and eliminates a newly defined logical connective, establish a kind of analyticity beyond the bare fact that the community has adopted a convention? Third, can the neo-Carnapian honestly deny that framework statements are themselves open to revision for pragmatic reasons while still explaining how the original Quinean critique could have missed the distinction? For each crux, specify a concrete test or case both sides would accept as genuinely bearing on it, record each side's prediction in advance, and finish by stating which components of the residual disagreement, if any, are verbal rather than substantive.
