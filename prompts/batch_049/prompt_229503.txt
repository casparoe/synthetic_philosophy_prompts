I write a philosophy-of-technology newsletter and want a "which account of AI deception do you actually hold?" sorting quiz for readers without philosophical training. Build eight multiple-choice items, each a concrete case with two or three plain-language answer options drawn, as the case requires, from "deceptive," "merely false," "honest," and "manipulative." Use cases such as: a large language model that states a falsehood because its training data was wrong; a customer-service bot that quietly omits an inconvenient clause; a chess engine that offers a sacrifice as a trap; a language model that tells a user what it predicts the user wants to hear; a game-playing agent that bluffs by misrepresenting its resources; and a model that produces a misleading answer only after the user insists the wrong answer is right. The scoring key should sort readers onto at least four accounts: an intentionalist account on which deception requires the system itself to intend that the audience form a false belief; a designer-intent account on which the relevant intention belongs to the builders; a functionalist account on which deception is fixed by the role the behavior plays, independent of anyone's mental states; and a deflationary account on which honesty and deception are misplaced descriptions of systems without beliefs. Each case should split at least two of these accounts, no item should ask readers which account they prefer, and the key should note any coherent answer pattern that none of the four accounts predicts, as well as any two accounts the quiz cannot cleanly separate.
