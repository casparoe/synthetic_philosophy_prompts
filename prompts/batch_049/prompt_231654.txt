I just came from a long argument with a friend about whether term limits, rotation in office, and sortition improve institutions, and I realized that I have firm verdicts about particular offices but no clear principle behind them. I would like you to work out what principle, if any, captures my verdicts.

Here are my verdicts, roughly in the form they came out in the argument.

1. I oppose a two-term limit on a directly elected city comptroller. The office is technical and poorly monitored by the public, and a limit mostly transfers power to permanent staff and outside auditors.

2. I support a two-term limit on a county sheriff. Long-serving sheriffs build personal machines, the office is coercive, and rotation interrupts that dynamic.

3. I support selecting a citizens' assembly by lot to redraw state legislative districts. The main risk is self-dealing, and ordinary citizens, with expert support, can handle a one-off map.

4. I oppose selecting a monetary policy board by lot. The role requires technical competence and credibility in financial markets, and sortition would be reckless.

5. I support keeping a state legislature's budget committee in the hands of long-serving career legislators. Continuity lets them master the details and push back against the executive's numbers.

6. I support requiring the same legislature's ethics committee to rotate its membership every session. Long service on ethics oversight makes it clubby, while frequent rotation keeps oversight adversarial.

7. I oppose term limits for neighborhood council chairs, though not strongly. Turnover is already high, and the main cost would be lost local knowledge.

8. I support a sortition-based climate assembly that recommends policy, but I would oppose giving it binding power: it is valuable as a one-off source of considered public judgment, not as a standing governing body.

I am most confident in (3), (4), and (6), and least confident in (1) and (7); my friend came closest to moving me on (2).

My tentative principle was that we should prefer career professionals where the main risk is technical error or lost tacit knowledge, and prefer rotation or sortition where the main risk is capture by a stable network. But that is loose, and my friend showed it can be gamed case by case. He proposed a sharper version: one-off distributions of power lend themselves to sortition, ongoing coercive or oversight roles lend themselves to rotation, and ongoing technical roles lend themselves to career professionals. I could not make that version fit the climate assembly, which is both one-off and technically dense, or the sheriff, which is both coercive and operationally complex.

What I want is this. Please work out the principle—or a small set of candidate principles—that best captures these eight verdicts, stated precisely enough to apply to a new case. For each clause of the principle, say which of my cases motivates it. Then apply the principle to a case I did not include: an elected school board subject to a three-term limit. Tell me what it predicts. Finally, be honest about which of my verdicts the principle cannot save without ad hoc amendments, and which of those verdicts I should be most willing to reconsider. Since I am trying to find out where my own judgment is fragile, please also tell me what would change your mind: which of my verdicts, if I changed it, would most alter the principle you propose, and where you think your reasoning is most likely to fail. Please write the answer in American English.
