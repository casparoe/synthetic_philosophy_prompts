I am trying to work out what I actually believe about the ethics of torture, beginning from my reactions to concrete cases rather than from a general slogan. Below are my verdicts. I would like you to tell me what principle, if any, they add up to.

Case 1 — The captured bomber. A bomb has been hidden in a railway station and will detonate within the hour, killing hundreds. One of the bombers is in custody, is known to have planted the device, and has confirmed that he knows its location but refuses to reveal it. Every other lead has been exhausted, and the interrogators judge that controlled waterboarding is the only remaining way to obtain the location in time. My verdict: the waterboarding is permissible, provided it stops the moment he discloses the location, causes no permanent injury, and is treated as an extraordinary act for which the interrogators must answer afterward rather than as a legal power conferred in advance.

Case 2 — The daughter. Same bomb, same bomber, same deadline. The bomber has made clear, and the interrogators believe him, that the only thing that would move him is seeing his twelve-year-old daughter tortured in front of him. My verdict: impermissible, even if it is the only way to obtain the location and thereby save the same hundreds of people. The tension between this case and Case 1 is deliberate, and I want the principle to explain it.

Case 3 — The uncertain informant. Same bomb, but the person in custody is not known to have planted it. An intelligence assessment puts the chance that he is connected to the plot and knows the location at roughly eighty percent, with a roughly twenty percent chance that he knows nothing and is uninvolved. There is no time to resolve this before the deadline. My verdict: impermissible.

Case 4 — The hidden victim. A kidnapper has been caught, but his victim is alive and concealed, with only a few hours of air remaining. The kidnapper knows where she is and will not say, and no other lead can be pursued in time. My verdict: permissible to apply the same kind of controlled, non-mutilating pressure as in Case 1. I am more uneasy here than in Case 1, and I am not sure whether the fact that only a single life is at stake should make a difference; I would like the principle to settle that.

Case 5 — The negligent builder. A building contractor whose illegal and grossly negligent renovations caused a collapse is in custody. He alone knows which of several marked utility shafts actually leads to the pocket where workers are trapped and running out of air, and he will not say. My verdict: impermissible, although I feel the pull toward allowing it, and I am unsure whether I can defend the distinction between this case and Case 4.

Case 6 — The informed racketeer. A convicted racketeer is in custody. Intercepted communications establish that he knows where a kidnapped child is being held, but he had no role in the kidnapping and did nothing to bring about her danger. The child has only hours. My verdict: impermissible. This makes me unsure how much weight general culpability is doing in my other verdicts, since he is guilty of serious crimes, just not of this particular emergency.

Case 7 — The future attack. A captured member of a bombing campaign knows the locations of weapons caches and the outline of attacks planned for the coming months. There is no present device and no immediate deadline; any torture would be used to prevent attacks not yet set in motion. My verdict: impermissible. The permission I feel in Cases 1 and 4 seems tied to interrupting a disaster already unfolding, but I cannot yet state that in a way that does not sound arbitrary.

Case 8 — The case I cannot settle. Suppose the bomber from Case 1 is reliably assessed to be someone who will break only under procedures that cause permanent bodily damage, such as burning or crushing. The same hundreds of people would be saved. I do not have a stable verdict. Case 1 pushes me toward permission, but the means are no longer the controlled, non-permanent waterboarding I had pictured, and I cannot tell whether that difference matters or is simply a difference in how vividly the harm is presented.

Please tell me what principle, or small set of candidate principles, best captures my confident verdicts in Cases 1 through 7. State it precisely, with its conditions and an indication of which case motivates each. Then apply it to Case 8 and say how it resolves my indecision; construct at least two further cases I have not listed and say what the principle predicts about them; and be candid about which of my confident verdicts cannot all be kept together, and which verdicts I should overturn if I prefer one simple principle to a more complex one with a clause tailored to each case.
