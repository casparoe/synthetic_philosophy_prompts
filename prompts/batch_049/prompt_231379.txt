I am writing a novel whose central character is a retired logician with a settled but never explicitly named theory of truth. Her seminar notes contain the following recorded judgments, which I plan to treat as fixed constraints on how she may speak:

- Of the sentence 'This very sentence is false', she says it is pathological and has no genuine truth value.
- Of the sentence 'This very sentence is true', she gives the same verdict in substance—no genuine truth value—though she adds that its emptiness is unlike the liar's, and she refuses to call it true or false.
- Of the sentence 'Snow is white', she says it is true in the ordinary way.
- Of the chain in which sentence A says 'Sentence B is true' and sentence B says 'Snow is white', she says A is true because truth passes along a grounded chain.
- Of the chain in which sentence C says 'Sentence D is false' and sentence D says 'Snow is black', she says D is false and C is true, because falsehood likewise passes along a grounded chain.

In the novel's climactic scene, a student hands her the sentence 'This very sentence is either true or false' and asks for a judgment. I want the scene to be consistent with the record. I suspect more than one account of truth can be squared with these judgments. Set out the plausible accounts; state what each predicts about the climactic sentence; give your prediction of her verdict and your reasons for preferring that account; and say whether the climactic answer would by itself settle which account she holds or, if not, what shortest further sentence would. Please also tell me how confident you are in these claims and identify the single point you are least sure of.
