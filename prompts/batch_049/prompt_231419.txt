I'm a PhD student in ancient history, finishing a dissertation chapter on how the fifth-century Athenian assembly talked itself into and out of decisions, with the Mytilenean debate as my central case study. In our department's political-theory reading group we discussed an unpublished book manuscript on empire and punishment, and its treatment of Cleon left me unsettled without my being able to say exactly why. The author's summary of Cleon's speech, as I understand it, runs like this: after the assembly wakes up regretting its vote to kill the adult men of Mytilene and enslave everyone else, Cleon rises to insist that the first vote stand, because his real target is the temperament of democracy itself — an assembly that governs unwilling subjects cannot afford to give way to the three emotions most fatal to empire: pity, sentiment, and indulgence. On the author's reading, Cleon grants that these are humane responses, but answers that compassion is owed only to those who would return it, and the Mytileneans are permanent enemies. Cleon's conclusion, the author says, is a purely deterrent calculus: allies obey out of fear, not gratitude, so Athens must carry out the collective punishment as an object lesson, because any softening teaches the empire that revolt ends in negotiation rather than death. Finally, the author treats Cleon as Thucydides' mouthpiece and ends the chapter with the lesson that the debate shows there was never a humane form of empire on offer for Athens — pity was a failure of nerve, not an achievement.

The author anchors all of this in the following passage from Thucydides, History of the Peloponnesian War, Book 3, section 40, in Richard Crawley's translation as printed on Perseus:

"I therefore now as before persist against your reversing your first decision, or giving way to the three failings most fatal to empire—pity, sentiment, and indulgence. Compassion is due to those who can reciprocate the feeling, not to those who will never pity us in return, but are our natural and necessary foes: the orators who charm us with sentiment may find other less important arenas for their talents, in the place of one where the city pays a heavy penalty for a momentary pleasure, themselves receiving fine acknowledgments for their fine phrases; while indulgence should be shown towards those who will be our friends in future, instead of towards men who will remain just what they were, and as much our enemies as before. To sum up shortly, I say that if you follow my advice you will do what is just towards the Mitylenians, and at the same time expedient; while by a different decision you will not oblige them so much as pass sentence upon yourselves. For if they were right in rebelling, you must be wrong in ruling. However, if, right or wrong, you determine to rule, you must carry out your principle and punish the Mitylenians as your interest requires; or else you must give up your empire and cultivate honesty without danger."

I can read the Greek, and I noticed that Crawley's "sentiment" stands for hēdonē logōn, the pleasure taken in speeches, which is not really a sentiment, and the surrounding lines number the three items among failings rather than emotions. My inclination is that the author has read Cleon through the translation and through a preexisting "Thucydides the realist" template, and has flattened something — but I do not trust my own ear for rhetoric enough to be sure, and I do not want to dismiss a competent scholar just because the reading is unflattering. I would be grateful for an expert audit of the kind I could act on when I take up this manuscript in my chapter. As an expert on Thucydides and fifth-century political rhetoric, could you first brainstorm the distinct claims Cleon is actually making in the passage, then work through the author's summary claim by claim and separate what is accurate, what is a plausible simplification, and what a careful reader of the speech would reject? Please be precise about how much of the author's concluding inference — that Thucydides endorses Cleon and that a humane empire was never an option — survives the corrections, and where the passage does and does not license such a conclusion.
