Next week I will present a seminar paper defending a distributional version of the argument from divine hiddenness. My thesis is that the strongest form of the argument is not the familiar claim that the bare existence of nonresistant nonbelievers is incompatible with a perfectly loving God. Instead, I argue that if a perfectly loving God existed, nonresistant unbelief would be at most rare, unstable across a single life, and spread thinly and evenly across cultures and biographies. In the actual world, nonresistant unbelief is common, often persists for entire lifetimes, and is strongly clustered by time, place, and upbringing. I contend that this distributional premise is more resistant to the standard replies than the bare existential premise, because free-will and soul-making defenses can explain local or temporary resistance far more plausibly than they can explain the global pattern of unbelief.

The audience is my department's philosophy of religion research seminar, roughly a dozen analytic philosophers, mostly theists. The members most likely to speak are: two skeptical theists who will insist that our cognitive limitations leave us unable to infer from any distribution of unbelief what a loving God would or would not permit; a Molinist who will argue that God actualizes the feasible world with the best overall balance of freely given love and that the distribution of unbelief reflects counterfactuals of creaturely freedom outside God's control; an open theist who denies that God could have known in advance who would resist and will argue that this pushes responsibility for unbelief onto creatures; the chair, an agnostic epistemologist sympathetic to the argument who nevertheless believes an argument this ambitious must be overreaching somewhere and who will press on inductive risk and the strength of the empirical premise; and a graduate student working on divine love who will resist any premise implying that a loving God must secure explicit, conscious relationship.
