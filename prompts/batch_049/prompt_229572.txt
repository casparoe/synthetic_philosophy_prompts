I help run a small family foundation, mostly grants in the tens of thousands. We get maybe ten applications a month, and lately alot of them — maybe a third — are long shots: the person admits the odds are tiny but the claimed payoff is enormous. A group wants $30k to print and mail leaflets they say have something like a 1-in-10,000 chance of preventing an AI catastrophe, a local engineer says there's a 1-in-50,000 chance his prototype pulls CO2 out of the air at a tenth of the current cost, and a voter-turnout campaign wants $15k to reach a few hundred people in one district. I read about Pascal's mugging somewhere and I have this uneasy feeling that some of these are exactly that: the expected value math says once the payoff is big enough you should hand over the money no matter how small the chance, which seems like a rule that would have me fund every crank who can do arithmetic. But I also don't want to become the foundation that only funds safe, boring stuff and never takes a shot.

Can you write me a decision procedure for these applications that I can actually run in under an hour, with no more math than arithmetic, and that my board would treat as respectable? It should be questions I ask in order, with what counts as a passing or failing answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it stops and says this one is a values call a human has to make. Then run the procedure on the three applications above, and make sure at least one of them gets an answer I won't like but the procedure says I should follow. Also tell me where the procedure will misfire or where following it would be a mistake. And if you can, give me a few sentences I can put in a rejection letter that don't just tell them their odds are too low in a way that starts an argument.
