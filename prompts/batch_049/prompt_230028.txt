I am a legal officer at a truth commission in a country emerging from a thirty-year armed conflict. Our statute uses the standard amnesty-for-truth test: an applicant is eligible only if the offense was associated with a political objective, the applicant has made full and truthful disclosure of the relevant facts, and the act is not grossly disproportionate to the political objective pursued.

Our precedents:

- B, a platoon commander, ordered an airstrike on a farmhouse where an insurgent leader was believed to be hiding; seven civilians in the house were killed. B fully disclosed the operation and named the politician who authorized it. Amnesty granted: the objective was political, disclosure was complete, and the civilian deaths, though grave, were collateral to a significant military target and not grossly disproportionate.
- M, a soldier, shot a shopkeeper during a search operation. He later gave a complete account of the killing, but the panel found that it arose from a private debt and that M had used the operation as cover. Amnesty denied: however complete the disclosure, there was no political objective.
- R, a nurse, treated wounded fighters from both factions and did not report them; she disclosed this fully. Amnesty granted: the acts were associated with the conflict, disclosure was complete, and the harm was minimal.
- D, a conscript, participated in the killing of thirty-two civilians, including several children, after his unit was ambushed. He fully disclosed, proved that the killings were ordered, and argued that he feared execution if he refused. Amnesty denied: even accepting a political objective and full disclosure, the act was grossly disproportionate, and duress does not cure that bar.
- T, a junior intelligence officer, participated in the beating of a captured suspected informer during an interrogation supervised by his commanding officer; the informer sustained a broken rib and recovered. T fully disclosed. Amnesty granted: the objective was political, disclosure was complete, and the beating, though violent, fell short of gross disproportionality.

The case before us now: K, another junior intelligence officer, participated in the beating of a captured suspected informer during an interrogation; the informer sustained a broken jaw and lost two teeth, and recovered. K has fully disclosed the beatings and the orders behind them. The complication is that the informer had, years before the conflict, married K's former partner, and K was once recorded saying he would "settle the score." K insists the private grievance had nothing to do with his conduct and that he would have beaten any detainee his commander told him to break down.

The panel is genuinely split. If we deny, we risk abandoning the rule that political acts with full disclosure earn amnesty; if we grant, we may be licensing private vengeance under the cover of the conflict.

I need your recommendation for our deliberative memo: how should we decide K's application if we aim to be consistent with our own precedents? In particular, identify the candidate principles that fit the five precedents and diverge on K; say which precedent is the closest analog under each principle; give your best guess as our decision and the reasons for preferring one principle over the other; and state what further case, or further fact about K, would most cleanly determine which principle we are committed to. In your response, lead with your bottom line—your recommended decision on K—in the first sentence, then give the reasoning. Do not restate the question or give any preamble.
