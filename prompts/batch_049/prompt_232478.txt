I teach an upper-level undergraduate ethics seminar and am writing the final examination. I want one essay question on Taurek's challenge to the standard view that, when you can rescue either one stranger or five strangers and all are equally easy to reach, you ought to rescue the five. The question should be answerable in 75 minutes, aim at about 900 words, and be worth 25 marks. Neither the question nor the model answer should require citations, page numbers, or a reading list.

The question must not be a survey of Taurek's paper. It should force students to do three things. First, reconstruct Taurek's argument with enough precision to locate its load-bearing step: the refusal to let several equal individual claims combine into a stronger collective claim, together with the thought that the deaths of five people do not aggregate into a single loss borne by any one subject. Second, identify exactly which step a defender of rescuing the five should reject, and formulate that objection in its strongest form. The objection should engage one or more of these pressure points rather than simply insisting that five deaths are worse than one: a pairwise comparison of the one against each of the five may pass every individual test while the choice between whole outcomes still seems lopsided; a policy of flipping a coin in each such case would, across many repetitions, knowingly leave far more people dead than a policy of always saving the five; and each of the five can complain that his life mattered no less than the one's even if no one of their complaints individually outweighs the one's. Third, assess whether a middle position is stable — for example, a weighted lottery that gives the five a larger but not decisive chance, or a contractualist test framed in terms of what each affected individual could reasonably reject when compared with each of the others.

Please write the question, a model answer of about 900 words, and a marking scheme that separates an A from a B by naming the specific moves each must contain. The marking scheme should say that an A answer must correctly identify the anti-aggregation premise and show how the other two tasks turn on it, engage at least one of the three pressure points, and take a clear argued position on the stability of the middle position. A B answer will typically reconstruct the fairness intuition accurately but then fall back on asserting that five lives are worse than one without locating the premise being rejected. Each wrong-turn description in the marking scheme should track a real student error, not a straw man.
