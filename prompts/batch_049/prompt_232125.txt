I'm working on a magazine piece about the handicap principle — the idea that animal signals are honest because they're costly, so only high-quality individuals can afford them (peacocks tails, gazelle stotting, all that). For the piece I need a thought experiment built to order. Construct an everyday human scenario where a signal separates high-quality from low-quality people, but the signal costs the same for both types. The separation should come from the fact that being correctly classified as high quality has more value for the high types than being seen as high quality has for the low types — in other words, the two types differ in benefits, not costs. Constraints: ordinary setting (a job interview, a negotiation, a first date, whatever), no money-burning, no peacocks or gazelles or other animal examples, no life-or-death stakes, and the scenario itself should fit in one paragraph. The case can't just stipulate that low types don't bother; the payoff numbers have to generate that. After the scenario, spell out the payoff matrix for both types, show that sending the signal is a best response for high types and not for low types, and confirm its a separating equilibrium. Then tell me which structural feature is doing the real work — that is, why the usual handicap story about differential costs isn't necessary here — and how a hardline handicap theorist would respond (would they say this is still a handicap in disguise because a difference in benefits is just a difference in opportunity costs?). Finally, if I removed that structural feature but kept the equal cost, would the case collapse back into a pooling equilibrium where nobody can tell the types apart?
