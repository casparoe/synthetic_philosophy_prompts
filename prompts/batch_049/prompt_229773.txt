In ordinary conversation, someone who asserts a claim she privately disbelieves is criticized for speaking insincerely, even if the claim turns out to be true; someone who asserts a claim that turns out false is criticized for getting it wrong, even if she believed it. Wikipedia's editing policy appears to run on neither standard. Editors are told not to insert material merely because they are sure of it; it must be verifiable in a reliable published source. An editor may be expected to present a reliably sourced claim she privately believes is false (as can happen when neutrally covering a contested topic), and unsourced material may be removed even if it is true and the editor who added it knows it from direct experience.

Reconstruct the implicit view of assertion behind this policy, if it has one. Does it presuppose a truth norm, a belief norm, a knowledge norm, or some distinct source- or provenance-based norm? In answering, distinguish what the policy strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, consider whether Wikipedia edits should even be modeled as assertions of the underlying claims rather than as reports about what sources say, and explain what would have to change if the encyclopedia were run instead on the rule that a claim may be included only if the editor knows it.
