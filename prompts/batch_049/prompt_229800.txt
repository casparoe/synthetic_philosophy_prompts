An article I read gives the following summary of the Molyneux problem as it appears in Locke's An Essay Concerning Human Understanding, Book 2, Chapter 9, Section 8. The article paraphrases Locke's report this way: Locke says that William Molyneux asked him to consider a man born blind, now an adult, who has learned by touch to tell apart a sphere and a cube of the same metal and nearly the same size. If the man were made to see, and the sphere and cube were placed before him on a table, would he, before touching them, be able to say which was the sphere and which the cube? According to the article, Molyneux answers no, on the grounds that the tactile idea he has of a sphere and the visual idea he would have of a sphere are two wholly distinct ideas with no intrinsic connection, so the man would have to learn from experience which visual appearance goes with which felt shape. The article then says that Locke endorses this answer, that it shows vision and touch are fully independent senses, and that the case demonstrates that our concept of three-dimensional shape cannot be acquired from vision alone.

How fair is this as a reading of Locke's passage? I'm especially suspicious of the phrase "fully independent" and of the last sentence: does Molyneux's question, as Locke reports it, actually support a claim about where our concept of shape comes from, or is it a narrower point about recognition across modalities? I'd like an accuracy audit: which parts of the article's summary are faithful to the passage, which are subtle distortions, and whether the conclusion the article draws would survive a more careful statement of the problem.
