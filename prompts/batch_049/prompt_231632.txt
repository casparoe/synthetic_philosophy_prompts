I am a software engineer at a mid-sized company whose consumer app has a large user base, and I have no formal background in philosophy. Several times a quarter I get drawn into product decisions with an ethical dimension: engagement features that may become compulsive, data collection and sharing, what we build for which customers, and how our analytics might be used downstream. These decisions are made quickly, by a product team with no ethical expertise, and my written recommendation carries weight with the product director who signs off. I want a reusable decision procedure, grounded in the idea of moral uncertainty, that I can run in an afternoon before I write that recommendation.

I only half understand the underlying theory, so let me set out what I take it to say. When someone is genuinely unsure which moral view is right, it is a mistake to pick whichever view seems most likely and then judge the decision only by that view. Instead one should treat the plausible moral views as if each might be the correct one, weight them by how much confidence one has in them, and choose the option that does best in expectation across all of them; a philosopher friend calls this maximising expected choiceworthiness. I have also been warned that the approach has known snags: different moral theories value different things in different units, so combining them can be like adding sums in different currencies without exchange rates; a tiny probability of an enormous outcome can dominate the whole calculation if taken literally; and the list of possible moral views is open-ended, so any calculation ignores an unknown number of candidates. I am not asking for a procedure that dissolves those snags. I am asking for one that acknowledges them and tells me what to do when they bite.

Please write the procedure for me: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each question, where the procedure stops with a no, where it hands the decision to a human being because the question is one of values rather than facts, and which steps cannot be made mechanical without falsifying the underlying idea. Then run the procedure on three cases. The first case is a proposal to add a streak and time-limited reward system to our app; some users say it helps them sustain habits they want, while a substantial minority report feeling unable to stop, and our data shows the feature would substantially raise daily active use, which is the metric the product team is judged on. The second case is a proposal to sell de-identified location histories to a data broker for significant recurring revenue; the de-identification is good but not bulletproof, and the broker's clients include employers and insurers. The third case is a request from a large corporate customer to adapt our internal productivity-analytics tool into a product that scores individual employees' activity for managers to use in performance reviews and redundancy decisions; the revenue would fund our team for a year. Run the procedure honestly on all three, and when the answer is one I will not like on commercial grounds, say so plainly rather than softening it. Finally, say what the procedure cannot do, and which sorts of decisions it will misclassify or leave undecided.

Please write the whole answer in continuous prose, the way one would write an email or a letter: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
