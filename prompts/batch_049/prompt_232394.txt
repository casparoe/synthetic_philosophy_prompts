I've come to hold a version of the scale argument about insect welfare. On my view, the sheer number of insects—many orders of magnitude beyond the number of humans and other vertebrates—means that even a low probability that an individual insect has morally relevant sentience suffices for insect welfare to swamp human and vertebrate welfare in expected-value terms. That yields a practical conclusion: a default moral or philanthropic focus on humans and large animals is hard to defend unless one can show positively that insects are not sentient; mere absence of evidence is not enough.

What would count against this position? I want the evidence and arguments that should move a holder of it, sorted by how decisive each would be. I already know the familiar line that insect behavior is largely reflexive; I want the deeper defeaters, such as findings about insect nervous systems or behavior that should lower the probability of sentience, theoretical claims about which capacities consciousness requires, challenges to the way the argument aggregates across a vast number of uncertain minds, and the worry that the position could be insulated against any refutation. Also assess whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that is a fatal defect or an acceptable feature of a precautionary moral stance. Finally, say how confident you are in what you claim and single out the point you are least sure of.
