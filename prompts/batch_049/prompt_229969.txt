I'm on the allocations committee of a small family foundation that funds education programs. Our charter commits us to maximizing total benefit per grant dollar, and we usually rank proposals by expected benefit per dollar and go down the list. This cycle we have about $400,000, and there are three bids. Two are standard early-literacy programs, each of which would reach roughly 2,000 kids with modest but well-evidenced gains. The third is a pilot for an intensive one-on-one 'cognitive acceleration' program built around a single child with a genuinely unusual learning profile: the more specialist time and materials you pour in, the more outsized the gains become, and by the numbers the marginal benefit per dollar stays above the literacy programs as far out as we can project—so putting the entire $400,000 into this one child would produce more total benefit than the other two programs combined, possibly several times over. A couple of committee members say the arithmetic leaves us no choice and want to fund only that one child. I find that conclusion a little obscene, but I cant articulate why it isn't just me flinching at large numbers.

Could you write the brief memo I can circulate before Thursday? It needs to land on a concrete allocation—all to one, a split, a capped amount, whatever you can defend—and it should present the strongest version of the maximize-total-benefit position before explaining why you'd stop short of it (or not). The committee is quantitative, so numberish reasoning will land better than appeals to vague fairness, but the document still has to tell us what to actually do.
