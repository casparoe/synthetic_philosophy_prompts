I'm a staff software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I've been asked to write an internal guide for how our engineering organization should make a specific, recurring kind of decision: whether to stay on a technology we are already using, or pay to move to something else. We make this call under pressure — for languages, frameworks, databases, build and CI systems, internal services, data formats, vendors — and right now the two available voices are "that's just the sunk cost fallacy, be rational and switch" and "we're too deep in to change." I've picked up terms like path dependence, lock-in, increasing returns, positive feedback, and contingency from blog posts, but I don't know which of those are rigorous distinctions or how to use them.

Please act as a professional philosopher and economist of path dependence: someone who can distinguish, for instance, a true self-reinforcing process from a one-time switching cost, and a factual claim about how an outcome came to be from a normative claim about whether we should now escape it. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming the main distinctions in the field and mapping each onto concrete engineering resources — cumulative team familiarity, tooling written against the old system, runbooks and institutional memory, the hiring market, deprecation risk, contract lock-in, integration surfaces, data migration. Flag the distinctions that have no real engineering counterpart and should be left out, and flag the engineering considerations that are doing the theorist's work under another name.

The deliverable is a reusable decision procedure, not a one-off recommendation. A senior engineer with no philosophy training should be able to run it in under an hour, so it cannot lean on undefined jargon: any term like increasing returns must be glossed in plain language or dropped. I want it to include, in writing: the questions to ask in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; the precise points where the procedure says this consideration is not path dependence and should be handed back to ordinary cost-benefit analysis; the points where it says this is genuinely self-reinforcing and should weigh accordingly; and the steps that are irreducible value judgments — about risk, optionality, and which downstream costs count — that no procedure can make for us. The procedure should enforce the distinction I half-understand from the theory: a sunk cost is gone, while path dependence is about whether the past has changed the future payoff landscape; don't let the guide collapse back into "we spent a lot, so we should keep going."

Then apply the procedure to three cases, step by step, showing what it would actually output:

1. A ten-year-old monolith that still works and that most senior engineers know well, but whose tooling, hiring market, and library ecosystem have moved toward smaller services. The main argument for staying is the accumulated familiarity and the cost of a year-long migration.

2. A homegrown wrapper around our message queue that is hooked into dozens of services, with on-call runbooks and failure modes written around its quirks. A managed queue service would lower per-year operating cost, but the wrapper's integrations and institutional knowledge would have to be rebuilt.

3. After a merger, we must pick one of two identity and access systems. One is newer and operationally simpler; the other is older but has many more users, integrations, and access policies already attached to it, so the switching burden falls mostly on the larger legacy organization.

Make at least one of these come out in a way our engineers will not like — for example, the procedure recommends staying with something we were planning to replace, or replacing something people are proud of — to demonstrate that the procedure can override instinct.

Finish with a short section on what the procedure cannot do: where the theory describes a mechanism but gives no verdict about whether we should stay, and how a team should act differently when the output is "you are locked in, and that is acceptable" versus "you are locked in, and that is costly." I am not looking for a bibliography; I need the concepts made usable without distorting them.
