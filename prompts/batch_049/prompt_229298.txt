I've been trying to work out what I actually think about punishment by going on gut verdicts, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, is behind them. A corporate officer who embezzled two million dollars from a pension fund should get a longer sentence than a teenager who shoplifted food, even if the embezzlement caused no one to miss a payment and the shoplifting helped sink an already-struggling corner store — I'm not happy with that verdict but I'd keep it. A failed murder attempt deserves less than a murder, even though the intention was the same. I would never punish someone I was sure was innocent just to stop a riot, not even if it clearly saved lives. But I would keep a repeat violent offender locked up beyond what he deserves if that's what it takes to protect people. Duress gets a big reduction from me, while a terrible childhood gets almost none, and I can't say exactly why. A genuinely remorseful fraudster who paid everything back could get probation, but a 20-year-old who killed a family while driving drunk should be punished even if he'll never offend again. Is there one principle that captures these, or close? Which of my verdicts does it force me to give up? And what would it say about a mercy killing where the victim had asked for it and the family is asking the prosecutor to drop the charges — the case I keep going back and forth on? I can't tell whether these verdicts make me a retributivist, a consequentialist, or neither.
