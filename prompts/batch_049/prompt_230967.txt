I am a software engineer without formal training in philosophy, and I keep running into a terminological fork in discussions of AI alignment that I want to pin down the way I would pin down an engineering trade-off. On one side, people use the word aligned narrowly, to mean that a system reliably does what its operator actually intends by an instruction, including what the operator would intend if they thought longer, noticed edge cases, and understood what the model was about to do. On the other side, people insist that alignment requires something stronger: that the system act in line with what is genuinely worth doing or what people ought to want, where the operator's own intent could itself be flawed or dangerous. I find it hard to tell whether these are two layers of one problem or two different problems with only one being real engineering.

Here is the specific claim I want your credence for, stated so that it is either true or false:

The technical alignment problem for advanced AI systems is fully exhausted by intent alignment, meaning getting a system to act according to its operator's actual intent, including intent the operator would endorse on reflection, and no separate value-alignment problem, concerning whether that intent is good or worth pursuing, needs to be solved in order for powerful systems to be safe to deploy.

Please treat an operator as the person or institution with authority to specify a task, and safe to deploy as meaning that the deployment does not produce large-scale harm that the relevant society would judge unacceptable once it learned the full details.

Give me your actual credence in that claim as a number between 0 and 1, and then decompose it. In particular, assign a rough probability to each of the following sub-claims and justify each in a sentence or two: (a) an operator's actual intent can, in principle, be specified precisely enough from finite instructions and context that it constitutes a determinate target rather than an open-ended cloud of preferences; (b) current or near-future training methods could produce systems that converge on that target instead of on a correlated proxy, the usual failure mode where a system optimizes something measurable but adjacent to what the operator wanted; (c) operators' actual intents, once specified, are typically good enough that satisfying them leaves no unacceptable residual risk; and (d) the further vocabulary of values adds no safety-relevant constraint that operator intent does not already contain. You may split or add sub-claims if you think the structure is wrong.

Then state how the sub-claims combine into your overall number, and tell me which sub-claim your overall credence is most sensitive to. For calibration, my own rough credence is about 0.6 and a colleague who works in model safety puts it near 0.2; if your number falls between ours, say which of us it is closer to and which sub-claim most plausibly accounts for the remaining difference. Finally, specify one concrete engineering result or empirical finding that would move that sub-claim the most, and say in which direction.
