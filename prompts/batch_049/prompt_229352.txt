I'm an undergraduate in a philosophy of artificial intelligence seminar. In our discussion section this week, two classmates got into an argument over how narrowly the alignment problem should be defined, and neither would concede. I was asked to referee and couldn't. Could you adjudicate their exchange move by move?

Here is the exchange as I remember it.

(1) Daniel: "The alignment problem in its narrow sense is the problem of getting an AI system to do what its operator actually intends, especially when the instruction is underspecified or the system optimizes a proxy that misses the point. That is the problem worth solving. Value alignment—making the system share or internalize human values—is a different project, and conflating the two only muddies the narrow one."

(2) Anika: "That can't be right. What the operator intends is not a determinate fact just sitting there waiting to be detected; it is only determinate against a background of the operator's values. Without that background, the instruction is consistent with too many readings. So the narrow problem cannot be solved without already doing value alignment."

(3) Daniel: "I can intend for someone to bring me a coffee without intending any particular theory of my values. A competent assistant infers that I want a coffee, not that it should also repaint my kitchen. You do not need a full model of someone's values to satisfy an ordinary intention on a particular occasion."

(4) Anika: "The coffee case is easy, which rigs the example. The moment the system must act sensibly when the café is closed, or when following the instruction would frustrate what you actually care about, it has to infer what you would want given your values. That is the value story, just under another name."

(5) Daniel: "There is still a difference between using values as evidence about what a person wants and aligning the system to values as its objective. Value alignment means the system adopts or optimizes for human values on its own. Intent alignment means the system defers to a particular human's particular instruction, corrigibly—ready to be corrected—even when that human is wrong. A system could be perfectly intent-aligned while knowing nothing general about human values."

(6) Anika: "But deference to a particular instruction still needs a stopping point for interpretation, and wherever you stop you have imported normative assumptions about which respects of the instruction matter and which complications can be ignored. A pure 'do what I mean' machine is a fiction: you are smuggling in a partial theory of the user's values while disclaiming it."

I could see that both had made genuine points, but I could not say who was right about which specific step, or what the two of them were both missing. Could you go through the six moves in order, rating each as clearly mistaken, mostly mistaken, mixed, mostly right, or clearly right, with a brief justification; then say who is more right overall; and add anything the exchange overlooks?

Please end with a single line beginning "Verdict:" that states in one sentence who is more right overall and on what crux the disagreement turns.
