The heterogeneity problem for hedonism begins from the observation that the episodes we call pleasures do not obviously share any single character: the sensory enjoyment of a warm bath, the intellectual satisfaction of solving a problem, the relief that follows an averted danger, and the malicious delight taken in a rival's setback can feel nothing alike. If hedonism is to remain the thesis that pleasure is the one thing good in itself, it owes an account of what makes all these instances of one kind. Two families of answer give very different accounts. On the felt-quality view, every pleasure shares one distinctive phenomenal quality, a common feel that is present in every instance however much the surrounding experience differs. On the attitudinal view, what unifies these episodes is not a common feel but a common stance: an episode counts as a pleasure when the subject bears a favorable, pro-attitude toward the object or state of affairs that the episode is about.

Give an example of a case in which these two views come apart—specifically, a case in which the attitudinal view classifies the episode as a pleasure while the felt-quality view would deny that it is one. Describe the case concretely, explain how each view reaches its verdict, and then assess which verdict better fits the case as it is actually experienced. In assessing the case, keep separate the question of whether the episode is a pleasure from the question of whether it is valuable, prudent, or worth seeking.
