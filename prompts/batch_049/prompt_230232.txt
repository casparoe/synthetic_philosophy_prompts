In a recent argument about whether platforms violate privacy when they infer sensitive facts—political views, sexual orientation, health conditions—from things a person voluntarily posted, one participant dismissed the worry by invoking the familiar formulation that privacy is control over information about oneself. Since the person had voluntarily shared the underlying posts, the reasoning went, they controlled the information, and therefore nothing done downstream with it could count as a privacy violation. But I have also seen privacy advocates invoke that same formulation to demand much more fine-grained user control over data collection and use. Both sides cannot be extracting the same thesis from the sentence, so I suspect it is hiding several distinct claims. Can you disambiguate the formulation for me? In particular, separate the precise readings it could carry: readings that differ about the scope of the information at issue (the raw post versus the inferred fact), about the sort of control required (control over access, over downstream use, over correction, or over inference itself), and about whether the formulation is meant to define privacy, to state its sole value, or merely to name one component among several. Then assess each reading: which are platitudes, which are substantive and defensible, which are false or overstatements, and which actually support the conclusion that inferring sensitive facts from voluntarily shared posts is no privacy violation.
