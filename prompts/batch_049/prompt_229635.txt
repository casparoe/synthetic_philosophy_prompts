I am a PhD student working on the methodology of moral thought experiments, and I need a matched pair of cases for a paper on how presentational choices can carry persuasive weight independently of the facts of a case. Please construct two short thought experiments about whether an omission that allows a preventable death is on a moral par with an act that causes the same death. The two cases must be identical in the persons involved, the options available, the agent's knowledge, the costs, the probabilities, and the outcomes; they may differ only in presentation — the order of information, the level of detail, what is foregrounded, and how the individuals are described. Engineer Case 1 as a vivid intuition pump whose first-pass intuitive verdict is that the omission is exactly as wrong as the act, and Case 2 so that, with every other feature held fixed, the first-pass intuitive verdict is that the omission is clearly less wrong. Keep each case under 150 words, set it in an ordinary professional or clinical context rather than a trolley or shallow-pond scenario, and do not instruct the reader what to feel or stipulate that one description is more adequate than the other. Then show that the design works: identify the exact presentational mechanism that produces the shift; argue that this mechanism is normatively inert, so the two verdicts cannot both be correct; say what the pair collapses into if the mechanism is removed; give the strongest defense available to someone who insists the first, pumped verdict is a reliable moral datum; and finish with one principle stating when a presentation-driven shift in intuitive verdict should be allowed to affect moral judgment.
