Had a long argument over drinks with my friend last night about whether individual action on climate change is pointless. He pulled out Parfit's Harmless Torturers thing from Reasons and Persons — the chapter called Five Mistakes in Moral Mathematics. I'd never read it, so he was working from memory and gave me the gist: imagine a thousand torturers, each one turns a switch, and each turn increases a victim's pain by such a tiny amount that the victim cant perceive any single turn. But after all thousand turns, the victim is in agony. Parfit's point, as my friend told it, is that if you think an act has to make a perceptible difference to be wrong, then each individual torturer gets off the hook — yet something clearly went wrong overall. My friend thinks this demolishes the "my emissions dont matter" excuse.

He then built this argument:

1. In the torturer case, each individual torturer's turn makes no perceptible difference to the victim.
2. If an act makes no perceptible difference to anyone, it isn't wrong.
3. So each individual torturer acts permissibly.
4. But the combined effect of all the turns is that the victim suffers terribly, and the torturers acting together do something clearly wrong.
5. So (2) must be false: an act can be wrong even if it makes no perceptible difference on its own.
6. Therefore, my individual choice to fly or eat beef or drive to work is wrong, even though my contribution is imperceptible, because it's one switch-turn in a global Harmless Torturers scenario.

Here's my problem. This seems to prove too much. By this reasoning, *every* action that's part of any harmful aggregate is wrong — taking any car trip, using any electricity, buying anything that was shipped. And not just emissions: paying taxes to a government that does some bad things, buying from a company with bad labor practices, even voting for a party whose platform includes some bad policies. Everything I do is a turn of some switch somewhere. If I accept his argument, I'd have to conclude that almost every ordinary action in an industrial society is wrong, which sounds insane. But I also dont want to let the thousand torturers off the hook, because that also sounds insane.

So where exactly does this argument go wrong, if it does? Is the place to push the disanalogy between a thousand torturers with one specific victim and a clear threshold versus millions of emitters with probabilistic, dispersed harms — or is that just me dodging the conclusion? My friend thinks the argument is valid and I should just accept step (6) and live with the guilt, but I suspect something slid in around step (2) or (5). I'd rather keep the torturers responsible without condemning every ordinary act I do, but I want to know what I'm paying for that.
