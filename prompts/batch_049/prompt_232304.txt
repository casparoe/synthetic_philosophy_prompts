I'm a journalist working on a piece about why the rationality crowd keeps treating expected utility theory as the default picture of good decision-making. The line I keep hearing is that if your preferences violate the standard axioms—completeness, transitivity, independence, and the rest—you aren't just making a mistake; you're incoherent, because someone can turn you into a money pump or show your choices can't be represented as maximizing any single utility function. I've been half-convinced, but I don't want to just launder it into the article, so I'm stress-testing it. What would have to be true for the coherence story to be wrong, or at least a lot less decisive than its defenders claim? Give me the strongest defeaters, ranked from fatal to merely awkward: cases where people prefer a sure thing to a slightly higher expected value gamble and then flip when the same choice is reframed, ambiguity aversion, evidence that real people violate the axioms in systematic and arguably adaptive ways, formal results showing the axioms smuggle in assumptions an opponent can reject, money-pump arguments that only go through because they exploit how the options are described rather than revealing inconsistent underlying values. Which of these would a committed coherence theorist concede count against the view, and which would they dissmiss as changing the subject? And I'd like to know whether the position ends up too well insulated—whether every apparent violation gets reinterpreted as a change in the person's true preferences—and whether that insulation is itself a mark against the view. An honest map of where the standard line is actually exposed would help me figure out how much of the article should treat it as settled.
