I've come around to the view that Bertrand's paradox basically kills the principle of indifference as anything more than a heuristic. The setup is familiar: ask for the probability that a randomly chosen chord of a circle is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle, and you get 1/2, 1/3, or 1/4 depending on how you generate the chord — each one looking like a natural way to be "indifferent." I take the lesson to be general: "equally probable because we have no evidence favoring one outcome over another" is underdetermined until you've already picked a description of the outcome space, so the principle can't deliver a unique prior or settle anything on its own. What would count against that? I want the strongest actual or possible defeaters, sorted by how decisive they'd be. For each, tell me whether a defender of my view would have to concede it or would dismiss it, and whether it's fatal or merely awkward. In particular: is the right response to say the principle only works when it comes with a symmetry constraint — I gather Jaynes tried something like this with invariance under coordinate transformations, which is supposed to dissolve the paradox once you specify the relevant transformation group — rather than to abandon indifference entirely? Does that actually fix the underdetermination, or just relocate it to the choice of group? Are there cases where the principle with the right symmetry story gives answers that look right and that rival methods don't — that's the sort of thing I'd find most persuasive. And tell me honestly whether I've insulated the view so much that nothing could count against it, and if I have, whether that's a problem for a view of this kind. One more thing: keep what's settled about the paradox itself separate from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this — don't present a contested reading as if it's the consensus.
