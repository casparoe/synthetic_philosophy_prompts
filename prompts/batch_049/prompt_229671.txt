A grad student of mine built the following argument before a seminar and now we're both stuck on it, because the conclusion is monstrous and yet the steps feel airtight.

(1) If what matters morally is the total welfare of all sentient beings, then a landscape full of short-lived, high-mortality wild animals — think voles, insects, nestling birds — may well be net-negative in welfare terms: most lives are brief, end badly, and contain far more pain than pleasure. (2) If a wild habitat is net-negative in welfare terms, then destroying it (so that the animals who would have filled it never come into existence) would, other things equal, reduce suffering and be good. (3) By the same reasoning, we ought, other things equal, to prefer that such habitats not exist at all: not restoring them, not rewilding them, and in principle actively suppressing them. (4) But this is absurd. It cant be that the right environmental policy is to wish for empty fields and pavement, or that the person who drains a wetland has done something merciful. (5) So either the initial welfarist assumption is false, or something goes wrong in getting from 'this habitat is net-negative' to 'we should keep it from existing.'

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? I'd like to hang on to the idea that animal suffering matters whether or not humans caused it — that feels non-negotiable to me — but I'm open to giving up the move from 'net-negative' to 'ought to prevent.' Maybe there's a population-ethics principle I'm missing, or an asymmetry between causing new beings and preventing existing ecosystems. Number the steps and tell me which one you reject, and what it costs. Also: if the argument only fails because the empirical premise (1) is shaky, say so — but I'm more interested in what the argument teaches us if we grant the empirical premise for the sake of argument. And if I've built the argument on a confused notion of 'net welfare for a population that would never have existed,' call that out plainly instead of polishing it.
