I am a PhD student in political philosophy, working on the ethics of collective memory after political violence. For the past year I have convened an adversarial collaboration between two colleagues who have repeatedly argued past one another in print. The first defends the view that a political community has a standing duty to remember large-scale wrongdoing: not merely to preserve the record, but to keep the wrong present in shared public life, because this is owed to the dead and to future potential victims. This position stands broadly in the spirit of Avishai Margalit's work. The second defends the view that forgetting can be an ethical and political achievement rather than only a failure: sustained official commemoration can stoke grievance, rank victims against one another, and feed ethnic mobilisation, so in some cases official remembrance should be deliberately wound down. This position stands broadly in the spirit of David Rieff's work. I want the statement to capture the strongest version of each view, not a caricature.

Their discussions have repeatedly returned to one shared test case, which both accept as fair. A fictional post-conflict country, thirty-five years after a massacre of a national minority, is deciding whether to renew for another decade a state-mandated annual day of remembrance or to let it lapse while keeping the digitised archive and a small museum open. The survivor generation is now largely gone; a nationalist party has begun appropriating the ceremony; public support for renewal is broad but increasingly passive. Both colleagues agree that the facts must remain publicly accessible and that denialism is not the same as forgetting; they disagree sharply about what the state should now do.

Write the joint statement they might both sign. It should include: (1) a numbered Agreed Claims section, in wording neither would regard as slanted or vacuous; (2) a disagreements table whose rows state precisely where they part ways, each side's own position formulated in wording that side accepts, and any third formulation one side uses to characterise the other but which the other rejects noted against it; (3) a Cruxes section listing the sub-questions on which the deeper disagreement turns. I suspect at least three: whether the value of remembrance is intrinsic or merely instrumental; whether the duty, if there is one, attaches to individuals, to institutions, or to both; and whether remembrance obligations expire across generations. They have also been bedevilled by an ambiguity between forgetting as the destruction or denial of the record and forgetting as the deliberate discontinuation of official commemoration while the record remains available; show how much of their dispute that ambiguity drives; (4) for each crux, a concrete case, observation, or consideration both would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. End by distinguishing which parts of their dispute are substantive and which are merely verbal.
