I'm a retired spectroscopist — spent most of my life on noise reduction and calibration curves — and I've only been reading political philosophy for a couple years, so go easy on me if I mangle the jargon. My neighbor teaches international relations and we argue about military intervention constantly. He's frustratingly consistant, but I can't work out the rule he's actually applying, and I'd like to predict him on a new case before I bring it up.

His track record, as I can reconstruct it:

Kosovo in 1999: he says justified. No Security Council authorization, since Russia would have vetoed, but it was NATO-led, the ethnic cleansing was already underway, and there was no plausible economic motive — just stopping a massacre and a refugee flood.

Iraq in 2003: not justified. The WMD case collapsed, there was no ongoing mass atrocity at the time, the coalition was small, and the post-invasion planning was so bad you couldn't honestly call the war humanitarian even in hindsight.

Libya in 2011: this is where he gets interesting. He thinks the early civilian-protection part was justified — Benghazi was about to be attacked, and there was Security Council support and Arab League backing. But he says the operation then slid into regime change and the country was left worse off. So his verdict on the intervention as actually run is that it was not justified, even though the narrow no-fly zone at the start would have been on its own.

Now the new case I want to spring on him. Next year, in a coastal West African country, a ruler has already carried out attacks on one ethnic group and there is solid intelligence he's about to assault the main city his opponents control — call it eight hundred thousand people. The African Union requests outside air support for a no-fly zone and safe areas, and says the deal is off if it turns into regime change. The Security Council is deadlocked because one permanent member will veto anything. A coalition offers to run the no-fly zone and promises not to target the ruler. But a few coalition members hold mineral concessions in the country, which makes the promise shaky. Analysts put the odds of protecting the civilians at 70 percent, the odds of getting dragged into a wider war at 40 percent, and post-war stability at about 50/50.

If he's applying the same thing he applied to those three cases, what does he say about this one? Give me your best prediction, the candidate rules that each fit his record, and say which earlier case is actually doing the most work in your answer. Then give me one further case — real or invented — that would cleanly seperate those rules, because I'd rather know what's doing the work before I start the argument.
