Two members of my weekly reading group got into an argument last week which none of the rest of us could settle, and I have been puzzling over it since. The dispute began with an ordinary example: a pear left on a windowsill, green and hard on Monday, yellow and soft by Friday. One of them, Ruth, defended an endurantist account of persistence, on which the pear persists by being wholly present at every moment of its existence. The other, Daniel, defended a perdurantist account, on which the pear persists by having temporal parts and is a four-dimensional object spread through time. I have reconstructed the exchange as faithfully as I can from my notes.

Ruth: "The pear on Monday and the pear on Friday are numerically one and the same pear. On Monday it is green and hard; on Friday it is yellow and soft. It — the whole pear, not a green bit of pear and a yellow bit of pear — changes. Your talk of temporal parts turns an ordinary persisting thing into something more like an event or a process, as if the pear were made up of stages. That is a different picture, not a solution."

Daniel: "But you have not explained how the very same thing can be both green and yellow. If greenness and yellowness are properties of one and the same object, then the object has incompatible properties, and that cannot be right. My picture avoids the contradiction: the greenness belongs to the Monday part, and the yellowness belongs to the Friday part. Change just is the having of different properties by different temporal parts of one temporally extended thing."

Ruth: "That avoids the contradiction only by changing the subject. If the greenness belongs to one thing and the yellowness to another, then nothing at all has altered; you have merely placed a green thing and a yellow thing side by side in time. A road is narrow in one place and wide in another, but the road does not change from narrow to wide. You have turned change into a static variation along a fourth dimension. On your view, strictly speaking, the pear never ripens at all."

Daniel: "I accept the road analogy, but I think it works in my favour. Change is a kind of variation, and variation is a matter of different properties at different locations, whether spatial or temporal. What I do not see is how you can say the whole pear has both properties. You seem forced to say that greenness is not really a feature of the pear, but a relation to a time, perhaps that the pear is green-on-Monday and yellow-on-Friday. That is a radical revision: shape and colour stop being intrinsic features of things, and every temporary predication becomes a relation to a hidden parameter."

Ruth: "Not at all. If 'the pear is green' means 'the pear is green at a particular time', there is no need to treat greenness itself as a relation. And you have a mirror problem: what unites the Monday part and the Friday part into one pear, rather than a pear part plus, say, the Friday part of a different pear? You need a criterion of unity across time, and 'temporal parts' alone does not give you one."

Daniel: "Every account of persistence needs to say which series of stages counts as one continuing thing. I can point to the right kind of causal and qualitative continuity through time. You, by contrast, must say that the same wholly present object simply shows up again at each later moment, numerically identical although it has replaced much of its matter and changed its qualities. Saying 'it is the same pear' is not yet an account of what that sameness consists in."

Ruth: "That is precisely what cannot be cashed out in terms of parts. A wholly present thing's identity across time is a basic fact, not something constructed out of stages. If you treat it as something constructed, you make every object's beginning and end arbitrary: any old collection of stages could be summed into an 'object'. That seems to me a high price to pay to avoid saying the pear was green on Monday and yellow on Friday."

Daniel: "And saying it was green on Monday and yellow on Friday is a high price to pay to avoid temporal parts. You have to relativise the copula itself, or treat every ordinary property as time-indexed. I am simply taking seriously the idea that the pear occupies a region of spacetime, and that its greenness and yellowness are qualified by the locations at which they hold."

The exchange ended there, with neither willing to concede. I would be grateful for a point-by-point adjudication of the argument as it actually unfolded. For each move, I want to know who is right about which claim, where each of them overreaches or dodges, and what, if anything, both sides are missing. In particular, please say whether Daniel is right that Ruth must treat greenness as a relation to a time, and whether Ruth is right that Daniel has replaced genuine change with a merely static variation. But do not simply award a winner overall; go through the moves in order.
