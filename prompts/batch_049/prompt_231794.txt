I host a small philosophy podcast, and next week’s guest is a political philosopher who defends a neo-Arendtian account of lying in politics. Her standard claim is that political lying is not a moral accident or a failure of will: it grows out of the same capacity that makes political action possible in the first place, so you can’t root the lie out of politics without rooting out action itself. She always grounds this in a passage from Hannah Arendt’s “Lying in Politics: Reflections on the Pentagon Papers,” and I want my questions to press hard on that passage rather than let her paraphrase it. Here it is:

“A characteristic of human action is that it always begins something new, but this does not mean that it is ever permitted to start ab ovo, to create ex nihilo. In order to make room for one’s own action, something that was there before must be removed or destroyed, and things as they were before are changed. Such change would be impossible if we could not mentally remove ourselves from where we are physically located and imagine that things might as well be different from what they actually are. In other words, the ability to lie, the deliberate denial of factual truth, and the capacity to change facts, the ability to act, are interconnected; they owe their existence to the same source, imagination.”

I dont want eight to ten questions that merely ask her to walk through the passage. I want them to push where the argument is strained. At least one question should press on whether action really requires mentally removing ourselves from where we are and imagining things otherwise: is that a necessary condition, and what about unreflective or habitual action? At least one should press on the phrase “owe their existence to the same source”: is she claiming a conceptual connection, a causal-psychological one, or just an analogy, and does anything in the passage decide between those readings? At least one should push on the political payoff: if moral outrage alone won’t make the lie disappear, then what is left of the criticism of lying, and does her view slide into cynicism about politics? And at least one should pick at the idea that facts are fragile and need testimony to be secured: does that make truth a political achievement rather than something we discover? For each of these, include a follow-up keyed to the answer she’s most likely to give, so she can’t fall back on the line that she’s only making a genealogical point and not a normative one. And please write the whole thing as continuous prose: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way you’d write an email to a colleague.
