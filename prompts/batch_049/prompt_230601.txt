I'm a novelist, and I've been trying to understand a pattern in my own judgments. The question behind it is this: what, if anything, makes the extinction of human beings bad beyond the deaths and suffering it would involve — and, more particularly, why would the prospect of no one coming after us drain the point out of some things I do while leaving other things untouched? I am not looking for a reading list; I want the principle itself.

Here is the common backdrop for the cases below. I learn, with certainty, that the present generation is the last: every person now alive will live out a natural lifespan, no new human beings will ever be born, and the species will end within a century. No one I love dies prematurely; no one is killed by the event. The only change is that there is no human future beyond the people who already exist.

My verdicts under that scenario:

1. The contemporary novel. I am two drafts into a novel that I expect to publish within three years. Under the scenario, it would be read by living readers during the decades that remain. My verdict: I would finish it, and I would regard it as worth doing. Something elegiac attaches to it — the craft would be the last of its kind for those readers — but the activity loses little of its point.

2. The posterity novel. I have also been writing a novel whose readers, I have always believed, would arrive only after my death; it is the sort of book that is rediscovered later. Under the scenario, those readers can never arrive. I could, in principle, rewrite it to suit living readers, but that would feel like a different book; the work as conceived is addressed to a future audience. My verdict: I would stop writing it. The book's reason for being was tied to a future that is now absent, and I cannot shake the sense that continuing would be mere typing.

3. The private journal. I keep a journal to clarify my own thoughts. It has no reader, not even a posthumous one. Under the scenario, my verdict is unchanged: I would keep it, and perhaps depend on it more.

4. The one-copy birthday poem. I am writing a long poem to be read once aloud at my daughter's eightieth birthday, after which the copy will be destroyed. She is alive in the scenario and will hear it. My verdict: I would write it with the same devotion as before. It would have its full value.

5. The archive. I have spent years preserving drafts, notebooks, and letters that a future biographer might use after my death. Under the scenario, my verdict is that this preservation is now pointless. I would likely burn the archive, not in anger but because its purpose has evaporated.

6. The teaching workshop. I teach a workshop for young novelists. Under the scenario, I would still teach. But the emphasis would change: I would no longer teach craft as part of an evolving literary tradition that will continue, and I would instead teach only for the artistic flourishing of the students in front of me during the time left. The activity remains worth doing, but a dimension of it is gone.

Now the tension. My verdicts in (2), (4), and (6) do not sit comfortably together. In (2), I abandon a work of the imagination because its intended audience lives after me and will not exist; in (4), a work of the imagination addressed to one existing person retains its full value even though it will vanish after a single hearing; and in (6), I say teaching retains its value even though the tradition it used to serve will not survive. What, exactly, is the human future supplying in (2) that is not being supplied by the living listener in (4) or the living students in (6)? And why does the contemporary novel in (1) keep most of its point while the posterity novel loses all of it?

I want you to do four things. First, state the best principle — or a small set of candidate principles, if no single one will do — that captures as many of the six verdicts as possible. State it precisely enough to apply mechanically, and say which clause is motivated by which case. Second, use it to generate verdicts on these further cases: (a) a song I compose privately and never perform or record; (b) medical research whose only beneficiaries would be people born after everyone now alive is gone; (c) a letter to my future self, opened at eighty; (d) a novel I write as an exercise and delete immediately; (e) a time capsule intended to be opened two centuries from now under the scenario; (f) a game I play alone. Third, say which of my original six verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and whether it should force me to retract that verdict or to complicate the principle instead. Fourth, say what the resulting view implies about the badness of human extinction itself, as distinct from the deaths that occur on the way to it.

Please write your answer in American English.
