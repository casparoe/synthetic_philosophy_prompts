I am an undergraduate taking a course on the philosophy and measurement of well-being, and I have a term-time research assistant post in the policy research unit of my country's national statistics office. The unit screens around a dozen policy proposals a month that are justified partly or wholly by the claim that they will raise the country's World Happiness Report ranking. My supervisor wants a reusable screening procedure our analysts can apply to any such proposal before it is passed to senior officials for a decision; a wrong call can commit public money to a program that only moves a score, or block a cheap intervention that would genuinely improve wellbeing. Please design that procedure for me.

The procedure should decide how much evidential weight a predicted change in the ranking deserves in a policy case, and should end by assigning the proposal one of three outputs: treat the ranking gain as primary evidence of welfare improvement, treat it as secondary evidence only, or disregard it for welfare-justification purposes. It should distill what the course has taught me about the conceptual issues into operational form. In particular, it should help an analyst distinguish between the narrow life-evaluation score the report uses and happiness in a richer sense; between a policy that improves the underlying state the survey tries to measure and one that improves only the measurement or the country's relative position; and between a policy that changes wellbeing itself and one that changes the score through adaptive preferences, gaming, changes in who is surveyed, or shifts in the index's underlying components. It should also take account of the fact that the country rankings are modeled from a small set of predictors, so the analyst should check whether a proposal is claimed to affect those predictors directly, indirectly, or only through publicity.

Format the procedure as a fixed sequence of questions or tests an analyst can run in under an hour per proposal, with a checklist and a short plain-language explanation of each question. The analysts have no formal philosophy background, so define any technical terms by what they require the analyst to look for, not by theory. Mark the steps where professional judgment cannot be eliminated and say what that judgment is about. Include two or three worked examples of the procedure applied to stylized policy proposals: at least one in which the procedure tells the analyst that a probable ranking gain should not count as welfare evidence; at least one in which the procedure says the ranking evidence matters but only alongside other indicators; and, if possible, one in which the procedure flags a case it cannot settle. Do not make the procedure depend on the latest year's rankings or on country-specific data; where examples need numbers, use clearly marked illustrative figures. Do not include citations or a bibliography.
