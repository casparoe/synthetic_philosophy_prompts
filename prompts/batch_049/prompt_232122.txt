Working up a short essay for one of those little magazines — more of an op-ed really — about akrasia and the way economists talk about self-control, and I'd love an outside set of eyes before I send it to my editor. I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so the risk is I've built the whole thing on a misreading of a model I only half understand. Below are my notes. I'm not after a reading list or citations; I just want to know whether the argument itself is sound, where a philosopher or someone who actually does decision theory would push back, and which parts are too hand-wavy to keep.

Working title: Discounting the Narrator

- Open with the familiar scene: a character makes a plan at noon and unsays it at midnight. Novelists have a standard way of telling this story — as a clash between two selves: the one who wants to be the kind of person who doesn't text the ex, and the one who, phone already lit, does it. The drama comes from the fact that both selves share a body and are jostling for the right to narrate.

- Hyperbolic discounting tells a version of this too, and it says: don't reach for two selves, you only need one self whose valuation of future costs and benefits is sharply curved. Far from the reward, he prefers the long-run good. Near the reward, the present payoff swells and he caves. That's preference reversal. Since the preferences are time-inconsistent, you also get a clean explanation for commitment devices — the locked drawer, buying cigarettes one at a time. It's the noon self outsmarting the midnight self from behind the curve.

- But notice what the model does to the experience. It turns akrasia into a error in weighting, not a struggle. A curve doesn't argue with itself; it just changes shape as the point moves. The person in the grip of weakness of will doesn't merely notice that the cigarette now looms larger. He still thinks the plan was right while opening the pack. That simultaneity — acting against a judgment you still endorse, even while acting — is what makes akrasia feel like a civil war, and it's exactly what a single time-indexed utility function has no vocabulary for. At best you get successive endorsements: the plan at noon, the lapse at midnight, the rueful re-endorsement next morning. But the datum isn't succession; it's the overlap, the self that says "this is wrong" and does it anyway at the same moment.

- Revealed-preference readings make this worse. If each choice reveals what he wanted at that moment, weakness of will dissolves into bad forecasting: he didn't know his own curve, and "akrasia" becomes the name for being surprised by himself. That's a possible view, but it's a refusal to describe the phenomenon rather than an explanation of it. A character who's merely surprised by his midnight preferences isn't weak-willed; he's naive. Weakness of will requires the norm to survive the violation.

- The obvious comeback is that decision theory has multiple-selves models, planner–doer models, all that. I think that actually concedes my point. The moment you need two agents who bargain, you've stopped doing it with a discount curve and started doing it with characters. My claim is just that novelists and economists are narrating the same events and picking different protagonists. The economist's protagonist is the utility function and its shape; mine is the self that was supposed to be in charge and wasn't.

- Where I want to land: hyperbolic discounting is weakness of will seen from the outside, as a pattern of choices stretched across time; the novelistic account is weakness of will from the inside, as a relation between two claimants to the same life. Neither has the right to explain the other away. The essay would be a plea to keep them in the same room.

I keep going back and forth on wheter that last move is real or whether I'm just relabeling "explanation" and "experience" and calling it a reconciliation. Anyway, comments very welcome. In particular: (1) Have I got hyperbolic discounting roughly right, or am I caricaturing it? (2) Is the simultaneity point trivial — can a decision theorist just add a second preference or a guilt term and capture it, and if so does that kill the essay or just change its target? (3) Is the ending too soft? I'd happily be told the whole argument collapses and why. (4) Any paragraph you'd cut immediately for being novelist-y handwaving?
