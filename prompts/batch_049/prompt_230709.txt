I teach a third-year undergrad seminar on archives, memory, and power — about 16 students, one 90-minute session. I want an activity that makes them feel, not just discuss, the idea that archives arent neutral: what gets recorded and kept is a product of power, and the silences in the sources are made by concrete decisions rather than just lost to time. The class is methods-y, so something hands-on works.

Here's the frame I want it built around. I'll stage a short, slightly messy in-class event once — maybe a mock planning meeting, a small dispute, or a bit of informal testimony — that everyone in the room witnesses. Then small groups get assigned different custodians with different interests (a state archive, a community archive, a corporate legal team, a private scrapbooker, that sort of thing), and each group has to decide what to preserve, how to describe it, and what to deliberately discard or leave out. The punchline should be that two archives of the same event end up recording different events: a silence in one is a keeping in another. I want them to reverse-engineer, from the finding aids alone, what each custodian thought mattered, and to see that silence gets baked in not just at the moment of keeping but earlier — at the moment something gets registered as a fact worth recording at all.

So the activity has to produce two things: (1) actual records that the groups make or select in the moment, plus their finding aids or metadata, and (2) a debrief that maps what happened onto the conceptual material — provenance, appraisal, the official record versus the counter-archive, and the idea that an absence in the sources is itself evidence of a decision, not just a gap. Please give me rules, materials, a timeline for the 90 minutes, and an honest note on what will predictably go wrong the first time (for instance, groups might treat it as a game of hiding things, or the staged event might be too thin for anyone to remember it). And be straight about which part of the idea the activity can't reproduce — I'd guess its how silences compound over generations, since we only get one afternoon.
