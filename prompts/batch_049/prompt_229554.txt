I have been reporting for several months on why public arguments about evolution so often become arguments about what can and cannot be tested, and in the course of that work I have come to hold a neo-Popperian view that I now need to pressure-test. At one point, Karl Popper judged Darwinism to be less like a refutable empirical theory than like an orienting framework within which genuinely testable theories get formulated, but he later retracted that judgement, and I find the retraction persuasive when it is reinforced by a modern philosophical reading of fitness: fitness should be understood dispositionally, as a property an organism or trait has in virtue of its characteristics and its environment, not merely as a post hoc label for the ones that happened to reproduce. On the resulting view, natural selection does make empirical claims and does generate risky predictions, above all in population biology, even though it is not refutable by any single dramatic observation in the way a tightly controlled laboratory hypothesis might be.

Before I build a feature around this position, tell me what would count against it. I do not want only the familiar circularity charge — roughly, that the claim that the better-adapted survive is empty if better-adapted just means those that survive — but the defeaters a sophisticated defender would have to take seriously: what the prevalence of drift and neutral molecular change implies about the theory's explanatory reach; whether claims about past selection are historical hypotheses that resist test in practice; whether the theory is flexible enough to accommodate any outcome after the fact, and whether that flexibility is a defect or a tolerable feature of a research programme; and any other argument or empirical development that should genuinely move me. I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, an honest account of which of them a proponent of the position would concede and which they would dismiss as resting on a misunderstanding, some sense of what the position predicts that a rival, deflationary reading of Darwinism does not, and a judgement about whether the position in its mature form has been qualified so thoroughly that it no longer runs any real risk of being wrong — and, if it has, whether that is a problem for a view of this kind.
