I have been reading a book that wants to give the old rhyme about the missing horseshoe nail a scientific upgrade. The author's claim is that history really is a chaotic system in the technical sense of the term: it is deterministic, in that each moment fully fixes the next, but it is so exquisitely sensitive to its starting state that a lost nail can compound into the loss of a shoe, a horse, a rider, a battle, and finally a kingdom, much as a butterfly stirring the air can change the track of a hurricane. From this he draws two conclusions. First, counterfactual histories diverge almost without limit: rerun the past with the nail present instead of missing, and before long nearly everything downstream would be different. Second, large historical outcomes are therefore unpredictable in principle, so explanations that appeal to large structural causes — economics, geography, class, institutions — are mostly retrospective stories we impose after the fact; the true levers are tiny, accidental, and unrecoverable.

I would like an audit of this analogy, because it feels as though the chaos-theory picture is doing all the argumentative work while its differences from history stay hidden. Could you lay out the mapping as literally as it will go: what in history would play the role of the equations of motion, the state space, the initial condition, the Lyapunov horizon (how far ahead prediction is possible before errors swamp the forecast), and the attractor (the long-run pattern the system settles into)? Then identify where the mapping breaks, and separate the disanalogies that matter for the author's two conclusions from the merely cosmetic ones. To seed that part: a chaotic system has an unchanging dynamics that already contains its possibilities and can in principle be rerun from identical conditions; history happens once and is made by agents who deliberate, choose, and sometimes deliberately do the surprising thing. A butterfly is a perturbation of an already-flowing stream; a missing nail changes a world for which no exact counterpart flow exists. I am less interested in a list of disanalogies than in the bottom line: does either of the author's conclusions survive them, and is the chaos-theoretic version really a stronger argument for contingency in history than the old rhyme was?
