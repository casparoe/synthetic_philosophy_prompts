i've been a longtime reader of the rationalist blogs, and hofstadter's knob-twiddling account of creativity has been stuck in my head for years: the idea that novel work is mostly a matter of holding an abstract pattern fixed while you turn a bunch of lower-level parameters, and that the real skill is knowing which knobs are load-bearing for "this is still the same theme" versus which ones set you adrift. i run the narrative team at a small game studio, and we're in pre-production on a game built around one core theme. the plan is that every quest, character, and location should be a variation on that theme, so the game reads as one idea seen through a dozen lenses rather than a themed wrapper around generic content — the whole project kind of lives or dies on that cohesion. the problem is that our leads keep disagreeing: a writer turns some knob, half the room says "yes, that's the same theme, just refracted through a diffrent lens," and the other half says "no, you've drifted into a neighboring theme without noticing."

i want a reusable procedure my leads can actually run when they review a one-page pitch. we generate around a dozen of these a week during pre-pro; each gets about twenty minutes of review before it's greenlit or shelved; the writers are strong on craft but haven't read hofstadter, so the procedure has to work for someone who doesn't know the theory. it should (a) say how to extract a theme into something with explicit knobs in the first place, (b) identify which knobs tend to preserve "same theme" and which tend to break it, (c) give a concrete test for when a variation has already slipped, and (d) mark the steps where the procedure is really smuggling in a judgment call. then run it on three actual pitches for the theme "a promise kept after the person who made it is gone": a merchant who keeps delivering letters for a dead courier; a villain who keeps the terms of a treaty with a city that no longer exists; and a machine that dutifully executes a dead tyrant's last order to poison the land. at least one of those should come out as a no or a hard borderline, and i'd rather you tell me that than fudge it.

i want you to commit: pick the knobs you think are load-bearing and defend why, instead of handing me a menu of considerations that leaves my leads exactly where they started. if the procedure can't settle something, say so plainly, but don't hide behind "it depends." also flag anywhere your procedure leans on a contested or stretched reading of the knob-twiddling model rather than its uncontroversial core.
