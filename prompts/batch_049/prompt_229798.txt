I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in political philosophy with about fifteen students, and I want a single 90-minute session on luck egalitarianism conducted as a participatory exercise rather than a lecture. The session should make participants feel, before they can name it, the attraction of the brute luck / option luck distinction associated with Dworkin: a person struck by an unavoidable misfortune and a person ruined by a fair gamble they freely accepted can end up in the same material position and yet seem to have very different claims on compensation. Then it should make them feel why that distinction is less stable than the first cases suggest. The destabilizing point I want them to arrive at themselves is that the notion of a "freely accepted, fair gamble" is doing enormous hidden normative work — once you ask what made the gamble genuinely fair, what information and self-control the chooser had, whether the option was a real option, and whether the taste for risk or the bad decision environment was itself bad brute luck, the clean boundary between chosen option luck and unchosen brute luck starts to blur. By the end, participants should see that the choice/circumstance cut is not simply read off the facts but partly drawn by prior normative commitments.

Design a complete, runnable classroom activity with this arc. Constraints and requirements:

1. No lecture up front. Participants should encounter the asymmetry through what they do — decisions under risk with real but token stakes (points or counters, no real money) followed by collective decisions about redistribution — and they should not hear the terms "brute luck," "option luck," or "luck egalitarianism" until the debrief.
2. The first rounds should be engineered so that the felt verdicts align with the distinction: most participants want to compensate the victims of an unchosen misfortune, and most resist compensating the losers of an accepted gamble, even when the material positions and the cost of compensation are identical. The later rounds should apply pressure the group has to resolve itself: for example, allow insurance against the misfortune at a price, or give some players better information, more alternatives, or less need to gamble than others, so that the group's own compensation criteria begin to conflict.
3. Give the full rules, materials list, scoring/payout scheme, and a timed sequence for a fifteen-person, ninety-minute session.
4. Provide a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the theoretical machinery: the brute/option luck distinction, the thought that a fair insurance market converts brute luck into chosen luck, and the deeper point that evaluating a choice as voluntary and fair already presupposes judgments about background luck, opportunity, and which preferences count as the chooser's own.
5. Include a section on predictable failure modes — what a group will do on a first run that breaks the intended arc — and how to steer back without explaining the target idea mid-game.
6. End with an honest account of what the game cannot reproduce about the philosophical position and what the facilitator must supply afterward.
