I'm working through the relation between two moments in Section X of David Hume's *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*. Hume writes, "A wise man, therefore, proportions his belief to the evidence." Later in the same section, his argument is usually read as saying that testimony for a miracle can be accepted only if the falsehood of the testimony would be more miraculous than the event reported. My confusion is this: if a miracle is defined as a violation of a law of nature, and laws of nature are established by uniform experience, then there is already a complete proof against the miracle before any particular report is even weighed. But then the wise man's maxim would seem to instruct us simply never to believe a miracle report, since the evidence for the law of nature will always be stronger than the evidence for the report. Why does Hume present his argument as a cautious weighing of testimonial evidence rather than as the outright impossibility of miracle reports? What in his account of evidence prevents the argument from collapsing into the claim that miracles are impossible? Please give a committed answer: take a definite view about the strongest non-circular reading of the quoted maxim and whether it succeeds.
