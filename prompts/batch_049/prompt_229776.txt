I'm the token philosopher on my university's animal care committee, and I've started to suspect the whole institution is running on a theory of which things can suffer that nobody's ever stated out loud. The committee reviews every protocol involving a vertebrate — mice, rats, zebrafish, you name it. If you're so much as handling a mouse you have to specify your analgesia plan, and the committee treats the mouse grimace scale as real evidence of pain. But two doors down, the computational neuroscience group is running an enormous simulation of a rat's nociceptive pathway, complete with simulated tissue damage that triggers simulated escape and avoidance learning, and no one has ever suggested this needs ethics review; if I raised it I'd be laughed out of the room. Meanwhile the bee lab shocks bees to study learning, the bees plainly treat the shock as aversive, and the committee says insects fall outside its remit, no protocol needed.

So the university seems committed to something like: real biological nociception in a vertebrate matters, but neither invertebrate nociception nor functionally similar computations in a model matter. But that can't be the whole story. A zebrafish's brain is not obviously doing something a bee's isn't, and the committee is perfectly happy to count general anesthesia as removing the relevant thing — which suggests the criterion is about some kind of ongoing brain activity, not just being made of meat. I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit theory of moral patienthood that would make this pattern coherent: what exactly is being treated as the difference-maker? Give me the best principled version, then say where the practice strictly presupposes that theory, where it's merely compatible with it, and where it's genuinely underdetermined. And tell me what the university would have to change if it actually held a purely functionalist view — that any system implementing the right computations matters, wether it's made of carbon or silicon.
