I can't find the exit in this absent-minded driver argument, and before I read yet another formal treatment I want it broken. Put the payoffs as: exit at the first opportunity pays 0, exit at the second pays 4, and driving past both pays 1. If the driver precommits to exit with probability p at whichever opportunity he finds himself at, his expected payoff is (1 - p)(1 + 3p), so the best plan is p = 1/3, for an expected 4/3. Now the argument that this plan can't be rationally carried out:

(1) When the driver is actually sitting at an exit, he can't tell the two exits appart, so his evidence is exactly the same either way.
(2) Equal evidence requires equal credence, so he should put probability 1/2 on being at each exit.
(3) Acting rationally means maximizing expected utility relative to one's present credences.
(4) Exiting now has expected value (1/2)(0) + (1/2)(4) = 2.
(5) Continuing now has expected value (1/2)(2) + (1/2)(1) = 1.5: if this is the first exit, continuing brings him to the second exit, where the original optimal plan has expected value 2; if this is the second exit, continuing ends the trip at 1.
(6) So at each exit he reaches, the same calculation recommends exiting.
(7) He therefore exits at the first exit and gets 0, even though the precommitted plan p = 1/3 would have gotten him 4/3.
(8) That's absurd: a theory of rational choice that makes the better plan un-executable and tells him to take the worst sure thing has proven too much, so something in (1)-(7) must go.

I'd most like to hold onto both the precommitment calculation and the idea that choice should answer to what the agent knows at the time of acting. Wich step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
