I am designing a course unit on reparations for historical injustice, and I keep returning to one case that seems to straddle the main justificatory divide. The U.S. Civil Liberties Act of 1988 issued a formal apology and a payment of $20,000 to each surviving U.S. citizen or lawful permanent resident of Japanese ancestry who had been interned during the Second World War. The money was distributed as individual redress to the surviving internees themselves, rather than to Japanese American community institutions or to descendants as a class, and the Act cast the wrong it was redressing as one of race prejudice and wartime hysteria directed at particular people. At the same time, the statute created a public education fund to ensure that the incarceration would be taught and remembered.

Run this episode through the two standard families of justification for reparations. On the backward-looking view, reparations answer to a completed wrong: the injurer incurred a debt by what it did then, and the payment is owed to the party it directly harmed, even decades later; the point is to make the victim whole, not to improve present conditions. On the forward-looking view, reparations answer to something still ongoing: a continuing harm, an unjust structure, or an unhealed civic injury, and the proper beneficiaries are those who bear that present burden, even if the original wrong is long past. For each lens, identify the parties, what exactly would be compensated or repaired, whether the 1988 settlement satisfies the lens's demands, and which features of the case the lens illuminates or misdescribes.

Then say what the case does to the distinction itself. Does it confirm that the two justifications can be cleanly separated? Does it show that in practice they run together — for instance, that the individual payments also do forward-looking work or that the education fund also does backward-looking work? Or does the case expose a gap that neither lens anticipated?
