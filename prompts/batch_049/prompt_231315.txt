In preparing a graduate seminar on deontological constraints, I have been canvassing my own reactions to a range of cases, and I have reached the point where my verdicts are clearer than whatever principle, if any, ties them together. I would like to see whether these reactions can be brought into something like reflective equilibrium, so I would be grateful for a statement of the best candidate principle or principles.

Here are the verdicts I am fairly settled on. (1) In the standard transplant case, it is not permissible for a surgeon to kill one healthy, unconsenting patient in order to harvest organs that would save five patients who will otherwise die; this remains wrong even though it produces a net saving of four lives. (2) It is also not permissible for me to kill one innocent person even when a third party credibly threatens to kill five others unless I do it; my refusing to become a killer seems right, even though five people then die who could have been saved. (3) Yet I do find it permissible to redirect a runaway trolley from a track with five people onto a track with one, foreseeing that the one will be killed but not intending that death as a means. (4) I do not find it permissible to push a large person off a footbridge to stop the same trolley, even though the numbers are identical and the death is again foreseen. (5) I do not find it permissible to torture a suspect even to learn where a bomb is hidden when the bomb will otherwise kill several people. (6) But I am fairly confident it is permissible to break a small promise — say, a promise to attend a student’s reading group that afternoon — if that is the only way to prevent someone else from breaking a far more serious promise, such as a promise to provide life-saving medication, and I do not think the difference in seriousness between the two promises is doing all the work by itself.

The tentative view I keep returning to is something like this: the wrongness of a constrained act is not a bad state of affairs to be minimized, but a relation between the agent and the person wronged; a constraint protects each person against being treated by me in a certain way, and the fact that others will act wrongly, or that more violations will occur, if I refuse does not give me a reason to perform the wrong-making act myself. That view fits cases (1), (2), and (5), but it sits badly with (3) and (6), and it does not by itself explain why (3) and (4) should come apart given that both involve my causally contributing to a death I foresee.

What I would like is a principle — or a small set of candidate principles — that captures these verdicts, or gets as close as possible, stated precisely enough that I can test it. For each candidate principle, I would like to know: which of my verdicts it accommodates and why; which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which ones I would have to give up; and what it says about cases I have not listed, such as a hostage situation in which I must shoot an innocent bystander to stop a gunman from killing several others, a physician who gives a dying patient a sedative only to relieve pain while foreseeing that it will hasten death, a case in which the one person consents to be killed to save the five, and a case in which I break one promise in order to prevent five equally serious promises from being broken by someone else. Finally, I would like an honest statement of whether the resulting principle dissolves the familiar paradox of deontology — that a constraint appears to forbid an agent from minimizing violations of the very same constraint — or whether it merely relocates the tension.
