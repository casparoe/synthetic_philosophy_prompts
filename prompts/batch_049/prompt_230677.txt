I just spent an afternoon arguing with a friend about the Liar paradox. I held that "This sentence is false" is neither true nor false, while he insisted it is both true and false, and we each came away convinced the other was refusing to face the obvious. I run a small philosophy discussion group whose members enjoy formal puzzles but have mostly never read the technical literature on truth, and I want an instrument that sorts people by how they actually respond to the paradoxical cases, not by which label they would volunteer for themselves.

Please write a "Which theory of truth do you actually hold?" quiz about the Liar paradox and self-referential truth, with eight to ten questions, framed for readers who can think carefully about cases but have not studied the named theories. Each question should present a concrete sentence or small set of sentences and ask one forced-choice question about its truth status, with answer options phrased in ordinary, label-free terms (true, false, neither, both, meaningless, no stable answer, and so on). Then provide a scoring key that maps answer patterns to positions.

Build the quiz from the logical space rather than from a list of names: first work out the diagnostic questions and the combinations of answers they allow, then locate the actual positions in that space, note which combinations nobody occupies, and say briefly why (incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored). Positions the instrument cannot tell apart should be flagged as such rather than forcibly distinguished.

At minimum the leaves must include: (a) a classical hierarchical/Tarskian view on which the liar is not a single univocal proposition (it is meaningless, ill-formed, or true/false only relative to a level), (b) a truth-value gap view on which the liar is neither true nor false, (c) a dialetheist/paraconsistent view on which the liar is both true and false, and (d) a revision-theoretic view on which the liar has a truth value but the assignment never stabilizes. You may add further leaves if the cases naturally produce them, but do not pad the list just to have more outcomes.

The cases should include at least the simple Liar, the Truth-Teller, a Curry sentence, a Yablo sequence, and a Revenge sentence aimed at the gap theorist (such as "This sentence is not true"); feel free to add a No-no pair or a contingent/empirical liar as tie-breakers. In each case the answer options should be contentious because the positions really do diverge, and the scoring key should explain in a sentence or two why a given pattern lands a respondent where it does. No question should simply ask "do you think contradictions can be true?" or otherwise let people self-identify by position.

For format: begin with the quiz itself (a title, a short one-paragraph framing note to respondents, then the questions), followed by the scoring key and the diagnostic commentary, with no preface, no restating of this prompt, and no commentary after the closing of the scoring key. You are an expert on formal theories of truth, and the distinctions should be precise enough that someone who knows the literature recognizes that the right joints are being cut. Before writing, think through for yourself how each named position answers each proposed case, then use that private mapping to draft the questions and the key; do not include those working notes in the final answer.
