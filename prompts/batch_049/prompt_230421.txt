I am trying to settle what I actually mean by "omnipotence," and my method is to lay out my untutored verdicts on a battery of cases and see which account of omnipotence they add up to. Here are the cases as they strike me, before I have worked out the theory.

First, the familiar stone: an omnipotent being cannot create a stone so heavy that it cannot lift it. I take this to be no defect, because the request describes a stone unliftable by a being who, by the definition of the power in question, can lift any stone. The task is internally inconsistent.

Second, the arithmetical and geometrical impossibilities: it cannot make 2 + 2 equal 5, and it cannot make a square circle. I count neither against omnipotence; a power directed only at what is possible is not restricted in any damaging way.

Third, free creatures: it cannot make a creature that is free and at the same time causally determined to choose the good, since the determination would cancel the freedom. I also count this as no defect. But I do think it can actualize a world in which that free creature freely chooses the good, without causally determining the choice. So "forcing a free choice" is out of its power, but "bringing it about that someone freely does the good" is, in my book, within its power, and I want an account that respects the difference.

Fourth, the past: it cannot now change the past. This one makes me uneasy. Unlike the square circle, I cannot see that the past's being otherwise now is internally contradictory, yet I still do not want to say an omnipotent being fails to be omnipotent because of it.

Fifth, sin and promise-breaking: it cannot sin, or lie, or break a promise. This bothers me most. Creatures sin, so sinning is not internally impossible; saying an omnipotent being cannot do it sounds like a genuine restriction, but I am not willing to give up calling that being omnipotent.

Sixth, self-cessation: it cannot cause itself to cease to exist, and I do not think this counts against omnipotence, though I could not say why this case is different from the sin case.

Is there one account of omnipotence — one precise statement of what "all-powerful" ranges over — that delivers all or nearly all of these verdicts? A principle I have been toying with is that S is omnipotent just in case S can actualize any state of affairs that is not internally contradictory. But that principle seems to tell me the being can sin, since a being's sinning is not internally contradictory, and it also looks as though it drains omnipotence of content by making it reach everything short of contradiction. Please test this principle against my verdicts, repair it if repair is possible, and state the repaired account precisely, indicating which clause each of my cases motivates. Say honestly which of my verdicts the repaired account cannot accommodate and why they must go. Then give the resulting account's verdicts on two further cases: an omnipotent being's creating a being with a power the creator can never revoke, and an omnipotent being's making itself temporarily unable to perform some task and later restoring the ability. Finally, say how confident you are in the analysis and single out the specific point you are least sure of.
