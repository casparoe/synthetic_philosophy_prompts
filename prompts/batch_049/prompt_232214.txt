I got into an argument with a friend who does economics about whether models with obviously fake assumptions can still be good science. He kept falling back on the Friedman line that assumptions dont need to be realistic and only predictions matter. That cant be right, I think, but I couldn't put my finger on it, so I tried to push his idea and ended up with an argument that seems to prove way too much:

(1) What makes a model a good scientific model is just whether it makes accurate predictions; whether its assumptions are true or false doesn't matter.
(2) So a model can have knowingly false assumptions and still be good science, as long as it predicts well.
(3) Astrology assumes that planets affect personality and life events, and that's false.
(4) But suppose someone finds a specific astrological rule — say, people born during Mercury retrograde are more likely to be in car accidents — and that rule keeps making correct, risky predictions on new data at about the same hit rate as a typical economics model.
(5) By (1) and (2), this astrological rule counts as good science.
(6) But that seems absurd: a rule built on a false assumption shouldn't become science just because it happens to predict.
(7) So either (1) is wrong after all, or one of those steps is doing something I can't see.

I can't find the exit. Walk me through which premise or step I should reject, why it looked so natural at first, and what the main ways out each have to pay. Keep it under 600 words and in American English.
