I'm putting together a small unit that pairs fiction with the philosophy of action, and I keep coming back to Moby-Dick as a test case for theories of collective intentionality and joint action. I'd like a reading of the novel through that lens. The philosophical material is this: when does a group do something together, as opposed to a collection of individuals doing adjacent things? One view says the mark of genuinely shared action is that each participant intends the joint activity and not only their own part in it, and so adjusts their plans to mesh with the others' plans and stands ready to support them if their contribution falters. Another view says what matters is joint commitment: a commitment of several people to one another to act together as a kind of single body, which binds each of them to the rest in ways no one can privately dissolve. A further intuition is that collective intentionality involves an irreducible we-intention, not decomposable into I-intentions plus mutual belief. I want the essay to use these as lenses rather than settle them in advance.

The obvious starting points are the novel's two faces of cooperation. The whaling scenes — boats lowered, the crew rowing to the mate's command, the harpooneer at the prow, the line paid out — look like a paradigm of shared cooperative activity, each man with a role, each adjusting to the others. But the goal that finally organizes the voyage is not the common end the men signed on for; it is Ahab's private revenge on one whale. The quarter-deck scene, where Ahab nails the gold doubloon to the mast and the harpooneers are made to drink and swear to the hunt, is the natural test case for whether a joint commitment has been formed, and, if so, whose intention is in it.

Where I suspect the book does philosophical work of its own is in the mismatch between the two accounts. Starbuck objects to the oath, says the pursuit is blasphemous, and yet continues to serve and even to act in ways that keep the voyage going. Is he a genuine part of a plural subject, or an unwilling instrument who has been made complicit? Does the novel suggest that joint commitments can bind people who never freely joined them and may privately reject them — which would favor the joint-commitment picture over the more voluntarist shared-activity picture — or would it just show that the whole crew has been recruited into one man's individual project? And at the end, does the Pequod plausibly act as one body, or does the book dissolve that image into monomania, accident, and the wages of following? In short, I want a critical reading, no more than 900 words, that is faithful to the novel and to the philosophy: it should say what the fiction dramatizes that the theories handle well, where it resists them, and what the book adds to the debate that argument alone tends to miss. No need to quote from the novel; describing scenes from memory is fine.
