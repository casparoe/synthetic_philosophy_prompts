I've been running a small reading group with some friends from work—not academics, just people who like arguing about American history—and we've spent the last month on Frederick Douglass's later thinking about the Constitution. We read his Glasgow speech "The Constitution of the United States: Is It Pro-Slavery or Anti-Slavery?" from 1860, and one sentence keeps getting quoted by half the group. Douglass writes: "Again, it should be borne in mind that the mere text, and only the text, and not any commentaries or creeds written by those who wished to give the text a meaning apart from its plain reading, was adopted as the Constitution of the United States." Some of us think that basically settles it: the words themselves never say slave or slavery, so the document can't be pro-slavery the way Garrison claimed. Others say that's a dodge, because the thing still has the three-fifths clause, the fugitive slave clause, and the slave-trade clause, and courts like Dred Scott read those to protect slaveholders no matter what the plain text says. We've realized we're actually several positions, not two, and we keep talking past each other—especially since Douglass himself used to be in the Garrison camp and changed his mind.

So I want you to write us a decision tree that sorts a person from "I've read the Constitution and this Douglass quote" to one of the named positions, on the question of what the Constitution actually did about slavery. Build it from the diagnostic questions first, not from the names, so people get sorted by what they say about cases and clauses rather than by which label they like. Each question should be concrete—a specific clause or a short interpretive scenario—and answerable without already knowing where it leads. The leaves should at least include: the Garrisonian position (it's a pro-slavery document, so the right response is disunion and refusing to vote or hold office under it), Douglass's Glasgow position (the plain text is not pro-slavery, so abolitionists should use the ballot and vote abolitionists into office), and the Taney/Dred Scott-style position (the Constitution, properly interpreted, protects slaveholders as a matter of law). Also tell us about any logically possible positions nobody actually holds, and whether that's because they're incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. And flag any leaves your tree can't cleanly seperate.
