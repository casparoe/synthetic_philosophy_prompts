I retired a few years ago from population genetics — mostly gene–culture coevolution models, if I'm honest, more simulation than bench work — and I've finally had time to read philosophy properly instead of just poaching ideas from it. Lately it's Dennett's four kinds of minds: Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, Gregorian. The way I understand them: a Darwinian creature lets natural selection test its hypotheses out in the world, slow and lethal; a Skinnerian creature adds trial-and-error learning and gets shaped by reinforcement; a Popperian creature adds an internal model of the world and can preselect among possible behaviors, so some of its hypotheses die before it has to; and a Gregorian creature adds mind-tools — language, mostly — that let it download other people's discoveries and start tinkering with itself. The part I keep chewing on is that the hierarchy is cumulative: each level leaves the lower ones running underneath. The Popperian creature still gets conditioned, and the Gregorian creature still has Darwinian selection doing its quiet accounting on whatever variation leaks out. I keep trying to get my old lab group too see that.

Anyway, I'm helping run a small philosophy reading group at our public library, and I'd like to close the last session with a poem I can read aloud — a pantoum, one quatrain for each creature in ascending order, with the pantoum's repetitions carrying the inherited capacities from the level below. You know the mechanics: lines 2 and 4 of each quatrain come back as lines 1 and 3 of the next, and the closing quatrain takes up the first stanza's opening lines, so the whole thing loops. I want the form to make the argument: each kind of mind carries the one below it inside it, and by the end the poem returns to the Darwinian bottom rather then letting the Gregorian creature feel it has escaped. Better if the stanzas don't announce their creature by name; let placement and the repeated lines do that. A wry tone would suit me, maybe a little suspicious of how much the Popperian creature congratulates itself on thinking before acting — as if the Skinnerian and Darwinian layers weren't still down there doing most of the work.
