Before I evaluate any further proposals about who should be entitled to vote, I want to fix what an adequate theory in this area has to deliver. Write the desiderata for a theory of the franchise and its character: who should be entitled to vote, and whether voting is best understood as a right or as a legal duty. For each desideratum, give the case or consideration behind it, and mark which are non-negotiable requirements that any plausible view must meet and which are merely preferences of a particular camp. Include any distinctions such a theory must respect — for instance, the difference between being subject to a polity's laws and being affected by its decisions, or between a right to vote and an obligation to vote — with the case for why each distinction carries weight. The pressure points I most want addressed: proposals to lower the voting age down through adolescence and toward children, extending the vote to long-term non-citizen residents, the status of non-resident citizens, disenfranchisement of prisoners and ex-felons, voters with severe cognitive disabilities, and compulsory voting systems that treat turnout as a legal obligation. Then flag any pairs of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which requirement does the most to exclude otherwise plausible candidates. For example, a principle that everyone significantly affected by a polity's decisions should have some say pulls toward very broad enfranchisement, while any competence condition pulls toward restriction; if that tension cannot be fully dissolved, say which side has to give and why. The part I most want is the diagnosis of tensions among the criteria themselves, not a scorecard of existing positions.
