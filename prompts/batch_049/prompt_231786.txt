I am drafting a short essay on Daniel Dennett's distinction between cranes and skyhooks, and I would be grateful for comments on the argument as it stands before I develop it further. The draft is below.

The crane/skyhook distinction is usually read as a methodological principle. Cranes are step-by-step mechanisms that build higher-level complexity out of lower-level parts and processes; skyhooks are unexplained top-down sources of design or purpose. On this flat reading, the distinction says only that one should not help oneself to skyhooks. But that makes the exclusion of skyhooks close to a stipulation rather than a substantive claim. Calling an explanation a skyhook and calling it non-naturalistic would then do no distinguishable work.

I propose instead that the distinction is best reconstructed as an inductive betting rule. The thought is that explanations which posit design, intelligence, or purpose at the top have a poor track record: the appearance of design in organisms was displaced by natural selection, vitalism by biochemistry, the Cartesian thinking substance by distributed neural processes, and so on. On this reconstruction, when Dennett rejects a skyhook he is not merely applying a definition of naturalism but predicting, on the strength of those past successes, that a crane account will eventually be found.

This reconstruction has a virtue, but it also exposes a structural gap. The success of crane explanations pushes toward ever more thoroughgoing bottom-up explanation, and yet Dennett distances himself from greedy reductionism; he wants to preserve beliefs, decisions, consciousness, and other intentional patterns as real while still explaining them through cranes. The crane/skyhook distinction supplies no criterion for deciding when a crane explanation preserves the target phenomenon and when it explains it away. A greedy reductionist and Dennett could agree that some phenomenon is a product of cranes while disagreeing sharply about whether the crane story shows that the phenomenon is real. The distinction settles the battle against skyhooks, but it is silent on the battle against greedy reductionism.

I would appreciate comments directed at four questions in particular. First, is the reconstruction of the crane/skyhook distinction as an inductive betting rule fair to Dennett's actual use of it, or does it turn a rhetorical contrast into a stronger claim than he intends? Second, does the draft correctly locate the point at which the distinction stops doing argumentative work? Third, what is the strongest defence a partisan of the distinction could offer at that point, and would it require importing a criterion from elsewhere, such as a theory of real patterns or explanatory relevance? Finally, are there formulations that would make a reader who knows Dennett's work distrust the essay, and how should they be revised? Please be specific and quote the relevant sentences from the draft when identifying problems.
