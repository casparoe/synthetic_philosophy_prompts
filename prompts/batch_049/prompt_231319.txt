I recently read a popular science article that summarized Dennett's attack on qualia, and I am not sure whether the summary is fair or whether my unease with the conclusion is clouding my judgment. The article said something like this:

Dennett's famous coffee-taster thought experiment, involving two tasters named Chase and Sanborn, proves that qualia don't exist. Chase reports that the coffee tastes exactly the same as ever, but he no longer enjoys it. Sanborn reports that he still enjoys it as much as ever, but it no longer tastes the way it did when he began. Dennett argues that since we cannot tell from their reports whether their qualia changed or their judgments about the coffee changed, there is no fact of the matter about their qualia. This is supposed to show that the whole notion of qualia—private, ineffable, intrinsic properties of experience—is an illusion generated by a bad theory. Critics object that Dennett is denying that there is anything it is like to taste coffee, but Dennett's real view is that what it is like is nothing over and above a matter of what one is disposed to say and do.

I would like to know which parts of this summary are accurate, which are subtle misreadings, and whether the article's concluding claim—that Dennett reduces experience to behavioral dispositions—actually follows from anything true in the summary or from Dennett's argument itself. In particular, I am unsure about three things: (1) whether Dennett claims to prove qualia don't exist, or rather to show that the concept of qualia is unworkable and should be abandoned; (2) whether he really denies that there is anything it is like to taste coffee, or only denies a certain philosophical gloss on that; and (3) whether his positive account is behaviorist in the way the article suggests. Please separate what is accurate, what is a common simplification, and what Dennett himself would reject.
