I'm not an academic; I first encountered this problem through a long argument with a friend about whether you should ever revise a plan you made when you had a clearer head. I moderate a Q&A for a small discussion group, and our next session features a decision theorist who defends the view that, in the absent-minded driver problem, the only normatively binding standard is the ex ante plan—the plan that maximizes expected payoff before the trip begins—and that the apparent self-undermining of that plan once the driver is at an indistinguishable exit is not a genuine objection to it. I'd like eight to ten interview questions that probe this position rather than simply asking the guest to restate it.

Please include at least one question on each of the following, with a follow-up keyed to the answer the guest is most likely to give:

1. In the standard version of the problem, exiting at the first exit yields the worst outcome, the second exit yields the best outcome, and continuing past the second yields the intermediate outcome, so the ex ante best plan is to exit with probability one-third. At that probability, the driver is exactly indifferent at each exit between exiting and continuing. Is that indifference all that keeps the plan from unraveling, and what happens if the driver makes a small mistake or has a slight bias?

2. A critic might say that, conditional on being at an exit, the driver must assign some probability to being at the first versus the second exit, and that this belief depends on his own strategy; the plan that looks best from this conditional, or ex interim, standpoint is not the same as the ex ante plan. Why should the ex ante standpoint have priority over the ex interim standpoint, and does the likely reply—that the ex interim standpoint illicitly treats the two exits as distinguishable—actually close the matter?

3. Suppose the driver could install an override-proof commitment device before the trip that forces him to follow the ex ante plan. He would apparently choose it. Does that show that being unable to reconsider at an exit is good for him, or does it instead show that ex ante and ex interim rationality are simply different standards that never directly conflict?

4. Some philosophers press the point that the driver's beliefs about which exit he is at depend on his plan, while his plan depends on those beliefs. Is the ex ante plan merely a fixed point in such reasoning? If so, why should a fixed point be normative, rather than just one possible equilibrium among others?

5. Does the defense of the ex ante plan require saying that a rational agent cannot really be absent-minded in the relevant sense—that imperfect recall is itself a failure of rationality and therefore outside the proper scope of decision theory? If not, how does the guest make room for a rational agent who genuinely cannot distinguish the two exits?

6. What limiting principle does the guest offer? If the ex ante plan is the only binding standard, does it follow that an agent who knows in advance that she will receive information she cannot use to distinguish states should ignore that information when it arrives? What stops this from licensing clearly bad policies in less contrived cases?

7. In this problem there is no opponent, so the usual game-theoretic rationale for randomization—making oneself unpredictable to another agent—is absent. What does randomizing at one-third accomplish for the driver, and why is it not an empty gesture given that he is indifferent at the equilibrium?

The questions should push the guest past slogans and toward the actual mechanics of the view, and they should be answerable without external sources.
