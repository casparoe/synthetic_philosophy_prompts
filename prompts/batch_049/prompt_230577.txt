Before I commit to any position on whether an ant colony could literally have a mind, I want the standard of evaluation itself rather than another candidate theory. Lay out the desiderata for a theory of mindedness in composed systems — the phenomena it must handle, the distinctions it must not collapse, the empirical facts about colony organization it must not contradict — and for each say whether it is a hard requirement on the question or a preference of a particular camp. Fix the purpose explicitly: the theory should be good enough to decide, without special pleading, whether the answer to "does an ant colony have a mind?" is yes, no, or a well-posed revision of the question.

The issue is sharpest as a parity problem. A brain is a society of neurons, none of which is minded; an ant colony is a society of ants, none of which has anything like the colony's problem-solving competence. Most people credit the brain with a mind and hesitate about the colony, so an adequate theory must either identify a difference in the kind of integration, information flow, or subject-formation that justifies the asymmetry, or concede that the asymmetry is bias.

Pressure points to address, in whatever order makes the cumulative case sharpest, but address all of them:

- The literal-versus-metaphor line. The theory must say what would make colony-level talk — "the colony decides," "the colony prefers site B" — literal rather than a convenient façon de parler. It must not set the bar so low that a stock market or a highway system counts as literally minded, nor so high that almost nothing does, and the difference between those failures matters.

- The no-central-locus problem. If there is something it is like to be the colony, the theory must explain how a subject of experience can exist without a central physical seat — no ant, pheromone trail, or nest chamber is the place where it happens. If there is nothing it is like, the theory must say whether a mind can be real without consciousness, or whether only cognition and not mentality is being claimed, and it must not equivocate between those two conclusions.

- The boundary and continuity problems. A colony founded by a single queen grows by degrees through hundreds of thousands of workers; a brain also grows and reorganizes continuously. The theory must specify what makes a bounded candidate system a candidate at a moment, and must say whether mindedness is a threshold or a gradient. If a threshold, it must name the non-arbitrary property crossed between one day and the next; if a gradient, it must say when the ascription becomes literal enough to settle the debate rather than merely stretching the word.

- The known facts of distributed colony computation. The theory must be consistent with the way real colony-level behavior — nest-site choice, foraging reallocation, defense — is produced by local interactions among ants that do not encode the overall plan, with much of the coordination achieved through modifications ants make to a shared environment: trails, refuse piles, nest structures. An adequate theory should either show how a representational or inferential capacity could emerge from such environment-mediated coupling, or explain why this stigmergic kind of integration is the wrong kind for mentation.

- The brain-parity loser cases. Any criterion that admits the colony must be applied honestly to entities we would rather not count as minded, and any criterion that excludes the colony must be applied honestly to the brain; a biological-chauvinist clause quietly inserted is a defect, not a solution. The theory should also say something about the hard middle cases: a slime mold reorganizing its network to solve a maze, a corporation deliberating through memos and committees whose human members know what they are doing, a bee swarm choosing a nest site by quorum, and a split-brain patient whose two hemispheres each appear to carry on their own performances. For each, the theory should give a verdict or explain why the case is ill-posed on its terms.

Then I want the tension analysis, which is the part I most want to see: point out which subsets of the desiderata are jointly unsatisfiable, or satisfiable only by gerrymandering. In particular, I suspect non-arbitrary boundaries, a clean literal-versus-metaphor cutoff, and continuous growth may form an impossible triple — say whether they do and what follows: one of the constraints is actually negotiable, the demand for a yes-or-no answer is misplaced, or the question should be replaced by a graded one.

Finally, tell me which single requirement does the most work in eliminating the usual candidate positions — roughly, integrationist views that appeal to information integration; functionalist views that require the right kind of representational contents; subject-of-experience views that take phenomenal unity as primitive; and deflationist views that call colony-mind a category error. Keep that scorecard brief; the desiderata are the deliverable, not the contenders.
