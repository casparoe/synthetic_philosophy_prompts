Next week I am leading a two-hour seminar that sits between philosophy and cognitive science, and the topic is how a thinker moves from a handful of examples to a concept that governs indefinitely many new cases. I want the entire session built around Bongard problems — the two-panel displays in which every box on the left satisfies some rule that every box on the right violates, and the solver must recover the rule from only six positive boxes and six negative boxes. The students will not have seen these before, and most will arrive assuming that induction is just tabulating the features shared by the positive cases. Do not give me the explanation; give me a plan for the explanation.

First, the order of ideas and the dependency structure behind it: what must be in place before negative examples can be taught as anything other than a test, and why the underdetermination problem — that indefinitely many rules divide the same twelve boxes — should be raised only after the students have caught themselves proposing a rule and then having it wrecked by a box they ignored. Second, one carrying example: a Bongard-style rule of your own invention, with a clear account of what it must do and avoid. It should resist any rule that is a mere conjunction of visible shape-properties, and its negative examples must be constructed to destroy the obvious wrong rules rather than merely to fail the target rule. Third, a misconception map, in order: that the concept is just the common features; that one more positive example would settle which of two rival rules is correct; that the right rule is whichever is simplest; that the negatives are only a check on a rule one has already formed. For each, say exactly where in the sequence to head it off and how. Fourth, a way to check at the end whether the students have grasped the relation between the examples and the rule, rather than merely produced a rule that sorts the panels. Finally, say what I should openly decline to settle at this level — for instance, whether concepts in general are Bongard-style rules — and why leaving that open helps the lesson.
