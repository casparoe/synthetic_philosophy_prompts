I'm on my city's memorials committee, and I need you to write the short decision memo I'll circulate before our vote next Tuesday. The case is a stone monument in a public park, put up in 1904. Its inscription honors the Ridgefield Volunteers who fell at the Battle of Hart's Mill in 1863. Problem is, our city's own history office has the muster rolls, pay records, and newspaper accounts from the time, and they show the Ridgefield Volunteers never fought at Hart's Mill. The unit had been disbanded two years earlier, and most of the men named on the plaque survived the war and are buried somewhere else. So the plaque isn't just slightly off; its wrong about the main thing it says.

People in the neighborhood want to keep it exactly as it is. They say the monument is part of who we are, the Fourth of July parade starts there, their grandparents helped pay for it, and fixing the text would feel like telling the community that its memories don't count. The other side says the city is paying to maintain a public lie, and a memorial is a claim about the past, not just a decoration.

I need the memo to recommend a concrete option — keep it as is, add a correction, replace the plaque, take it down, or something in between — with a time frame, and I want it to genuinely argue with the memory-and-identity side instead of dismissing it, while still being a real recommendation. I've been going back and forth on my own about whether a community's memory has its own kind of authority or whether it has to answer to the record when the two come apart.
