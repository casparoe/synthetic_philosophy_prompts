A friend walked me through this and I keep coming back to it because the conclusion seems mad, but I cant find the exact slip. I'll lay it out so you can tell me which numbered step you'd refuse and what refusing it costs.

(1) The judgement you get from an intuition pump appears to be hostage to the telling. Take the standard pair: a runaway trolley heading for five people, and either you pull a lever so it diverts onto a spur where it kills one person, or you push someone off a footbridge to stop it. Same numbers, same deaths, same basic trade-off, yet the first feels at least permissible and the second feels awful. Tinker with the wording — call the push a 'divert', foreground the one person's face, or restyle it as a drainage problem — and people's verdicts shift again.

(2) Since the underlying facts are held fixed, whatever is doing the moving must be in the description rather than in the case itself.

(3) But there is no unframed way to present the underlying case. Every statement of a scenario selects what is salient, what is loaded, what is treated as mere staging. An intuition pump is never a neutral window; it manufactures the very reaction it then reports.

(4) A reaction that can be reversed by re-description cannot count as evidence about the case. A scale that reads higher when you rest your thumb on it is not measuring the object.

(5) So intuition pumps — the trolley, Twin Earth, the Chinese room, Mary's room, the lot — are instruments of persuasion, not inquiry. They can nudge, but they cannot confirm or disconfirm anything.

(6) Therefore none of the verdicts we draw from such cases carries any evidential weight, and a theory of morality, mind or meaning has to get by without them. His conclusion: case-based philosophy is empty and our intuitions should be treated as noise.

I find (6) impossible to live with, because the same reasoning would make reasoning from precedent in law and every thought experiment in physics mere theatre as well, and that seems to prove too much. But I dont know whether to reject (2), or (4), or some slide between them, and I'd rather not give up on thought experiments altogether.

So: which step do you reject, and what do you have to buy once you reject it? I'd like the answer in British English please — spelling, grammar, the lot.
