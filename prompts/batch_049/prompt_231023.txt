In Frederick Douglass's speech "The Constitution of the United States: Is It Pro-Slavery or Anti-Slavery?" (Glasgow, March 26, 1860), he lays down a principle of interpretation:

“Again, it should be borne in mind that the mere text, and only the text, and not any commentaries or creeds written by those who wished to give the text a meaning apart from its plain reading, was adopted as the Constitution of the United States. It should also be borne in mind that the intentions of those who framed the Constitution, be they good or bad, for slavery or against slavery, are so respected so far, and so far only, as we find those intentions plainly stated in the Constitution.”

A friend extracts the following argument from this passage: (1) The Constitution, as law, is just its text; framers' unstated intentions are not part of it. (2) So a clause of the Constitution has the legal effect of guaranteeing a right to hold property in man only if the text plainly says so. (3) The text nowhere plainly says that any person may be held as property. (4) Therefore the Constitution does not sanction slavery. (5) But by the same test, the three-fifths clause, the fugitive-slave clause, and the clause postponing prohibition of the slave trade do not sanction slavery either, since none of them says "slavery" or "property in man." (6) That conclusion seems to prove too much: it implies that the provisions widely understood as the Constitution's proslavery compromises had no proslavery character at all. But my friend insists this just follows from Douglass's own textualism. Where does the argument go wrong—in one of the premises, in the move from "text only" to "plainly says," or in the assumption that Douglass's principle commits him to this result? What would it cost Douglass to reject the step you identify?
