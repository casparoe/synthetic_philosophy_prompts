I facilitate a philosophy working group, and two members who’ve been arguing about just war theory for months have finally agreed to produce an adversarial-collaboration statement instead of another round of dueling emails. One is a Walzer-style defender of the moral equality of combatants. His line: the in bello rules are independent of the ad bellum question, so a soldier who obeys the war convention—discrimination, proportionality, the uniform, no torture—is morally on a par with enemy soldiers however unjust his side’s war, because individual soldiers lack the epistemic position to judge the justice of their war and the convention depends on that symmetry. The other is a McMahan-style revisionist. Her line: that’s a defensible legal convention but false as deep morality, because who may be permissibly killed is fixed by liability, and liability tracks the objective threat someone actually contributes to; so most soldiers fighting for an unjust cause lose the ordinary permission to kill, even when they sincerely and reasonably believe their cause is just.

Draft the document these two should sign. I want it organized like this: (1) a numbered list of agreed claims—substantive ones, not truisms; (2) a two-column disagreements table, with each side’s own view phrased exactly as that side would accept it; (3) a cruxes section: each crux should be a sub-question such that settling it would move at least one of them, paired with a concrete case or argument both agree bears on it and each side’s prediction recorded; and (4) a short note on which parts of this fight are really about words. I have my own hunch that one crux is whether liability is objective or relative to what the killer can reasonably believe, and another is what a just combatant may do to an unjust combatant whose contribution is real but marginal, like a conscript driving a supply truck—but don’t feel bound to those if the document needs different cruxes. Don’t resolve the debate; making the residual disagreement exact is the whole point.
