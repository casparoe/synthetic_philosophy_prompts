I've come to defend a view in the neighborhood of Langdon Winner's work on technology and politics: the claim that at least some artifacts have politics in a strong sense. I don't mean the banal point that technologies are designed, funded, and used inside political economies, and I don't mean the crude determinist claim that a given technology inevitably produces one social order. I mean the intermediate claim that the material form of certain technical systems — their physical layout, operating logic, required maintenance regimes, and institutional supports — can embody particular distributions of power and authority, and that a narrower class of systems is strongly coupled to a specific kind of political arrangement no matter who owns or operates them.

I want a map of what would count against this position, treated as a live empirical and conceptual thesis rather than a slogan. Which cases would have to come out differently for me to retreat from it? In particular: how would a deflationary account that locates the politics entirely in designers, users, or surrounding institutions have to be formulated in order to genuinely compete with the artifact-level claim, and what would make it the better explanation? Is there a principled way to distinguish a technology that merely reflects a political arrangement from one that materially enforces it? And does the view, once qualified to handle cases where the 'same' artifact does very different things in different settings, survive only by becoming unfalsifiable — and if so, would that be a decisive objection or a cost a view of this kind can reasonably pay?

Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say which of them a committed defender should concede as counting against the view rather than dismiss as changing the subject, and close with an honest assessment of how exposed the position actually is.
