I am a medical student, and I have been struck by the way clinical pain assessment treats a patient's self-report as at once authoritative and suspect. In training we are told that pain is subjective, that it is whatever the experiencing person says it is, and that a patient's score on the 0-to-10 scale is the gold standard, not to be overridden simply because the patient looks comfortable; the patient is supposed to have privileged access to their own pain. Yet the same training teaches us to look for pain behaviors, to notice when a reported nine sits oddly with the patient's affect or their willingness to move the affected limb, and to document such inconsistencies. In disability evaluation and medicolegal settings the identical self-report is treated as easy to feign, and assessment instruments are explicitly built to detect overreporting.

I suspect this practice carries an implicit theory of what pain-language is, and I want that theory reconstructed. The practice appears to presuppose a private-language picture: pain is a private object each person knows through an inner act of ostension, the word pain gets its meaning by standing for that object, and a first-person pain report is a description of an inner state only the patient can observe. The private language argument in Wittgenstein's Philosophical Investigations, on the standard reading, denies the coherence of just such a picture: a term like pain cannot acquire meaning by private ostensive definition, the beetle in the box is meant to drop out as irrelevant to the public word, and first-person expressions of pain are not reports of a private object but refined extensions of natural expressive behavior, answerable to public criteria.

What, then, does the practice actually presuppose? Can it be reconstructed as assuming only the modest claim that patients are often better placed than observers to know their own pain, or does its reliance on the rating scale commit it to the full private-language picture? Is the practice internally consistent when it treats self-report as authoritative in the clinic but as easy to fabricate in the courtroom, and which of its parts are strictly ruled out by the private language argument rather than merely fitting badly with it? Finally, if one took the argument's deflationary lesson seriously, what would a coherent pain-assessment practice look like: would the 0-to-10 scale survive, would the maxim that pain is what the patient says it is survive, and how should first-person reports be used in disability, compensation, and malpractice settings?
