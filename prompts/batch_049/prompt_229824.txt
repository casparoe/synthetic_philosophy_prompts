I've basically come around to the view that the whole-brain criterion is the right account of death, and that circulatory death isn't really a rival definition so much as a clinical proxy for "the brain has irreversibly stopped" — which is why its reliable in nearly every case. That reframes the debate for me: the interesting question is where the proxy breaks down and what those breakdowns do to the position. So tell me what would count against the whole-brain criterion, properly understood. I don't need the stock objection that brain-dead bodies on ventilators are pink and warm; I want the less rehearsed stuff — donation after circulatory death (DCD) protocols and the dead donor rule, what ECMO and other extracorporeal support do to the irreversibility condition, the cases where the two criteria come apart in awkward directions, and the worry that the brain isn't the integrating organ the early defenders assumed it was. For each, say whether it's fatal, genuinely awkward, or just messy clinical practice. And be honest about whether the whole-brain view has gotten quietly redefined over the years to absorb these — and whether that kind of insulation is a bug for a definition of death or just what you'd expect from a concept doing clinical work.
