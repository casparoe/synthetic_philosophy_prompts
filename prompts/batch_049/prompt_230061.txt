I started as a program officer at a family foundation six months ago, and the same fight keeps happening in our grant meetings. We make maybe thirty or forty grants a year, mostly $25k to $200k, in global health and farmed animal welfare. A proposal will have a clear, well-evidenced near-term benefit: bednets in a malarial region, or better water quality on a salmon farm. Then somebody raises the long-run ripple effects. The bednet program could cut childhood mortality and thereby shift population growth, labor markets, and migration over the next thirty years, and we can't even sign those effects. The salmon-farm work could lower the price of farmed fish, shift consumer demand, and expand or contract total aquaculture over decades, and we have no idea whether its good or bad on net. A board member has started calling this cluelessness and says it means we should stop pretending we can compare total consequences and just fund whatever has the best measurable near-term outcomes. Another staff member says that's giving up and smuggling in a preference for the near term.

I gather philosophers call this issue consequentialist cluelessness. I don't need a literature review or citations; I need a working procedure. Please write a decision procedure my program staff can run on each proposal in about twenty minutes, without philosophy backgrounds. It should include: the questions to ask in order and what counts as a good-enough answer before moving on; how to tell when we're in a situation where we can at least bound the far-future effects versus one where we can't guess the sign, and what to do differently in each; where the procedure should default to near-term measurable outcomes and where it shouldn't; and which steps are really value judgments dressed up as analysis, so we flag those for the board instead of pretending the checklist decided them. Then run the procedure on two proposals: (1) a bednet distribution program with good evidence on short-term mortality reduction but unknown demographic knock-ons over thirty years, and (2) a corporate cage-free egg campaign that, if it succeeds, could change the economics of egg farming in ways that might either improve or worsen long-run aggregate farmed-animal welfare depending on factors we can't observe. Finally, say honestly where the procedure will let us down.
