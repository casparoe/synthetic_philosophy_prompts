I teach a grad seminar called "Probability, Evidence, and Inference" — about 10 students, all philosophy PhDs but not all comfortable with the math — and I have one 90-minute session to spend on this. I want a hands-on activity that makes them really feel why the principle of indifference is so slippery, using Bertrand's paradox. I don't want to open with a lecture on it; I want them to run face-first into it and only later hear the name.

Ideally the activity has them physically generate random chords of a circle through different randomization procedures — paper, pins, a spinner, a transparent overlay with grid lines, randomized endpoints versus randomized midpoint versus randomized radius point, whatever actually works — and then record whether each chord is longer than the side of an inscribed equilateral triangle, with the groups aggregating their results on the board. The key moment should be that the groups report quite different long-run frequencies, and somebody gets agitated because each group was convinced they'd done "the" random chord. The takeaway I want them to arrive at themselves: "random" is only well-defined relative to a procedure, and the principle of indifference silently picks a symmetry each time without saying so.

Give me full rules, a materials list that is cheap and actually workable in a classroom, timing for a 90-minute block, a structure for pooling results, and a debrief that maps each group's procedure onto a different invariance assumption and onto the principle of indifference. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — people measuring too loosely, procedures blurring together, one group peeking at another's method, or just nobody being surprised — and how to fix that. And be frank about which part of the point the physical activity can't get across and I'll have to supply myself afterwards.
