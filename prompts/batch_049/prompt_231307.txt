I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm developing an interactive feature for a general-interest magazine about a quietly puzzling fact concerning taste: people who are perfectly willing to form beliefs about politics, science, and the weather on the strength of what others tell them often insist that aesthetic judgment requires seeing, hearing, or reading the thing for oneself. As a centerpiece, I want a short instrument, tentatively titled Which account of aesthetic testimony do you actually hold?, that sorts readers according to what they actually think testimony can and cannot accomplish in aesthetic life.

The instrument should have eight to ten questions, each a concrete case with two or three response options, and a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto named positions. The questions should never ask readers to self-identify with a label or assert an abstract principle; they should ask what the reader would say or do in the situation. Please build the instrument from the logical space rather than from a menu of textbook positions. I want it organized around at least these axes: whether another person's report gives one a reason to believe something is aesthetically good; whether such reports can by themselves justify an outright aesthetic belief or assertion; whether they can yield aesthetic knowledge of the thing's character or whether first-hand acquaintance is irreplaceable; and whether the real epistemic work of aesthetic talk is instead to direct attention to features one must experience for oneself. From those axes, locate and name the occupied positions—I expect something like a strict acquaintance requirement, an evidentialist view on which testimony justifies belief but not full aesthetic judgment, a full-transmission view on which reliable criticism can ground aesthetic knowledge, and a deflationary or conversational view on which so-called aesthetic testimony is really advice or attentional scaffolding—and flag every unoccupied combination, saying whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Also flag any positions your questions cannot separate.

Please commit to a definite instrument rather than surveying options: give the exact questions, the answer options, the scoring key, and the leaves it sorts readers into, with a short rationale for why each question is diagnostic. Include at least one question each on: relying on trusted reviews to assert that a film is good; choosing between two exhibitions on a friend's recommendation; whether detailed tasting notes or a sommelier's description can give someone knowledge of a wine's aesthetic merits; and a person who has abundant second-hand descriptions but no first-hand experience, asked what exactly they lack. Where the scoring key cannot separate two positions, or where an answer combination has no occupant, say so explicitly and explain why rather than papering it over.
