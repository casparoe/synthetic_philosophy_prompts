Two philosophers, one a committed moral particularist and one a committed moral generalist, have spent a semester arguing about whether moral reasons can be captured in finite principles. They have now agreed to an adversarial collaboration. Write the joint statement that collaboration should produce.

Begin with a preamble in which each position is stated in wording its own adherent would accept, without caricature, and in which the two parties agree on what the debate is and is not about. In particular, both should be able to sign a statement clarifying that context matters on either view, and that the debate is not about whether circumstances affect what one ought to do, but about how they affect it and whether that dependence can be expressed in principles.

Then provide Section 1, the agreed claims, as a numbered list. The claims should include shared characterizations of the phenomena any adequate view must handle: for example, why it is at least reasonable to say that the fact that an act would cause pain has one kind of relevance in a case of torture, another in a necessary medical procedure, and another in a consensual endurance practice; and likewise for pleasures taken in another person's humiliation. They should also include shared claims about the structure of moral thought that both sides accept, such as the distinction between contributory reasons and overall verdicts, between pro tanto and prima facie readings of considerations, between a reason that is invariant and one that is merely treated as a default, and between explaining a verdict and merely restating it. Finally, they should include shared methodological commitments about what would count as evidence against each view. None of these claims should be vacuous; each should be a proposition the other side could plausibly have resisted at the outset.

Then provide Section 2 as a disagreements table. Each row should give: the issue stated in neutral wording; the particularist's formulation, phrased so a particularist would sign it; the generalist's formulation, phrased so a generalist would sign it; whether the disagreement is substantive or partly verbal, with an explanation wherever it is partly verbal; the crux, meaning the sub-question on which this row turns; and a concrete case or argument both parties agree bears on that crux, with each side's prediction or commitment about it recorded in advance. Cover at least these rows:

- The polarity of reasons: whether a feature such as causing pain, or causing pleasure, can change its polarity as a reason between contexts, or whether only its weight and the overall balance change while its polarity is fixed.
- Default reasons: whether some reasons have a standing valence that can be outweighed but not reversed, and what is being claimed when such a reason is called a default.
- Codifiability: whether any finite set of true principles could in principle cover the relevant moral terrain, and what would make a candidate formulation count as a principle rather than a gerrymandered summary of decisions already reached case by case.
- Moral knowledge and competence: whether the competent judge's verdicts involve subsumption under principles, or an uncodifiable judgment about the particular case.
- Moral education and advice: whether improving a learner or advising an agent requires shareable principles, and how each side accounts for improvement without the other side's favored machinery.

In Section 3, state what remains after the table. Identify which rows both parties now regard as the load-bearing ones, and which, if any, they regard as settled or dissolved. For each load-bearing row, specify the observation or theoretical result that would actually move at least one party, and say exactly what the embarrassed position would have to concede if that observation or result came out against it. Do not resolve the dispute. The output should be a constructive document both philosophers could put their names to, with the residual disagreement made exact rather than papered over.
