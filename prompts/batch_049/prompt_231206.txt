A friend of mine has, over the years, given verdicts on a series of Gyges-ring-style cases, and I have been keeping a small record of them. In every case the question is the same: once detection, punishment, and reputational cost are removed, does the agent have any reason—possibly one that could be outweighed—to do what morality requires? The underlying issue is the old Glauconian worry: strip away the fear of consequences and the advantages of appearing just, and it becomes unclear whether anything still speaks in favor of doing what morality demands.

Her verdicts so far:

1. An agent can steal a fortune from a stranger under conditions of guaranteed invisibility; no one will ever know. The agent would feel substantial guilt afterwards. Verdict: there is a reason to refrain.
2. The same situation, but the agent can take a drug that fully blocks guilt and self-reproach. The agent does not care about the victim's interests, but does care about being the kind of person who does not steal, and identifies with that self-description. Verdict: there is a reason to refrain.
3. An agent made a promise years ago to someone now dead. Breaking it would materially benefit the agent, no one would ever know, the agent would feel no guilt, and the agent no longer regards the promise as mattering or as connected to anything she presently values. Verdict: there is no reason to keep it.
4. An agent borrowed a minor but useful item and can keep it unnoticed; keeping it would violate an image the agent values—being reliable in small matters—though the agent would feel no guilt. Verdict: there is a reason to return it.

I can fit at least two rules to this track record. One grounds reasons in identification: the agent has a reason just in case the act conflicts with a standard she counts as part of who she is, and none otherwise. The other grounds reasons in present cares: the agent has a reason only if some current desire, aversion to anticipated guilt, or second-order wish to be a certain kind of person stands against the act, and none when the motivational set is silent. All four verdicts sit comfortably with both rules.

The case I need to predict is designed to pull them apart. An agent sincerely holds that keeping a promise to a new colleague is central to who she is; if asked, she would list promise-keeping among her defining values. But on the day she must decide, the identification is motivationally inert: she feels no pull toward keeping the promise, and she has no first- or second-order desire to keep it. She is not impaired, exhausted, or otherwise excused; the value simply is not moving her right now. She can break the promise with guaranteed non-detection and no anticipated guilt, and she will gain substantially. The colleague will be set back in a way they will never discover.

What should I predict her verdict is: does the agent have a reason to keep the promise, or none at all? Which rule, if either, would that verdict implicate? Alternatively, is there a third rule that fits all four earlier verdicts and diverges differently on this fifth case—one I have missed? And what further case would be the cleanest test of whichever ambiguity remains, and what would it show in each direction?
