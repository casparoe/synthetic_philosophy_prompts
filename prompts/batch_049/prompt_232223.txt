I cover the science and ideas beat, and I have been working on a long feature about whether it is ever correct to say that a large language model believes what it says. In trying to get my own position straight before interviewing philosophers and AI researchers, I found myself attracted to a broadly Dennettian way of settling the question: what matters is not some inner sentence in a belief box but whether the intentional stance — treating the system as a rational agent with beliefs, desires, and goals — earns its keep by making the system's behavior more predictable and more explainable than the alternatives. That starting point led me to an argument whose conclusion makes me uncomfortable, and I cannot tell whether the discomfort is a discovery or an error.

The argument runs like this. (1) A system has beliefs just in case adopting the intentional stance toward it is predictively and explanatorily useful. (2) The stance is very useful for a contemporary large language model: treating the model as an agent that knows certain facts, wants to produce relevant and coherent text, and chooses its words in light of those states predicts its behavior far better than any description at the level of weights or token statistics alone. (3) But the same test is passed by a thermostat, which we predict by treating it as believing that the room has fallen below the set point and wanting the temperature restored, and by a vending machine, which we predict by treating it as believing it has been paid and wanting to dispense the product. (4) It is absurd to say that a thermostat or a vending machine has beliefs in the substantive sense at issue when we ask whether an AI does. (5) One natural reply is that LLMs differ from thermostats in flexibility, breadth, and linguistic sensitivity, so the stance does vastly more work in the LLM case. (6) But that looks like a difference of degree, not of kind: the inferential move from predictive usefulness to belief is identical, and only the amount of usefulness changes. (7) If only degree separates the cases, then either belief is already present in the trivial cases, contradicting (4), or some unexplained threshold must be imposed between them.

So, as things stand, (1) plus (2) and (5) forces me to give up (4). Where does this go wrong, if it does? I would most like to keep (1) as a serious instrument for reasoning about AI and to keep the substantive reading of (2), on which LLMs have beliefs worth taking seriously. I would rather not earn those conclusions by conceding that a thermostat is a believer. Which premise or step should I reject, and what would rejecting it cost?
