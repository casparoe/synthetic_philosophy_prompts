I am a PhD student working on the ethics of aggregation, and I have been trying to pin down a defensive maneuver I keep encountering in both the philosophical literature and policy-adjacent discussion, without finding a name for it. The maneuver arises when someone objects to a utilitarian or cost-benefit proposal that imposes a serious, concentrated loss on one person for the sake of a tiny benefit to each of a great many others. For instance, a health system delays one identified patient's urgent surgery for a year in order to shorten everyone else's waiting time by a few minutes, and an objector says the loss to that patient cannot be compensated by the scattered gains to the others. The defender replies that the view does not confuse persons: each person's well-being is entered into the calculation as its own separate item, so the distinctness of persons is in fact respected by the formalism. The objector replies that the separation is only in the entries; the calculation then adds those entries together, and the addition is precisely the operation that treats one person's loss as cancellable by another person's gain. The defender answers that this only shows the view is aggregative, which was never denied — and the exchange starts over.

I have seen the same shape of argument in a discussion of pension reform, where a present cohort's accrued benefit was to be reduced in order to fund a small improvement for every future cohort, and the defense was that each cohort was modeled separately in the projections. My question is whether this maneuver — conceding the objector's point at the level of how persons or groups are represented, while continuing to apply an operation that undoes that representation — has a recognized name or a literature of its own. I am not asking for the general name of the objection to adding up well-being across persons; that I already know. I am asking specifically about the defensive move and the mismatch between the bookkeeping and the operation performed on it. If it has no established name, how would you taxonomize it, and what would you call it? In particular, I would like to know how to distinguish it from ordinary question-begging, from a motte-and-bailey shift between a modest representational claim and a strong aggregative conclusion, and from a merely correct clarification that the objector has misdescribed the formalism — and whether there are cases in which a defender is entitled to make this move rather than merely committing it.
