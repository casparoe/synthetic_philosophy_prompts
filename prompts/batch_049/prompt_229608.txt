In my third-year philosophy of mind module, I have been assigned to lead a fifty-minute section for first-years on eliminative materialism about beliefs and desires, and I would like to work from a proper teaching plan rather than simply deliver the view as an explanation. My instinct is that certain things have to be in place before the thesis can even be stated without sounding merely perverse: in particular, the claim that our ordinary mental vocabulary—belief, desire, intention—is itself a theory about unobservable posits rather than a neutral description of what is given. Is that really the load-bearing first step, and what should be placed after it: the general idea that a theory's posits can be abandoned wholesale once a better theory arrives, or something else? And what should actually carry the hour? I am unsure whether to use a single worked example from the history of science, such as the abandonment of phlogiston or caloric fluid, or an example drawn from the students' own mental talk; whichever I choose, I would want to know what the carrying example must have and must not have if it is not to overstate the eliminativist's claim.

I am equally worried about the misconceptions that will predictably form: that eliminativists think people are literally empty-headed, that the position is self-refuting because defending it uses belief-talk, and perhaps that it is a claim about the disappearance of conscious experience. My guess is that these need to be defused at different moments rather than all at once, but I do not trust my sense of the ordering. At what point in the sequence should each be headed off, and are there any I should deliberately let surface before answering? Finally, what should I openly say I am leaving out at this level, and what single question could I ask at the end to tell whether the students have understood the position rather than merely found it outrageous? I would be grateful if your answer were written in British English.
