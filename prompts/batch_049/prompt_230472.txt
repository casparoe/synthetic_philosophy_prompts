I teach a philosophy of technology seminar — 18 upper-level undergrads, one 80-minute session — and I want a runnable classroom game instead of a lecture. The idea I want them to feel in their bones is the impossibility result in algorithmic fairness: roughly, when two groups have different base rates (say, different rates of repaying a loan or of reoffending), a risk score cannot be perfectly calibrated across both groups and at the same time have the same false positive rate and the same false negative rate across both groups. Something has to give. Its a weird result because each condition sounds like something any fair algorithm should obviously satisfy, and I want them to discover the conflict themselves rather than be told it.

Design the activity. It should be runnable with materials from the room — cards, chips, dice, envelopes, whatever — and it should not depend on students knowing any particular dataset or case. Put them in the role of building or auditing a decision rule (loans, pretrial release, admissions, hiring, your call), with fake small stakes and no real money. The rules should get them to want two fairness properties at once: something like a promise that the score means the same thing in both groups, and a promise that both groups get the same proportion of wrongful rejections and wrongful acceptances. Then let them fumble until they notice that different base rates push the two demands in opposite directions. The instructor should not say tradeoff, base rate, calibration, or impossible before the game; those words should only show up in the debrief.

Then write me a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the formal notions (calibration, false positive and false negative rates, where the threshold gets set), shows where each step could have been resisted, and says what resisting would have cost. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I suspect at least one group will just quietly use different thresholds for the two groups and call it fixed, and another group will decide the whole thing is rigged — and how I should handle that without lecturing.

Finally, be honest about which feature of real algorithmic fairness the tabletop game can't reproduce and that I'll need to supply in the debrief — whether that's the way real risk scores get trained on messy historical data, feedback loops, the fact that real protected-class status is less clean than the tokens in the game, or something else. You decide and tell me.
