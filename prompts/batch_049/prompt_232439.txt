I teach an undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of social science, about twenty students, and I have a seventy-five-minute block. Design a classroom game that makes participants feel path dependence without hearing the term until the debrief: early moves that could have gone either way accumulate into an outcome nobody individually intended, and by the end that outcome looks inevitable even though it was contingent. The core should be repeated rounds of a coordination problem with increasing returns — a choice's payoff rises with how many players made it in earlier rounds — plus a small asymmetry that lets one standard pull ahead if enough people happen to try it early. Use only index cards, a whiteboard, and a modest point bonus (extra credit or candy), and assume the students are strategically self-interested. Participants should come away able to hold two thoughts at once: the outcome was not inevitable, yet by the end it could not easily have been otherwise. The debrief should map specific moments of play onto the formal ingredients — multiple equilibria, increasing returns or network effects, switching costs, and the gap between ex ante contingency and ex post inevitability — and should say what predictably goes wrong (players coordinating on the better standard too early; players refusing to switch later even when switching would be collectively better) and how to handle it. Close by stating what the exercise cannot reproduce about historical lock-in, such as long timescales and the fact that real history cannot be rerun for comparison.
