In an ethics class I'm taking we read G. E. Moore's Principia Ethica, and the professor gave us a one-page handout summarizing Moore's view of what has value in itself. The handout says: 'For Moore the only things good for their own sake are two kinds of experience: friendship (more broadly, the pleasures of human intercourse) and the enjoyment of beautiful objects. Beauty itself, however, is not intrinsically good—a great painting sealed in a vault that no one ever sees is no better than a blank canvas, because all the value is in the conscious appreciation. Knowledge and virtue are at best means to these two goods, never good in themselves. And Moore is wonderfully additive about all this: the value of a whole is just the sum of the values of its parts, so sizing up a possible world means totaling the friendship and aesthetic enjoyment it would contain.'

I've only read parts of the book, but I vaguely remember Moore having a thought experiment where a beautiful world is better than an ugly one even if nobody can see either, which sounds like it points the opposite way from the vault painting. I also remember him making a fuss about organic unities, but I honestly can't tell wether the handout is a fair compression or whether the professor is quietly disagreeing with him. Could you audit this summary: which parts are accurate, where does it go wrong or overstate, what would a careful Moorean object to, and does the conclusion about the hidden painting actually follow from Moore's view as he defended it?
