As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I have been reporting on a large city's decision to retire cash and token fares on its public transit system in favor of fully contactless payment. The transit agency describes the change as a neutral efficiency upgrade: riders tap a bank card or phone app at an electronic reader, boarding moves faster, and the agency gains richer data for route planning. Yet the details emerging from my interviews unsettle that framing. The readers were installed first, and far more densely, in stations serving higher-income neighborhoods; the system presupposes a smartphone or bank account that many riders do not have; and riders who still wish to pay in cash are directed by the fare gates themselves into a slower, staffed line where their transactions are recorded, while card-paying riders pass through quickly and unobserved.

When I proposed structuring the article around the idea that this payment architecture itself redistributes access and exposure to oversight, my editor pushed back. He argued that machines do not have politics; only the agencies and politicians who deploy them do. In his view, the technology is a neutral instrument, and any unequal outcomes are matters of policy and operation rather than properties of the system's design.

I find his position tidy but inadequate, and so far I have struggled to say exactly why. Is my editor right that the politics of such a system enter only through its use, or is there a defensible sense in which a technical design can be political in itself? And if so, what should that mean for how I frame the story responsibly rather than turning it into a polemic?
