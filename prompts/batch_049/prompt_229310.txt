I was a chemist for 35 years, mostly spectroscopy, and since retiring I've fallen into philosophy of machine learning. I run a small reading group of other retired scientists, and we keep going around in circles about whether the "features" that interpretability tools pull out of neural networks—sparse autoencoders, linear probes, saliency maps—are really in the network, or are just useful summaries we impose on it. I'd like to set the group a take-home essay question that can't be answered by muttering "it depends what you mean by representation." Please write the question, a model answer of about 500 words, and a marking scheme out of 20. The scheme should seperate people who can (a) tell apart a claim about the network's internal states actually having content from a claim about our descriptions of those states being faithful, (b) say what would actually settle the dispute, and (c) resist the lazy move of treating every predictive success of an interpretability tool as automatic evidence for realism. I care less about whether they know the literature than whether they argue carefully. Apologies if I've mangled the philosophical vocabulary—I'm new at this.
