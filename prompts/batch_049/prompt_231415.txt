Recently I attended a climate-policy seminar in which a speaker dismissed the whole contest between carbon taxes and direct regulation as a debate about how to arrange deck chairs. Her move was to invoke a single line: "You can't have infinite growth on a finite planet." In her use, this was not merely a warning about the limits to growth; it was an argument-stopper. From it she concluded that a carbon tax is no more serious than a fuel-efficiency standard, because both leave the underlying commitment to growth intact, and that the only honest response is planned degrowth. I want to know exactly what that sentence asserts before I let it do that much work.

Please lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise claims the slogan could be used to express. In particular, distinguish readings along at least these lines:

(a) Growth of what? Does the slogan mean growth in real GDP, growth in material and energy throughput, growth in extracted raw material, growth in population-plus-consumption, or something looser about an economy aspiring to "more"?

(b) Finite in what sense? Does the finitude consist in a limited stock of fossil carbon, in the limited capacity of waste sinks such as the atmosphere and oceans, in the finite rate of solar inflow, in the constraints of mass and energy balance, or in the fact that ecosystems have tipping points beyond which costs become nonlinear?

(c) What kind of "can't"? Is the claim one of physical impossibility, biophysical unsustainability over the long run, sharply rising marginal social cost, distributional injustice, or a moral-normative claim that continuing to pursue growth is wrong even if it were feasible?

For each reading, tell me whether it is a near-tautology, a substantive and defensible empirical claim, or false, and whether it actually licenses her conclusion that a carbon tax cannot be a serious policy. In particular, I would like to know which readings a defender of carbon pricing is forced to accept, which readings that defender can consistently reject, and which reading a degrowth advocate would need but is probably not the one most people have in mind when they repeat the phrase. I am not looking for a verdict on whether degrowth is correct; I am trying to stop the slogan from doing several incompatible jobs at once.
