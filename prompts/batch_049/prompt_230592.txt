I am a student in a course on nineteenth-century philosophy, and we are reading Nietzsche’s essay in the Untimely Meditations that is usually translated as On the Uses and Disadvantages of History for Life. I am trying to work through the part near the beginning where he lays out the three ways history belongs to a living person. In my own words, the passage says something like this: history belongs to us insofar as we are active and striving, insofar as we preserve and revere, and insofar as we suffer and need deliverance. To these three relations correspond three kinds of history: monumental, antiquarian, and critical. Monumental history serves the active person by presenting past greatness as a model; antiquarian history serves the preserving person by tending continuity with what has come before; critical history serves the suffering person by dragging the past before a tribunal, condemning it, and cutting away what oppresses life.

What I cannot work out is what makes critical history a mode of history at all. Monumental and antiquarian history both seem to depend on remembering and carrying forward a connection to the past, whereas critical history seems to break that connection, judging the past and in some way severing us from it. Is critical history therefore a different way of remembering, or is it better understood as a disciplined form of forgetting? And if all three modes serve life, but they appear to recommend opposite attitudes—reverence toward the past and condemnation of it—then what is the standard by which one tells when each mode is appropriate? I would like help reconstructing the unity behind the three modes, and especially understanding whether Nietzsche can consistently recommend both preserving the past and passing destructive judgment on it for the sake of the same life.
