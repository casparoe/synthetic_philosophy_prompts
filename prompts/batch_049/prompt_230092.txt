Here is an argument I built for a seminar on risk-weighted expected utility theory, and I have become suspicious of it because its conclusion seems far too strong. I want you to tell me exactly where it goes wrong, if it does, and what it would cost to reject the offending step.

Risk-weighted expected utility (REU) is a theory of rational choice on which a gamble may be evaluated by taking its worst possible utility and adding each successive improvement in utility, weighted by a risk function r applied to the probability of obtaining at least that improvement. A risk-averse agent has r(p) ≤ p, with r(0) = 0 and r(1) = 1. REU was introduced to accommodate, among other things, the following pattern of preference, which expected utility theory cannot represent with any utility function.

Decision One:

* A: £1,000,000 for certain.
* B: £5,000,000 with probability 0.10, £1,000,000 with probability 0.89, and £0 with probability 0.01.

Decision Two:

* C: £1,000,000 with probability 0.11, and £0 with probability 0.89.
* D: £5,000,000 with probability 0.10, and £0 with probability 0.90.

Call preferring A to B and D to C the Allais pattern. REU says this pattern can be fully rational.

Now the argument.

(1) Both decisions can be replaced by dynamic decision trees without changing any outcome or probability.

Tree One: with probability 0.89 you receive £1,000,000 and the decision ends; with probability 0.11 you face a second-stage choice between:

* A*: £1,000,000 for certain;
* B*: £5,000,000 with probability 10/11 and £0 with probability 1/11.

Choosing A* at the second stage yields exactly A from Decision One; choosing B* yields exactly B.

Tree Two: with probability 0.89 you receive £0 and the decision ends; with probability 0.11 you face the very same second-stage choice between A* and B*. Choosing A* yields exactly C; choosing B* yields exactly D.

Thus in the two trees the second-stage decision node is identical: same acts, same consequences, same conditional probabilities. The background payoff is not received when the second stage is reached. The only difference is what would have happened had the first stage ended the decision: £1,000,000 in Tree One, £0 in Tree Two. Call this the background payoff.

(2) A background payoff that is received only in a branch not reached at the second stage cannot make a difference to the second-stage choice. If the available acts, their consequences, and their probabilities are identical at a node, then a rational agent's ranking at that node cannot depend on what would have happened elsewhere. Rational preferences therefore satisfy the sure-thing principle: changing a common consequence in a state where one's choice makes no difference leaves preference unchanged.

(3) A rational agent's preferences at the outset of a tree must cohere with what she would actually choose at later nodes; otherwise she endorses a plan she knows she will abandon without any new information. So if she prefers the Tree One plan "choose A*" to the Tree One plan "choose B*", she must prefer A* to B* at the second stage. And if she prefers the Tree Two plan "choose B*" to the Tree Two plan "choose A*", she must likewise prefer B* to A* at that same second stage.

(4) The Allais pattern consists precisely in preferring the A-plan to the B-plan in Tree One and the D-plan to the C-plan in Tree Two. By step (3), the Allais pattern commits one to both A* > B* and B* > A*. Nothing but the background payoff distinguishes the two second-stage nodes, and step (2) rules that out as relevant.

(5) A rational agent cannot strictly prefer A* to B* and also strictly prefer B* to A* in the same circumstances with no new information between the two rankings. Strict preference is asymmetric: at most one of X > Y and Y > X can hold. So the Allais pattern is not a coherent pair of preferences at all.

(6) Therefore the Allais pattern is not merely contrary to expected utility theory; it is internally incoherent.

(7) Since REU declares the Allais pattern rationally permissible, REU declares an incoherent pattern rational, and must be rejected. And since the Allais pattern is the principal phenomenon REU was designed to accommodate, the entire project of treating risk attitude as a weighting of probabilities rather than as curvature of utility collapses with it.

I cannot accept (7). I do not believe that robust, deliberatively considered Allais preferences are a logical blunder, and I do not believe the only options are expected utility theory or incoherence. Yet each of steps (1) to (3) looked, when I wrote it down, like a banal restatement of the requirement that the framing of a decision should not change what one chooses. If I had to choose what to keep, I would most want to keep step (2); but I also want to keep the datum that the Allais pattern is not obviously irrational.

Please write a diagnosis of this argument in four parts. First, identify the exact step or inference that should be rejected, and state whether the argument contains a subtle flaw or is valid with a costly premise. Second, explain why the rejected step looked so plausible, and which general principle of choice it is really invoking. Third, price the alternatives: what an expected utility theorist must give up if your diagnosis lets REU survive, and what a risk-weighted expected utility theorist must give up if the argument stands. Fourth, say what becomes of the Allais pattern once the argument is properly located: rational, irrational, or something else. Define any technical terms you use, and write your answer in British English.
