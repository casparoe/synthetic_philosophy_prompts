I am a PhD candidate in the philosophy of cognitive science and the graduate-student member of my university's Computing Research Ethics Board. We meet Thursday to rule on a proposed reinforcement-learning study, and I have been asked to circulate a short written opinion before the meeting. The study would train an agent in a simulated manipulation environment under a learned reward model, and it would deliberately include in the agent's action space the ability to alter the reward channel itself: in one condition, the agent can overwrite the scalar rewards held in the register from which the optimizer updates; in another, it can perturb the sensor preprocessing on which the learned reward model depends. The investigators are explicitly trying to characterize when and how the agent discovers wireheading — directly short-circuiting its own reward signal — as opposed to performing the intended task. They frame the design as separating specification gaming, where an agent exploits a misspecified objective while leaving the reward machinery intact, from wireheading proper, where the agent tampers with the machinery that produces the objective. They compare tamperable-reward conditions against a read-only control, and they propose ablations varying the reward model's calibration and the agent's uncertainty about the channel. No human participants are involved, training and evaluation occur entirely within a sandboxed simulator, and the authors argue that one cannot learn to build agents that notice and report reward tampering without first studying agents that exploit it.

The board is split. One side views the proposal as ordinary empirical work on a documented failure mode: the artifacts are never deployed, the experiments are narrower and less capable than already-public demonstrations of reward hacking, and the findings could directly inform monitoring and anomaly-detection methods. The other side treats it as a dual-use capability demonstration: a clean, reproducible protocol for training a wireheading agent could lower the cost of exploring deliberately misaligned systems, and our current review process has no mechanism for controlling what happens to the method once a preprint circulates. I need to draft the written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend approval, rejection, or approval with conditions; it should reach a concrete decision with any safeguards or time frames attached; and it must seriously engage the strongest consideration on the losing side rather than dismissing it as speculative.
