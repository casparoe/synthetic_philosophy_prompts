You are a moral philosopher who specializes in metaethics and moral psychology and who is skilled at turning philosophical distinctions into practical guidance for non-philosophers. Work through the request carefully before writing; begin by brainstorming the main ways a piece of left–right moral psychology can go wrong when it is used to reach a normative conclusion, then build the procedure from that list.

I am the standards editor at a large online magazine that publishes weekly opinion and analysis on political polarization. A recurring problem: writers pitch pieces that cite moral foundations theory—the finding that self-described liberals and conservatives weight a set of moral "foundations" (roughly care, fairness, loyalty, authority, sanctity, and sometimes liberty) differently—and then conclude that one side's moral outlook is more complete, more balanced, less self-serving, or more likely to be correct. Please write a reusable procedure my editorial team can run on any such pitch or draft.

The procedure should be a checklist of ordered questions, aim for 8–12. For each question, specify what counts as a sufficient answer to move on; where the procedure should stop and rule that the piece cannot support its normative claim as written; and where the question is a values question the editor or the publication must settle rather than something the psychology can settle. The procedure should make the is–ought step explicit: a psychological difference between left and right is, by itself, neither evidence that one side is right nor evidence that the difference is mere bias. It should flag the common failure modes—for example, moving from descriptive breadth of foundations to normative completeness, treating an explanation of a view as a debunking of it or as a vindication of it, assuming the six foundations are the right or exhaustive list, and using average group differences to license conclusions about individuals or particular policies. It should also be honest about the narrow conditions, if any, under which a descriptive moral-psychological finding can properly bear on a normative dispute.

Then run the procedure on three hypothetical submissions:

1. A piece claiming that because conservative participants in the classic studies rate all the foundations as relevant while liberal participants rate mostly care and fairness, conservative moral judgment is more comprehensive and therefore more likely to be correct.
2. A piece claiming that because both sides rate care and fairness highly and disagree only about how to apply them, the left–right moral divide is smaller than assumed, so majoritarian aggregation of both sides' shared values settles most contested policy questions.
3. A piece claiming the reverse of the first: that the extra foundations are rationalizations for in-group bias, and therefore liberal moral judgment is more likely to be correct.

For each, say what the procedure would require before publication and where the editor is left making a value call. At least one of the three should produce a verdict a publication like ours would rather not hear, and say so plainly.

The procedure will be used by editors without philosophy training, in under 30 minutes, on pieces that reach a large audience; it must be defensible to the editor-in-chief and to readers who disagree with our own politics, and it must not rely on external databases or citation checks, which we handle separately.

End with a short self-audit: which argument or consideration would most change the procedure's verdicts if it were established or abandoned, and where is the procedure's reasoning most likely to fail?

Hard limit: 1,500 words for the whole response, including the checklist, the three worked examples, and the self-audit.
