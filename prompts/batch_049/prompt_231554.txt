I'm an undergrad in a seminar on environmental history and disease, and next week I have to present a short argument paper. The claim I'm defending is that historians should count non-human things — pathogens, climate shifts, volcanic eruptions — as historical actors in a deflationary sense: they have causal powers and trajectories we can narrate, but no intentions or goals. My three moves are: (1) a lot of standard accounts already treat these forces as "background conditions" but won't give them real explanatory credit, which leaves the explanation kind of underspecified; (2) treating them as actors in a stripped-down sense forces us to specify mechanisms — transmission routes, crop failures, dust veils, that kind of thing — rather than gesturing at "context"; and (3) this doesn't imply environmental determinism, because an actor can be blocked, redirected, or amplified by other actors, including humans.

The room is a mix. Most of the seminar are social and cultural historians who think agency-talk belongs to intentional beings and who assume any climate-and-disease story is a step back toward environmental determinism; they'll hear "historical actor" as either a metaphor gone feral or an excuse to let humans out of the causal story. There are also two environmental historians who are sympathetic to giving non-human forces a bigger role but think "actor" is exactly the wrong word because it seems to smuggle in intention.

So my ask: give me a Q&A prep sheet for the actual objections this audience will raise, not the ones a philosopher of action would raise. Order them by how likely and how damaging they are, phrase each one the way one of these people would really say it, give me a two-minute reply, and flag where each reply is softest. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks. Also, separate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this — don't present a contested view as the consensus.
