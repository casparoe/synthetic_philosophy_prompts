I have been working through the standard account of the thermodynamic arrow of time, and I keep encountering what looks to me like a circularity. The story runs as follows: the fundamental dynamical laws are time-reversal invariant and therefore cannot by themselves distinguish past from future; the needed asymmetry is supplied by the second law of thermodynamics together with the past hypothesis, the claim that the universe began in an extraordinarily low-entropy state. But the second law, as it is usually stated, already says that entropy tends to increase toward the future. So we seem to explain why there is a future-directed asymmetry by appealing to a law that already contains that asymmetry. The past hypothesis seems to have the same problem: positing a low-entropy boundary condition at one temporal end, with time-symmetric dynamics, would yield entropy increasing away from that end in either direction, so the time-reverse of our universe would look structurally identical with past and future swapped. What justifies calling that low-entropy end "the past" rather than "the future"? I would like to know whether the standard explanation is circular, or whether it merely relocates the asymmetry into a brute boundary condition, and whether that distinction makes a real difference. Please answer in American English.
