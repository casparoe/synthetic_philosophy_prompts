I am a novelist, and I want to understand from the inside what it is like to be forced to draw a bright line through a continuous gradient; the protagonist of my current project sits on a board that must judge, applicant by applicant, whether a young person is 'old enough' to be held criminally responsible as an adult. I help run a small writers' workshop—about twelve adults with two hours available—and I would rather stage this experience than discuss it. Please design a runnable activity with rules, small real stakes, and a debrief in which participants, through their own successive judgments, assemble a forced march—a sequence of cases that differ only slightly—along a single vague predicate such as age, height, or income, and find themselves committed both to accepting every small step and to rejecting where the sequence has taken them. The facilitator should not lecture or name the paradox beforehand; what participants should come away seeing is that individually untroubling judgments can jointly produce an unacceptable verdict, and that each way out changes something else one had wanted to keep. In the debrief, map particular moments in the activity onto the main lines of resistance—denying one conditional step, giving up classical logic or bivalence for vague language, positing a sharp but unknowable boundary, treating the threshold as shifting with context or stakes—and say what each line of resistance costs. The debrief should also anticipate what predictably goes wrong when the activity is run, explain how to handle those failures, and be explicit about which feature of the sorites the exercise cannot by itself make participants feel and must instead be supplied by the facilitator.
