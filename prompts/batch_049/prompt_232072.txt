At my writers' group I described the outline of my novel-in-progress: an empire that has stood for four centuries collapses within nine years of a boy-emperor's accession, amid a silver shortage and two frontier revolts. Two of the others began arguing about whether the collapse is plausible, and I could not referee them.

Elena said it is contrived. Among empires that reached a comparable size and survived to a comparable age, a fall this rapid is rare; the outline therefore needs stronger causes or a slower decline.

Marcus replied that those are the wrong comparators. What matters is not age and size but the structural weaknesses the empire actually has — a child emperor, a debased currency, overextended frontiers — and in that class rapid collapse is ordinary.

Elena answered that choosing the comparison group after deciding the outcome is circular: any collapse can be made to look probable if the class is tailored to the case, so the class must be fixed in advance if it is to carry weight.

Marcus countered that comparing only by age and size discards nearly all the diagnostic information in the story. A comparison that deliberately ignores distinguishing features is not neutral but impoverished; how fine-grained the class should be is an empirical question, and no principle says the coarsest class is always right.

Elena retorted that Marcus's view would allow any ending to be made to look inevitable by adding detail, emptying plausibility of content. Marcus replied that Elena's view would forbid using anything distinctive about a case, reducing plausibility to a handful of coarse averages.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: who is right about what at each step, and what, if anything, both of them are missing?
