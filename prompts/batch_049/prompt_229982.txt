I recently started a job at a policy research institute where I am expected to work with economic models whose assumptions I can see are plainly false: that people are rational, that information travels instantly, that transaction costs do not exist. When I said this made me uneasy, a senior colleague told me Friedman settled this long ago, and she summarized his view as follows:

"Friedman proved that the realism of a model's assumptions is irrelevant; the only thing that matters is whether the model predicts well. His billiard-player example makes the point: you can predict an expert player's shots by assuming she solves the relevant physics equations, even though she obviously does not. So our models' false assumptions—perfect rationality, no transaction costs, full information—are just like that billiard player. Since the model's forecasts have been good enough for the clients, we don't need to worry about the assumptions."

I can see how this would make my life easier, which makes me worry I will simply accept it. Then tell me whether the practical conclusion my colleague draws—that I can safely ignore the false assumptions as long as the forecasts are decent—follows from anything true in the summary.
