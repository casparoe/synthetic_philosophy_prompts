I keep getting tangled up in something about how governments handle the future in cost-benefit analysis, and I want you to reconstruct what the practice is actually presupposing. Heres the practice as I understand it. When an agency evaluates a regulation that saves lives or prevents harm decades out — a flood barrier, a pollution standard, climate adaptation — it discounts those future benefits at an annual rate, commonly around 3 percent, often with a 7 percent sensitivity run on top. At 3 percent, a death prevented forty years from now counts for a bit under a third of a death prevented next year; at 7 percent its more like a fifteenth. My question isn't whether that's good policy, it's what you have to believe about time, about future people, and about the comparability of welfare for the practice to hang together. The standard formula splits the rate into two pieces: pure impatience, and the expectation that future people will be richer. I want you to tell me what each piece presupposes. Does the impatience piece commit you to the view that later lives matter less than earlier ones, merely because they're later? Does the growth piece commit you to treating a person's welfare as worth less when they're rich — and is that the same idea that would make income redistribution look like a wash? And I've noticed a strange duplicity: people will say the discount rate is a technical, descriptive matter of observed interest rates, and then admit that choosing 3 percent rather than 5 percent flips whether a climate policy is worth doing, which sure sounds like an ethical judgement. Reconstruct the implicit theory of value here, and be careful to separate what's strictly presupposed from what's merely compatible with the practice, and to flag where the practice is underdetermined so that several different views would license the same number. Then be concrete: if we swapped in the view that pure time preference is zero — future lives count the same as present ones, and only the growth term does any discounting — what specific features of standard cost-benefit analysis would have to change, and what would stay exactly the same?
