Two people in my reading group got into an argument about Justice Brewer's opinion in *Church of the Holy Trinity v. United States* (1892), and the argument turned on this passage:

"It is a familiar rule that a thing may be within the letter of the statute and yet not within the statute, because not within its spirit nor within the intention of its makers. This has been often asserted, and the Reports are full of cases illustrating its application. This is not the substitution of the will of the judge for that of the legislator; for frequently words of general meaning are used in a statute, words broad enough to include an act in question, and yet a consideration of the whole legislation, or of the circumstances surrounding its enactment, or of the absurd results which follow from giving such broad meaning to the words, makes it unreasonable to believe that the legislator intended to include the particular act."

One of them, Daniel, said the passage is a specimen of exactly what textualism warns against: Brewer admits the church's contract falls within the enacted words, then substitutes his own view of what Congress probably would have wanted because applying the words feels harsh. Daniel argued that once a court says the text is not the law whenever the result is "absurd," the enacted language stops doing the work of fencing the judge in, and "absurdity" becomes a placeholder for the judge's moral and political priors. He added that Brewer's examples show the problem: if courts have been departing from the letter since Plowden, that is a history of judicial rewriting, not evidence that the rewriting is legitimate.

Priya pushed back. She said Daniel was treating the statutory text as if its contribution to law were exhausted by its literal semantic meaning, but legal meaning is not identical to literal meaning. The fact that the words, read in isolation, cover a case is only the beginning of interpretation, because legislators draft against shared conventions about what statutes are for and how far their remedies extend. She argued Brewer is not asking what Congress privately wanted in 1885, but what the statute, read as a public instrument with a recognized mischief, commits the law to. On her view, "absurd results" are not an all-things-considered moral veto; they are evidence that a proposed reading cannot be what the legislature enacted, because legal texts are not senseless commands.

Daniel replied that this collapses the distinction between evidence and authorization. He granted that absurd consequences can be evidence of a drafting mistake, but said the court still has to choose which mistake to attribute to Congress: the inclusion of clergymen, or the absence of an exception for them. If the text is clear, he said, we know which choice the legislature actually made, and the only reason to prefer the other choice is that it yields a policy the judge likes. He added that the exceptions in section 5 — for actors, artists, lecturers, singers, and domestic servants — show Congress knew how to carve out categories, and the fact that it did not carve out clergy is the strongest evidence that it accepted their inclusion, however odd that seems to us now.

Priya answered that this begs the question, because the statute's title and the surrounding debate make the "mischief" the importation of ordinary manual laborers under contract, not the hiring of a rector by a church. She said once the interpreter fixes the statute's object, the specific exceptions in section 5 are intelligible as exemptions for people whose labor did not threaten the evil the statute addressed. She argued that Daniel's method cannot explain why even he would balk at a statute that, by its literal words, required a court to sentence a child to death for a misdemeanor: the reason we balk is not that we dislike the result, but that we recognize the law does not mean what those words, read without context, would command.

Daniel said that was the strongest version of her point, but that it proves only that interpretation needs a threshold of extreme absurdity, not that it should aim at recovering the "spirit" of the statute. He said the test then becomes whether a case is more like the rector or more like the child, and nothing in Brewer's passage tells us how to draw that line without smuggling in an independent theory of legislative purpose.

They asked me to referee, but I found myself moving back and forth. Please work through their exchange and adjudicate it. I want the specific points separated: (1) whether Brewer's passage is best read as a claim about legal meaning or as a claim about equitable discretion; (2) whether Daniel or Priya has the better account of what the section 5 exceptions show; (3) whether Priya's child-misdemeanor example successfully rebuts Daniel's view that clear text must control unless the legislature amends it; and (4) whether Daniel is right that the "absurd results" canon lacks a principled stopping point. Then say who is more right overall, and what, if anything, both parties are missing in the way they frame the disagreement.
