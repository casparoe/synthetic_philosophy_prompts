I have no formal background in economics or decision theory, but I have spent a long time trying to understand why two reasonable people can encounter the same evidence and remain just as far apart. In that rabbit hole I kept running into a claim associated with Robert Aumann: if two perfectly rational agents begin from the same prior, then once each one's probability for a given question becomes common knowledge between them, those two probabilities must be equal. The takeaway people usually draw is that rational, mutually-informed disagreement is nearly impossible, so lasting disagreement between rational people has to come from different priors, from imperfect mutual knowledge, or from a failure of rationality.

I would like to treat that takeaway as a position under pressure rather than accepting it as settled. What would have to be true for that conclusion to be false, or true but much less significant? For each load-bearing assumption — common priors, exact Bayesian updating, common knowledge of each other's probability assignments, full awareness of the same set of relevant possibilities — what would the world have to look like for the assumption to fail in a way that matters, and which of these failures are live features of actual human disagreement rather than merely logical possibilities? I want an honest map of how exposed the standard takeaway really is: rank the possible defeaters from decisive to merely awkward, and say which of them someone who defends the theorem's broader relevance would themselves concede. Please separate what is mathematically settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not present one interpretive camp as if it were the consensus. I suspect the common-prior assumption is doing most of the work, but I would rather you tell me if that suspicion is misplaced.
