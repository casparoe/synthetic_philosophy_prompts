I'm putting together a short position statement for a philosophy of science reading group, and the more I look at my commitments the less sure I am that they hang together properly. I'd rather have someone poke at them before I put my name to it. I'm broadly working within a Kuhnian picture, and here is where I've landed:

1. Normal science and revolutionary science are genuinely differnt kinds of activity, not just normal science with the volume turned up.
2. After a paradigm shift, practitioners live in a different world in more than a metaphorical sense: what counts as data, what counts as a solvable problem, even what the instruments are measuring, changes.
3. Consecutive paradigms are locally incommensurable: there is no neutral observational language or neutral conceptual scheme into which the central claims of both can be fully translated.
4. Even so, theory choice during a crisis is a rational affair. Shared values such as accuracy, scope, simplicity and consistency guide it, though they do not algorithmically pick out a winner.
5. Across revolutions, science makes progress in a strong sense: our theories get closer to the truth about a mind-independent world.
6. The Ptolemy-to-Copernicus shift strikes me as a genuine empirical discovery that the Earth moves, not merely a change of convention or a tidier way of saving the appearances.

Could you audit this for me? I suspect there's friction somewhere but I can't localise it, and I don't want to be told that everything is equally in tension with everything else. Please say which pairs genuinely pull against each other and which only appear to, rank the genuine tensions by severity, and for each say what the cheapest repair would be and what it costs. If any of these commits me to more than I've noticed, I'd want that flagged too.
