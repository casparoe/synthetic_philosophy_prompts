I am writing up a position statement for a graduate seminar that has spent the term on the epistemic regress problem, and I would like a consistency audit before I present. The seminar has worked through foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism, and I have come to hold a broadly foundationalist picture. But I also find myself committed to an assortment of further claims about experience, reasons, and the transmission of justification, and while each claim seems right to me on its own, I cannot tell whether the full set hangs together. I have tried raising pieces of this in office hours, but the advice I got pulled in opposite directions, so I would rather have one careful, structured treatment.

Here is the set.

(1) Foundationalism: the regress of justification terminates, and some beliefs — simple perceptual beliefs are my paradigm — are justified without deriving that justification, even in part, from other beliefs.

(2) Access internalism: if a belief is justified, then the subject can, on sufficient reflection, bring to mind the considerations in virtue of which it is justified. Justification is not a status that floats free of the believer's ability to see what supports the belief.

(3) Only justified beliefs can transmit justification. When one belief makes another justified, the justifying belief must itself already be justified; I find the idea of a belief handing on a justification it does not itself possess nearly incoherent, and I have never had occasion to doubt this principle within chains of belief.

(4) Perceptual beliefs are justified by the sensory experiences that give rise to them, not by further beliefs about those experiences. My belief that there is a red patch before me is justified by the visual experience itself, not by an inference from a further belief that I am having that experience.

(5) Sensory experience is non-doxastic and non-propositional. It has a qualitative character, but it is not a belief or a judgment, it has no truth conditions, and it is neither justified nor unjustified.

(6) A justifier must be able to stand in an evidential or inferential relation to what it justifies — it must be capable of functioning as a reason. Only items with propositional content, the sort of thing that can be true or false and can enter into inferences, can stand in such relations. A bare sensation can cause a belief, but I do not see how a mere cause can be a reason.

Could you work through the set and tell me whether it is jointly tenable? I would like the audit to distinguish the commitments or pairs that are genuinely in tension from those that only appear to be, and to rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally serious. For each genuine tension, say what would have to be given up, weakened, or reinterpreted in order to restore consistency, and what the revision would cost; for each apparent tension, say briefly what the dissolution is. Please write the audit in American English.
