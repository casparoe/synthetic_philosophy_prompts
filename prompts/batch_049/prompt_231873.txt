At a dinner party last month, two friends of mine got into a long argument about what I understand people call origin essentialism: the idea that a person could not have come from a different sperm and egg than the ones they actually came from. I have been turning the exchange over ever since and still cannot settle it, so I would be grateful for a careful adjudication of it. Neither of them studies philosophy formally; they were just arguing it out on their own, and I was mostly listening.

Daniel said he thinks a person could not have come from a different sperm and egg. If a different sperm had won the race, or if conception had happened a month later with a different egg, the resulting child would have had a different genome. And he just is his organism — the organism with this particular genetic makeup — so a child with a different genome would not have been him; it would have been some other possible child of his parents. Priya answered that this was too fast: identical twins come from the very same sperm and egg and have, for all practical purposes, the same genome, yet there are two of them and they are different people. So a given genome, or even a given pair of gametes, cannot be what makes a particular person the particular person they are. She said the DNA argument therefore does not get you to origin essentialism.

Daniel came back that she was conflating necessity with sufficiency. The claim was never that the same sperm and egg could only produce one person — twins show it can produce two. The claim is only that a person could not have arisen from different ones. If his own history traces back through this particular zygote, then a different sperm would mean a completely different starting point with no continuous line to the life he is now living. There is no route from that other gamete to him. Priya replied that, because of the twins, he had quietly stopped identifying himself with the zygote or with a genome, and had made "origin" just a point along a continuous history. But histories can fork or overlap, and the further back one goes, the less that continuity picks out one person rather than another. If he cannot be identical with the zygote — since two people can share it — then his personhood cannot be anchored in that particular bit of early biology the way he originally claimed.

Daniel said he did not see the fork. A different sperm could form a different embryo that grows into a child with a similar upbringing, but that child would simply be someone else; nothing about that possible child would make it him rather than a sibling who never actually existed. Identity questions need some basis, and for living things, origin is the least arbitrary basis there is. Priya answered that this only looks least arbitrary because we fixate on fertilization. She said the person with her name, her parents, and a broadly similar psychological life could have been realized by any number of slightly different early embryos without anything essential about who she is changing. Unless one already assumes origin is essential, she said, one cannot read the individual person back into that point in the causal chain. She suspected origin essentialism is an intuition about labels, not a discovery about people. They asked me to referee, and I honestly could not.

Could you go through their exchange point by point and say who is right about which specific move, where each argument goes wrong if it does, and who is more right overall? Please also tell me how confident you are in your overall verdict, and single out the specific claim or step in the exchange that you are least sure about.
