For a grad seminar on the dogmatism paradox I have to write down where I stand so the rest of the class can pressure-test it, and I honestly can't tell whether the package hangs together. Here's what I find myself wanting to say:

(1) Knowledge is factive: if I know that p, then p is true.

(2) Knowledge is fallible: I don't need entailing evidence or subjective certainty to know something.

(3) Closure: if I know p, and I know that p entails q, then I'm in a position to know q.

(4) If I know p, then any evidence against p would have to be misleading, and usually I can know that before I've even looked at the evidence.

(5) But when new evidence comes in that counts against something I take myself to know, I should normally take at least a small hit — lower my confidence a bit, go check, and not just brush it off as automatically junk.

The case I keep coming back to is this. A detective knows the butler did it on the basis of a confession and matching fingerprints. A new lab report says someone else's DNA was found under the victim's fingernails, and the detective says "I already know the butler did it, so this report has to be misleading somewhere" and doesn't read it. I want to say that detective is being irrational. But I can't tell whether that verdict is the thing that has to go, or whether it sits fine with (1)–(4) and my unease comes from somewhere else.

Audit this for me. Which pairs are actually in tension and which only seem to be? What's the cheapest revision that makes it all cohere, and what would it cost? Rank the tensions by severity, and tell me which single commitment you'd push me to revise first. Also, my classmate keeps insisting that (1) and (2) already conflict; say explicitly whether he's right.
