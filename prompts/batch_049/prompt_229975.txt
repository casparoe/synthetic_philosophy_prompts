My friend Dana is my go-to person for secession arguments. Shes not a lawyer but shes read basically everything on Scotland, Catalonia, and Quebec, and she has strong verdicts she can defend. As best I can reconstruct them:

- Scotland 2014: if Yes had won it would have been a legitimate independence and the UK should have let it happen. The vote was clean, turnout was enormous, and the UK had agreed in advance to respect the result.
- Catalonia 2017: the unilateral declaration was illegitimate and shouldn't have been recognized. But her reason was mostly that the referendum itself was a bust — it was illegal, the unionists boycotted, police were breaking it up, turnout was in the low 40s, so no clear expression ever emerged.
- Quebec 1995: a bare 50-plus-1 in favor would not have been enough, but a genuinely clear majority would have created a real obligation on Canada to negotiate instead of pretending nothing had happened. She basically accepts the Clarity Act idea of a clear majority on an unambiguous question.

Now here's the case she hasn't ruled on. Imagine a prosperous stable democracy where one region has a distinct language and identity, no recent history of oppression, but the national constitution flatly says the territory is indivisable. A regional movement runs its own referendum with international observers: turnout above 70%, and 58% vote for independence. The central government says the constitution settles the matter, refuses to authorize an official referendum, and refuses to negotiate.

If Dana wants to be consistent with her earlier verdicts, what should she say about this new case, and which of her three earlier cases is the closest analog? Walk me through (1) the rule or rules that fit all three of her verdicts, (2) which principle each candidate rule commits her to and what each predicts about the new case, (3) your best guess at her verdict and why you weight one rule over the others, and (4) one further case, real or invented, that would cleanly settle which rule shes actually running.
