I retired from a career in the natural sciences a few years ago and have since been reading systematically in moral philosophy and the historiography of large-scale political violence. A reading group I host has spent several months on the question of comparative atrocity, and two stable positions have emerged. The disagreement now has a name and a shape, but we have never managed to write down exactly what is settled between the two camps and what remains open. I would like you to draft the document the two camps ought to be able to produce together: an adversarial-collaboration statement, not a compromise and not a verdict.

The first camp, the comparativists, holds that ranking historical evils is not only possible but already inescapable: words such as unprecedented, exceptional, and of a different order commit their users to comparative judgments, and doing comparisons openly and carefully is less dangerous than doing them covertly. The second camp, the incomparabilists, holds that ranking — in the sense of issuing graded verdicts about whose catastrophe was worse — is inherently obscene: it forces incommensurable historical experiences onto a single metric and makes victims compete posthumously for standing.

Please draft the statement in four parts.

1. A numbered list of claims both camps can sign. This section should be substantive rather than diplomatic. At minimum, it should state what both camps accept about the role that attention to scale, mechanism, duration, and perpetrator intent plays in historical explanation; what both camps accept about the political misuse of comparisons by states and movements; and what both camps accept about the difference between a coarse ordinal judgment and a cardinal or quantitative score.

2. A disagreements section, set out as a table or a clearly marked equivalent. For each row: a neutral statement of the question; the comparativist answer; the incomparabilist answer. Each camp's answer must be worded so that someone holding that position would accept it as a fair statement of their own view, not a caricature from the other side.

3. A cruxes section. For each crux — a sub-question such that settling it would move at least one camp — give one concrete case, sentence, or kind of evidence that both camps would accept bears on the question, and state in advance what each camp predicts careful examination of that case would show.

4. A short note on how much of the dispute is verbal. I suspect some of it turns on what ranking must mean — a complete ordering with a single worst, as against loose local comparative claims — but do not let that suspicion drive your conclusion. Identify any residual disagreement that survives disambiguation, and say which parts dissolve once the terms are fixed.

Do not adjudicate who is right. The aim is a text both camps could put their names to, with the remaining disagreement made exact. Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this material, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
