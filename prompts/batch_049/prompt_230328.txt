For a graduate seminar on the foundations of game theory, please write the desiderata: what must an account of common knowledge of rationality get right before it earns its place in the foundations? I am asking for a specification, not a survey, and certainly not a reading list.

State the purpose first. The account should be adequate for explaining how rational agents coordinate, or fail to coordinate, under shared and private information, and for underpinning solution concepts such as equilibrium and backward induction. Then give the full requirements list, each with the case or consideration that motivates it. At minimum, address the following.

1. Hierarchical structure. Common knowledge is not simply everyone knowing p; it is everyone knowing p, everyone knowing that everyone knows p, and so on without bound. Why can an adequate theory not collapse this hierarchy into first-level mutual knowledge, and what would be lost if it did?

2. Publicity versus shared private information. A public announcement of p generates common knowledge of p; merely correlated or overlapping private observations do not. The theory must explain this difference and say why it matters for conventions, coordinated action, and coordination failure.

3. Fragility and approximate common knowledge. Consider two commanders who must attack at the same time but can communicate only by messengers that may fail to arrive. Each additional message can confirm an earlier one, yet the infinite chain needed for common knowledge of the plan is never secured. The theory must handle this case and explain why making message loss arbitrarily unlikely is not the same as eliminating it.

4. Modal distinctions. The theory must separate knowledge from belief, certainty from high probability, and common knowledge from common belief or common certainty, and it must say which of these the assumption of rationality actually requires.

5. Content of the rationality assumption. The account must specify what it means for rationality to be common knowledge: whether it requires common knowledge of a unique prior, of utility functions, of the structure of the game, and of the solution concept, or whether some weaker epistemic condition suffices for the results people typically invoke.

6. Constraints from results. The theory must be consistent with, or reproduce, the result that two agents who begin with a common prior and whose probability assignments become common knowledge cannot rationally disagree; and it must say what happens when the common-prior condition is dropped or weakened.

Then mark each requirement as non-negotiable or as a preference of a particular camp, and identify the requirement or pair of requirements that does the most work in excluding candidate accounts. I want special attention to apparent conflicts. For instance, the demand that common knowledge be attainable by actual agents communicating through fallible channels appears to conflict with the hierarchical and fragility requirements; and the demand that common knowledge of rationality underwrite backward induction appears to conflict with the need to respect off-path beliefs. Say whether these conflicts are real or merely apparent, and whether resolving them requires an idealization that quietly forfeits one of the desiderata. Finally, close with a brief scorecard of the main contender frameworks — the syntactic approach via multi-agent modal logic, the semantic approach via partitions over a state space, and the type-space approach that represents the infinite hierarchy of beliefs explicitly — against the list. Keep the scorecard brief; the criteria and their conflicts are where the thinking belongs.
