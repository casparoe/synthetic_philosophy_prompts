I've spent years reading the decision-theory threads on rationalist forums, and I keep noticing a family of moves that people deploy but never quite pin down, and I'd like to know whether it has an established name and exact boundaries. The cleanest case is a one-shot prisoner's dilemma between two players who know they face identical payoffs, identical information, and, as far as either can tell, the same decision procedure. The move says: either both players run the same procedure, or they do not. If they do, then whichever option the procedure selects for me, it selects for the other player in the same situation, so I should compare cooperate-with-cooperate against defect-with-defect and choose cooperate. The usual dominance argument for defecting applies only if my choice can vary while the other player's choice stays fixed; on the symmetry-plus-common-procedure assumption, that cannot happen. The same shape of reasoning shows up in a multiplayer case I keep seeing people use as an illustration: a thousand isolated players are each offered a large prize if exactly one of them sends in a card, and nothing otherwise. If everyone commits to a shared randomizing procedure that sends with probability 1/1000, someone wins with probability about 1/e, and each player's expected payoff is about the prize divided by 1000e. But once a player has seen his own private draw, taking the others' behavior as fixed, he would prefer to send regardless of the draw, since sending is the only way to win and gives roughly a 1/e shot at the prize; so the policy is stable only if he treats the shared procedure, not the individual action after seeing his draw, as the thing he can choose.

What I am trying to identify is what, exactly, this move is. It is not causal decision theory, because there is no causal path from one player's choice to another's; the only link is the hypothesized identity of the decision procedures, or later a common cause that made the players similar. It feels close to evidential decision theory's idea that your act is evidence about what similar agents will do, but the move is usually deployed more narrowly than that: it is invoked to license a particular option or mixed strategy in symmetric interactions where you expect the counterpart to run your exact reasoning, rather than to solve Newcomb-style problems generally. It also seems to need a boundary that people rarely draw. Does the move require literally shared source code, or only a sufficiently strong common cause such as shared training or culture, and how strong is "sufficiently"? Does it require that the choice point be the shared procedure rather than any action that occurs after a private random draw, and does it survive when players have indexical information about their own position? I want to know whether there is an established concept for exactly this move, and, if the nearest concepts only partially match, which one fits best, where it diverges, and what a precise statement of the valid core would say. I will not be satisfied with a bare label; the point is the carving, not the name. At the very end of your reply, please give a single machine-readable line of the form \answer{...}, with the best-fitting established term inside the braces, or a proposed term of your own if no established term fits.
