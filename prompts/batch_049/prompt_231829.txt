I am a journalist covering science and ideas, currently writing a feature about the rhetorical afterlife of limitative results. One analogy I have met several times in interviews and opinion pieces runs as follows: Gödel's incompleteness theorems are to mathematics what Heisenberg's uncertainty principle is to physics. The people who reach for this analogy tend to take it to license a strong conclusion: that any formal, computational, or algorithmic account of a sufficiently rich domain — the mind, an economy, a society — must fall short in principle, because there are built-in, specifiable limits to what can be captured, just as there supposedly are in mathematics and physics. They often add that this should make us modest about modelling, about artificial intelligence, and about the dream of a complete theory of anything interesting.

I would like you to audit the analogy rather than simply restate the mathematics or the physics. In particular:

1. Spell out the intended correspondences — what is supposed to map to what between the Gödelian and Heisenberg settings (a table is fine).
2. Lay out the precise content of each limiting result so the comparison can be tested against the actual claims: on the one side, that any consistent formal system capable of a modest amount of arithmetic will contain sentences that are true in the standard interpretation but unprovable within that system, and that such a system cannot prove its own consistency; on the other, that certain pairs of observables, such as position and momentum, do not admit simultaneous sharp values, with a lower bound on the product of their spreads, and that this is a structural feature of quantum states rather than mere measurement clumsiness.
3. Identify where the analogy breaks: for example, whether the situation of an unprovable but true arithmetical sentence corresponds to the situation of no simultaneous sharp values in anything more than a metaphorical way; whether the Gödelian limit attaches to formal systems while the uncertainty limit attaches to physical states, observables, or measurement situations; and what the analogy quietly imports about truth, knowledge, and modelling when it is extended to economics, psychology, or artificial intelligence.
4. Say, on the basis of that audit, whether the strong conclusion — that there are principled limits to any formal or algorithmic account of the mind, the economy, or society — survives once the disanalogies are made explicit, or whether the analogy has been doing the argumentative work the whole time.

If there is a better comparison for what Gödel's results actually do and do not show outside mathematics, please supply it. Please write the response in British English.
