I'm a retired chemist who's been reading philosophy of mind for a couple years, and last month I fell down the Bongard-problem rabbit hole — those puzzles where you see six little drawings on the left that follow a rule, six on the right that break it, and you have to find the rule. I brought one to dinner with my old labmate Roman and a philosopher friend, Dana. The problem I picked had a large circle and a small triangle in every panel. In the six left panels the big circle sat in the middle and the triangle was inside it, with small variations in position and rotation; in the six right panels the big circle was still in the middle but the triangle was outside it, off in a corner. Both of them got the rule — triangle inside the circle versus not — in about five seconds. Fine. The argument started afterwards, when Roman announced what the two of them had just accomplished.

(1) Roman said the fact that Dana got the rule from only six matching and six contrasting examples shows you can induce a whole concept, inside-versus-outside, from a handful of cases. So much for the idea that concept learning needs huge datasets.

(2) Dana said no, you just noticed one feature the left panels share and the right panels lack. The left panels also share having the triangle near the middle of the frame, and the right panels share having it in a corner. The examples are equally consistent with "the triangle is inside the circle" and "the triangle is central rather than peripheral," among indefinitely many other rules. So the data didn't single out "inside." She picked it.

(3) Roman said that's the usual skeptical move and it proves too much. If the mere existence of alternative rules that fit the examples meant you hadn't induced a concept, then nobody ever induces any concept from any finite data, because you can always cook up a gerrymandered rule that fits everything so far and then flips on the next case. Yet people in fact converge on nestedness here rather than centrality, and on new panels they sort cases the way the intended rule predicts. So the examples, plus a normal person's reasonable biases, determine the concept well enough. Induction doesn't require a uniquely possible rule, just a uniquely sensible one.

(4) Dana said but now you've put the concept's content in the biases, not in the examples. What the six panels did was at most select among concepts you already brought to the table. That makes a Bongard problem a game of guessing what the designer had in mind, not a demonstration that few examples can build a new concept. If I'd already favored "central versus peripheral," that's the rule I would have "induced."

(5) Roman said every learning system brings a hypothesis space or a prior, so the line between "selecting from concepts you already had" and "inducing a new one" is arbitrary. What matters is whether the rule you settle on generalizes to cases you haven't seen. Here it does: give either of us a new panel and we sort it the same way. When that happens, you've earned the word "induction."

(6) Dana said but in a puzzle like this "generalizes" just means "agrees with the rule the author used to build the holdout items." The author made the unseen panels from the intended rule, so passing them only shows you recovered the author's concept, probably because you share her visual habits. Out in the world there's no author choosing the test set. So Bongard problems might be lovely little mind-reading puzzles, but they don't settle whether concepts can be induced from a few examples.

They went around a bit more along these lines, then both looked at me to referee, and I honestly couldnt. Can you adjudicate it move by move? For each numbered move, say whether it's clearly right, clearly wrong, or partly right and why, then say who's more right overall and what both of them are missing. And please seperate what's actually settled in the concept-learning literature from what's genuinely contested, and don't present a contested view as if it were the consensus.
