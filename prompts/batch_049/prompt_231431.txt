I'm on my city's charter comission, and we're about to vote on a term-limit proposal, and I keep going back and forth. My friend Dana is the person whose judgment I trust most on this stuff — she's basically spent a decade in local democratic-reform fights. She's out of contact this month, so I'm trying to reconstruct what she'd say from the positions I've watched her take over the years.

Dana pushed hard for our state's twelve-year term limits on state legislators; her line was that a legislator is supposed to be a temporary citizen, not a career, and once the job becomes a career the reelection tail starts wagging the policy dog. But she testified against a ballot measure to term-limit state supreme court justices, saying judges need long, secure tenure to build expertise and not have to worry about their next job when they rule against powerful people. She helped set up our state's randomly selected citizens' redistricting commission, because the one thing you can't let politicians do is draw their own districts. But she campaigned against a proposal to abolish the state senate and replace it with a lottery-selected chamber of ordinary citizens, saying a standing legislature needs people who actually know how to legislate and who can be thrown out if they're bad. She also opposed term-limiting our elected state treasurer, on the grounds that the office is technical and forced turnover would just leave the state outgunned by bond counsel and investment consultants who've been in the game for twenty years.

Our new case is this: we're considering a rule limiting city councilors to three consecutive terms. The council is part-time on paper but full-time in practice, and a lot of its actual work is technical — zoning appeals, land-use law, water and trash contracts, the city pension fund — and the professional staff is thin, so councilors themselves are expected to know a lot. At the same time, no incumbent has lost in fifteen years, and the same person has chaired the zoning committee for basically two decades. Given Dana's track record, would she support or oppose the three-term limit, and which of her earlier positions is the closest analog? I can see at least two principles that would fit her past calls, and I suspect they point in different directions on this one, so spell out the best candidates, tell me which you think she's actually running, and give me a further case that would cleanly separate them. Don't just give me an "it depends" — I need your best committed guess and your reasons, with a note on where you're least certain. And please write the answer in American English.
