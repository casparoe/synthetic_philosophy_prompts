Last weekend a friend and I had one of those arguments that starts in a group chat about a ballot measure and somehow runs three hours. At some point he said his real reason for supporting the housing measure was that his church's social teaching tells him to. I gave him the standard public-reason line: in a pluralistic democracy you shouldn't lean on reasons that only make sense from inside one religion or comprehensive view; if you're going to support a law that binds everyone, you need reasons other citizens could in principle share. He didn't buy it. He said that's just asking him to lie about his actual reasons. When I offered the usual patch — religious reasons are ok as long as you're also prepared to offer a shareable reason eventually — he said that still treats his real motive as second-class and assumes a clean line between religious and public reasons that almost nobody can draw honestly. Then he added that 'shareable' usually just means 'secular-sounding,' that ordinary voters can't be expected to run this filter whenever they vote (and a vote isn't coercion anyway, it's just my say), and that in a deeply plural society there may be no shared reasons left, so the test is empty. We went in circles for a while and didn't settle any of it.

I'm a political theory MA student, and the argument made me realize I've been using 'public reason' as a label for a position I don't understand below the surface. I'm now thinking of spending a three-year PhD on this cluster, and I can't do all of it; I'd rather come out the other end having actually moved one or two debates forward than having written a tidy overview. If you were me, which two or three open questions underneath that argument are most worth spending the three years on, and why?
