I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a piece about whether some very old philosophical debates are, in part, terminological pileups. I keep flip-floping on the Mencius–Xunzi dispute about whether human nature is good or bad, so I want you to give me a number and make the reasoning behind it explicit.

Here's the proposition: On the most careful, charitable reading of both, Mencius and Xunzi have no substantive disagreement about human moral psychology. By substantive I mean a claim about how humans actually are before socialization, or about what moral education does to us, that Mencius accepts and Xunzi rejects, or vice versa — not a difference in emphasis and not just a difference in which word gets the label "nature." Their famous clash over whether human nature (xing) is good or bad is, on this reading, mostly an artifact of the two of them using xing for different slices of the human animal (Mencius's incipient moral sprouts vs. Xunzi's raw, unworked desires) and using "good" in different ways.

I want two things. First, state a single probability, between 0 and 1, that the proposition is true. Second, and more important, decompose that number. Break it into the sub-questions it actually depends on: whether Mencius is using xing normatively rather than descriptively; whether Xunzi defines xing in a way that simply excludes learned or acquired traits by definition; whether Xunzi would accept something like the sprouts if you just called them "capacities" instead of "nature"; whether they genuinely differ about whether moral development transforms something already present or imposes something external (wei) on an indifferent or disorderly material; and whether the residual disagreement, if any, is about how plastic humans actually are or only about what deserves to be called "good." Assign each sub-question a rough probability or range, show how they combine, and tell me which one your final number is most sensitive to. Also tell me what would move your number most — not a citation list, but a kind of evidence or a conceptual distinction (say, a settled reading of xing as "inborn constitution" vs. "distinctive potential," or a reconstruction of how both authors use the nature/cultivation contrast). For context, my own number is around 0.35 and my editor, who is more invested in the idea that the ancients were talking about the same things we are, sits around 0.7. If you land closer to one of us, say which sub-question most likely explains the 0.35–0.7 gap.
