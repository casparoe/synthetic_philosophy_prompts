I am a political philosopher working on institutional design, and I have been trying to stress-test the familiar contrast between presidential and parliamentary systems against a concrete case. I would like you to take on the role of an expert political philosopher and comparative institutional analyst. Think through the question slowly and systematically, and begin with a brief sketch of the analytical framework before attempting your final verdict.

The case is the United States federal government shutdown of 2018–2019, the 35-day partial shutdown that began when the president and Congress could not agree on an appropriations bill including funding for a border wall, and that ended with a temporary funding measure that did not provide the requested wall money. I want the shutdown analyzed through the lens of the classic critique of presidentialism associated with Juan Linz — the thesis that directly elected presidencies tend to produce rival claims of democratic legitimacy, rigid fixed terms, zero-sum winner-take-all competition, and constitutional deadlock, whereas parliamentary systems, by making executive survival depend on legislative confidence, let conflicts be absorbed and corrected midstream. Lay out the structural contrast and what it predicts about the players in a presidential budget fight: who holds which mandate, what their time horizons are, and where the veto points lie. Then map the actual episode onto that structure. Identify the relevant actors — the president, the two chambers, party leadership, rank-and-file legislators — and reconstruct their options, incentives, and payoffs, with particular attention to the way each side can claim to speak for the electorate. Then give an honest assessment of what the critique explains and what it does not: how much of the impasse belongs to the presidential form as such, and how much to party polarization, divided government, or the peculiarities of the American appropriations process. Finally, reverse the direction of analysis and say what the episode shows about the theory — confirmation, anomaly, or a case the theory never anticipated. Be explicit where the theory is silent, and consider whether a parliamentary democracy facing an analogous budget conflict would actually have escaped it, or merely staged it differently. I am less interested in a chronology than in a disciplined two-way mapping between theory and case.
