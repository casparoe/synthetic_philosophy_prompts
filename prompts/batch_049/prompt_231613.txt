I have been trying to think through what it means to say that a machine or a building carries politics in its very design. The examples that started me thinking are familiar ones: an underpass built too low for buses, which keeps people who ride buses away from a beach; a harvesting machine that is economical only at a scale that drives small farms out. I wanted to find the principle behind such examples, and the more I tried, the more the principle seemed to prove too much. Here is the argument as I now have it.

(1) If an artifact is political in its design, this must consist in something the design itself does: the physical arrangement, independently of anyone's intentions, opens certain courses of action to some people and closes them to others.
(2) Every design does that. A staircase opens an upper floor to people who can climb it and closes it to people who cannot; a door that swings one way is easier for the person pushing than for the person pulling; a bench with armrests permits sitting but not lying down. Each of these arrangements shapes who can do what.
(3) So if the low bridge and the harvesting machine are political in virtue of their design, then staircases, doors, and benches are political in exactly the same way.
(4) But if staircases and benches are political in exactly the same way as the low bridge, then the difference between the famous cases and ordinary objects is not one of kind but at most of degree, and the claim that artifacts have politics is true of everything made. At that point the claim seems to lose its force: it no longer singles out the cases that made it worth taking seriously.
(5) The low bridge and the harvesting machine do seem worth taking seriously. Their politics is not merely the generic shaping that any staircase does; it is something more pointed.
(6) That more pointed character cannot come from the design itself, because by (2) the design itself only ever yields the generic shaping that even a staircase has.
(7) So the pointed character must come from outside the design: the designer's aim in choosing this arrangement, or the surrounding system of interests and constraints in which the object works.
(8) But if the pointed political character comes from outside the design, then the design itself is not what carries the politics. For the interesting cases, the politics of the artifact depends on intentions and context after all.
(9) Therefore the original thought seems trapped in a dilemma: either it is true of every designed thing and thereby trivial, or it is interesting only by ceasing to be a claim about the design itself.

I do not want to accept that dilemma, because the bridge example still strikes me as a genuine case of politics built into a thing. But I cannot see which step I am entitled to give up, or what giving it up would cost. Which step would you reject, and how should the original thought be restated so that it keeps the bridge without turning every staircase and bench into a political case in the same sense?
