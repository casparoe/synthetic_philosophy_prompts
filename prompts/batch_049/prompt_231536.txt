At work I keep hearing AI safety people reach for the same analogy to explain what a low-impact agent is meant to do: it should behave like a good houseguest, or a Leave No Trace camper — leave the place as you found it, don't rearrange the furniture, don't burn firewood the next person would have used. The work this analogy is being made to do is fairly specific: people use it to argue that you can define "avoid side effects" without first specifying what humans actually value. A houseguest doesn't need a utility function over the whole house, the argument goes; they just need a few default rules about tidiness and respect, so likewise an AI can be kept safe-ish just by keeping the world near its baseline rather than knowing what the world should become.

I'm suspiscious the analogy is carrying more weight than people admit. I'd like an audit of it. Give me the correspondence table first: what plays the role of the house, the host, the house rules, the guest's desire to be invited back, the mess left behind? Then the places the mapping breaks. A camper knows roughly what a nice trail looks like to the next human camper, but an AI doesn't automatically know what matters to the beings affected by its actions. The houseguest has a host whose preferences are the point of the whole exercise; an AI might not have a single host, or a host at all. And "as you found it" quietly assumes you noticed the baseline correctly and that your actions are reversible, which a lot of real-world effects aren't. Which of these disanalogies actually matter for the intended conclusion, and which are just decoration? Then tell me straight: once the disanalogies are on the table, does the conclusion survive — can you specify low impact without a full account of what's valuable — or was the analogy doing the argumentative work the whole time? If it was, is there a better framing for low impact, or do we have to admit it can't be made precise without smuggling in some value assumptions?
