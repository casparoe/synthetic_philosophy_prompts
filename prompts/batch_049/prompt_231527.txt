People in my AI safety reading group keep falling back on the same picture for why debate scales to superhuman systems: the courtroom. Two AIs are prosecution and defense, a human judge (maybe with a human jury, maybe with an overworked eng checking the transcript) decides who argued better, and because each side tries as hard as it can to tear apart the other's case, the truth comes out even though the judge could never have produced or checked either argument on their own. The conclusion they draw is that oversight scales: you don't need an overseer as smart as the system, you just need a judge who can tell which side got cross examined hardest.

I'd like an audit of how far the courtroom analogy actually carrys. Give the correspondence table first—what maps to the prosecutor, the defense attorney, the judge, the jury, the rules of evidence, cross-examination, discovery, perjury, closing arguments, appeals. Then walk through where the mapping breaks. I want you to seperate the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion (weak judges still get truth, so debate is a scalable oversight mechanism) from the ones that are just cosmetic. Then tell me whether the conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the courtroom picture was quietly doing all the work. If it can't carry the conclusion, what's a better analogy or model for what debate is actually doing—if it's doing anything truth-conducive at all?
