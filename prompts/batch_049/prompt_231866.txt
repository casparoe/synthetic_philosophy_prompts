I am a philosophy professor and have been asked to give the opening remarks at a university art museum's exhibition of politically engaged work by Black American artists from the 1920s through the 1960s. The audience will be mixed — art historians, practicing artists, students, and visitors from outside the university — and the room will divide over the word "propaganda": some will hear it as an insult to the work, others will insist that serious art must stand apart from politics. I would like the speech, about seven minutes when read aloud, to be organized around W. E. B. Du Bois's argument in his 1926 essay Criteria of Negro Art that art is always and unavoidably propaganda, whether or not it announces itself as such. Please do not quote him verbatim; the audience has not read the essay, and the idea should be rendered in plain, nonacademic language. The central work of the speech should be to dissolve the dilemma between propaganda and art, and to replace it with a sharper distinction — between work that is honest about what it is trying to do to its audience and work that conceals its advocacy inside a pose of neutrality. The aim is to make that distinction do genuine work: it should neither reduce the paintings to political messages nor let the audience retreat into treating art as self-sufficient and above politics.
