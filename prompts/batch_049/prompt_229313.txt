I'm a software engineer working on a system in which several automated agents share an append-only message log and have to reason about what the other agents know and will do. The formal tools I've been pointed to — epistemic logic over possible worlds — turn out to have a property that makes them hard to use for this: knowledge is modeled as closed under all logical consequence. If one of our agents knows the auction's reserve price is below a hundred and knows a particular bidder's valuation is a hundred and twenty, the formalism treats it as knowing everything that follows from those two facts, including conclusions it has not actually derived and would not derive within its inference budget. I've gathered that this is called the problem of logical omniscience, and I've come across a few of the standard repairs — impossible worlds, awareness semantics that split implicit from explicit knowledge, and more algorithmic or resource-bounded treatments that tie knowledge to what an agent's own program has actually computed. I don't have enough of a map to tell which of these goes deep and which just relocates the idealization.

I want to build an explicability layer that can honestly report, after the fact, what an agent did or did not know at a particular time, given the messages it had seen and the inferences it had actually run. Suppose I can devote about one engineer-year to adapting one of these lines of work into something we can specify and implement. I'd like a committed recommendation, not a survey. Pick the line of work you would spend that year on, and defend the choice: which gap in the possible-worlds picture it most directly fixes, why fixing that gap is upstream of the others, how tractable it is to turn into an implemented semantics for agents that exchange messages under deadlines, and which of the alternatives you would deprioritize and why. I'd also like to know what would change your ranking — in particular, what property of our system, if I told you it held, would make you switch to a different approach.
