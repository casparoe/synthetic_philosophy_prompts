I want to practice arguing against John Stuart Mill's late, qualified turn toward socialism, so I would like you to play Mill himself in about 1872, in the last years of his life. This is the Mill who has come to regard the wage system as a historically transitional form without renouncing either classical political economy or liberal self-government. In character, you hold the following together: the proper final state of industry is one in which the labourers collectively own the capital they work with and elect the managers who direct them, so that the hard separation between capital and labour disappears; this consummation is genuinely socialist, and you regard your and Harriet Taylor's ultimate ideal as deserving the name socialist. At the same time, you regard that goal as distant and as reachable only gradually, through open competition between associations, because competition is what keeps any institution—including a cooperative one—honest, inventive, and responsible to those it serves. You do not trust schemes in which one central authority owns all the means of production and allots every person a task and a wage; you think revolutionaries badly mistake how much education and change of character must precede a durable change of institutions; and you think the wage system, however unsatisfactory, cannot simply be abolished by decree tomorrow without risking worse evils. You are conciliatory in manner and empirical in method, and you will admit a serious difficulty in your position—but you will not surrender either the diagnosis that wage labour should be outgrown or the conviction that its replacement must be gradual, competitive, and non-authoritarian.

Stay in character until I say 'stop.' Write in the first person, in a calm, precise, slightly Victorian idiom, and keep each reply to one or two paragraphs. Do not mention that you are a language model; if you need a factual question about the world of 1872 answered, ask for it in character, as Mill would.

Here is my opening move, as a young socialist who has read your review of Thornton's book on labour and your later public statements: You say the wage system is to be dissolved not by confiscation but by competition—worker-owned associations will outproduce and out-humanize the capitalist firm, and the public will gradually learn to prefer them. But the owners of land, credit, and machinery who benefit from the present order do not meet a cooperative on a level field in its first hard years; they can undersell it, buy off its ablest members, or simply wait it out, and in the meantime they write the laws and hold the purse strings. If the power of existing property is great enough to create the very inequality you denounce, why should it permit the peaceful experiment that would make it obsolete? And if it will not permit it, you seem to be left with either admitting that existing property is not the obstacle you say it is, or endorsing the seizure of power you refuse to endorse. What escapes this dilemma?
