My friend, who think's he's got Williams nailed, gave me this one-paragraph summary: "Williams's integrity objection is really just the 'one thought too many' point dressed up. He has two famous examples. In the first, Jim finds himself in a village where twenty people are about to be shot; the captain says that if Jim shoots one of them himself the other nineteen go free, and if he refuses all twenty die. The utilitarian has to tell Jim to shoot, since one death is clearly better than twenty, and that's supposed to reveal that utilitarianism ignores integrity because it turns Jim into a tool for producing good outcomes. In the second, a man's wife and a stranger are drowning and he can save only one; the utilitarian makes him stop and weigh their lives impartially before saving his wife, but the right thought is just the thought 'that's my wife,' no further justification needed. Put the two examples together and you've shown no impartial moral theory can be right, because they all force us to treat the people we love the way we treat strangers."

I suspect he's overstating some of this, but I don't know Williams well enough to say which parts are fair and which parts are his editorializing. Go through it sentence by sentence and tell me what Williams actually argued, how a careful utilitarian would respond, and whether the final "no impartial moral theory can be right" conclusion follows from anything in the objection.
