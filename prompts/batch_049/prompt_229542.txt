Since retiring from scientific research, I have been attending a philosophy reading group, and at a recent session two regular members fell into a long argument about the discursive dilemma. One presented a toy case. A three-person panel must judge whether a candidate satisfies criterion A, whether the candidate satisfies criterion B, and whether the candidate should be hired; every panelist treats the hiring judgment as following from the two criteria together. Panelist 1 judges A satisfied, B not satisfied, and votes against hiring. Panelist 2 judges A not satisfied, B satisfied, and votes against hiring. Panelist 3 judges both criteria satisfied and votes to hire. So a majority judges A satisfied, a majority judges B satisfied, and yet a majority votes against hiring. The presenter said this shows that majority rule can turn three individually consistent sets of judgments into an inconsistent collective one, which is a deep problem for democratic procedures. The other member replied that the panel has not contradicted itself, because a committee is not a mind: the three tallies count different things, and no individual panelist holds the inconsistent combination. The presenter answered that if the panel is only a tallying device, it should never have been asked about the criteria at all; once the panel has counted them, it cannot disregard what the majority affirmed without making its own conclusion look ungrounded. The objector countered that the criteria votes are inputs for the members, not commitments of the group, and that demanding group-level coherence would force one to privilege either the criteria judgments or the hiring judgment, which is precisely what a vote was meant to avoid. Neither gave ground. I would welcome a careful adjudication: at each stage of that exchange, who is right about which specific point, where does anyone overstate or conflate, and what is the real dilemma, if any, underneath their dispute?
