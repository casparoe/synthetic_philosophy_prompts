I’ve been reading William James’s 1884 article “What is an Emotion?” for a seminar on emotion theory, and I keep getting tripped up by the basic move he makes. The passage that sticks with me is:

“Our natural way of thinking about these standard emotions is that the mental perception of some fact excites the mental affection called the emotion, and that this latter state of mind gives rise to the bodily expression. My thesis on the contrary is that the bodily changes follow directly the perception of the exciting fact, and that our feeling of the same changes as they occur is the emotion.”

I’m not sure I even agree with James, but when I read modern appraisal theorists who say emotions are evaluative judgments (anger is the judgment that I’ve been wronged, that sort of thing), it seems like they’re rejecting exactly what James is asserting here, not just adding nuance. But I can also see a reading where James’s “feeling of the same changes as they occur” just is the judgment, or includes it, and they’re actually talking past each other.

Could you write a dialogue between William James and a contemporary appraisal theorist (invented or unnamed is fine) where they really chew on that passage? I want both of them to sound smart—no strawmen. The appraisal theorist should push back on James’s ordering of perception, bodily change, and emotion, and James should push back on the idea that a disembodied evaluative judgment could be an emotion rather than just a thought. Have them respond to each other’s actual points, and maybe by the end the disagreement gets sharper even if they don’t converge. Something around 900–1000 words, natural back-and-forth.
