I've been reading Kierkegaard's remarks on the press and the public in *The Present Age*, and I find myself taking his diagnosis more seriously than I expected. As I understand the relevant passage, he claims that the public is not a determinate body of people but a kind of abstraction or phantom that the press brings into being, and that its effect is to level qualitative distinctions by turning individuals into detached spectators rather than committed actors. I want to know how exposed this claim actually is.

If I were to state the position in my own terms, it would be something like this: mass media, and perhaps a fortiori social media, do not merely transmit individual opinions; they constitute a peculiar kind of collective subject – a 'public' – which abstracts from the individual's capacity for resolute action and tends to reduce qualitative distinctions to matters of disinterested commentary. What would have to be true for this position to be wrong? I am not asking for standard objections from defenders of the press. I want a map of where the position is vulnerable: which empirical findings about media effects, which theories of collective agency, which changes in media practice, or which conceptual considerations would count decisively against it, and which would merely show that its scope is narrower than Kierkegaard thought. Please distinguish between developments that would refute the core claim about leveling, developments that would force me to restrict it to particular media ecologies, and developments that a committed Kierkegaardian could absorb without cost. I am also interested in whether the position has been insulated into unfalsifiability by its own terms, and if so, whether that should worry me.
