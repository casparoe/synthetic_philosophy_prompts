Question. Let (F,d) be a two-person bargaining problem, with F⊂R² compact and convex and d∈F. (i) Define the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution geometrically. (ii) State the four axioms in the Kalai–Smorodinsky characterization theorem and explain how they differ from Nash's axioms, with particular attention to individual monotonicity and independence of irrelevant alternatives. (iii) Let (F,d) and (F′,d) be two bargaining problems with the same disagreement point. Suppose that, for every utility level u₁ of player 1, the maximum utility that player 2 can attain in F while player 1 receives u₁ is weakly lower than the corresponding maximum in F′. State exactly what the individual monotonicity axiom implies in this case, and contrast this with what Nash's axioms permit.

Student answer. The Kalai–Smorodinsky solution is the point where the line from d to the ideal point b=(b₁,b₂) meets the Pareto frontier of F, where bᵢ is player i's maximal feasible utility. Equivalently, after normalizing d to (0,0), it is the Pareto-efficient point satisfying u₁/u₂=b₁/b₂, so the players' gains from disagreement grow in proportion to their best possible gains. The characterization theorem says that KS is the unique solution satisfying Pareto optimality—specifically, weak Pareto optimality—symmetry, invariance under positive affine transformations of utility, and individual monotonicity. Nash differs by replacing individual monotonicity with independence of irrelevant alternatives. Individual monotonicity says that if the feasible set expands, then both players should receive at least as much as before; otherwise a player could be punished merely because new options became available. This condition is sometimes called aggregate monotonicity, and it is the reason KS avoids the Nash pathology in which expanding the feasible set can lower a player's utility. In the situation described in part (iii), player 2's conditional maxima have weakly increased, so individual monotonicity requires player 2's KS utility to be weakly higher in F′. Nash's axioms, by contrast, place no such requirement, because independence of irrelevant alternatives permits the solution to ignore options that would not have been chosen under the old problem.
