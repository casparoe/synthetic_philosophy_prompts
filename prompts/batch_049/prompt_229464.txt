I'm a philosophy student currently taking a course in the philosophy of mind, and we've been working through whether Searle's distinction between original (or intrinsic) intentionality and merely derived intentionality can be made to do real work, and through Dennett's challenge to that distinction. I've been trying to see whether my own verdicts about several cases add up to one coherent account, or whether at least one of them has to go.

Here are my verdicts.

First, when I think right now that my seminar meets at ten, my thought is genuinely about the seminar. It has original intentionality, and its aboutness does not depend on anyone else's interpretation. I would be very reluctant to give this up.

Second, a paper timetable listing the seminar at ten is about the seminar too, but only because the people who wrote it and the people who read it understand it that way. That is, for me, a paradigm case of derived intentionality.

Third, the mercury column in a thermometer does not, on its own, mean that the room is twenty-three degrees. It merely responds to temperature, roughly the way a puddle responds to rain. I do not think it is representing the temperature except as a loose way of speaking.

Fourth, when I look at the Müller-Lyer lines (two equal straight lines whose fins point inward on one and outward on the other so that they look unequal), my visual system presents them as unequal even though I know they are equal. I think there is a genuine, contentful misrepresentation somewhere in my visual system, not merely an outside interpreter's projection onto it. I am fairly settled on this too.

Fifth, a vending machine whose coin slot accepts quarters and rejects most slugs can be fooled: a slug trips the mechanism and the machine drops a beverage. I do not want to say the machine misrepresented the slug as a quarter, any more than a mousetrap misrepresents a twig as a mouse. Its coin-slot states seem to have no content of their own.

Sixth, a honeybee's waggle dance conveys the direction and distance of a nectar source to other bees, and I am inclined to say the dance really is about that location in some sense, even though I doubt the bee has a thought with that content. This pushes me toward admitting a further kind of aboutness, fixed by natural selection or by the colony's behavior rather than by an individual mind.

The principle I keep reaching for is something like this: only conscious minds, and perhaps subpersonal states that are integrated into a conscious mind (like my visual system), have original intentionality; sentences, dials, and code have at most derived intentionality, assigned to them by minds; and mere natural covariation, like the thermometer, has no intentionality at all unless someone starts using it as a sign. But I can feel that principle straining. It puts the vending machine's coin slot and my visual system on opposite sides of a deep divide even though both are subpersonal mechanisms that can be fooled, and the obvious difference between them seems to be just that one is inside a conscious organism and the other is not.

Is there a single precise account that delivers all six verdicts, or should I overturn at least one? If the only accounts that save all six do so by piling on complications, please say so, and tell me which verdict is the most expensive to keep and why. Please state your resulting account clearly, explain how it handles each of my cases and which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and say what it predicts for a case I have not listed: a large language model that answers my questions as if it understood them but, I assume, has no conscious mental life. I am also open to a Dennett-style answer on which the original/derived divide is a matter of interpretive stance rather than a deep difference between kinds of systems, if that is where the cases lead.

Finally, please tell me how confident you are in the main claims you make, and single out the specific point you are least sure of.
