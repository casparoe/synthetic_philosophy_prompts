Write the joint statement that a committed epistemic game theorist — the kind who thinks an equilibrium is what falls out when everyone is rational and everyone knows everyone is rational, all the way up the hierarchy — and a committed evolutionary/learning game theorist — the kind who thinks common knowledge of rationality is a cute idealization that mostly hides the actual process by which equilibria get reached — could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. They've been talking past eachother for years, so the whole point is formulations that are neither vacuous nor slanted, and separating real disagreements from ones that were just about words.

The document should have three parts. First, a numbered agreed-claims section covering at least: what a Nash equilibrium is and why it's a self-enforcing pattern; what rationalizability gets right and where it's too weak; the status of common-prior assumptions and the agree-to-disagree and no-trade-style results; and what they jointly make of the standard experimental findings (beauty contest games, centipede, that sort of thing). Second, a disagreements table where each remaining difference is stated twice — once in the wording the epistemic side would accept as the strongest fair statement of its own view, and once in the wording the learning/evolutionary side would accept. Third, a list of cruxes, with for each crux a concrete game or experiment both sides agree bears on it, and each side's prediction recorded in advance.

I suspect one crux is the electronic mail game (two generals coordinating over email that can fail silently): does the fact that you can never get common knowledge of the message mean rational players should almost never coordinate no matter how many confirmations they've exchanged, or is that a modeling artifact that would wash out under any realistic learning process? Another crux is what to make of the beauty contest data where most people do maybe two or three levels of reasoning — is that a failure of rationality to live up to the epistemic story, or exactly the kind of transient behavior a learning story predicts? You can add others if they're genuinely load-bearing.

Keep the two voices distinct enough that you can tell at every point which camp is talking, without turning it into a dialogue.
