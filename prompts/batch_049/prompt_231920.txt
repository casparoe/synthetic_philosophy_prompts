I have been struggling to reconcile a particular passage in John Stuart Mill's *On Liberty* with his public defence of British rule in India. The passage is the one in Chapter I where Mill states that his doctrine of liberty is meant to apply only to human beings in the maturity of their faculties, and that those who are in a state requiring others to care for them must be protected against their own actions as well as against external injury. He then says that despotism is a legitimate mode of government in dealing with barbarians, provided the end be their improvement, and that liberty, as a principle, has no application to any state of things anterior to the time when mankind have become capable of being improved by free and equal discussion.

I am not asking for a historical verdict on Mill's attitude to India, but for help understanding how the argument of this passage is supposed to work. In the earlier parts of Chapter I, Mill treats liberty as a protection against the tyranny of rulers, and he seems to be arguing for a principle that should limit the power of society over the individual in general. Yet in the passage on barbarians, he appears to suspend the principle for entire peoples on the grounds of their backwardness. I would like to understand the structure of this exception.

Please explain in British English what work the phrase "in the maturity of their faculties" is doing in Mill's argument. In particular, address these questions. Is the exception for "barbarians" best understood as an application of Mill's general principle to the particular circumstances of such peoples, or as a suspension of the principle altogether? How does the claim that despotism is legitimate if its end is improvement relate to Mill's earlier argument that rulers cannot be trusted with unchecked power? Does the exception depend on an empirical judgement about the capacities of particular peoples, or on a conceptual point about what liberty presupposes? Finally, whichever way you answer, indicate one place where the passage seems to you to be in tension with another claim Mill makes in Chapter I, and say how you would resolve that tension.
