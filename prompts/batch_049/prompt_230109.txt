Okay, here's one from my colleague across the hall. He has an argument that seems to prove way too much — that every disposition ascription is either a brute unanalyzable primitive or just false, so we should stop saying glasses are fragile, wires are live, and people are irascible; dispositions, he says, can go the way of phlogiston. I think he's overplaying his hand, but I can't find the exact step where it goes off the rails, and he's annoyingly good at repairing it in discussion. I'd like to keep something like the conditional analysis, since we teach it in intro every year, and I really don't want to be an eliminativist about dispositions. Here's the argument as he laid it out:

(1) For x to be disposed to M in response to stimulus S at t just is for x to be such that, were x to undergo S at t, x would M — provided conditions are right.
(2) If 'conditions are right' just means 'conditions in which x would M', the analysis is circular; if it means 'conditions in which the disposition is present and unblocked', it's empty.
(3) So the right conditions have to be spelled out in non-dispositional terms — impact speed, temperature, absence of saftey devices, that sort of thing.
(4) But no finite, non-dispositional list can close the door: for any list you give, you can build a fink (the live wire with the current-detecting cut-out, which goes dead on contact before it can shock) or a mask (the bubble-wrapped glass, still fragile but it won't break when struck) that slips through the list.
(5) So the conditional analysis can't be patched: every candidate specification either ends up circular, or gets refuted by a fink or mask case.
(6) But the conditional analysis, patched somehow, is the only way to say what dispositions are.
(7) So there is nothing informative to be said about what dispositions are; disposition talk is either unanalyzable or false.
(8) If disposition talk is false, fragility explains nothing and there's no real difference between the glass and the sponge; if it's unanalyzable, then it isn't explanatory in the way we thought either.

He concludes with a shrug that dispositions should go the way of phlogiston, and asks me what I'm going to do about it. Which step should I reject? Explain why it looked fine, and what each of the live alternatives — the intrinsic-base fix, taking dispositions as primitive, or something else I haven't thought of — has to pay to reject it. And if there's an equivocation or a quantifier slide hiding between two steps, I want to know exactly where.
