Write me a deadpan insurance-claim denial letter, around 500 words, from a homeowners' insurance adjuster refusing to pay out after the policyholder's clay statue got squashed. The joke is that the adjuster takes the metaphysics of material constitution fully seriously: the clay and the statue are distinct things that currently coincide, the clay existed before the statue and survives the squashing, so the insured item (the clay) is fine and the thing that got destroyed (the statue) was never covered by the policy. Let the comedy come from the adjuster doing careful metaphysics in corporate-ese — sortals, coincidence, one thing constituting another, a sniffy aside about people who think they're identical, maybe a line distinguishing being squashed from being destroyed — and from him following the position all the way to a straight-faced conclusion a normal person would find deranged but that's internally consistent. Format it like a real claims letter, with a claim number and a sign-off. A metaphysician should recognize the constitution view and think the reasoning is fair; a reader who's never heard of the puzzle should still get the joke and feel why it's hard. Don't let him slip into monism halfway through unless that's the joke. American English.
