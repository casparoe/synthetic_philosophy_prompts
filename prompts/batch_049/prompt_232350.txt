A friend and I spent most of yesterday evening arguing about the measurability critique of effective altruism, and I came away unsure whether the critique can be made coherent or whether it collapses under its own weight. The critique, as I understand it, holds that cost-effectiveness reasoning of the sort effective altruists favor systematically privileges interventions whose results are legible and countable—malaria nets, deworming programs, direct cash transfers—and implicitly undervalues interventions whose main effects are diffuse, delayed, or institutional, such as policy advocacy, movement-building, or reform of public agencies. On this view, the difficulty of measuring an outcome gets converted, without argument, into a judgment that the outcome matters less.

By the end of the evening, however, my friend had convinced himself that the critique proves too much. He restated his reasoning as follows. (1) If the measurability critique is correct, then any serious assessment of an intervention must account for its diffuse and systemic downstream effects, not only its directly measurable ones. (2) Diffuse and systemic effects ramify without any natural stopping point: a policy reform shifts incentives, which alters how firms and bureaucracies behave, which changes the coalitions that can form, which changes which further policies become available, and so on. (3) An assessment that must account for effects with no natural stopping point cannot assign a justified expected value to any intervention, since any point at which one stops tracing consequences is arbitrary and the untraced remainder might reverse the verdict. (4) If no intervention can be assigned a justified expected value, then no comparison of interventions by expected cost-effectiveness is warranted. (5) But at least some such comparisons are warranted; for instance, other things equal, we are justified in ranking a program with well-measured evidence of large positive effects above a program with well-measured evidence of no effects. (6) Therefore, the measurability critique cannot be correct: generalized in this way, it would abolish the practice of comparing interventions rather than improving it.

I find the argument difficult to shake, yet I also think the original critique is getting at something real. Where exactly should one push back? Identify the premise or inferential step most worth rejecting, explain why that premise or step initially looked secure, and say what a defender of the measurability critique would have to accept, or give up, in order to avoid the conclusion that no intervention can ever justifiably be ranked above another.
