I am giving a paper next month to a workshop in ancient philosophy whose regulars work mostly on Aristotle's ethics and politics, with a few Hellenistic specialists. My thesis is that when Aristotle says money is "a guarantee of exchange in the future," he is not giving a storage-of-value account of money in the modern sense. He is saying that money allows citizens to remain in a continuous scheme of reciprocal justice across periods when their needs do not coincide, so that a transaction can be completed now without the other party needing anything from me now. The guarantee is not between present goods and future goods, but between members of a political community who cannot always need one another at the same time. I want to argue that this future-guarantee function is a piece of political justice, not merely a convenience, and that it explains why Aristotle anchors money in nomos rather than phusis.

The passage around which the talk turns is Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics V.5, 1133b (trans. Rackham):

"Now money serves us as a guarantee of exchange in the future: supposing we need nothing at the moment, it ensures that exchange shall be possible when a need arises, for it meets the requirement of something we can produce in payment so as to obtain the thing we need. Money, it is true, is liable to the same fluctuation of demand as other commodities, for its purchasing power varies at different times; but it tends to be comparatively constant."

I need a Q&A prep sheet, not a restatement of the paper. The audience likely contains: one reader of the Ethics who insists that "future exchange" just means deferred payment or credit; one Politics specialist who will say my reading makes money too natural a feature of the polis, when Aristotle's treatment of usury treats money-making as contrary to nature; one Hellenist who will hear anachronistic echoes of fiat money and social contract theory; and one scholar of justice in the Ethics who will say that reciprocal virtue is about proportional equality between unlike producers, not about a community's promise across time. Give me the eight or so objections I will actually get, phrased the way those people would put them, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks. End the prep sheet with a single sentence beginning "Summary:" that states the most dangerous objection and the core concession or reply in one breath.
