Write a dialogue between two philosophers who run into each other at a workshop on causation in tort law. One of them holds that omissions are full-blown causes: a failure to do something can cause an outcome in the same sense that a positive action can. The other holds that calling an omission a cause is always normatively loaded shorthand, and that the real causal work is done entirely by positive processes and events. Neither should be a strawman, and neither should change their mind just to be polite; I want real back-and-forth, with each character actually responding to the other's best moves and conceding when a case is genuinely awkward for their own view. Work through concrete cases: the gardener who doesn't water the plant, a doctor who withholds a ventilator in triage, a bystander who doesn't throw a rope to a swimmer whos drowning, and a case where someone fails to close a floodgate, contrasted with a second floodgate that is actively opened. Have them also press on whether the metaphysics and the law can come apart here: does anything change for liability or blame if omissions aren't literally causes? End by having them locate the residual disagreement: is it about the world, about our concept of causation, or about which contrasts we care about for legal purposes?
