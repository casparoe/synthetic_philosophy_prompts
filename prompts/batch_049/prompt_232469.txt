I spent most of my career as an analytical chemist, and since retiring I've gotten into philosophy, mostly ethics. Lately I've been going back and forth with my daughter about what to do with some savings, and the same question comes up on a small conservation trust I sit on, where we have to decide how much of a bequest to spend now versus invest for projects decades down the line. My daughter works in finance and insists the rational answer is to spend now: a dollar today is worth more then a dollar in forty years, so future benefits should count for less and we should get on with enjoying the present.

I see the investment logic, but I keep thinking that a benefit which actually lands in someone's hands in forty years doesn't become less important just because it's late in time. That seems like a different kind of claim than saying the money will grow if invested, or that the people then will likely be richer and need it less. In fact I suspect my grandkids will be better off than I was, and maybe the money really does more good now. But I don't want my conclusion to be driven by confusion. There's another wrinkle: I notice I care a lot less about a nice meal next month than about that same meal tonight, yet I don't care any less about a nice meal forty years from now than about one in thirty-nine years. That inconsistency makes me distrust my gut on this whole question. How should a philosopher sort this out? Is there a defensible reason to treat a benefit in forty years as worth as much as the same benefit now, or is my daughter basically right, and what should the answer depend on?
