I’m giving the toast at my former PhD advisor’s retirement dinner. He spent forty years defending something close to Sidgwick’s line in The Methods of Ethics that no moment in time is metaphysically special just because it’s “now,” and that pure time preference—discounting something merely because it lies in the future—is as arbitrary as discounting something because it lies to the left. I’ve known him since I was his graduate student twenty years ago, and he actually lived this: he would tell people panicking about deadlines that a due date is just a future “now” with delusions of grandeur, and he meant it as consolation, not evasion. Now the room is half philosophers and half family and old friends. I need a five-minute toast that does three things at once. First, it has to honor the man warmly—this is a retirement, people are here to feel affection, not to hear a seminar. Second, it should make the time-neutrality idea do real work in what the toast can and cannot say: a normal retirement toast talks about the value of looking back on a career, but that framing quietly privileges the moment of retrospection, and I want the speech to notice that and find its tenderness in his own terms—perhaps by treating this evening as no more and no less important than all the ordinary Tuesdays ahead of him, and that being exactly the point. Third, the philosophers in the room should feel the idea was handled with genuine precision, while his sister in the back should not feel condescended to or bored. One structural constraint: don’t turn the toast into a disguised lecture, and don’t make the joke in the opening so byzantine that only the three people nearest to it catch up five seconds later. The speech should land as a toast from someone who understands the theory well enough to sit with it lightly. Please write it.
