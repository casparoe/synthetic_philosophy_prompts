I'm a software engineer working on a planning system that has to make allocation decisions under uncertainty, and I've been reading outside my field to understand an argument that keeps resurfacing among my colleagues: whether the system should treat known probabilities and unknown probabilities differently. That reading keeps leading me back to the Ellsberg paradox and the long-running debate about ambiguity aversion, and I've been struck by how little the debate seems to have converged even though the basic empirical phenomenon is not in serious dispute. In the standard two-urn version, with equal monetary stakes, people prefer a bet that pays off if a red ball is drawn from an urn known to contain fifty red and fifty black balls to a bet that pays off if a red ball is drawn from an urn with an unknown mix of one hundred red and black balls; they also prefer the corresponding black-ball bet from the known urn. Those two preferences cannot be represented by any single probability assignment over red and black in the unknown urn, and the pattern is usually chalked up to ambiguity aversion. Beyond that, however, the field splinters: some treat ambiguity-averse choice as a bias that a rational agent should correct, some treat it as a reasonable response to missing information, some argue that the right formal response is a model with sets of priors or non-additive probabilities, and some argue that risk and ambiguity are different kinds of uncertainty and should not be forced into a single preference framework at all.

Since nobody appears to disagree about the choices people actually make, and since the competing theoretical responses have coexisted for decades without moving one another, I've formed a working hypothesis: the disagreement is less about evidence than about what a theory of rational choice is supposed to be accountable to — whether revealed choices constrain what rationality can require, or whether our standards of rationality determine which choices deserve to count as informative. But I'm outside my training here and I may be conflating several debates. I'd like a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement rather than a verdict for one side: is this a hidden factual or formal dispute, a difference in normative starting points, a partly verbal disagreement, or a case of the two sides answering different questions under one heading? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a formal result, a conceptual distinction, a different experimental design, or a reframing of the question — and if no progress should be expected, why not?
