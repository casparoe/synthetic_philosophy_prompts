I am a PhD student in philosophy working on the epistemology of aesthetic judgment, and much of my dissertation turns on how one understands the pessimist position in the aesthetic testimony debate. I recently encountered the following characterization in a survey chapter, and although it struck me as broadly plausible, I keep worrying that it moves too quickly in its final steps. I am not looking for references or a reading list; I want an accuracy audit of the characterization on its own terms.

The chapter summarizes the pessimist roughly like this. Aesthetic judgments draw their authority from the judger's own felt response to the object; a judgment that a sonata is graceful or a film is harrowing counts as one's own in the full sense only if it is grounded in first-hand experience. Feelings, unlike facts, cannot be handed across by description, so testimony can at most tell me that someone else has responded in a certain way — it cannot give me the aesthetic judgment itself. Kant is credited with establishing this point: on his account a judgment of taste rests on the subject's own feeling of pleasure rather than on concepts, and a verdict borrowed from another person would therefore be heteronomous rather than a genuine judgment of taste. From this the pessimist is said to conclude that trusting critics cannot put one in a position to appreciate or evaluate a work, and that someone who calls a film brilliant on the strength of reviews speaks without authority. When the optimist replies that we rely on expert testimony in every serious domain, the chapter reports, the pessimist has a decisive answer: ordinary testimony transmits facts, whereas aesthetic testimony transmits only preferences, and preferences cannot serve as evidence. The chapter therefore closes with the claim that, on the pessimist's view, deferring to critics in matters of beauty is always second-rate and, in the final analysis, rationally indefensible.

What unsettles me is the last inference. The early Kantian-sounding points seem to establish at most that a testimonial belief is not my own aesthetic judgment, not that treating critics as guides is irrational. I have seen a distinction floated between an epistemic reading of the pessimist's thesis — testimony cannot justify aesthetic belief — and an autonomy reading — testimony cannot by itself produce taste or first-person appreciation — and I suspect the summary slips from the second to a much stronger version of the first, but I am not confident enough to say precisely where. Could you go through the summary claim by claim, separating what a careful pessimist would accept, what is a common simplification, and what is a misrepresentation or overstatement? I would also like to know whether the chapter's closing conclusion follows from the accurate parts once the corrections are made.
