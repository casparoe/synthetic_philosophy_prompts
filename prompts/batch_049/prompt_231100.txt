I want you to draft the joint statement that a committed provenance-theorist and a committed perceptualist about the value of art could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. The provenance theorist holds that part of an artwork's value depends on facts about how and by whom it was made, facts that need not show up in anything you could see in the object itself. The perceptualist holds that an artwork's value supervenes on its perceptible properties, so a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of the Mona Lisa would have the same value as the original, and any extra value people put on the original is sentiment or status, not something the work itself grounds.

They've been talking past each other about perfect forgeries for years, so I want the document to earn its keep. Structure it like this:

1. A numbered agreed-claims section — things both sides will genuinely sign: about what happens to a work's market value and to our treatment of it when an attribution is debunked; about which valuations of an original are instrumentally sensible (as evidence, art-historical record, social coordination) rather than fetishist; maybe about which standard arguments each side is happy to abandon. Keep it from being vacuous (no 'people hold different views about fakes').

2. A disagreements table. Columns: the sub-question; how the provenance theorist would phrase their answer; how the perceptualist would phrase their answer; and whether you think the disagreement is substantive, partly verbal, or just about words. Each side's phrasing has to be wording that side would actually accept, not a strawman.

3. The two or three cruxes — the sub-questions such that if you settled them, at least one side would actually move — and why those are the load-bearing ones.

4. For each crux, a case or test both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Include at least one of these scenarios: a forger who, by chance, produces a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of an existing painting without ever having seen it; a famous painting that turns out to be by the master's student rather than the master, with nothing about the canvas changing; and a fire where someone can save only one of two visually identical works and knows which is the original.

Also flag any place where you think the residual disagreement is really a disagreement abotu the word 'value' (or about how to weigh different kinds of value) rather than about the objects themselves.
