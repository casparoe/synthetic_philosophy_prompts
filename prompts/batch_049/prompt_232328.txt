As a novelist who moves between writers and critics, I have watched a debate about aesthetic testimony run in place for years without shifting. The dispute always takes the same shape. One side holds that an aesthetic judgment about a novel, a poem, or a performance can be acquired and justified only through direct first-person acquaintance with the work. On this view, a critic may tell me that a book is masterfully constructed, but that report tells me something about the critic, not something that gives me an aesthetic reason of my own. The other side finds this position close to mysticism about private experience, or perhaps an overvaluation of epistemic autonomy. They point out that in science, law, and ordinary practical life we routinely depend on competent testimony, and they insist that no principled line has been drawn showing why aesthetic matters should be different. Both camps agree on the surrounding social facts: people are plainly influenced by reviews, taste is formed in part by communities, and even a firsthand encounter can be shaped by what one has been told beforehand. Yet the argument does not move, and the same examples are traded back and forth as if they were new evidence.

My working hypothesis is that the two camps are not really answering one question. The first side is trying to describe the first-person phenomenology of literary evaluation, while the second is trying to describe the social division of cognitive labor. But I am not sure that diagnosis is right rather than self-serving. The impasse might instead reflect a hidden disagreement about what an aesthetic judgment is, or about whether deference in matters of taste is a defect or a reasonable response to limited time and expertise, or about bedrock intuitions concerning the value of arriving at one's own evaluation. I want a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement itself. What kind of disagreement is this, and what would actually count as progress given that diagnosis? A sharper distinction, an empirical result about how people's aesthetic responses change under testimony, a different way of framing the question, or perhaps an argument that the debate should be dissolved rather than resolved. I would like the diagnosis to engage the strongest versions of both positions, not the caricatures, and to say honestly whether anything could move it.
