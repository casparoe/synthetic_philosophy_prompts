I'm giving a talk next month at a solid but not fancy philosophy department, and I want to walk in knowing exactly what the Q&A is going to do to me. The room is basically analytic normative ethicists: two Kantians, a consequentialist who does population ethics, a virtue ethicist, and a couple of people in applied medical ethics; there's also a grad student who works on care ethics and will probably be the friendliest person there. My talk defends a Confucian-style role ethics. The thesis, bluntly, is that what someone ought to do is not derived from a universal principle — the categorical imperative, maximizing welfare, whatever — but from the roles they actually occupy (daughter, teacher, neighbor, colleague) and the virtues internal to those roles. My argument has three moves: (1) our actual moral life is role-structured in a way impartial principles flatten out, (2) a universal principle can't by itself tell you how to weigh the claims of people whose lives are entangled with yours, and (3) role ethics explains why agent-relative obligations feel weightier than impartial ones without treating that feeling as bias to be corrected.

What I need from you is a prep sheet for the question period, not a defense. Give me the objections these particular people will raise, in roughly the order they'll come and phrased the way each one would actually put it — the Kantian will not object the way the consequentialist does. For each: does it have real force or can I swat it, what should I concede up front so I don't look like I'm dodging, and what's the two-minute reply? Mark where each reply is softest. And tell me the one question I should be praying nobody asks. Also: the virtue ethicist and the care-ethics grad student are going to say role ethics is just their view in different language — should I fight that or lean into it, and where's the actual difference?
