Question: Explain the difference between dialetheism and paraconsistent logic. Does adopting a paraconsistent logic for a given subject matter commit one to dialetheism? If not, describe one non-dialetheist reason someone might adopt a paraconsistent logic.

Student answer: Dialetheism is the view that there are true contradictions — that at least one sentence of the form A and not-A is true. Paraconsistent logic, by contrast, is a family of logics in which the principle of explosion fails; the principle of explosion says that from a contradiction any sentence follows. Classical logic validates explosion, so any inconsistent theory formulated in classical logic is trivial, in the sense that every sentence is derivable. Dialetheists therefore need a paraconsistent logic; otherwise their true contradictions would force them into trivialism, the view that every sentence is true. But the dependence runs the other way as well. One would reject explosion only if one already thought some contradictions were true; if no contradiction is true, a rule blocking the inference from a contradiction to everything else is pointless. Moreover, in practice paraconsistent logics all give up the law of non-contradiction, and giving up the law of non-contradiction is exactly what dialetheism consists in. Adopting a paraconsistent logic therefore commits one to dialetheism, and the two views stand or fall together: there is no coherent non-dialetheist motivation for paraconsistent logic.
