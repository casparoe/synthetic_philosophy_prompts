Two colleagues of mine spent most of an incident postmortem arguing about a single sentence in the report, and neither would concede. The system in question is an LLM-based robotic picker in our warehouse. During the incident it repeatedly tried to restock bin B-14 despite the bin's being full; the items were present but obscured by translucent shrink-wrap, and the picker's occupancy system failed to register them. The report I had drafted said the picker "believed bin B-14 was empty." One colleague wanted the sentence kept; the other insisted it be struck before the report reached the review board. What began as an editing question turned into a dispute about whether taking the intentional stance toward such a system licenses belief-language, or only makes it a vivid shorthand.

(1) Priya: Keeping the sentence is defensible. It is a compact and behaviorally accurate description: the picker acted on the information available to it, and had the bin actually been empty its behavior would have been correct. Deleting "believed" obscures the diagnostic point that the failure lay in the picker's model of the world rather than in its motor control or its goals.

(2) Marcus: But "believed" names a relation to a proposition, and the picker stands in no such relation. What happened is that a camera image was processed, a segmentation model classified the visible region, an occupancy estimate crossed a threshold, and the planner selected a restock action. The word "believed" presents that pipeline as a mind with a point of view on the world, and that is exactly what we should not put in front of a review board.

(3) Priya: That pipeline story is a lower-level redescription, not a rebuttal. We use belief-talk for systems like this because the higher-level state — the occupancy estimate — tracks the world and systematically supports counterfactuals: had the shrink-wrap been absent, the estimate would have been high, and had the estimate been high, the planner would not have restocked. A state that can misrepresent the world and that guides action in virtue of its content is precisely what "belief" is for.

(4) Marcus: But that pattern is too cheap. A miscalibrated thermostat can "misrepresent" the room, and its state guides action in virtue of what it covaries with, yet no one would write that the thermostat believed the room was 22 degrees. Counterfactual robustness and the possibility of error show only that a system has a variable that can come apart from the world; they do not show that it has a representation.

(5) Priya: The thermostat is different because its covariance is so impoverished that the lower-level description remains perfectly usable. For the picker, the content of the estimate does explanatory work across a range of environments and goals, and the same state issues in different actions depending on context. If that is not enough for belief, then your standard would also rule out attributing beliefs to people, since I infer your beliefs only from what your behavior covaries with.

(6) Marcus: With a person I have evidence of another kind: the person can report and revise, and I share the same reasons myself. But even granting that asymmetry, my point is about what we should write in a postmortem. "The picker believed the bin was empty" reads as an explanation in terms of a mistaken judgment, and that quietly moves the review from "what should we fix" toward "who made the wrong call." If the sentence is kept at all, we should say that the occupancy estimator misclassified a partially obscured bin, or that the picker acted as if the bin were empty.

Then give an overall verdict: who is right about which specific point, where each side overreaches, and what the two are jointly missing. In particular, assess whether Priya's charge in (5) — that Marcus's standard would also rule out attributing beliefs to people — is fair, and whether Marcus is right in (6) that "believed" does different work in a postmortem than "acted as if." A mixed verdict is acceptable, but be precise about the concession each party should make.
