I am a novelist at work on a book whose protagonist is someone whose scrupulous attempts to act for the right reasons keep misfiring in worldly terms. That project has pushed me toward a specific view of moral worth, and I want it stress-tested rather than defended. The view is this: an action has full moral worth only when the features of the situation that the agent treats as her reasons for acting coincide in content with the features that actually make the action right; mere conformity to the right-making features is not enough, and neither is a subjectively upright intention that has no grip on the actual normative situation. Treat this position as fixed and tell me what would have to be true for it to be false. Which cases, intuitions, or arguments would count against it, and which would merely be awkward for it? What does it predict that its main rivals—motive-of-duty views, outcome-based views, and views that bracket the agent's cognitive grip on the facts—do not predict? Where is it most exposed, and where is it well insulated, and are those points of insulation a strength or a weakness? I would personally find most persuasive either a case in which the agent's reasons coincide with what actually makes the action right and yet the action seems to lack full moral worth, or a case in which the reasons fail to coincide and yet the action seems to have full moral worth. I want a map of the view's exposure, including what its defenders would themselves have to concede counts against it.
