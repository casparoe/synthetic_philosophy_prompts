I'm trying to get clear about what the living owe the real dead when they turn them into characters. A historical novel gives a real nineteenth-century poet a long affair with her neighbor; the affair is invented, the poet's surviving letters are conspicuously cool about the man, and the book is plainly fiction — I'm fine with it. A biopic marketed as the true story shows a real politician burning a letter that in fact survives unburned in a public archive — not fine; it passes off a falsehood about a real person's acts as documented fact. A television series gives two real people an unrecorded private conversation on the evening before one of them dies, every line invented but consistent with their known characters — fine. A docudrama supplies the last words of a well-documented general, though the family holds a private letter recording different final words and nothing supports the screenwriter's version — not fine. A clearly fictionalized screenplay imagines a real abolitionist's private journal in which he repudiates abolition as a youthful error — not fine, even though the film never claims the journal is authentic. In another draft, the same imagined journal restates the abolitionist's documented positions in plainer language than anything surviving in his papers — fine. A novelist writes a novel-in-letters in which a real twentieth-century memoirist confesses that the childhood hardship at the center of his famous memoir was fabricated — not fine, again despite its being announced as fiction. A painter said repeatedly that her private journals should be burned; after her death her estate releases them, and a screenwriter uses them to depict her final years accurately — I keep going back and forth: the film is true to the journals, but using them at all feels like a violation I can't yet name. The principle I keep reaching for is that invention is permitted when it fills what the record leaves open and forbidden when it contradicts what the record fixes. But that principle seems to license the repudiating journal and the fabricated-memoir confession as easily as the restating journal, since all three are explicitly fictional and contradict nothing on record, and I still want to forbid the first two. So either the principle needs a further clause, and my statement of it is underdeveloped, or some of my verdicts should go. Which is it? State the strongest candidate principle or principles precisely, with the case that motivates each clause; say what they predict about further cases I haven't listed; and tell me honestly which of my verdicts they cannot accommodate. In particular, I want to know whether the line between the two drafts of the imagined journal can be made principled, and whether my unease about the burned journals commits me to something stronger about posthumous privacy than my other verdicts do.
