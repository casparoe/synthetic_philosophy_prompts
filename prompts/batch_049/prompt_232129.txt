Below is an exam question and a student answer from an upper-level ethics course. Grade the student answer and provide point-by-point comments justifying the grade. In particular, identify any claims that are accurate, any that are inaccurate or overstated, and any steps in the argument that do not follow.

Exam question:

In W. D. Ross's translation of Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics, Book II, chapter 1, Aristotle writes:

"Again, it is from the same causes and by the same means that every virtue is both produced and destroyed, and similarly every art; for it is from playing the lyre that both good and bad lyre-players are produced. And the corresponding statement is true of builders and of all the rest; men will be good or bad builders as a result of building well or badly. For if this were not so, there would have been no need of a teacher, but all men would have been born good or bad at their craft. This, then, is the case with the virtues also; by doing the acts that we do in our transactions with other men we become just or unjust, and by doing the acts that we do in the presence of danger, and being habituated to feel fear or confidence, we become brave or cowardly."

Explain why a situationist critic might take this passage to support the view that Aristotle is committed to robust global character traits, and evaluate whether Aristotle's account of habituation contains resources to answer the situationist challenge.

Student answer:

The passage shows that Aristotle treats virtue as a habit produced by repetition, just as skill in a craft is produced by repeated practice. The situationist critique says that small changes in a situation, such as being in a hurry or the presence of an authority figure, are better predictors of behavior than supposed character traits. So if Aristotle were right that doing just acts makes somebody just, then someone who has repeatedly done just acts should stay just even in high-pressure situations. But experiments like Milgram's obedience study and Darley and Batson's Good Samaritan study show that people do not stay consistent. Therefore Aristotle is wrong to think that character traits explain behavior. Aristotle could reply that habituation is only the acquisition of virtue, not the full exercise of it, and that full virtue involves reason and choice. He could also say that these experiments show only that many people are not fully virtuous, not that no one is virtuous. So the situationist critique does not really damage Aristotle's account.
