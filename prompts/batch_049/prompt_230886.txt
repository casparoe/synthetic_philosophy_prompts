I'm building a handout for an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of mind, on functionalism and the multiple realizability argument. Please write a decision tree (or a flowchart, if that works better) whose entry point is a single question: can two systems with almost nothing physiological in common—for example, a human, an octopus, and a silicon robot of the appropriate kind—nonetheless be in literally the same mental state, such as being in pain? Build the tree from the distinctions rather than from a list of names: each internal node should be a concrete case or a commitment an undergraduate could judge without already knowing which position it leads to, and each leaf should be a named position in the multiple-realizability debate with a one-sentence account of its most awkward commitment. The leaves should include at least type identity theory, classical (role) functionalism, realizer functionalism, a view on which each species or structural kind receives its own local reduction of the mental, and a skeptical view on which genuine multiple realization is far rarer than the canonical examples suggest. Use the nodes to press the distinctions that actually split the territory: whether multiple realizability is an empirical claim or a conceptual possibility, whether it is the role or the realizer that does the causal work, and what to say when a role is realized by a wildly disjunctive collection of physical states. Also flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot cleanly separate, and note any empty combination of answers with a brief diagnosis of whether it is empty because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely underexplored. Ten minutes should be enough for a student to work through it.
