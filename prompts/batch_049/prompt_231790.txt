A friend and I have just spent a long evening arguing about whether we are ever warranted in saying that a large language model believes, knows, or intends something. My friend thought the resistance many people feel to such talk is a confusion, and he supported his position almost entirely with one analogy. We do not hesitate, he pointed out, to say of a chess engine that it wants to control the centre, thinks a knight is hanging, or fears for its king, even though nobody imagines a homunculus inside the machine. Those ascriptions, he argued, are instances of the intentional stance: a predictive strategy we adopt toward a system whose behaviour is too complex to track usefully at the level of code, and which carries no commitment to hidden qualitative states. Saying that a language model believes that Paris is the capital of France, he concluded, is the same sort of move — a way of making its outputs intelligible and predictable, not a report of private goings-on. The analogy was doing the real argumentative work: he took it to show that ordinary belief-talk about language models is perfectly defensible, and that those who object to it are either confused or inconsistent.

I would like you to audit how far that analogy actually holds. First, give an explicit mapping: what in the language-model case is supposed to correspond to the chess engine's board representation, its evaluation function, its search, its fixed objective of winning, and the well-defined standard of good play it is scored against? Second, identify where the mapping breaks. Which features of the chess-engine case does the analogy quietly import into the language-model case — a fixed goal, a bounded problem domain, an external success criterion, a reasonably transparent causal path between the state it represents and the move it selects, a stable policy, and so on — and how much does each disanalogy matter for the specific conclusion my friend drew? Third, deliver a verdict: does his conclusion that belief-talk about language models is respectable in just the way chess-engine belief-talk is respectable survive the audit, or was the analogy carrying an overgeneralisation? If you think a better analogy captures what we are doing when we attribute beliefs to language models, say what it is, and what it can and cannot support. Please write your answer in British English.
