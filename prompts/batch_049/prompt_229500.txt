I am chairing a university committee that must approve or deny family-care leave under a policy requiring sustained caring for a seriously ill family member, and the committee has built up precedents. In Nel Noddings’s Caring, she distinguishes caring-for a particular other, understood as engrossment in the other’s particular reality and displacement of motivational energy toward that other’s good, from caring about someone at a distance; she also says the caring relation is completed only when the cared-for receives the caring, though caring can continue toward someone unable to give an ordinary response. Our prior decisions do not settle which strand the committee has implicitly adopted. In past cases, the committee granted leave to a nurse caring for a temporarily sedated patient by tracking subtle physiological cues; granted leave to a daughter caring for a mother with advanced dementia who no longer recognizes her but whose facial expressions and restlessness the daughter reads; denied leave to a son who pays for a home health aide for his father from across the country and never visits; denied leave to a husband who performs all his wife’s physical care mechanically and resentfully without ever looking at her face; and granted leave to an aunt who sits with her depressed nephew daily and follows his shifting worries. The new case is an employee who seeks leave to care for his spouse in a persistent vegetative state after an accident: he spends four hours a day at her bedside, reads to her, tracks changes in skin tone and breathing, and says he can tell when she is comfortable, but there is no evidence that she is aware of him. Predict the committee’s decision, identify the rival rules that fit the precedents, state what each rule yields for this case, give your best guess and reason, and name the further case that would settle which rule they are running. Please respond as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
