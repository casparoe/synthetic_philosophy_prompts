Me and a friend spent a while last night going back and forth about Thomson's lamp, and he ended up defending a claim that seems way too strong to me. He says the lamp doesn't just show that this one made-up task is underdescribed—it shows that any process chopped into infinitely many sub-acts is incoherent, so ordinary motion is in the same boat. Heres the argument as I wrote it out afterwards:

(1) Suppose a lamp gets switched on and off at times before t=1 like this: on at 1/2, off at 3/4, on at 7/8, and so on, so that all the switches happen within one minute. (2) At t=1 the lamp has to be either on or off—it can't be both and it can't be neither. (3) It can't be on: if it's on at t=1, then some switching-on must have been the last switch, not followed by any later switch, but in this series every switching-on is followed by a switching-off. (4) It can't be off: same reasoning, every switching-off is followed by a switching-on. (5) So the lamp is neither on nor off at t=1, which is impossible. He says that contradiction shows there's no coherent way to complete infinitely many acts in finite time. (6) But a ball rolling from A to B can be described as covering half the remaining distance, then half of that, and so on—same structure. (7) So continuous motion is no less incoherent than the lamp, and our whole idea of a process unfolding over time is broken.

I feel like something slipped around (3) or between (6) and (7), but every time I tried to name it he said I was just insisting the math is on my side. Which step should I actually reject, and what does rejecting it force me to say about what a supertask description has to include before it fixes a final state? Id rather keep (6) than give up on motion, but I want to know what im paying. And write the answer in American English.
