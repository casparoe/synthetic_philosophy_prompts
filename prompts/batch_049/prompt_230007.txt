I serve on the programming committee of a small independent cinema, and for several months we have been deadlocked over a proposal to stage a retrospective of a director whose early films are, by the committee's own admission, important and formally accomplished. After those films were made, the director was reported in detail, and by many people, to have committed serious and repeated abuse; several collaborators have described long-term harm. Within the committee, the facts of the abuse are not disputed, and neither is the claim that the early films are in an ordinary sense good. What is disputed is whether we can or should present them, and the discussion has by now settled into two positions that no longer engage each other.

One side argues that the films are autonomous aesthetic objects: the abuse is not in them, the compositional and performative qualities are what they are, and a programming committee's business is to show works of artistic importance rather than to adjudicate the private conduct of their makers. They say refusing the retrospective collapses artistic judgment into moral judgment and amounts to a quiet form of censorship. The other side argues that, whatever may be true of private appreciation, separation is impossible in an act of programming. A retrospective is a public act of honor that trades on and reinforces the director's reputation, often returns money and prestige to the director or the estate, and asks the abused, and the audience, to set the harm aside. They add that these particular films were made under working conditions the abuse helped create, so to call them good while bracketing those conditions already takes a moral position.

I am not asking which side to take, and I do not want a survey of familiar arguments for each. I want a diagnosis of why the argument has stalled. My working hypothesis is that the two sides are answering different questions under one slogan — one side about properties of the work, the other about the ethics of the act of presenting it — but I am not sure that is right, and I do not know what would follow if it were. Is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one about the word 'separate', a collision between kinds of reasons that cannot be weighed on a common scale, or something else? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress here — a distinction, a changed question, a different institutional frame — or should no one expect the two positions to move each other? The season must be announced regardless, so I need a diagnosis that lets us see what we are actually disagreeing about, even if it does not tell us what to book.
