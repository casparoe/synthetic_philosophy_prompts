Student's answer:

The disjunction problem challenges theories that explain a mental state's content by reference to what causes it. If a representation R is caused by F and also by G, a simple causal theory seems committed to saying that R means "F or G." In the standard example, a frog's fly-detector fires for flies but also for bee-bees (small dark pellets). Read off its causes alone, the detector would mean "fly or bee-bee." But that is the wrong content: the state means fly, not a disjunction. So a causal theory needs some principle that selects one cause, rather than the disjunction, as the content.

Millikan-style teleosemantics supplies that principle by appealing to function. A state's content is fixed by what it has the proper function of indicating. The frog's detector means fly because indicating flies is the function the detector has, and bee-bee tokenings do not get into the content because the detector never had the function of indicating bee-bees. On this view, the relevant selection history is the frog's learning history: during the frog's development, tokenings caused by flies were followed by successful feeding while tokenings caused by bee-bees were not, and this differential reinforcement is what recruits the detector to represent flies. That is also what explains misrepresentation. When the detector fires at a bee-bee, it is exercising its function of indicating flies under nonstandard conditions, so it misrepresents the bee-bee as a fly rather than veridically representing a disjunction.

Fodor's asymmetric dependence theory is often advertised as a non-teleological alternative. For Fodor, R means F just in case Fs cause R-tokenings and, for any G distinct from F that also causes R-tokenings, the fact that Gs cause R-tokenings depends asymmetrically on the fact that Fs cause R-tokenings. In the frog case, bee-bees cause the detector to fire only because flies cause it to fire — the bee-bee connection rides on the fly connection — whereas the fly connection does not depend on the bee-bee connection. Fodor takes this asymmetric dependence to be a historical fact: the fly-to-tokening connection has evolutionary priority because it was selected for in the frog's ancestors, while the bee-bee connection is parasitic on that already-selected connection. So, although Fodor avoids appeals to the organism's current purposes, he still fixes content by way of the species' selection history.
