I've been trying to get clear about what it takes to follow a rule, as opposed to merely behaving in a way that happens to accord with one. Below are my gut verdicts on several cases, all involving the rule for addition. I'd like you to say what principle — or small set of candidate principles — best captures these verdicts, stated precisely, with the case that motivates each clause of it.

1. Anna is taught addition using only examples in which both addends are below 57: 2+3=5, 34+22=56, and so on. Asked 68+57 for the first time, she answers 125. I think she is going on in the same way she was taught and is correct.

2. Ben has the same public history as Anna — the same lessons, the same examples, the same answers — but when first asked 68+57 he says 5. He insists that he always understood the sign as follows: if both addends are below 57, answer their sum; otherwise answer 5. I think Ben is not following the rule Anna learned, but he is following a different rule, which I'll call quus. I can't point to any piece of evidence that distinguishes him from Anna, but the verdict still seems right to me.

3. Carla also has the same public history as Anna, but when asked 68+57 she answers 7, and says the rule simply tells her to answer 7 here. I think Carla isn't following any arithmetic rule at all; she's making it up and dressing it in the language of rule-following. But I can't yet say what, if anything, distinguishes her from Ben.

4. Duarte lives in an isolated village where everyone is taught from childhood to answer 5 for any sum with an addend of 57 or more, and otherwise to give the ordinary sum. They call this practice 'plus.' I think the villagers are not making a mistake when they answer 5 to 68+57: 'plus' in their mouths just is quus. They are not following our rule, but they are following their own rule correctly.

5. Elena is taught ordinary addition, then spends forty years alone, and in that time works out that 68+57=125. I think she can be right, and that her being right does not consist in anyone's being present to agree with her. But this sits uneasily with 4: if a village's agreement can make 5 the correct answer, why can't the absence of any community make that correctness lapse?

6. Felix knows addition but is distracted and answers 124. I think he was still following the plus rule and made a slip; occasional errors do not stop someone from following a rule.

7. A pocket calculator is wired to compute sums but has a fault that makes it output 5 whenever an addend is 57 or more. I think the calculator is not following any rule at all, even though its behavior is quus-like. Rule-following seems to require a standpoint or a standard that the calculator lacks.

Please give me an account of rule-following that gets as many of these verdicts as possible, and be candid about which of my verdicts, if any, it cannot accommodate. Say what the account predicts for each of the seven cases. Then apply it to a case I haven't listed: a person raised with no community, who invents a private rule for sorting stones, follows it successfully for years, and then applies it to a new case in a way no one could have anticipated; is she following her earlier rule? Finally, say how confident you are in the account you recommend, and identify the single point you're least sure of.
