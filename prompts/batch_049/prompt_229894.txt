I have spent my career in the physical sciences, where a theory earns its keep by specifying in advance which phenomena it must reproduce and which anomalies it must not explain away. Now that I am reading moral philosophy seriously, I find myself wanting the same discipline before I go further into Adam Smith’s account of the impartial spectator. In Part VI, section iii of The Theory of Moral Sentiments, Smith distinguishes two standards a person may use in judging his own character and conduct: an idea of exact propriety and perfection, and a more forgiving standard set by the degree of approximation to that idea which is commonly attained in the world. He observes that most people rest content with the second, while the wise and virtuous keep their principal attention on the first.

Write the desiderata that a theory in this area must meet. I want a specification: what any adequate account of the impartial spectator as the mechanism of moral self-assessment has to deliver—which cases it must get right, which distinctions it must respect, which psychological facts it must explain or accommodate, and where it is allowed to remain silent. In particular, address the tension introduced by Smith’s two standards: an adequate theory should explain how the same person can apply both standards, why the lower standard exerts such a strong pull, and how the impartial spectator is supposed to correct for this without collapsing into an empty ideal. Flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied. Then, briefly, score Smith’s own account, a contemporary affective-mirroring account, and a normative constructivist account against the list, but keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on the scoring.
