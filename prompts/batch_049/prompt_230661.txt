I chair the naming and monuments review committee for a mid-sized city. Each quarter we receive a staff research memo on a historical figure whose name stands on a school, park, or monument in the city, and we must recommend whether to remove or relocate the honor, keep it unchanged, or keep it with added interpretive or counter-commemorative material such as a revised plaque, a paired memorial, or a classroom unit. The meetings are public and heavily attended, and in nearly every case someone rises to say that the figure should not be judged because he was a man of his time. The city council requires a written rationale for each recommendation, and our committee includes historians, teachers, neighborhood representatives, and descendants of the communities most directly affected; most members have no background in philosophy. We have roughly two hours of deliberation per case, and our procedure must be defensible to a council auditor who reads the rationale without attending the meeting.

I would like you to turn the "man of his time" defence—taken at its strongest and most charitable, not as a lazy slogan—into a reusable decision procedure we can actually work through in those two hours. The procedure should be a sequence of questions in order, with a clear account of what counts as a sufficient answer to each; it should say where it stops with a definite recommendation, where it hands the question to a person because it calls for a value judgment rather than a historical or conceptual one, and where it sends us back to the researcher for missing facts. Please be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, where the procedure is likely to misfire, and what it cannot settle—for example, how to weigh a living community's experience of a name against the blamelessness of a long-dead person when the two point in opposite directions.

Then run the procedure on the three cases currently on our docket: Thomas Jefferson (a high school named after him), Winston Churchill (a park statue whose plaque emphasizes wartime leadership), and Christopher Columbus (a downtown monument proposed for relocation to a museum). For each, state which factual premises the procedure takes from the staff memo rather than asserting on its own, identify the conduct actually under review, and show where the procedure yields a clear answer and where it does not. Include at least one case in which the procedure's output would go against what the committee—and I myself—initially wanted, and say what would have to be true for us to override the procedure honestly rather than merely changing the rule to get the answer we wanted to begin with.
