For a philosophy of physics seminar on the ontology of time after special relativity, write a decision-tree instrument that sorts someone who has just worked through the Rietdijk–Putnam argument into the position on time they actually hold. The instrument should diagnose by first-order commitments, not by self-description.

The tree must use exactly two cases as its diagnostic material. The first is the Andromeda walkers case: two people walking respectively toward and away from Andromeda pass each other; because their now-slices cut the galaxy at different days, one says the invasion fleet has already launched while the other says the launch is still future. The second is a three-event relay case, which you should restate explicitly so the reader can follow the tree: Alice and Bob are in relative motion and pass each other; Alice's now-slice contains two spacelike separated events G1 and G2, while Bob's now-slice contains G1 but not G2, so Bob places G2 in his future.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of named views. First identify the diagnostic questions and the combinations of yes/no answers that are coherent; then locate these positions in that space as leaves: block universe or eternalism, on which every event exists tenselessly and there is no objective passage; presentism committed to a privileged global foliation, on which only one privileged present slice exists; growing block with a privileged global edge, on which past and present exist but the future does not; moving spotlight with a privileged foliation, on which all times exist and an objective present moves along a privileged slice; the deflationary or indexical response, on which being real and being now are frame- or perspective-relative and the argument's transitivity step fails; and an open-future view, on which events that are future for a given observer lack determinate truth values. For every coherent answer-combination with no named occupant, state whether it is unoccupied because the position is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Every branch question must be a concrete yes/no question about one of the two cases, answerable without already knowing which position it leads to, and must not use any -ism vocabulary in the question itself. The tree must include a question about the transitivity of co-reality — whether G2 is real for Bob, given that G1 and G2 are both real for Alice and G1 is real for Bob. It must also include a question about whether the physics requires an objective global present, and a question about whether there is a single frame-independent fact about whether the fleet has launched in the Andromeda case. You may add further questions if the logical space requires them.

Flag explicitly any pair of leaves your questions cannot separate; in particular, if presentism and the growing block cannot be told apart by your tree, say what additional case would be needed. At each leaf, give the position's name and one sentence on its most awkward commitment in light of the two cases.

Audience: advanced undergraduates with one semester of special relativity and no formal training in metaphysics. Keep the tree to at most four branch questions and no more than ten leaves. End by compressing the tree into a three-question sorting quiz with a scoring key. Include no citations, reading lists, or bibliographic information.
