I'm a software engineer, not a philosophy person, but I got asked to give a short talk at our local rationality meetup (maybe 30 people, mostly devs, a couple of EAs, some people into crypto) defending Pascal's wager as something more interesting than the usual "believe just in case" argument people meme on. My angle is roughly: Pascal's wager is best read as a decision-theoretic argument about what it's rational to do when you have nonzero credence in an infinite payoff, and the standard objections (which god? infinite expected value is incoherent, etc.) aren't as fatal as people think, especially if you treat the "act as if you believe" part as a bootstrap procedure rather than a demand for instant belief. I keep coming back to that part in the Pensées where Pascal, after laying out the betting logic, basically says if you're still not convinced, don't retreat into more arguments: imitate the life of the faithful, go through the motions — he mentions taking holy water, attending Mass, and says that will naturally deaden your cleverness and bring you to belief. That move strikes me as the most important and most misunderstood part of the whole fragment, because he's not just doing decision theory, he's doing something about the feedback loop between behavior and belief. Anyway, my talk is a defense of that reading.

Before I give it, I'd like a Q&A prep sheet. The audience is smart but not academic-philosophy trained; they'll think in terms of expected value, simulation arguments, Pascal's mugging, and maybe some of them are allergic to anything that smells like apologetics. Draft the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this crowd, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way a skeptical engineer at this meetup would actually phrase them. For each: a two-minute reply, and a note on where the reply is soft. Also tell me the question I'm hoping nobody asks, because it'd force me to give up more than I want to. I should add that I'm not religious myself and I'm not trying to convert anyone; I'm defending the structure of the argument as a piece of decision theory, and I want my answers to stay in that register rather than slipping into apologetics.
