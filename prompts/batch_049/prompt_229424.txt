Act as a professional philosopher who specializes in Keynes’s ethical essays, and who also happens to write luxury brand copy on the side. First think carefully and brainstorm three possible angles for a deadpan parody piece; then pick the best one and write it.

The genre is a luxury wealth-management brochure, around 600 words, for a firm that sells a “post-scarcity portfolio” to clients who can’t stop worrying that they might no longer need to worry about money. But the subtext should be Keynes’s view of the good life: that productivity is on track to solve the economic problem, that the love of money as a possession rather than as a means to the good life is a kind of sickness left over from an earlier stage, and that the real test will be whether we can learn to enjoy leisure and do things for their own sake instead of dressing up our old money anxieties in fancier clothes. Do not name Keynes or the essay, and don’t use any direct quotes from him. The satire should land the real tension—the idea that a world that’s solved scarcity would be terrifying for people whose identity is built on earning, hoarding, and signalling competence through labour—without ever explicitly saying so. Don’t make it too on-the-nose.

After the piece, write a short note outside the satire saying how confident you are that this is a fair reading of Keynes, and flag the one point you’re least sure about.
