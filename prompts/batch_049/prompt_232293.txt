I am a clinical psychologist at a regional hospital and have recently been put in charge of a new multidisciplinary service for adults with persistent physical symptoms that lack a sufficient structural explanation: chronic widespread pain, functional neurological symptoms, persistent dizziness, and similar presentations. Over the past few years I have become convinced that predictive processing and the free energy principle describe what I see in this clinic better than the older habit of dividing these patients into organic or psychogenic categories. But I cannot hand my colleagues a theory; I need something they can actually run.

Please write me a reusable, thirty-minute initial-assessment procedure, grounded in predictive processing and active inference, that a physiotherapist, a psychiatric nurse, or a junior doctor could follow without reading the underlying theory. It should be a step-by-step procedure: what to ask or observe first, what a given answer tells you, where a step stops and refers back to a physician, and where a step is a clinical judgment call rather than an inference from the framework. It should distinguish the work done by the person's prior expectations, the confidence assigned to sensory prediction error (especially interoceptive error), and the person's attempts to manage uncertainty through action, including the ways those actions may confirm the very model they are meant to reduce, but it must not use that vocabulary with patients. By the end, the procedure should produce a one-page formulation identifying the most plausible maintaining loop, one intervention aimed at that loop, and a short note on what early evidence would indicate the formulation is wrong. A consultant physician must countersign the formulation before it reaches the patient, so the procedure must flag anything that requires medical review first. My team sees these referrals weekly, and the stakes are real.

Then run the procedure on two hypothetical referrals, showing each step:
1. A 41-year-old man with three years of widespread pain and fatigue following a minor road traffic collision, entirely normal imaging and blood tests, high health anxiety, repeated internet searching and requests for reassurance, and progressive avoidance of physical activity.
2. A 27-year-old woman with panic attacks, constant pulse checking, and avoidance of exercise after one episode of palpitations.

For at least one of the two, let the procedure deliver an answer the team will not like, for instance discontinuing a reassurance practice the clinic currently regards as kind or standard. Be explicit about what the procedure cannot rule out, including new or previously missed medical disease, about where it could mislead someone without specialist training, and about which parts are value decisions that theory alone cannot settle.
