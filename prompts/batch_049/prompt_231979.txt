I am a student in a philosophy of education course, and we have been working through Whitehead's educational essays. I would like a counterfactual reconstruction, as rigorous as you can make it, of Whitehead's likely verdicts on two developments that came after his death. The first is adaptive, LLM-based tutoring systems that continuously estimate a student's current level and adjust explanations and exercises to it. The second is high-stakes standardized testing of the sort used to rank schools and gate progression. Please derive each verdict explicitly from commitments Whitehead actually defended: above all, his critique of inert ideas (ideas received without being used, tested, or connected), his account of the rhythm of education as moving through romance (fresh, half-formed interest), precision (disciplined command of detail), and generalization (active, integrated use), and his views on freedom, discipline, and the connectedness of knowledge. Do not rest the answer on general impressions of his temperament. Where the two cases cut differently, say so—I suspect the same philosophy might pull in opposite directions for individualized software and for standardized assessment, but I want to see exactly which principles do the pulling. Give me your best-supported definite reading in each case, specify the premises doing the work, and then flag the points at which the evidence underdetermines the answer and a rival Whiteheadian reading could reasonably diverge.
