I am taking a seminar on early modern philosophy of education this term, and we have just spent two weeks on Locke's *Some Thoughts Concerning Education*. I want to prepare for a paper by arguing against Locke's use of play as a pedagogical technique, and I would like you to play Locke himself to help me see whether my objection actually lands.

Please stay in character as John Locke as he writes this treatise — observant, attuned to the actual conduct and temper of children, willing to grant that some children are exceptions or that a parent may need to adjust the method to the child's constitution, but never willing to give up the central claim that learning should be made as much like play as possible and that compulsion is generally self-defeating. Speak in the first person, keep your replies to a paragraph so it feels like a conversation, and use only concepts available in 1693 plus whatever I tell you about later findings. If you need me to explain a term that would not have been available to you, ask me as Locke might ask, but do not break character.

Here is my opening move. You say in *Some Thoughts Concerning Education* that a child should learn to read before he is aware of it as a task — that, for example, we should give him ivory letters and a dice-like game in which he forms syllables, so that he thinks he is merely at play and does not perceive the design upon him. But later you also say that the foundation of virtue is for the child to deny his own desires and submit his will to his parents' reason, and that this habit of self-denial should be established as early as possible. My objection is this: if the child's first experience of learning is that it must arrive disguised as a game, how is he ever supposed to form the habit of doing what must be done simply because reason and duty require it? Does not your method teach him that any activity not wearing the costume of amusement is something from which he may legitimately withhold his attention? In short, is the child you form in the nursery capable of later sitting through the grammar lesson, the sermon, or the ledger, that no one has made into a game? I suspect your play-based method and your discipline-based account of virtue are in tension, and I would like to hear you answer from within your own account rather than merely restating that play works.
