im trying to settle, once and for all, whether talk of DNA carrying information or being a genetic code is literal or just a really useful metaphor, and I keep noticing that people argue past each other because one person means Shannon-style correlation and the other means semantic content. Before I read another paper on it, I want the spec. Write me the desiderata for a theory of genetic information: the list of requirements any account has to meet for the claim "DNA carries information" or "the genetic code is a real code" to be more than a loose way of talking. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it, and mark which you think are non-negotiable and which are just preferences of one camp. Then flag any pairs of requirements that might not be jointly satisfiable -- if the list itself amounts to an argument for or against literalism, I want that part especially. a table is fine.

Some things I already suspect need to be handled, but I want you to make the case for or against each rather than just take my word for it: the arbitrariness of codon assignment (the triplet-to-amino-acid mapping is not forced by chemistry, so an account cant fall back on chemical necessity to explain why a sequence says one thing rather than another); the no-homunculus problem (the "reader" is tRNAs, aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases, and the ribosome, not a mind); translation as blind molecular machinery; synonymous codons, wobble, and how a misreading differs from a changed message; noncoding DNA, pseudogenes, regulatory sequence -- does information attach to all of it or only to what gets translated; what the information is supposed to be about (protein sequence? phenotype? something else) without the account circling back on itself; the fact that the same sequence can do different things in different cellular contexts (splicing, methylation, developmental environment); the fact that high Shannon information and functional importance come apart (a random sequence can be information-rich in one sense and do nothing in the other); how genuinely new coding sequences can arise by mutation and selection without an outside informant; and the bootstrapping one -- the genome encodes the very synthetases and ribosomal components that implement the reading of the code, so the account has to allow the code to specify its own interpreter without a vicious regress or a designer. If you think some of these are red herrings, say so and why.

Finally, keep the scoring short: a paragraph each on how the main camps (causal/Shannon, semantic/teleosemantic, and outright eliminativist/metaphor-only) fare against your list. I care about the criteria more than the scorecard.
