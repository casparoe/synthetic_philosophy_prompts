While preparing a piece on how small expert panels make high-stakes decisions, I spoke with two political scientists about a three-member research ethics board that had to decide whether to approve a clinical trial. The board's own rules said the trial could be approved only if two conditions both held: the consent procedure was adequate, and the risks to participants were acceptable. The members recorded these votes:

- Member A: consent adequate — yes; risks acceptable — yes; approve — yes.
- Member B: consent adequate — yes; risks acceptable — no; approve — no.
- Member C: consent adequate — no; risks acceptable — yes; approve — no.

A majority therefore found the consent procedure adequate, and a majority found the risks acceptable, but a majority also voted to reject the trial. The minutes recorded both affirmative findings alongside the rejection.

The first scientist argued that this is a collective inconsistency, and that the board should have used premise-based aggregation: settle each condition by majority vote, then apply the board's own rule mechanically — which would have approved the trial. A body that affirms both stated conditions and then declines the conclusion that its own rule requires is, he said, answerable for a contradiction in its record.

The second scientist countered that a committee is not a mind and does not have beliefs. Different majorities can form on different questions; there is no entity, "the board," that simultaneously holds both findings. The only defensible course is conclusion-based aggregation: vote on the approval itself and let that verdict stand, without imputing group positions on each premise.

The first replied that conclusion-based aggregation merely relocates the problem: if the board still records the reasons for its decision, the two affirmative findings reappear next to the rejection; if it does not record them, the decision becomes opaque. A body that gives reasons cannot disown the coherence of those reasons. The second answered that premise-based aggregation has its own defects: it combines different majorities — the majority for consent is not the majority for risk — and then attaches a conclusion that no majority was ever asked to endorse. It also invites members to vote strategically on the individual conditions, rather than sincerely, in order to steer the final result. He added that the real lesson is that no aggregation rule can satisfy every attractive desideratum at once, so choosing between them is a design trade-off, not a correction of error.

Please adjudicate the argument point by point: which specific claims is each scientist correct about, where does either one overstate, and what, if anything, are they both missing?
