I've been reading on my own about whether made objects can be political—whether a bench, a bridge, a turnstile, or a piece of software can carry values in its very design rather than only in how people choose to use it. Recently I got to hear a live version of the argument at a community meeting about some new park furniture, including a bench with a center armrest that makes it impossible to lie down flat. Two residents went back and forth, and I've been replaying it ever since.

One resident said the bench is a political object: its shape is designed to prevent lying down, and the people most affected are those with nowhere else to sleep. Once a design reliably makes some actions easy and others impossible, he argued, it distributes power without anyone having to issue an order. He called that politics built into metal. The other replied that this mistakes an effect for an intention: the bench has no intentions, no views about homelessness, and enforces nothing; only people do. She said that if the armrest deters sleeping, that is because of choices made by people, not because the object is political, and that by this logic a steep hill would be political because it keeps out wheelchairs. The first responded that the hill is not designed and the bench is, and that this is the whole difference: when the people who make an object know—or refuse to know—who its shape excludes, and they keep the armrest anyway, the discrimination is now built into the environment and persists even when no decision maker is present. The second came back that every design excludes someone—stairs exclude wheelchair users, a high hoop excludes short players, a narrow doorway excludes people with strollers. If all such artifacts are political, she said, the word covers everything and explains nothing; what the first resident was really objecting to was a choice made by powerful people, and that is a property of the choosers, not of the bench.

I could not tell who was getting the better of the exchange. Please adjudicate it move by move: rate each of the four moves on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, state which specific claims each side has right, and say what, if anything, both parties are missing.
