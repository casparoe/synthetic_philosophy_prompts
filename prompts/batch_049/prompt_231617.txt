A friend of mine and I recently spent a long evening arguing about the gender-equality paradox in career choices — the much-discussed finding that, by various indices, more gender-egalitarian countries often show larger rather than smaller sex differences in fields such as STEM versus care-oriented work, alongside related patterns in math confidence and reading performance. My friend insisted the lesson was simple: once formal discrimination and material constraints recede, people's underlying inclinations express themselves more freely, so the pattern is at least partly "natural," and egalitarian policy should stop expecting parity. I kept pushing back that this conflates the removal of formal barriers with the removal of cultural influence; that gendered scripts may persist, or even become more salient, in exactly the cultures that most loudly endorse equality; and that the finding might partly be an artifact of how "gender equality" and "STEM" are measured, or of broad occupational categories that hide finer-grained differences. The argument ended, as these arguments do, with each hypothesis able to tell a post hoc story about the same data.

I have since been asked to help a foundation shape a five-year portfolio of roughly ten research projects on this exact question. I do not want a literature survey or a reading list. I want a defended research agenda. What are the main open questions about the gender-equality paradox, and which of them should a limited funder prioritize if the goal is to determine what actually follows for policy — and what does not? For each question you would prioritize, I would like to see the reasoning: whether it is upstream of other questions, whether its answer would change a concrete recommendation about education, labor-market policy, or the design of equality indices, and how tractable it is with available methods such as cross-national surveys, natural experiments in educational reform, cohort studies, or conceptual analysis of the measures themselves. I would also like an honest account of which popular implications are overrated or simply do not follow — for example, that the paradox refutes social construction, that it shows parity in STEM is the wrong target, or that it shows cultural intervention is futile — and of what sort of evidence over the next few years would move a question up or down your ranking. Finally, I suspect some of the most important unresolved questions are conceptual rather than empirical: what, exactly, should count as a free or autonomous choice of career; whether choices formed under cultural influence require different treatment from choices formed under formal discrimination; and whether parity is even a defensible benchmark. Please tell me how much of the portfolio should be spent on that conceptual work relative to the empirical questions, and why.
