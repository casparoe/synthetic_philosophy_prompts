The marketplace app I run trust and safety for makes users do their deals in plain chat messages, and we have to decide whether a flagged message is a promise or offer we'll enforce, a threat we remove the user for, or just someone venting. The moderators making these calls are contractors with no philosophy background and about ten minutes a case, and every week decisions get reversed because two people read the same sentence differently. I've been told speech-act theory is the right lens here — that saying something can do something, that promises and threats are acts rather than descriptions — but I've never seen it turned into something my team can actually run. Write me a decision procedure for a flagged message: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to move to the next step, where a message fails to be a promise/offer/threat rather than merely reporting the speaker's state of mind, and where the procedure has to stop and punt to a human because the theory itself has a live dispute underneath it. Keep it grounded in the real distinctions — explicit vs implicit performatives, felicity conditions, uptake, sincerity, direction of fit, the difference between the act succeeding and its perlocutionary effects — but translate them into moderator language, no jargon dumps.

Then run the procedure on three test cases: (1) "I promise I'll pay you Friday" where the sender later says it was a joke among friends; (2) "I'll give you 50 if you hand me that thing" where the sender claims they were just musing about a hypothetical, not making an offer; (3) "you probably should delete that before it gets out, just a thought." Make sure one of your worked examples reaches a call my team would hate, because a procedure that always agrees with my moderators isn't doing work.

Finally, for each major branch, tell me which bit of speech-act doctrine it leans on and what alternative view would flip that branch — say, whether uptake is necessary for a promise, or whether an insincere performative still performs — and be explicit about where your flowchart is most likely to fail or what would have to be true for you to draw the boxes differently.
