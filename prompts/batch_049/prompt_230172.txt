I'm a PhD student in STS, mostly political theory of technology, and I've been in a reading group where the old debate about whether artifacts have politics has been going in circles for two years. Honestly I've started to suspect the argument itself is the problem. One side treats it as obvious that technologies can be political all by themselves: the classic parkway overpasses built too low for buses, or the mechanical tomato harvester designed in a way that favored large farms over small ones. For them the political content is in the design, not just in how the thing gets used. The other side says no, those are just causal effects in the world plus human choices; the bridge or the machine is inert until social arrangements put it to work, so calling the artifact itself political is a category mistake or at best a metaphor. Both sides know the same examples and the same history, and nobodys position has moved at all. My working theory is that this is actually several disagreements wearing one trench coat: one strand is semantic, because they mean different things by 'politics' — maybe 'built with political intent' vs. 'reliably produces politically relevant outcomes no matter the intent'. Another strand might be about standards of evidence, since I don't know what would even count as showing the artifact itself carries politics rather than showing that politics surrounded its production and use. Maybe there's also a background disagreement about where to locate agency, or about whether explanation should stop at the artifact or include it.
