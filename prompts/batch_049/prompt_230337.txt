I'm a longtime reader of LessWrong and the alignment forum, and next month I'm giving a talk to the safety team at a mid-sized AI lab; the audience is mostly technical alignment researchers, plus two economists from the lab's governance team. My thesis is that the alignment tax is not a fixed price paid for a fixed amount of safety but an endogenous cost set by the competitiveness constraint: in a racing dynamic, a lab can only pay for safety up to the point where it doesn't get outcompeted, and when alignment techniques get cheaper, the savings are mostly reinvested in capability rather than taken as a lower tax. I'll argue that the main lever for reducing the effective tax is therefore coordination—shared safety standards, audits, and credible verification that make it less costly, competitively, to pay the tax—rather than marginal alignment engineering alone.
