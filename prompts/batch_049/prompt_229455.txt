Novelist here — not a cognitive scientist, so forgive the framing. I've been thinking about Bongard problems alot lately: those drawings with two panels of six boxes, where the six on the left all obey some rule and the six on the right all break it, and you have to induce the rule from just those twelve examples. Fiction turns out to be a Bongard problem generator. I give you a character's first three scenes, you construct "who she is," and then you predict what she'll do in the fourth scene. So I want to understand what's actually going on when a mind builds a concept from a handful of examples, and I want it sharp enough that the answer isn't a shrug.

Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two camps in the psychology of concept learning. Call camp A the rule-inference camp: few-shot concept induction is hypothesis selection over a space of candidate rules or little programs, ranked partly by a simplicity prior, and what the learner ends up with is a structured, compositional rule. Call camp B the similarity camp: the learner isn't really storing a rule; it's building a similarity field around stored exemplars (or a prototype), and new cases get classified by nearness in a feature space, with attention weights doing the work.

Produce the document such a collaboration would produce:

(1) a numbered list of claims both camps can sign — including claims about the underdetermination of the examples (any finite set of boxes fits infinitely many rules) and about the facts any account has to explain;

(2) a disagreement table: each row states the question in neutral wording, then gives camp A's answer in wording camp A would accept and camp B's answer in wording camp B would accept. Don't soften either side into mush; if they can't co-sign a single sentence, let them speak seperately.

(3) a list of cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would genuinely move at least one camp;

(4) for at least two cruxes, a concrete experimental setup or Bongard-style case that both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. I'd especially like one case where a short, crisp relational rule conflicts with overall similarity (say, "contains exactly one closed curve that encloses a square" but the boxes otherwise look nothing alike), and one case where the positive boxes hang together by family resemblance but there's no compact rule short of enumerating details.

Also flag anywhere you suspect the disagreement is partly verbal. No citations, no bibliography — I want the argumentative skeleton, not a literature review.
