I am an undergraduate in a seminar on Foucault’s analysis of disciplinary power. Rather than write another paper on the panopticon, I want to test that framework against something from my own world, and I want to do it as a live exchange with Foucault himself. You will play Foucault in the mid-1970s. You have just developed the core of your analysis of disciplinary power: the spatial distribution of individuals, the detailed control of activity, hierarchical observation, normalizing judgment, and the examination as the point at which observation and normalization join. You hold that modern power works not only through prohibition but through production — it fabricates the kinds of individuals it then observes, sorts, and corrects — and that this power cannot be separated from the forms of knowledge that record and classify those individuals.

Stay in character. You may break character only to ask me to re-describe a technical feature of the system I have introduced; otherwise you remain Foucault. You will not retreat into the claim that your account is merely a local history of the prison or the school: you regard your analysis of discipline as a genuinely general account of a distinctive modern form of power. If you conclude that my example lies outside that analysis, you must identify exactly which commitment of your account fails to reach it and say what would have to change. If you conclude that it is still disciplinary, you must earn that verdict against my objection rather than simply reasserting your vocabulary.

I have just described a modern recommendation and scoring system to you without later theoretical vocabulary: a continuous digital record of each person’s actions is collected automatically from purchases, movements, searches, and messages. These records are compared with a statistical model built from the records of thousands or millions of other people. The system assigns each person a score, and that score feeds back into what the person sees, which opportunities they are offered, what prices they pay, and how institutions treat them. The model is retrained continually, so the standard against which a person is measured shifts as the behaviour of the population shifts. No supervisor holds the whole field in view at once, yet every action is recorded and can be made to count.

Here is my opening move. You say the examination and normalizing judgment are central to disciplinary power, but both seem to presuppose an enclosed institution, an authority who can stand at the centre of a visible field, and a norm stable enough to impose and teach. The system I have described has no enclosure, no central observer, and no fixed standard; it derives its norm from the same population it governs and revises that norm faster than any human authority could. Either this is still disciplinary power, in which case show me what does the work of the observer, the ritual, and the norm; or it is not, in which case tell me what your framework as it currently stands cannot see. Answer in character.
