The other night two people in my reading group got into it about the word "genocide," and I came away thinking both of them landed punches, though I honestly could'nt sort out who won. Here's the exchange, as close to verbatim as I can remember.

Priya opened: people now use "genocide" for any massacre, forced displacement, famine, or even hostile rhetoric toward an ethnic group, and that drains the word. The UN Genocide Convention requires acts done with the specific intent to destroy a protected group, partially or wholly, and it only lists national, ethnic, racial, and religious groups. If the label gets stretched to cover every ethnically charged atrocity, it stops doing its legal work. Genocide is supposed to mark something distinct, not just any crime with an ethnic dimension.

Sam answered that the legal category was never a neutral description. The drafters narrowed it on purpose—left out political groups, set the intent bar high—so that states would almost never have the word used against them. Defending "the precise legal meaning" isn't defending a dictionary; it's defending a political settlement.

Priya came back: even if the drafting was political, that's a reason to fix the definition through law, not to stretch the word until it means "atrocity I dislike." Inflating genocide into an all-purpose moral cudgel hands a ready-made slogan to the people who say the category is only propaganda. There are other terms for other things—war crimes, crimes against humanity, ethnic cleansing.

Sam replied: but the other terms don't carry the same consequences. It's "genocide" that triggers public alarm, inquiries, sometimes intervention. So insisting on narrow usage, while institutions still only move when that one word is said, is itself a political act with political risks. You don't get to claim you're merely being accurate.

Priya answered that this turns the word's power into a reason for its own abuse. If the public only cares when someone says "genocide," the fix is to make people care about war crimes and crimes against humanity too, not to corrupt the one category that still has a clear, demonstrable standard.

Sam said: what standard, exactly? Intent has to be inferred from a pattern of conduct, and whether a pattern shows intent to destroy a group as a group already depends on judgments about what a group is and which harms count. There is no reading of the facts without those judgments. The objectivity you're invoking is one more political choice.

It more or less ended there. I honestly couldn't say who had the better of it.
