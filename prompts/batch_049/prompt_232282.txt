So here's something I can't get out of my head. The replication crisis has been grinding on for over a decade now and the statistics-reform argument seems completely stuck. One camp says the tool itself is the problem — null hypothesis significance testing, p-values, the whole apparatus — and we should switch to Bayes factors or posteriors, or at least effect sizes with uncertainty intervals. Another camp says the tool is fine and the real problem is p-hacking, file drawers, and perverse incentives; fix those and p-values work fine. A third camp says the stats squabble is beside the point, because the crisis is really about weak theories that don't make precise predictions. My working theory is that the deadlock isn't mathematical at all: it's about what a scientific claim even is. On one picture, a claim is a statement that a procedure with known long-run error rates would rarely go wrong, so a failed replication is an error-control verdict. On the other picture, a claim is a report of how much a rational agent's credence should move given the data, so a failed replication is a question of how much the effect estimate shrinks and whether it crosses some "too small to matter" line. Those are two different questions being answered under the same name. But maybe I'm wrong and there's a real technical disagreement underneath — optional stopping, multiplicity, p-hacking in the analysis stage that preregistration just makes auditable rather than impossible, whatever. I want a diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement, with reasons, not a survey of positions. And given the diagnosis you land on, what would actually count as progress — a theorem, a formal equivalence, an incentive change, a distinction, or admitting the question was badly posed in the first place? Also, tell me how confident you are in your diagnosis, and single out the point your least sure of.
