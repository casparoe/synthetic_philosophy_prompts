Okay, so in my development economics reading group we've been going back and forth for over a year about the same question: why are some countries rich and others poor. We've split into three camps and nobody moves. The institutions camp says the whole story is about rules and incentives: secure property rights, constraints on whoever holds power, inclusive rather than extractive institutions. They lean on cases like North and South Korea or Botswana versus Sierra Leone, and on the claim that a lot of places that were relatively rich before colonization are poor now, while some backwaters took off. The geography camp says the institutions people have the causality backwards: bad institutions are mostly what a place's soil, disease burden, access to navigable water, and location happened to permit, and those initial differences persist through agglomeration and trade. The culture camp says both are downstream of norms: the reason some societies built good institutions and used their geography is that they had different ideas about cooperation, trust, time, and work. What drives me up the wall is that each side happily reduces the other two — geography explains institutions, institutions are frozen culture, culture is just an adaptation to geography — so every new fact fits all three at once.

My working theory is that the real disagreement isn't a straight factual one but a disagreement about what the word "why" is doing. When the institutions people ask "why is country X rich?", they seem to want the nearest manipulable lever: something a policy or an outside power could in principle change. When the geography people ask it, they want an origin story: what got the whole chain started, even if the chain now runs mostly through institutions and culture. When the culture people ask it, they want a story about how groups coordinate — what selects the institutions and makes them stick. On top of that, each camp has different standards of evidence: institutions people treat a historical natural experiment (say, a colonial boundary that divided a people) as clean identification, geographers say those episodes are so weird the can't support general claims, and culture people say the "instrument" usually shifts norms too, so it wasn't really holding culture fixed. Is my diagnosis right? If it is, is this a verbal disagreement, a difference in standards of evidence, or a difference in accepted starting intuitions that each side can't argue for? Or is there a hidden factual question at the bottom that better data could actually settle? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a particular comparison, a distinction, a different question — or should I stop expecting convergence?
