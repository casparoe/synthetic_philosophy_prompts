After a long career in the natural sciences — I was a physical chemist and spent decades with models whose validity depended on exactly how far they could be transferred — I have recently taken up the philosophy of law, and I keep finding myself in the same argument with a friend, a retired federal prosecutor. When I complain that judicial review seems to allow a handful of unelected judges to override a democratic legislature, he reaches for an analogy I have now heard many times: a judge is like a baseball umpire. The umpire does not make the rules, does not play for either team, and does no more than call balls and strikes as they actually are; a judge, likewise, is not supposed to make law or serve a constituency, but only to apply rules that are already there. My friend then concludes that judges overstep whenever they strike down a statute on the basis of a contested moral or policy judgment rather than clear constitutional text, and that judicial review is therefore illegitimate in exactly those hard cases.

I suspect the analogy is carrying more weight than it can bear, and I would like you to audit it. Please act as an expert in jurisprudence and the logic of analogy, think carefully, and begin by brainstorming the full set of correspondences: what in the law is supposed to correspond to the rulebook, the strike zone, the teams, the umpire, the call, and so on. Then separate the points where the comparison is informative from the points where it strains or breaks, and say which disanalogies actually matter for my friend's conclusion and which are merely incidental. In particular, consider whether constitutional and statutory language has anything like the determinate geometry of a strike zone; whether judges sometimes have to make and revise rules rather than merely apply them; whether an umpire's call settles only the pitch before him while a judge's ruling becomes part of what later judges must reckon with; and whether the "game" of law comes with a preexisting rulebook in the relevant sense at all. Finally, tell me whether my friend's conclusion — that judges should almost never override legislatures once the text is unclear — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was quietly supplying that conclusion the whole time. If there is a better analogy that preserves what is sound in the umpire picture while avoiding its distortions, I would be glad to hear it.
