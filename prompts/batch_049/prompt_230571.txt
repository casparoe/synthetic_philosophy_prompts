I help run a philosophy discussion group for about fourteen teenagers, mostly sixteen to eighteen years old, and I want to build our next session around a puzzle I keep walking into in my own thinking. We say, without any strain, that a plant died because no one watered it, that a patient got worse because the nurse never checked the line, that a disaster unfolded because a warning was not passed along. In each case, the thing credited with doing the causal work is something that did not happen—an absence, a silence, a gap. But I also hold, when I step back, that causes have to be actual occurrences in the world, things with a kind of push or pull to them; a non-event cannot reach out and kill a plant. I do not think the right outcome is simply to pick one of these two thoughts and discard the other, because each leaves something obvious unexplained: denying that the omission caused the damage makes our ordinary explanations false for no clear reason, while accepting it seems to let any number of absences count as causes and leaves us with the question of why only the missing watering, and not every other absent thing, gets the title.

I would like you to design a 75-minute activity for this group (ours is 12–16 people, and the room allows small-table work) that makes them feel that tension rather than have it announced to them. The rules, roles, or scoring should lead participants to issue causal verdicts on matched pairs of cases—situations that are as close as possible except that the damage flows from what someone did in one and what someone failed to do in the other—along with a few cases where the missing thing is someone's clear responsibility versus merely a nonexistent event. The aim is for them to catch themselves insisting both that the omission caused the outcome and that nothing can cause anything. Then provide a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity to the underlying ideas: the pull of "if only" reasoning, the worry that absences cannot be genuine causes, the role of background expectations and duties in selecting which absence gets promoted to "the cause," and the proliferation problem of too many absent causes. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it—for instance, the group collapsing the causal question into a question about who is to blame—and how to steer back without lecturing, and be explicit about which part of the puzzle the activity cannot produce on its own and that I will need to supply afterward.
