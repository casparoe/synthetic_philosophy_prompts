I retired some years ago from a career in physical chemistry and have lately been working my way through the philosophical literature on reduction and emergence. I have noticed that in these debates, when someone wants to defend the autonomy of a higher-level science, they often reach for an analogy: the relation of chemistry to physics, or of chemical compounds to their elements, is treated as the model for the relation of sociology to psychology, or of a social formation to its individual members. The underlying thought is that just as water is not simply a mechanical mixture of hydrogen and oxygen, so a social group or a mental state is not simply an aggregate of its components. I should like to know how far this analogy can genuinely be made to work.

What first drew me to the question was a passage in John Stuart Mill's A System of Logic, in the chapter on the composition of causes. As I recall, Mill distinguishes two modes of composition in nature. In the mechanical mode, the joint effect of several causes is exactly the sum of their separate effects: when two forces act on a body along the same line, one can compute the resultant by simple addition. In the chemical mode, by contrast, Mill remarks that when two substances combine chemically, the properties of the compound are not the sum of the properties of the two substances taken separately; the product has, in many respects, no trace of the properties of the ingredients from which it arose. He treats this as showing that the principle of the composition of causes has limits, and he then draws consequences for the sciences of mind and society. I am less interested in whether Mill's reading of chemistry is outdated than in whether the analogy built on it survives closer scrutiny.

I would like you to act as a philosopher of science who knows both the history of emergence and the contemporary reductionism debate. Please think step by step, and start by brainstorming the distinct readings one might give of Mill's distinction before committing to an answer. Then give me the correspondence table for the analogy as it is usually pressed: what plays the role of the elements, the mixture, the compound, the emergent property, and the different levels of description. Identify the points at which the analogy breaks down or quietly imports misleading assumptions, and separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended use from the ones that are merely incidental. Finally, tell me whether an advocate of the autonomy of the social or mental sciences can legitimately appeal to this chemical mode of composition, or whether the analogy does more rhetorical work than argumentative work. Where the analogy fails, be explicit about whether the conclusion can be reached by a better route.
