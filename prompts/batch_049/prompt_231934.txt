I've ended up defending a version of process supervision in my research group, and I want to know how exposed it is. The view I've been pushing is not just that process-based reward models outperform outcome-based ones on the MATH benchmark, but that process supervision is the right kind of supervision for alignment in any reasoning-heavy domain. I know the Lightman et al. paper "Let’s Verify Step by Step" (2023) points out that earlier work found outcome and process supervision gave similar results in grade-school maths, so the empirical story is less one-sided than people sometimes assume. Still, the conceptual argument seems strong to me. They write:

"It provides more precise feedback, since it specifies the exact location of any errors that occur. It also has several advantages relevant to AI alignment: it is easier for humans to interpret, and it more directly rewards models for following a human-endorsed chain-of-thought."

My position is roughly: when a task is solved through a decomposable chain of reasoning and human evaluators can reliably label intermediate steps, process supervision should be the default; outcome supervision is a useful fallback when step-level labels are unavailable, but it is always a compromise because it allows models to earn reward through bad reasoning that happens to land on the right answer. What I'd like from you is a serious map of what would count against this position, or at least force me to qualify it. Include experimental results that already exist, possible future findings, and conceptual objections. Rank them from fatal to merely awkward. Also tell me honestly whether the view as I've stated it has been insulated into unfalsifiability, because I can feel myself wanting to say that any failure is just a failure of label quality or benchmark design, and I don't know how seriously to take that impulse. And please write the answer in British English.
