Marco: But even if they're linked, the counterfactual still works. Take away the Rhine crossing in 406, and you still have the Goths, internal usurpers, and money problems. Rome still falls. The specific events change, but the outcome doesn't, because the system had multiple pressure points.

Dana: That kind of counterfactual is doing too much work. In real history, if you take away the Rhine crossing, you change later events in unpredictable ways. Maybe no crossing means the Western Empire limps along for another century. We can't just assert "it still falls" from our armchair; we'd need a model.

Marco: Historians use counterfactuals all the time, they just have to be plausible. And the fall of the Western Empire is one of those cases where the outcome looks robust across small changes. That's exactly what overdetermination is: not that each cause was independent, but that the outcome was overdetermined by the whole structure.

Dana: Then you've changed the meaning. If the causes all feed each other, then the real explanation is the feedback loop itself, not "multiple sufficient causes." Calling it overdetermined just names the robustness; it doesn't explain anything.
