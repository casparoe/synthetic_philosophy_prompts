I'm a PhD student in philosophy of science, and at our reading group last week two students—one from the CS theory group, one from philosophy—got into an argument about whether the no-free-lunch theorems actually say anything about the problem of induction. I took notes but I genuinely coudln't referee it, and I'd like someone to go through it move by move.

D (the CS student) said Wolpert and Macready basically upgraded Hume: they proved that, averaged over all possible target functions, every learning algorithm generalizes exactly as badly as every other. So there is no method that works "from the data alone." Any learner has to buy some inductive bias, and which bias you buy is not determined by the data. Induction can't be justified from experience without presupposing the very uniformity you're trying to prove. So the problem of induction is now a theorem rather than a vibe.

R (the philosopher) said that's a bait-and-switch. NFL averages over all possible functions with each one weighted equally, and that uniform weighting isn't a neutral "no assumptions" stance—it's a very specific prior that treats every possible labeling of every unseen point as equally likely. But real induction doesn't happen over the space of all mathematically possible functions. It happens in one world, and the question is whether extending this world's patterns is reasonable. Averaging over all functions and concluding that no method wins tells you more about the choice of prior than about learning.

D came back: "this world is special" is just one more inductive assumption. The theorems show that whatever prior you pick, you pick it for reasons that can't be grounded in the data. Old-school inductivists wanted a free lunch by appeal to the uniformity of nature, and NFL shows the lunch is never free. R has moved the unjustified assumption up a level, from "nature is uniform" to "nature is simple" or "this world is compressible," but it's still an assumption.

R said that's too fast. Simplicity-biased methods can get a non-circular rationale via a kind of dominance argument: if the world is simple, a simplicity-biased learner will find the pattern; if the world is complex, no finite learner finds the pattern quickly anyway, so the simplicity-biased learner loses nothing by trying. You don't need to average over all functions for that. In the worlds where learning is possible at all, a learner that searches in roughly increasing order of complexity succeeds. So NFL rules out only the fantasy of a method that dominates in every logically possible world—which nobody needed.

D countered that the dominance argument smuggles in a large assumption: that the class of "simple" targets lines up with whatever simplicity ordering the learner happens to use, and that the true target shows up early in that ordering. There are infinitely many simplicity orderings, and the data won't select among them. One ordering works relative to one world, another relative to another, and the fact that some ordering works relative to some world doesn't tell you which ordering to use in this world. So the original problem—which bias?—remains, and relabeling it a "prior" doesn't dissolve it.

R's last move was that this sets up a false dilemma: the alternatives are not "justified by the data alone" versus "arbitrary." A prior can be justified pragmatically, by its performance in the kinds of worlds and with the kinds of tools we actually occupy, and by the fact that inquiry is impossible without some ordering. Demanding a data-free proof that our ordering is the right one is demanding something no epistemic position provides—deduction included, since it also rests on presuppositions.

Please adjudicate this move by move: rate each of the moves above (D's opening, R's reply, D's second, R's second, D's third, R's third) from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and briefly justify each rating. Then say who is more right overall, and what, if anything, both parties are missing or papering over. I'm less interested in getting the exact Wolpert–Macready theorem right in all its details and more interested in whether the argumentative moves themselves work.
