I have been following two regulars on this forum who have spent months arguing about whether the sciences carve nature at their joints. One defends single-hierarchy realism: there is a unique, mind-independent ranking of classification schemes from more to less natural, the mature sciences progressively approximate that ranking, and cross-cutting taxonomies are best treated as partial views of a single underlying order rather than as rivals. The other defends cross-cutting pluralism: the world supports many equally objective but non-nested ways of grouping things at different grains and for different investigative purposes, so treating one scale as the set of real joints is a projection of our practical priorities. They have agreed to replace their next three reply essays with an adversarial collaboration, in the style of a double-crux writeup: rather than trying to persuade a third party, the document isolates the sub-questions on which each side would actually update, with each side precommitting in advance to what would count as evidence.

Write the document they should publish. It should contain four sections, and every claim attributing a view to either side must be worded so that side would sign it.

1. Agreed claims. A numbered list of substantive claims both endorse. Do not pad it with bare observations such as that there are objects or that prediction often works; include claims about ordinary-language categories, purely conventional classifications, and the predictive success of mature taxonomies. Also include at least two claims stating dialectical moves each side now regards as question-begging or off-limits.

2. Residual disagreements. A table with three to five rows. Each row gives a neutrally worded question both regard as well-posed, then the single-hierarchy realist's answer, then the cross-cutting pluralist's answer, each stated as that side would state it. No row may be answerable from the agreed claims alone.

3. Cruxes and tests. Identify three to five cruxes, where a crux is a sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side. For each crux, give a concrete case or predictive test both agree bears on it, record each side's prediction in advance, and say what result would make that side update. Ground this section in at least the following hard cases, unless the two would jointly reject one as uninformative and explain why: biological species delimitation, where reproductive isolation, gene-tree history, ecology, and morphology cross-cut; psychiatric and psychological kinds, where clinical course, genetics, and symptom structure cross-cut; and one domain with a famously tight classification, such as chemical substances or stellar types, used to test whether its tightness generalizes to messier domains or is bought locally at one scale.

4. Dissolved and surviving disagreements. After tabooing the central terms and replacing each with what it predicts or what would count as evidence for it, state which apparent disagreements dissolve as merely verbal and which two or three disagreements remain irreducibly substantive.
