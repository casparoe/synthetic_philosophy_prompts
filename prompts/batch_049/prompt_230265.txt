I have been trying to think through how much of history was genuinely open at the time it happened, rather than merely looking settled in hindsight. A friend of mine has become convinced that a great many important outcomes are path-dependent. By this he means that a major later result traces back to some small early accident — a decision made in haste, an institution that nearly failed in its first years, a standard adopted without much thought — and that once the accident got locked in, everything downstream was built around it. He illustrates the idea with familiar examples such as the layout of typewriter keyboards and the width of railway tracks, but he insists the pattern is completely general.

When I pressed him on how far the idea could be taken, he wrote out the following steps, and I have not been able to find the point where the argument goes wrong:

(1) If a later outcome is path-dependent, then it was caused at least partly by an early event that could easily have gone differently.

(2) If an early event could easily have gone differently, then at the time that event occurred the eventual outcome was not already singled out as the likely result; some alternative outcome was at least as likely, or nearly as likely, as the one that actually came about.

(3) If at the time of its early causes an outcome was not already singled out as the likely result, then the outcome's later dominance cannot be explained by that outcome's being better than the alternatives.

(4) Therefore, for any path-dependent outcome, its later dominance is not explained by its being better than the alternatives.

(5) Virtually every major historical outcome — which technologies, languages, institutions, and economic systems came to dominate — is path-dependent in the sense defined in (1).

(6) Therefore, virtually no major historical outcome came to dominate because it was better than the available alternatives. The winners won by accident, and their apparent superiority is only read back into them afterwards.

(7) But (6) is absurd. It would mean that no technology ever won out because it worked better, no institution ever survived because it functioned better, and no language ever spread for any reason having to do with the language itself. That cannot be a correct picture of how the world works.

My friend concludes from (7) that path dependence must be false, or at best a trivial local curiosity, because its general form leads to absurdity. I do not think his conclusion follows, but I also cannot accept (6), and I cannot identify where the reasoning became too strong. Which step should be rejected, and what would rejecting it cost? I would most like to keep two things: that history was genuinely open at many forks, and that some winners won at least partly because they had real advantages. Can both be kept, and if so, which premise is the one to give up?
