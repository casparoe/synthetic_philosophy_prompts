Question: Opponents of legalizing euthanasia sometimes claim there is a morally significant difference between active euthanasia, such as administering a lethal injection, and passive euthanasia, such as withdrawing life-sustaining treatment. Explain the distinction such opponents typically rely on, and discuss whether the doctrine of double effect provides a successful defense of it.

Student response: The relevant distinction is killing versus letting die. Active euthanasia is a case of killing because the doctor performs an act that brings about death, while passive euthanasia is a case of letting die because the doctor merely omits further treatment. Since we are more responsible for what we do than for what we allow, killing is worse than letting die. The doctrine of double effect supports this distinction: in active euthanasia the doctor intends the patient's death as a means to relieve suffering, which is impermissible, whereas in passive euthanasia the doctor merely foresees death as a side effect of withholding treatment. Therefore, active euthanasia is impermissible while passive euthanasia is permissible, and the distinction between them is defensible.
