After a career in geology and paleobiology, I have lately been reading the philosophical and methodological literature on archaeological inference — specifically, the long-running dispute about how far one may legitimately move from mute material remains to claims about past beliefs, rituals, and institutions. My native professional vocabulary is the one I worked in for four decades: taphonomic filters (the processes that bias what survives and what vanishes), time-averaging (the mixing of residues from different moments into a single deposit), actualism and the uniformitarian assumption (interpreting the past through processes observable today), trace fossils as records of behavior rather than of bodies, functional morphology, convergence, and equifinality (the recognition that different processes can leave physically indistinguishable traces). I find myself involuntarily translating every argument I read into these terms, and I would like that translation performed deliberately and candidly rather than by intellectual habit.

I would ask you to restate, in that vocabulary, the main positions in the debate as faithfully as you can. To make the exercise concrete, use these anchoring cases: a row of postholes (sockets left in the ground by decayed timbers) with no surviving superstructure; a cache of deliberately shattered vessels; a burial ground in which one interment embodies an order of magnitude more labor than its neighbors; and the recurrence of a fixed arrangement of animal parts in many separate deposits. Across these cases, I am especially interested in how each position treats the move from regular physical patterning to shared norms, what place (if any) conscious intention has in explaining a trace, and what would count as testing such an inference rather than merely illustrating it. Please translate the optimistic program that sought lawful material correlates of behavior, the later interpretive reaction that held meanings to be context-bound and not lawfully recoverable, the scientifically-minded attempts to study symbolism through constrained material residues, and the skeptical ordering of inference by difficulty from subsistence through social organization up to religion.

Then give me the residue report. Which of these claims survive translation into taphonomy, actualism, and equifinality without distortion? Which claims had to be fudged, and where does my vocabulary have no adequate expression at all — for example, conventions, normative institutions, or meanings that are arbitrary with respect to physical function? Finally, I want your judgment on whether the residue reflects a defect in my vocabulary, a genuine difference between archaeology and paleobiology because archaeologists study intentional agents who interpreted their own world, or a sign that some positions in the debate were less clear than they looked when stripped of their surrounding prose.
