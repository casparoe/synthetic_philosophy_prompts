I am the public access reviewer at a county historical society readying its digitized diaries, letters, and photographs for open online access under a large grant. The archivists who will run the review are trained in preservation, not in law or philosophy, and the board wants one reusable procedure they can apply to each item in under half an hour. We have legal counsel for copyright and privacy statutes, so the procedure should not be about current law; it should be about when making private material public does the kind of harm described by the passage below.

The board has directed me to ground our policy in this passage from Samuel Warren and Louis Brandeis, "The Right to Privacy" (1890):

"Nor is the harm wrought by such invasions confined to the suffering of those who may be the subjects of journalistic or other enterprise. In this, as in other branches of commerce, the supply creates the demand. Each crop of unseemly gossip, thus harvested, becomes the seed of more, and, in direct proportion to its circulation, results in the lowering of social standards and of morality."

I read the passage as claiming that the harm of exposing private life is not exhausted by the immediate pain of the named individuals: circulation itself creates an appetite for more exposure, and the wide, repeated circulation of such material lowers social standards, so even material whose subjects are dead, unidentifiable, or too trivial to upset anyone may still do a kind of harm. If you think that is a misreading, say so and build the procedure from the better reading.

Please turn the passage into a reusable decision procedure, not an advisory about these three items. The procedure should specify: the questions an archivist should ask, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each question before moving to the next; where the procedure returns "do not post"; where it routes an item to the board; and which steps are judgment calls that the procedure cannot eliminate. Then apply it to the following three items:

- an 1840s diary that records a neighbor's suicide and the author's suspicion that the neighbor had been stealing; no living descendants are traceable, and the director wants it posted as local history;
- a series of letters from a prominent local merchant to a woman not his wife, with no sexual detail but enough identifying detail to name her and her children, offered jointly by both families;
- a 1930s foreman's ledger containing caustic comments about named millworkers, all long dead, which the society's director wants posted as a labor-history document.

End by saying which case the procedure handles least well, what the procedure cannot decide, and where a person rather than a procedure must take over.
