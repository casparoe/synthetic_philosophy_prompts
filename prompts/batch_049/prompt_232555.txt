Analyze the late-1920s United States customs litigation over Constantin Brancusi's Bird in Space through the lens of the epistemology of aesthetic testimony. In the case, customs officials classified the polished bronze sculpture as a manufactured metal object subject to duty rather than as a work of art, while expert witnesses—artists, critics, and curators—testified on both sides about whether the object was art, with some arguing that its failure to represent a bird in natural proportions should be irrelevant to its status. The court eventually ruled that the piece was a work of art.

For this prompt, treat the court as an epistemic agent trying to form a judgment about the sculpture's aesthetic or art-status. Your task is to give a disciplined mapping of the case onto the debate about aesthetic testimony. In particular: (1) What would an optimist about aesthetic testimony say the court was doing, and was the court justified in relying on the testimony it heard? (2) What would a pessimist, or someone who holds that aesthetic judgments require first-hand acquaintance with the object, say the court could and could not legitimately do? (3) Where in the trial does the distinction between testimony about sensory appearance and testimony about art-historical or institutional status matter? (4) What does the case show about the limits of aesthetic testimony—for example, does it support the view that aesthetic knowledge can be transmitted by testimony, or does it instead reveal that the court was deferring to social authority rather than forming a genuinely aesthetic judgment? Finally, say where the analogy between the court's legal task and ordinary aesthetic judgment breaks down, and whether the episode is best read as a confirmation of, an anomaly for, or a case outside the scope of standard views about aesthetic testimony.
