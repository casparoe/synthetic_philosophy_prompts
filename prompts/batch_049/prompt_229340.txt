I'm on the ideas-and-tech beat at a general-interest magazine, and I've been trying to sort out my own reactions before I write anything about the worry people keep raising: as machines get more autonomy, there are harms where you can't find any human who was properly in control and properly informed, so blame seems to slip through the cracks. I don't trust my theoretical views on this; I want to work from gut verdicts on cases and see if a principle falls out.

Case 1. A hospital deploys a sepsis-scoring algorithm the vendor trained mainly on the hospital's own past patients, and nobody ever checked whether the score performed worse for Black patients. A Black woman arrives with textbook sepsis signs, gets a low score, is sent home, and dies. My verdict: the hospital and the vendor are both to blame, and the blame lands on the actual people who decided to sell and deploy the thing, not on "the algorithm." That one is clear.

Case 2. A self-driving car's new perception model mistakes a plastic bag on the road for a concrete block, swerves, and hits a cyclist. The engineer who signed off on the update ran all the standard tests and nothing came up. I don't blame the engineer. But I'd say the company is responsible — except that feels more like fining a corporate treasury than blaming, and I'm not sure it's the same thing.

Case 3. An autonomous cargo ship loses containers in a storm because its routing software and its ballast control interacted in a way neither engineering team had predicted. A container strikes a small fishing boat and kills a fisherman. I can't find a single human where I'd say "that one messed up." And yet "it's just one of those things, nobody's responsible" sticks in my throat. This is the hardest one.

Case 4. A military drone in autonomous targeting mode misidentifies a wedding party as a convoy and fires. The human oversight officer had about eight seconds to veto and didn't, because the video feed genuinely looked ambiguous. I blame her some, but much less than if she'd ordered the strike herself. Something about the system settling on the target before she was in the loop changes the moral color for me, though I can't articulate what.

Case 5. A big-box store installs off-the-shelf gait-analysis software to flag shoplifters, and security detains a man it identifies. It turns out the system has a very high false-positive rate for people with a certain movement disorder, and a thirty-second read of the documentation would have shown the store this. Here I blame the store heavily and the vendor hardly at all. But in Case 1 I blamed the vendor heavily. Why? Maybe the store was closer to the harm, or maybe because the store could reasonably have known, or maybe because the harm was a false detention rather than a death. I'm not sure, and it bugs me.

The case I keep going back and forth on: a home-care robot feeding a stroke patient doesn't detect that she's choking — detecting that was never in its spec — and it keeps running its routine while she dies. No caregiver was in the room. The family bought it becuase the brochure made it sound like caregiving, not like a fancy kitchen appliance. Some days I think the manufacturer oversold it and is responsible; other days I think it's a terrible accident and looking for someone to blame is just grief talking.

I want someone to try to extract a principle from the five verdicts, or as many of them as can be saved. What does that principle say about the home-care case? And which of my verdicts does it force me to give up, if any? If the best principle is messy, say so — I'd rather have an honest mess than a clean slogan.
