Two people in my philosophy group got stuck on Avicenna's flying man. Quick setup, since not everyone in the group knew it: imagine a person created all at once, fully formed, floating in empty air with his eyes covered and his limbs not touching anything or each other — no sensation, no memory, no mental images, a complete blank from the senses and the body. Avicenna says this person would still be aware that he himself exists. One person in the group took this to show that the self is not a body: here's a case where the self is fully present to itself and the body is fully absent to it, so they can't be the same thing. Another said no — the setup only shows that whatever produces self-awareness can do so without sensory input, which is perfectly consistent with the self being the brain, since a brain can produce self-referential activity on its own. The first replied that in the imagined case the person doesn't perceive a body at all, so if the self just were the body, the thing he calls "I" would also be absent from his awareness — but someone exists and is known from the inside, so it has to be something else. The second said the setup quietly stipulates a single, indivisible first-person subject and then acts suprised when that subject doesn't show up as a bodily object in its own awareness; introspection is not a brain scan, so the fact that it's silent about the brain isn't evidence against the self being the brain. The first came back with the point that first-person access has a special immediacy: each of us knows himself without observing anything, and no description of neural firing contains that vantage point. The second said first-person access might just be a mode of presentation of the same underlying thing, not a separate substance. They both think the other is dodging their point. I want you to adjudicate this, not survey it. Go through the exchange and say who's right about which specific moves, where each argument genuinely works and where it breaks, and then give your actual verdict on whether Avicenna's flying man can establish anything beyond the claim that self-awareness can occur without sensation.
