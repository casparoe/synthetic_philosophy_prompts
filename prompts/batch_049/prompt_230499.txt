I'm a retired field geologist, and after a career of notebooks, maps, and half-broken GPS units, I've gotten into philosophy of mind. The extended mind thesis (Clark and Chalmers, the Otto notebook case) has been on my mind a lot. As I understand the parity idea: if something outside the skull does work that we'd call cognitive if it were done inside the skull, then we should count it as cognitive too. Fair? Sort of.

I'd like you to pretend you're a professional philosopher of cognitive science and think carefully about a scoring system for deciding whether some external thing is part of a person's extended thinking. To keep this from being just a literature recap, start by brainstorming three or four criteria you'd actually use yourself. Here's one from my old field days as a nudge: "uptake cost" — how much translating I had to do between the external thing and the thought I needed next. Low uptake cost felt more mind-like, high uptake cost felt tool-like. Use mine, replace it, whichever.

Then score each case below from 0 to 10, where 0 is "just a tool, no more cognitive than a hammer" and 10 is "part of the person's thinking system, full stop." After scoring, put them into tiers (say top/middle/bottom) and give a sentence for each explaining why it landed where it did. Be honset about the borderline ones.

Cases:

1. A paper field notebook, indexed by date and location, where I wrote observations plus the interpretations I was slowly working out. I often only knew what I thought after writing it down, and losing it meant losing whole weeks of reasoning, not just records.

2. A handheld GPS that converted my position into grid coordinates. It ran an algorithm I couldn't do in my head, or on paper in any usable time, and I didn't know its internal steps.

3. A printed tide table from the government that I consulted every morning before working on mudflats. I understood the basic idea of tidal cycles but couldn't reconstruct the predictions myself.

4. A long-time field collegue with a phenomenal memory for logistics and outcrop details — "where'd we park the truck," "what was the dip on that ridge." He was right far more often than I was, but he wasn't always around and you couldn't query him at will without being mildly irritating.

5. The vocabulary and drawing conventions of geological maps themselves — colors, symbols, the way strike and dip get marked. Early on they were just marks I looked up; later I literally saw terrain through them.

6. A precomputed spreadsheet with corrections for sample ages, where I typed raw numbers and got adjusted dates. I understood roughly what it was doing and trusted it completely, but I would never have done the arithmetic by hand.

Then maybe tell me which one of the six you'd most want to argue about with a colleague, and why.
