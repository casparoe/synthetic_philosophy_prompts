In my philosophy club we were reading Maimonides’ Guide for the Perplexed, part 3 chapter 32, and this passage caused a whole argument:

“It was in accordance with the wisdom and plan of God, as displayed in the whole Creation, that He did not command us to give up and to discontinue all these manners of service; for to obey such a commandment it would have been contrary to the nature of man, who generally cleaves to that to which he is used; it would in those days have made the same impression as a prophet would make at present if he called us to the service of God and told us in His name, that we should not pray to Him, not fast, not seek His help in time of trouble; that we should serve Him in thought, and not by any action.”

Josh said Maimonides is basically saying the whole sacrificial system in Leviticus was a divine workaround: God would have preferred worship in pure thought but couldn't take the people from one extreme to the other, so He let them keep sacrifices. He said that's actually a problem because then God commands something He doesn't really want, which makes the law feel manipulative.

Maya said that's not what "contrary to the nature of man" means. Maimonides is saying God works through human nature the same way He works through physical development, like infants getting milk before solid food. A command suited to a stage isn't fake; it's the real form of service that fits where people are. If God had tried to ban sacrifices outright, the people would have stayed idolatrous, so the concession is actually the working out of a fixed goal.

Josh came back with: if the form of the law is shaped by the local custom of sacrificing animals, then the same goal could have been reached through different forms in a different culture. That makes the specific Torah commands look arbitrary, not necessary. Maya said no, the goal determines the route, but gradual doesn't mean arbitrary, and Maimonides still thinks the specific form matters because it was the actual form that moved the people away from idolatry.

We couldn't settle it. Please adjudicate their back-and-forth point by point: who is right about what, and is there a reading that makes Maimonides consistant here, or does one of them just win?
