I'm a PhD student in behavioral economics, and a friend of mine—whose very into the pop-econ corner of Twitter—gave me his confident summary of the sunk cost fallacy the other day. He said: "Sunk costs are costs you can't recover, and rational choice theory says they're totally irrelevant to what you should do next. Once the money or time is spent, it's water under the bridge; the only thing that matters is whether continuing has better future benefits than stopping. So every time you think 'I should finish this because I've already put so much into it,' you're committing a fallacy. That means finishing a degree you hate after three years, staying in a bad relationship because of the years invested, and sitting through a terrible movie because you paid for the ticket are all just irrational. There's no defensable reason to let past investment influence a decision." He then concluded that honoring sunk costs is always a mistake, and that a rational person should decide as if they'd just walked in with no history at all.

I know the standard line in econ is that sunk costs shouldn't enter your forward-looking cost-benefit calculation, so part of me feels like he must be mostly right. But the "always" and the "decide as if you have no history" parts strike me as too clean. I'd love a careful audit: which sentences would a decision theorist endorse, which ones conflate two different claims or overstate the lesson, and does his conclusion survive once the accurate parts are separated out? I'm especially interested in whether there are defensible ways of letting past investment matter—commitment effects, reputation, information about your own preferences, an aversion to waste, keeping a self-narrative together—and whether his summary rules those out unfairly just because they're not the classic fallacy.
