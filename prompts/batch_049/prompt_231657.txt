Something's been eating at me for a few months and I'd like help figuring out what rule I actually use, because my own gut reactions don't seem to match any of the rules I hear people state.

I'm talking about when it's okay to state something as a fact—flat out, no hedging—and when you should've said "I think" or "probably" or just kept quiet. I'm setting aside actual lying, since in every case that follows the person believed what they said. By "wrong" I don't mean wicked; I mean something off about the saying itself, even if nobody gets hurt. I've since found out the people who study this call it something like the norm of assertion, but I want the rule built from my cases, not from a label.

My gut calls:

My uncle once announced to a bunch of people at the track that a longshot horse was going to win. He had no inside info, just a feeling, and he said it like it was a sure thing. The horse won. I still think he shouldn't have said it the way he did, even though he turned out to be right.

My coworker told a client the meeting was at 2, because that's what our shared calendar said. The calendar hadn't been updated and the meeting was actually at 3. I can't bring myself to say she did anything wrong, even though what she said was false.

A couple years ago I told my neighbor their cat was in my yard, because I could see a cat through the fence that looked exactly like theirs. It turns out the cat I was looking at was a nearly identical cat from two doors down. But their cat really was in my yard, hidden behind the shed. So what I said was true, and I had what looked like a perfectly good basis for saying it, and I still came away feeling like I shouldn't have said it.

My friend read in the Sunday paper that the museum is free this weekend, and told a group of us "the museum is free this weekend." It was free. That seems totally fine to me.

I've tried to turn those four verdicts into a rule and I can't. "Only say what's true" fits the uncle and the museum, but not the coworker, and it makes no sense of the cat case. "Only say what you have good reason to believe" fits the coworker and the museum, but it seems to let the uncle and the cat case off the hook, and that doesn't feel right. So maybe one of my verdicts just has to go, or maybe there isn't one rule and I've got a patchwork.

Can you work out what principle—or small set of principles—would have to be behind my verdicts, and state it precisely enough that I could test it on a new case? Tell me which verdicts the principle keeps and which it forces me to give up, and whether I should keep the principle or the verdict. If the best option is a patchwork that saves all four, say what the patchwork rule actually is and what it costs in simplicity. Then take whichever rule you land on and tell me what it says about the case I keep going back and forth on: a relative calls after an accident and says "he's going to be fine" before any doctor has said anything.

One last thing. I know people have studied this properly, so please separate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among them, and don't present one contested view as if it's the obvious consensus.
