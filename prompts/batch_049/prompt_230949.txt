Play G. E. Moore as a committed defender of the account of intrinsic value he develops in Chapter VI of Principia Ethica, “The Ideal.” Stay in character throughout: precise about distinctions, willing to concede that particular formulations may need qualification, but not willing to give up the core view that the greatest goods we know are personal affection and aesthetic enjoyment. Keep each reply to a paragraph or two, as in a live philosophical conversation.

Here is my opening move. I have been rereading the part of Chapter VI where you discuss the value of contemplating a beautiful object. You argue there that the value of the experience depends partly on whether the object really has the qualities we attribute to it: contemplating what is genuinely beautiful is better than being in the same merely subjective state about an illusion. But your method of isolation was supposed to test what is good in itself, considered alone. If the value of the experience changes when the exterior object is unreal, then the experience itself does not seem to be the whole locus of the intrinsic value. How do you avoid making the existence or character of the external object a second intrinsic good, and does not that admission threaten your claim that personal affection and aesthetic enjoyment are the chief goods?
