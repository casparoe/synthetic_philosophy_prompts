I run an undergraduate reading group on the history of social thought, with about fourteen regulars, and we are about to spend several sessions on David Lewis's account of convention and the way arbitrary regularities harden into norms. Before I assign any reading, I want to run a session that lets students feel the phenomenon from the inside, so that when they later encounter Lewis's characterization of a convention — a regularity in a recurring situation in which everyone prefers to conform given that everyone else conforms, even though some alternative regularity would have served just as well — they recognize what they did rather than merely learning a definition. The room has movable chairs and a whiteboard, we have ninety minutes, and I am willing to use small real stakes, such as chocolate coins or a modest prize.

Design a runnable activity with actual rules and payoffs, no front-loaded explanations, and no mention of Lewis or the word "convention" until the debrief. It should take students through three distinct situations. First, a repeated coordination problem with two equally good ways of succeeding, where only salience or precedent can break the symmetry, so they experience the arbitrariness of whatever solution emerges. Second, a situation in which a new and objectively somewhat better solution becomes available after the group has already settled into the old one, so they feel the real cost of abandoning precedent and can gauge how much improvement it takes to dislodge an established regularity. Third, a situation in which newcomers join who lack the shared history, so the insiders are tempted to teach, correct, and mildly punish the newcomers, and the group can watch a bare regularity begin to present itself as something one ought to do.

Please supply exact instructions for each round, the payoff structure, what the facilitator should record on the board, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto Lewis's conditions for a convention and onto the difference between a convention and a sanction-backed norm. Also say what predictably goes wrong when this is run with real students and how to handle it, and be explicit about what a one-off, self-aware classroom game cannot reproduce about the historical, unplanned emergence of conventions and what the facilitator will need to supply afterward.
