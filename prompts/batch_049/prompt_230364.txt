Exam question:
"Explain the distinction between the inside view and the outside view. Then discuss how the distinction should guide (a) a project manager estimating how long a firm's enterprise software migration will take, and (b) a research committee assessing a proposal for a radically novel scientific program with no close historical analogues. In each case, state whether and how the outside view should constrain or override the inside view, and under what conditions the inside view may legitimately win."

Student's answer:
"The inside view is when you look at the details of the specific case—the particular tasks, team, resources, and obstacles—and build an estimate from those components. The outside view is when you ignore those details and instead use base rates: you find a reference class of similar past cases and use its distribution of outcomes. The planning fallacy literature shows that the inside view is systematically over-optimistic, since people neglect base rates and construct plans that assume everything goes well. Therefore, whenever a usable base rate exists, the outside view should generally override the inside view, because the base rate is the summary of all the information that matters and extra case-specific detail is mostly a source of bias. For the software migration, the manager should simply adopt the industry average completion time for similar migrations and plan around that; the internal task-by-task estimate is just an optimistic story and should be set aside. For the radical research program, however, there is no reference class of similar programs, so the outside view is unavailable to us; the only remaining choice is to assess the proposal on its internal intellectual merits and fund it if those merits look strong. That is what epistemic modesty demands: use statistical reasoning where exact statistical evidence exists, and refrain from pretending to knowledge where it does not."
