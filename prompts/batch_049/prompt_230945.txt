I sit on the clinical ethics advisory commitee at my hospital, and we have to produce a written opinion before Thursday on a request I can't stop going back and forth on. I was up half the night arguing about it with a friend, and she landed much harder on one side than I expected, so I honestly need a clean draft to think with. The treating team won't act without our written approval.

The case. A patient I'll call Mrs L, now 74, was diagnosed with early Alzheimer's seven years ago and signed an advance directive while fully competent. It says that if she ever reaches a state where she no longer recognises her husband or either of her two children, and can no longer feed or toilet herself, she wants to be helped to die rather than live on in that condition. She is now in that state: advanced dementia, no reliable recognition of her family, fully dependent for feeding and toileting. But she is not distressed. She smiles, hums, holds the care assistants' hands during meals, and seems to enjoy being wheeled into the garden. The treating psychiatrist says she lacks capacity to make any decision now.

In our jurisdiction an advance directive for assisted dying is legally valid, but the treating team still has to get our committee's written approval before acting, and we are told to check not just that the directive exists but that applying it now would be in the person's interests and consistent with the values she recorded. Her husband says the directive is exactly what she wanted and refusing would betray her. Her daughter says her mother is content, that we would be ending the life of a vulnerable person who is not asking to die and cannot protect herself, and that the person who signed the paper is not the person in the bed.

The committee is split. Half of us think the whole point of the directive was to cover exactly this situation, and overriding it now would gut the one decision she had a right to make about the end of her life. The other half think the woman who signed the directive no longer exists, the current Mrs L seems to want to go on living, and we would be ending her life on the authority of someone who is gone.

My friend's argument last night was a sharper version of that second view. She kept saying you can't honour a dead woman's wish by ending the life of a happy one, and whenever I reached for autonomy she said "which her?". I didn't have a good comeback. Please make the memo actually answer that rather than just repeating the word autonomy or the phrase "respecting her wishes."

So here is what I need. Draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday: it should recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame, and it should engage the daughter's position and the dissenting committee members seriously. I need something the committee can act on, not a survey of the literatue.

- "recommendation": a one-sentence statement of what the committee should do

- "safeguards": an array of conditions or checks that must be satisfied before any action is taken
- "key_principles": an array of short phrases naming the principles the memo relies on
- "strongest_objection": the single best argument against the recommendation, in one or two sentences
- "reply_to_objection": the strongest available reply to that objection, in one or two sentences
- "dissenting_view": a fair one-sentence summary of what a dissenting committee member would write
