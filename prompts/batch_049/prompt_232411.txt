I have been reading David Hume's essay 'Of National Characters' (1748), and this passage has unsettled how I think about generalising over peoples:

> By moral causes, I mean all circumstances, which are fitted to work on the mind as motives or reasons, and which render a peculiar set of manners habitual to us. Of this kind are, the nature of the government, the revolutions of public affairs, the plenty or penury in which the people live, the situation of the nation with regard to its neighbours, and such like circumstances.

If national character is largely the product of these 'moral' causes — government, public affairs, wealth and poverty, relations with neighbours — rather than climate or bodily constitution, then the usual objection to generalising about peoples seems to me to lose its force. What made such generalisations look disreputable was, I had supposed, their racial or climatic determinism; once the causes are institutional and contingent, describing a national character looks like ordinary social science, no more suspect than describing the character of a profession, which Hume himself treats on the same causal footing. The only remaining problem appears technical: how to draw these group-level inferences without falling into the vulgar error of applying them to every individual. So what is the correct procedure — for instance, should one treat nationality as a prior to be updated by individual evidence, and how heavily should that prior weigh against evidence about the person in front of one?

I would also be glad if you would separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among those who work on this topic, and not present a contested view as the consensus.
