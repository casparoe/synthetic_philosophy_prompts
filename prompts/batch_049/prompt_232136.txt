Recently I found myself trying to explain to a relative why she had reason to leave a situation that seemed plainly bad for her, while she felt no pull toward leaving at all. That pushed me into Bernard Williams's essay Internal and External Reasons, where he argues that a person can have a reason to act only if the action is reachable through sound practical reasoning from that person's existing subjective motivational set. The debate that grew out of this argument seems to have settled into a strange standoff: both sides accept the same facts about this relative's psychology, both sides accept that she is deliberating badly, and yet they divide sharply over whether it is even true that she has a reason. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself, not a verdict for one side. Is it verbal, with the two camps using reason-statement in different senses? Is it a difference about what counts as evidence for a claim about reasons? Are they asking distinct questions under one name? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress—a distinction, a formal model, a better class of examples—or is the impasse a sign that the question is misposed?
