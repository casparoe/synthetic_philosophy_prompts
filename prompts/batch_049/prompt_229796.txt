I'm teaching an undergraduate seminar on moral status, and I want to pair Kant's account of our duties regarding animals with Anna Sewell's Black Beauty. Kant is standardly read as holding that we have no direct duties to animals, because animals lack the rational nature that would make them ends in themselves; on this reading, cruelty to animals is wrong only indirectly, because it deadens the sympathies that dispose us to treat human beings well, while kindness to animals cultivates those same dispositions. Read Black Beauty through that lens. The novel is narrated in the first person by a horse who continually sizes up the moral character of the humans who own, drive, and tend him, and it plainly asks us to feel the suffering of its horses — the bearing rein, Ginger's breaking, the beaten and exhausted London cab horses — as mattering in itself, not merely as raw material for human moral development. Yet Sewell's moralizing is also persistently interested in what the treatment of horses does to human beings: cruel boys become cruel men, kind treatment of animals is tied to good character, and several episodes read as lessons in humanity rather than as claims about the horse's own standing.

Give me a critical reading that takes both works seriously and is faithful in both directions. Does the novel's decision to give the horse a voice and a moral point of view simply beg the question against Kant's denial of direct duties, or does it dramatize something Kant's framework lacks the resources to register? Where does Black Beauty actually support an indirect-duty reading, and where does it resist one? Which scenes would you send a student to, and what would you say the novel adds to the philosophical debate that the argumentative texts do not? Be explicit about the places where the reading strains in either direction.
