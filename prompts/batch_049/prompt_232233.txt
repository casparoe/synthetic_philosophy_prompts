Over break my housemate and I got into an argument about whether you can use money to prove that people's beliefs have to obey the probability axioms. He's been reading up on Bayesian epistemology and kept insisting that a degree of belief just is the price you'd accept for a ticket that pays one dollar if the proposition is true. Then he walked me through the Dutch book argument: if my prices don't obey the axioms, a clever bookie can sell me a bundle of bets, each of which looks fair to me on its own, but which loses me a fixed amount no matter what happens. He took that to show that having incoherent credences is irrational in exactly the same way as accepting a guaranteed loss is irrational, full stop. I've also seen a version where the bookie doesn't just sell me the package up front but waits and offers me a trade later, after I update, and supposedly can pump money out of me even if my credences were only badly conditionalized.

I can't shake the feeling that the betting picture is doing a lot of silent work. Believing isn't denominated in dollars; I don't carry around a buy and sell price for most propositions; my utility for an extra dollar isn't linear; and real people have a bid-ask spread rather than a single fair price. But mabye those are just the usual idealizations and the argument's core survives them. So audit the analogy directly. Give me the correspondence table: what is the degree of belief, what is the price, who or what the bookie corresponds to, what the payout stands in for, and what the guaranteed loss is supposed to map onto. Then separate the disanalogies that are harmless modeling choices from the ones that actually break the inference to probabilism. Does the conclusion that rational credences must satisfy the probability axioms survive once the money metaphor is fully on the table, or is there a better way to run the coherence argument?
