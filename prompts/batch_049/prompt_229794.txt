I'm on the steering committee of my university's Mind-and-Brain Initiative, which gives out small interdisciplinary pilot grants (up to about $150,000), and we're deadlocked over a proposal I've been asked to write the decision memo for. The proposal is, in effect, to run Molyneux's problem as a real longitudinal study. For the two people on the committee who somehow missed it: Molyneux wrote to Locke asking whether a man born blind, after learning to tell a sphere from a cube by touch alone, could identify which was which by sight alone the moment he regained sight. The team — an ophthalmologist, a vision scientist, and a philosopher of perception — has a partner clinic in South Asia that does late sight-restoration surgery on adults with dense congenital cataracts. They want to enroll maybe twenty patients, train them before surgery to distinguish three shapes (sphere, cube, pyramid) purely by touch, then, as soon as the bandages come off, hold up the shapes one at a time before the patients can touch them, and follow them over six months to trace how visual-tactile matching emerges. The philosopher on the project says the goal is to replace the old armchair either/or with an empirically measured learning curve.

The comittee has split. The vision scientist says this is the closest anyone has come to a clean test, the clinical partner already has the infrastructure, and the money is small enough that a pilot is a steal. The philosopher on our committee says it's conceptually naive: the patients are not the idealized newly-sighted adult of the thought experiment — they have residual vision in many cases, unsteady gaze, poor acuity, post-surgical haze, years of tactile experience under blindness, and a terrifying first morning — so a null result can't tell us what the moment of first sight contains, and a positive result would just show rapid cross-modal learning, not the answer to Molyneux's question. She also says the rationalism-versus-empiricism framing is a bad map, and that funneling grant money into a study whose conceptual payoff is vague sets a bad precedent. One clinic-savvy member adds that the clinic's own ethics board approved it, but we should separately worry about putting university money behind testing someone's brand-new vision in the first post-operative hour. I want you to draft the memo I'd send to the committee.

Sections, in order: "Recommendation" (one paragraph, a bottom line the committee can vote up or down — fund, reject, or fund with conditions), "Background" (about 200 words, what Molyneux asked and what the study would concretely do), "The case for funding" (about 250 words, put as strongly as you honestly can), "The case against" (about 250 words, again put strongly, and make sure the strongest consideration on the losing side gets a fair run), "Recommended conditions and what the results would show" (about 300 words), and "What would actually count as answering Molyneux" (about 150 words). Be blunt in the last section about which parts of the original question the study cannot settle no matter how clean the data are.
