Okay, here's a thing thats been bugging me. I went down a rabbit hole on the Liar paradox — the sentence 'this sentence is false,' where if it's true it's false and if it's false it's true — and the more I read the more the debate looks totally stuck. You've got Tarski saying ordinary language is inconsistent and 'true' has to be stratified into a hierarchy of languages so no sentence can talk about it's own truth; that saves consistency but basically writes off the concept of truth we actually use. Then Kripke's approach, where truth is a partial predicate and the Liar is neither true nor false. Except then the 'strengthened Liar' — 'this sentence is not true' — sort of says exactly what the Kripkean has to say about it, which feels like the paradox just moved house. Then there's the dialetheist line from Priest and others: the Liar is both true and false, some contradictions are true, so you keep bivalence and the T-scheme but give up non-contradiction and have to retune logic so contradictions don't explode into everything. And then the revision theory of truth, where the Liar never gets a stable truth value — it just flips true, false, true, false forever across revision stages. Each of these has beautiful formal machinery and each has a supposedly lethal objection from the other camps, and as far as I can tell nobody has actually changed their mind in decades.

My working theory is that the fight isn't really about which theory of truth is true. It's about what you're willing to sacrifice first — consistency, bivalence, non-contradiction, classical logic, the T-scheme, or the assumption that ordinary 'true' is a coherent unified notion — and maybe about the fact that the participants are answering different questions: one is cleaning up formal languages, one is describing English, one is doing proof theory. Is that the right diagnosis? And if it is, what would actually count as progress, rather than just a new formal system that the other camps ignore?
