Next term I am giving a workshop paper to a mixed epistemology group. I shall defend a strong non-propositionalist reading of Wittgenstein's On Certainty: hinge certainties are not beliefs, not even unearned or non-evidential beliefs, but forms of unreflective practical competence. The talk turns on the riverbed analogy — I argue the riverbed is not another kind of water, so asking whether the hinges are justified mistakes the kind of thing a hinge is. I expect my audience to divide three ways. One senior epistemologist works with a version of Crispin Wright's rational-entitlement strategy and will press that if hinges are not beliefs, they still need a warrant of some sort to explain why we are entitled to trust them. A decision theorist in the department treats all doxastic states as graded degrees of confidence and will object that non-propositional competence can still be modeled as high credence in low-level propositions. And a Wittgenstein scholar will resist from the other side: the later philosophy is therapy, not a theory, so my talk is already doing the thing On Certainty warns against. There will also be a few graduate students who know the standard closure-based skeptical arguments and will ask whether my view simply changes the subject.

I want a Q&A prep sheet: the objections in the order I am likely to get them, phrased in the questioners' own vocabularies, each with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is weakest. Tell me which I should concede up front and which I should hold. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks. Aim for around 800–1,000 words.
