You are to prepare a speaker for the question period after a seminar. The speaker is defending the following thesis: the simulation argument, taken seriously rather than parked as a puzzle, substantially dampens fanaticism about the far future. By 'fanaticism about the far future' I mean the view that the likely astronomical size of the future makes the expected far-future effects of our actions the dominant consideration in deciding what to do, so that near-term, robustly good interventions are almost entirely crowded out by interventions aimed at shaping the long run. The speaker is not arguing that longtermism is false. The claim is narrower: once one assigns a non-negligible probability to being in a simulation, and in particular to being in a simulation whose far future is truncated, rerun, or not continuous with the base-level future in the way longtermist expected-value calculations assume, the expected value of far-future-shaping work is dampened. The practical upshot, the speaker will argue, is not abandonment of longtermism but a flattening of the portfolio: near-term goods regain weight and the most distinctive longtermist bets lose their claim to dominance.

The audience is a monthly working group of committed longtermist researchers and grantmakers. Most of them accept expected-value reasoning and the core 'astronomical value' argument, and most have set the simulation argument aside as an anthropic curiosity with no practical upshot. Two members are anthropics specialists who believe the argument can be dissolved; one is a moral philosopher who suspects that charges of fanaticism are usually risk-aversion wearing a costume. None is hostile to the idea that the far future matters enormously.

Prepare the speaker. The deliverable is a Q&A prep sheet, not a reading list and not a bibliography: the objections this specific audience will raise, phrased in something close to their own vocabulary and ordered by how likely and how damaging they are; which objections have real force and what the speaker should concede, including the concessions to make before being pressed; a reply of roughly two minutes for each serious objection, with a note on where that reply is soft; and the one question the speaker should most hope nobody asks. Write the prep sheet in British English.
