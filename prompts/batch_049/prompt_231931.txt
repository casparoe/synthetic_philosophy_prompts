I keep seeing people invoke the discursive dilemma to argue about what groups "believe" and what they're allowed to do, and then they stop at one funny voting case. Before I read another paper on this, I want the full spec instead. What would a procedure for turning individual yes/no judgments on connected propositions into a group judgment actually have to get right? Give me the requirements list: the cases it has to handle (the three-judge setup where each of two premises gets majority support but the conclusion they jointly imply doesn't, so premise-by-premise and conclusion-by-conclusion majorities come apart; ties; abstentions; members with different expertise or different stakes on different propositions; adding a proposition to the agenda after the initial decision), the distinctions it can't collapse (group belief vs group preference, aggregating premises vs aggregating the conclusion directly, a real collective judgment vs a merely practical decision, and things that depend on voting order vs the group's underlying view), and the results it can't contradict, like the fact that premise-majority voting can force an inconsistent joint position on some agendas. For each requirement say why it bites and whether it's non-negotiable or just one camp's taste. Then tell me which single requirement rules out the most standard aggregation rules, and whether the non-negotiable ones are jointly satisfiable at all. A table is fine, but keep the scoring of existing rules brief.
