I chair the three-person disputes panel for our housing cooperative, which has about eighty units and no professional management. We sit twice a month and hear the usual frictions: noise, pets, use of the shared garden, unpaid common fees, unauthorized sublets, and occasional harassment between neighbors. None of us has legal training, and I have no formal background in philosophy; we have forty-five minutes for each case, our written decisions can impose fines or suspend privileges such as use of the workshop or guest parking, and they stand unless the full board votes to review them. The part that weighs on us most is the written decision itself, which we have to explain in plain prose to people we will meet at the mailboxes the next morning.

I have been reading Adam Smith on moral judgment, not the economics but the idea that we judge conduct fairly only by entering in imagination into the situation of everyone involved, and then asking whether a spectator who had done that fully, and who had no personal stake in the outcome, would approve of what the person felt and did. The part that has stayed with me is that this spectator is not all-knowing, only well informed about the case at hand, and that his approval is not a test of sincerity so much as a test of proportion: whether what a person felt, and how strongly, matched what set it off, so that a stranger of ordinary goodwill seeing the whole thing would go along with the person's conduct rather than draw back. It seems to me this could correct for our panel's habit of siding with whichever resident we happen to like or recognize ourselves in, and I want to turn it into something we can run the same way every time, especially when we dislike where it points.

Please write us that procedure: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure should stop and say no finding is warranted, and where it should hand the decision back to us as a judgment call no checklist can settle. Say which steps are doing the real work and which are ceremony, and say plainly where Smith's test is a poor fit for deciding a dispute rather than sizing up a person. Then run the procedure on three recent cases. First, a resident feeds a colony of stray cats in the shared courtyard; two neighbors complain about the smell, and one says she was bitten; the feeder says she is the only person looking after animals that would otherwise starve. Second, a resident moved his elderly mother into his one-bedroom unit for what he says is a two-month stay after surgery; this violates our occupancy rule, no neighbor has complained, and he did not ask the board first. Third, a resident practices piano for two hours every evening, which is within our quiet-hours rule, but the downstairs neighbor works night shifts and says she cannot sleep through it. Be honest about which of these the procedure would decide in a way we would rather not explain, and about what the procedure forbids us to do in response to that discomfort.
