I chair the selection committee for a small private foundation's annual early-career prize. The founding document says the award is to recognize work of outstanding distinction, but a later amendment adds that the committee must give due weight to the obstacles a candidate has overcome in producing that work, and the amendment offers no operational guidance. This year we are deadlocked between two finalists. Candidate A is stronger on every forward-looking measure we have—publication record, letters, proposed research program, expected future contribution—and A enjoyed every advantage: two academic parents, no student debt, uninterrupted research time, and excellent mentorship from the start. Candidate B's file is good but clearly weaker overall, though it includes one solo paper that outside reviewers call unusually original; B is a first-generation student, worked twenty hours a week throughout the degree, and had no real research mentor until the final year. One faction on the committee says choosing B would turn a merit prize into a consolation prize and would penalize A for advantages A never chose. The other faction says a system that ignores starting points is not a meritocracy at all: B's record, relative to the obstacles B faced, is the stronger demonstration of desert, and the award should track earned achievement rather than inherited head starts. I need to circulate a three-page decision memo before our meeting that makes a concrete recommendation—A, B, divide the prize, or revise the criteria and defer—states a principle future committees can apply, and engages the strongest version of the losing position rather than a weakened version of it.

Years of reading rationalist commentary have left me suspicious that the word merit is doing several jobs at once here, and I want the memo to keep at least three of them apart: backward-looking desert, meaning what a person has earned given where they started; forward-looking expected value, meaning what the prize will buy in the way of future work; and need, meaning who would benefit most from the resources. I also want the memo to reason about the choice ex ante, as the selection of a decision rule future committees could commit to, rather than as a retrospective verdict on two particular biographies. It should guard against the common failure mode of settling on a principle that happens to flatter the candidate one already preferred. Without reaching for citations or name-dropping, the memo should say whether the committee's disagreement survives that separation, whether either side is smuggling in a different principle while calling it merit, and what decision rule a future committee should adopt. It should end with an actionable bottom line the foundation can implement this year.
