I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so I'm less interested in getting the taxonomy right for its own sake than in getting the moral feel of a scene right. The book I'm writing is set in the world of online media: a stand-up comic gets kicked off every major platform in a single week, and i keep going back and forth about whether the platforms are "punishing" him or just refusing to associate with him. The real case I keep returning to is Alex Jones being removed from the major platforms — Apple, Facebook, YouTube, Spotify — in 2018 after years of violating platform rules. He lost a large chunk of his distribution overnight, but he wasn't fined, jailed, or tried in any court. I'd like you to analyze that deplatforming as a case study in social sanction as punishment. Be disciplined about the mapping: who is the punisher, who is the punished, what is the offense, what is the "hard treatment," and what message, if any, was being sent.

Run the analysis in both directions. Using retributivist, consequentialist, and communicative/expressive theories of punishment, say which (if any) best captures what the platforms did, what each theory would say the platforms faced and should have done, and what the punishment frame explains about the episode versus what it misdescribes — for instance, the platforms' insistence that this was just content moderation not punishment, the lack of trial, proportionality, and appeal, and the fact that private companies rather than a state carried it out. Then flip it around: what does the Jones episode show about the idea that deplatforming and other social sanctions should be judged by the standards of punishment — a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case the theory never anticipated? Say where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak. And because I'll be hanging plot points off whatever you conclude, end by telling me which argument or consideration would change your mind about whether deplatforming is best understood as punishment, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
