I'm grading a short take-home final for an intro to ethics course, and I'd like a second opinion on one answer before I write the comments. The question was:

"Explain the Euthyphro dilemma. Lay out the two horns, say what each one costs a divine command theorist, and then say what the dilemma actually establishes: does it refute divine command theory, or does it only refute something narrower?"

The student wrote:

"The Euthyphro dilemma comes from Plato's dialogue between Socrates and Euthyphro. Socrates asks whether the holy is loved by the gods because it is holy, or holy because the gods love it. On the first horn, holiness would exist before and independently of the gods' approval, so their love would just be a reaction to it and could not be what makes something holy. On the second horn, the gods' love is what makes something holy, but then there is no further fact about the act itself that explains why they love it, so their love looks arbitrary: they could have loved murder or theft, and then those would have been holy. Euthyphro picks the first horn, and Socrates turns that against him by pointing out that being god-loved would then be a mere affection of the holy rather than its essence, so the definition fails. When we move this over to divine command theory, the two horns become: either God commands right acts because they are right, in which case the command adds nothing and is not what makes them right, or acts are right because God commands them, in which case morality is arbitrary. Since a theist can't accept either horn, the dilemma refutes every version of divine command theory, and any account that grounds morality in God's will has to be false."
