I teach a final-year undergraduate course in formal epistemology with a substantial epistemic game-theory component, and I am writing the final examination. I want one essay question, to be answered in ninety minutes, on Aumann's agreement theorem and its relationship to common knowledge of rationality. The question should be built around a precise crux: the theorem is habitually glossed as showing that rational agents cannot agree to disagree, yet its proof turns on a common prior and on the posterior probabilities being common knowledge, not on the agents being rational in the expected-utility sense. A strong question would confront students with two Bayesian agents who are fully rational by ordinary decision-theoretic standards, who have common knowledge of each other's posterior probabilities for some event, and who nevertheless disagree; students must explain why this situation is compatible with Aumann's theorem, identify which of the theorem's assumptions fails, and then assess what, if anything, the theorem really establishes about the possibility of rational disagreement among agents with different priors or different information. Please write the examination question itself, a model answer of about eight hundred words, and a marking scheme for twenty marks. The marking scheme should separate a first-class answer from an upper-second answer by naming the specific moves each must contain, and it should explicitly flag at least two identifiable errors: first, reading the theorem as a result about rationality rather than about common priors; and second, treating common knowledge of the posteriors as implying common knowledge of one another's evidence or information partitions. For each error, give one sentence on why students make it and state how many marks it should cost. Every part of your response, including the examination question, the model answer, and the marking notes, should be written in British English.
