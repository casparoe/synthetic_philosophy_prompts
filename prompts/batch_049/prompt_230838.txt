I write novels, so I spend my working life arranging the order in which things enter a character's awareness, and I have come to think there is something philosophically loaded about the way a later event in a scene can seem to rewrite the felt character of an earlier one. I have recently been reading about the colour phi phenomenon, in which a red spot and a green spot are flashed in quick succession at two different positions, and subjects report a single spot gliding from the first position to the second and changing colour roughly halfway along its path. That is, the colour change is placed at a point that falls before the second flash in the timeline of the physical display. The natural reading is that the second stimulus reaches backwards and redrafts what came before, so that what the subject first experienced is not straightforwardly recoverable.

The position I am weighing, which seems to be the working view among many of the philosophers and scientists who discuss this, is that there is no fact of the matter between two possible descriptions of what happened. On the Orwellian description, the subject first had a red moving-spot experience and then, once the green stimulus had arrived, that earlier stretch was edited into a seamless red-to-green motion. On the Stalinesque description, the subject's experience was delayed and assembled only after both stimuli were in, so that no pre-revision red experience ever existed. The position holds that the two descriptions differ only in emphasis: consciousness is a stream of drafts under continuous revision, and the notion of a first draft that is later altered presumes exactly the kind of privileged inner theatre that the evidence does not license. I find this compelling, partly because it fits something I know from my craft: the first draft is largely a story one tells about a text that was, from the start, being rewritten.

What I want is an account of what would count against that position, taken seriously rather than straw-manned. What observations, experimental results, first-person reports, or theoretical arguments should move me? I want the defeaters sorted by how decisive they would be, and I want you to distinguish evidence that would be decisive from evidence that would merely be awkward. Which commitments of the position are load-bearing, such as the claim that first-person reports are just further drafts, or that no timing marker is privileged, or that introspective indistinguishability settles the absence of a fact of the matter, and what would the world have to look like for each of those to fail? Which escape routes remain live, and which are merely logically possible? Does the position predict anything its rivals do not, or has it been qualified into taking no empirical risk at all? If it has become unfalsifiable, I want to know whether that is a tolerable feature of a claim about the structure of temporal experience or a serious defect.

For my own part, I would be most moved by a demonstration in which a later stimulus alters something that had already been put to some measurable use, such as a completed report, a motor response, or a recorded shift of attention, at a moment that ought to precede the later stimulus, and in which no redescription in terms of delayed assembly is available. I am less moved by bare appeals to the unity or indivisibility of experience. Answer in British English.
