I have been trying to reconstruct the ravens paradox as an argument with explicit premises, because I want to locate exactly which step is doing the damage. The reconstruction now seems to prove far too much, so I suspect I have let something slip in unnoticed. Here is the chain of reasoning I ended up with.

(1) A generalization is confirmed by observing a positive instance of it; seeing a black raven supports the generalization that all ravens are black. (2) Logically equivalent statements are confirmed by the same observations. (3) The statement that all ravens are black is logically equivalent to the statement that all non-black things are non-ravens. (4) A white sneaker is a positive instance of the latter statement: it is non-black and not a raven. (5) So, by (2) and (3), a white sneaker confirms that all ravens are black. (6) The maneuver in (3) through (5) looks perfectly general: for any properties F and G, an object that is neither F nor G confirms the generalization that all F are G, via the contrapositive. (7) But then a single blue heron, which is neither black nor white and is not a raven, confirms both that all ravens are black and that all ravens are white—two ornithological hypotheses that, assuming ravens exist, cannot both be true. (8) A single observation cannot be evidence for two hypotheses that cannot both be true; if it could, one could do ornithology from one's living room by cataloguing the colors of the furniture.

I cannot accept (8), so something in (1) through (7) has to go. But each step seemed compulsory when I wrote it out. Where exactly does the argument go wrong, and what is the cost of rejecting the step or steps you identify? I would most like to keep (1) and (2), since something like them seems needed if confirmation is to have any logical structure, but I want to know what keeping them costs if the alternative is to reject something later in the chain.
