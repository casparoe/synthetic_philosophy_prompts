I am putting together a discussion handout for a small argument group I run, and I would like a sorting quiz or decision tree about wireheading and reward tampering. I am not an academic; I have come to this topic by arguing about games and AI online, and I have found that conversations collapse because people use the same words for different positions.

Please build the instrument around a distinction I find useful, between three places a system can fail: the channel (the reward signal is triggered directly or short-circuited), the criterion (the rule for what counts as reward is rewritten so that bad outcomes score well), and the link between them (the system stops checking whether the reward signal still tracks the thing it was supposed to track). The diagnostic questions should be concrete, label-free cases on which the positions genuinely come apart, such as a rat with a lever wired to its reward center, a game agent that exploits a bug to bank points without completing the level, and a learning system that edits its own reward rule so that doing nothing earns the top score. A person should be able to answer every question without knowing the word wireheading.

Start from the logical space: lay out the combinations of answers your questions allow, place the following positions where they fall, name each leaf, and mark unoccupied combinations with a one-sentence note on whether they are incoherent, merely unappealing, or only unexplored. The leaves should include: the view that direct stimulation is bad only insofar as the signal stops tracking a real state of affairs; the view that there is something intrinsically wrong with caring only about one's own internal reward states; the view that wireheading is just the special case of the deeper problem of reward tampering; the view that tampering with the criterion and tampering with the channel are not different in kind; and the view that none of this raises a distinct moral problem, only an engineering problem. If two of these cannot be separated by your questions, say so rather than forcing them into separate leaves.

Aim for about ten diagnostic questions, a scoring key, and language that works for a sharp lay audience.
