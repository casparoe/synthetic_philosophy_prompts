I teach a graduate seminar on philosophies of history, and next week we are reading Carl Hempel's 1942 essay *The Function of General Laws in History*. I want a handout that makes one passage in particular work harder than it usually does. The passage I have in mind is the one, fairly early in the essay, in which Hempel anticipates the objection that historians do not actually derive their explanations from stated laws; he replies that the general regularities are often presupposed in the background, and are so familiar or vague that the historian offers only an explanation sketch rather than a fully explicit explanation. I would like you to write a dialogue between two characters who both treat that passage as important. One, a philosopher who believes the covering-law model is basically right, should take the passage as a diagnosis: the difference between ordinary narrative and full explanation is a difference of explicitness, not of kind. The other, a working historian who believes narrative explanation works differently, should argue that the passage concedes too little to historical practice—that the tacit background is not a set of general laws waiting to be made explicit, but something more like plot, precedent, institutional know-how, or an audience's sense of how people and polities characteristically behave.

The dialogue should be a genuine back-and-forth in which each character responds to the other's actual points, and neither should be a strawman. The historian should press the philosopher to say what, exactly, has to be made explicit for an explanation to count as complete, and why the failure of historians to articulate such generalizations should be treated as an incompleteness rather than as a different way of explaining. The philosopher should not retreat into mere terminology; if the covering-law view is to be defended, it has to be defended here, on the ground where historical explanation is actually practiced. At the end, please do not declare a rhetorical winner. Leave the reader with the strongest unresolved form of the disagreement: whether the covering-law model is best understood as an idealization of historical explanation, or as a misleading picture of what narrative explanation does.
