I cover science and ideas for a living, and I keep noticing the same arguementative move in very different fights. The pattern: someone proposes getting rid of an old rule, practice, or institution on the grounds that it no longer does anything useful. The reply comes back, in effect: but do you know why its actually there? Becuase if you can't give the original reason for the thing, then you're not in a position to say it has no reason. The person making this point usually doesn't defend the rule on the merits; they just say the reformer hasn't earned the right to tear it down until they can say what problem it was built to solve. I've watched this play out over a zoning setback rule, a hospital's legacy scheduling software, and a fight about a university's distribution requirements. I've seen self-described Burkeans do it, and I've also seen engineers and left-leaning journalists do it without any conservative theory behind them. I'm not just trolling for a label, though a label would help. Is there an established name for this move, and is it one argument or a cluster? To me it looks like two different claims: an epistemic one (the thing probably has a hidden justification) and a burden-of-proof one (you have to show it doesn't before you remove it). Does the name, if there is one, cover both, or only one? And where does the move stop being useful caution and start being a recipe for never changing anything?
