Just had a long argument with a mate about inheritance tax, and now I'm trying to see whether my verdicts on cases add up to a single principle. Going by my gut: I'm fine with heavily taxing a £15m estate left by a self-made founder to children who are already wealthy. But I'd feel wrong taxing the same £15m at the same rate if it went to a daughter who'd given up her job to care for him for ten years, even though she'd then be rich. I don't want to tax at all a £400k terrace left to a daughter who's a nurse and rents her flat. But a £400k holiday flat left to a son who already owns his home: tax it, no complaint. If grandad left £2m to dad, and dad leaves the same £2m to his son, I'd tax the second transfer more than the first. I think a £2m plumbing business left to a son who's worked in it since he was 16 shouldn't be taxed in a way that forces a sale, but the same business left to a son who never touched it and just wants the cash should be taxed like everything else. The case my mate pounced on: I don't think lottery winnings should be taxed at all, even though its a windfall just like an inheritance. My only defence was that it feels different, which I know isn't a principle. Is there a principle that captures most of this, which of my verdicts does it force me to drop, and what would it say about a generation-skipping trust that pays out to grandchildren?
