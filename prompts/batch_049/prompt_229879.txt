Last month I watched a biopic with two friends, Elena and Marcus. The film concerns a real, now-dead writer—call her R. In life R. remained estranged from her adult daughter, but the film invents a deathbed reconciliation, and the director has admitted the scene was invented to give the film a redemptive shape. The film is advertised as a dramatization, though most viewers will probably absorb the scene as history. Afterward, Elena and Marcus argued, and I could not settle it. My memory of the exchange runs as follows.

(1) Elena: The invented scene wrongs R. herself. A life is not only the events that compose it but the story those events add up to, and someone who has spent decades building a body of work and answering for her choices has a stake in how that story is told—even after she is gone. Falsifying the ending is not merely inaccurate; it is a wronging of the person the story is about.

(2) Marcus: The dead are no longer subjects. They have no experiences, no preferences, no interests that can be set back. Whatever is wrong with the scene must therefore be a wrong to the living: to viewers who are misled, and to the public record of who R. was. You cannot wrong someone who no longer exists.

(3) Elena: That puts the wrong in the wrong place. If the wrong were only to misled viewers, then a version clearly labeled as fiction—one no one believed—would be beyond criticism. But I would still object, because what the image says about her is demeaning or false. The error is directed at R., not merely at the audience's beliefs.

(4) Marcus: I would object too, but to something else. The problem isn't only that this or that viewer is fooled; it's that the shared story the living tell about R. has been corrupted. A culture that retouches the past manufactures a false inheritance. That harms the living, and it degrades the practice of remembering—it doesn't require a posthumous rights-bearer.

(5) Elena: Then notice the asymmetry. Imagine the invention were flattering: a film that erases some genuine cruelty, leaving the living comfortable rather than cheated. On your account, no one is wronged if the living welcome the story. But I don't think you believe that is fine—and if it isn't, the dead person's standing is doing work you refuse to name.

(6) Marcus: It isn't fine, but for a reason that stays on the side of the living. We owe truthfulness about the dead because dishonest memory corrupts later inquiry and leaves vulnerable people's own stories shaped by false precedents. That's an epistemic and social duty to the living, not a debt to a posthumous subject. You keep treating "the story of a life" as a possession the dead never relinquish.

(7) Elena: I am not postulating ghostly wellbeing. Person-directed duties—fidelity, recognition, non-defamation—do not simply expire with the heartbeat. That is why nobody accepts "she's dead, so tell any story you like about her" as a sound principle. The duties run to her, not only to the archive or to the audience.

(8) Marcus: Then consider the difference in gravity between a scholarly biography that lies about the same reconciliation and an openly fictionalized film. My account explains why the first is usually worse: it does more damage to the living practices of inquiry and memory. On your account the central wrong—a wrong to R. herself—is the same in both cases, and the public damage is tacked on as an afterthought. That seems backwards.
