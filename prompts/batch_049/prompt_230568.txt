I am a retired biochemist who has recently begun attending a philosophy of mind reading group, and I would like to convert our recurring dispute about multiple realizability into a diagnostic instrument rather than another round of definitional argument. The dispute concerns what follows from the claim that a mental state such as pain could be realized by very different physical configurations in different organisms or machines: some members take this claim to refute any mind-brain identity thesis, while others think it shows only that the relevant kinds must be individuated either more finely or more abstractly than our initial descriptions suggest. Please write the instrument itself.

Produce a nine-question forced-choice quiz, titled "Which Position on Multiple Realizability Do You Actually Hold?", together with a complete scoring key. The intended respondents are scientifically literate non-philosophers, several of them retired chemists, physiologists, and engineers, who will answer about concrete cases but will not know the theoretical labels. Every question should present a concrete scenario—for example, a cephalopod whose nociceptive system is organized very differently from a mammal's but which behaves as if in pain, a brain-computer interface that restores a lost visual capacity, an artificial pattern-recognizer that meets every operational criterion for a perceptual state, or two systems that share a functional profile but share no components—and should offer two or three answer options. No question may ask respondents which theory they accept, which label describes them, or which philosopher they agree with; respondents should be sorted entirely by what they say about cases.

The scoring key should map answer patterns onto at least the following positions, glossed briefly within the key:

- Classical type identity: mental kinds are identical to neurophysiological kinds, and apparent cross-system realization is an artifact of coarse-grained description that disappears once the kind is correctly specified.
- Token physicalism, that is, singular identity without kind identity: each particular mental event is a physical event, but the same mental kind may be realized by different physical kinds, so type-to-type identities fail.
- Role functionalism: mental kinds are second-order functional kinds, constituted by their causal role rather than by whatever realizes that role.
- Realizer functionalism: the shared functional role is a useful common-sense abstraction, but the real kind in a given system is the realizing physical kind, so differently realized systems are in different state kinds.
- Eliminativism: the ordinary category corresponds to no unified natural kind and should be eliminated from serious explanatory discourse.
- Kind pluralism: the ordinary category should be split into several distinct natural kinds, with no important shared higher-order kind.

Rather than writing one question per label, please work from the separating distinctions and then locate the named positions within the resulting answer space. The questions should collectively make the following separations, and the scoring key should indicate which questions do so:

(1) separate classical type identity from token physicalism by means of a case in which the respondent must decide whether two very differently realized episodes are nonetheless instances of a single mental state kind;
(2) separate role functionalism from realizer functionalism by means of a case in which the respondent must decide whether replacing the realizing substrate while preserving the causal role preserves the original mental kind; and
(3) at least one question should probe whether the respondent is treating the multiple-realizability claim as an empirical finding about actual organisms, as a conceptual constraint on mental terms, or as a bare modal possibility, so that the instrument does not silently presuppose one reading of the evidence.

After the scoring key, briefly identify answer combinations that correspond to unoccupied or unstable regions of the space, and flag any pair of the positions above that the instrument cannot reliably separate, with a sentence explaining why.
