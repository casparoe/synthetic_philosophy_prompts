I've been working through Wittgenstein's On Certainty, and I keep coming back to the idea that some of our most basic convictions — that there is an external world, that the earth has existed for a long time, that I have hands — are not ordinary beliefs we have checked against evidence, but something more like hinges: the taken-for-granted background that makes doubting and checking possible in the first place. I can see why the view says these hinges cannot be supported by evidence without begging the question, and why calling them knowledge feels uncomfortable. But I get stuck when I try to apply this picture to disagreement. If two people, or two traditions, have hinges that point in opposite directions, can they really be disagreeing, and can one of them be wrong? Since each side's hinge sets the standard by which all evidence is assessed, it looks as if neither side can ever be reached by the other's arguments, which sounds like a relativism that undermines the whole view. Please explain what hinge epistemology can and cannot say here, and whether its inability to criticize someone else's hinges is a fatal problem for the view.
