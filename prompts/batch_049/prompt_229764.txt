Before I read any further in the vagueness literature, I want the specification settled first: what does a theory of vagueness actually have to deliver? I keep seeing the debate between epistemicists and supervaluationists run through the same few cases — the heap, the bald man, the color series — and each side seems to be scoring points for satisfying requirements the other side does not accept as requirements at all. So I want the desiderata written out explicitly, with the case or consideration behind each, and an honest marking of which are genuine constraints on any theory and which are parochial commitments of one camp.

Please give me a requirements list for an adequate theory of vagueness. It should cover at least the following: sorites susceptibility, and why full competence with the relevant terms does not by itself stop the argument; borderline cases, and the difference between a case's being borderline and its merely not being known; the apparent absence of sharp boundaries, and the question whether there is nonetheless a fact about where the boundary lies; higher-order vagueness — borderlineness about borderlineness — and the question whether it genuinely iterates upward without settling into definiteness at some finite level; penumbral connections, where the verdict for one vague term constrains the verdict for another on the same precisification; the behavior of vague predicates under negation, under the truth predicate, and under a determinacy operator; and the logical constraints that may be retained or must be relinquished, including bivalence, excluded middle, classical validity, and the T-scheme for truth.

For each requirement, say what forces it, and mark it as non-negotiable, structural (something any theory has to address even if it ends up rejecting it), or parochial (something one camp insists on but the other can reasonably refuse). Then do three things with the list.

First, identify the largest family of requirements you can find that look individually compelling but cannot be jointly satisfied. The familiar candidate is a tolerance principle for the relevant predicate together with the classical validity of modus ponens and the sharp verdicts at the endpoints of a sorites series; but I want you to test whether the tension runs deeper — through bivalence together with a ban on arbitrary cutoffs, for instance.

Second, say which single requirement, if enforced uncompromisingly, excludes the most currently popular theories, and explain why an epistemicist and a supervaluationist would locate the cost of enforcing it in different places.

Third, add at least one requirement that the standard lists tend to omit, and defend it as a requirement rather than a preference. Candidates you might use: a theory must say what kind of fact, if any, a borderline sentence reports when it is true on one admissible sharpening but not on all of them, and why this is not just relativizing the sentence's meaning; a theory must explain why a vague term can be sharpened without being changed, where sharpening differs from disambiguation or stipulative redefinition; or a theory must explain the felt difference between placing a case in the borderline region and being irremediably ignorant of a determinate status. Use whichever candidate you find strongest, or propose a better one.

Keep the weight on the criteria and the cases that force them. Spend no more than a short closing section scoring the main views against the list, since I am interested in the list itself.
