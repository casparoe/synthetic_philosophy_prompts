I'm an undergraduate currently serving as the student representative on my university's Student Success Analytics Committee, and I'm enrolled this term in a philosophy of artificial intelligence course that has spent several weeks on impossibility results in algorithmic fairness. The committee votes next month on whether to adopt an early-warning system that uses a predictive model to flag first-year students at risk of not returning for their second year, so they can be offered proactive advising and tutoring.

The analytics office has put the following pilot findings before us. The model's risk scores are well calibrated, both overall and within the two groups the committee has agreed to track, first-generation and continuing-generation students: among students whose predicted risk falls in any given band, the observed non-return rate closely matches the band. The base rates, however, differ substantially: about 29 percent of first-generation students in the pilot did not return, versus about 17 percent of continuing-generation students. When a single threshold is applied to everyone that flags roughly the top 20 percent of predicted risks, the error rates come out unequal: compared with continuing-generation students, a larger share of first-generation students who did return gets flagged, and compared with first-generation students, a larger share of continuing-generation students who did not return gets missed.

The committee has split three ways. One camp says to deploy the model with the single calibrated threshold and accept the unequal error rates, because the score means the same thing for everyone. A second camp says the university should require equalized error rates, meaning equal false-positive and false-negative rates across the two groups, which on the pilot data would almost certainly require group-specific thresholds, so that two students with the same predicted risk are treated differently depending on which group they belong to. A third camp says the real concern is equal access to support, so the system should flag equal shares of each group, whatever thresholds that requires.

I know from my course that there are now-standard impossibility results in algorithmic fairness showing that, when base rates differ and the predictor is imperfect, a single-threshold risk rule cannot in general satisfy calibration and equalized error rates at the same time, and that the usual fairness criteria cannot all be combined. I have, however, only a partial grasp of the formal details, and I need a defensible memo that turns the general lesson into a recommendation the committee can act on.

Adopt the role of an expert in the philosophy and mathematics of algorithmic fairness who is experienced at writing institutional policy memos. Before drafting, spend time reasoning through the alternatives: what exactly the impossibility results do and do not rule out, what each camp is really trying to secure, and what a fair-minded member of the camp you ultimately reject would say in its strongest form. Then write the memo I would submit.

The memo should:

- be addressed to the committee and end with a clear bottom line: adopt with the single calibrated threshold; adopt with equalized error rates; adopt with equal selection rates; adopt only under specified conditions; or do not adopt until some further question is settled;
- state the facts and the relevant theorem precisely enough for a non-specialist committee member to act on them, without citing papers or including a bibliography;
- explicitly separate what is mathematically settled from what is genuinely contested among informed people, and not present a contested reading of the impossibility results, for example that they show algorithmic fairness is impossible tout court or that they settle which criterion the university must choose, as if it were the consensus;
- give an honest treatment of the strongest argument against your recommendation and explain why you nonetheless recommend what you do; and
- end with one short paragraph naming the crux on which the decision really turns and, where useful, describe a pilot analysis or institutional safeguard that would bear on it.
