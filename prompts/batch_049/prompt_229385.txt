I'm in a grad seminar on collective decision-making and the ethics of emerging tech, and we just spent a week on the unilateralist's curse — the idea that when any one person in a group can take an action that commits everybody, the group's de facto decision ends up tracking its most optimistic member, not its median member. That's fine when someone's just being cautious, but it gets dangerous when an action is okay if the whole group backs it and harmful if one person does it alone.

I also sit on my department's data-sharing committee, and I have to circulate a memo before Fridays meeting. A postdoc in one of our labs has a big dataset scraped from a forum where people talk about self-harm, and it includes usernames and free-text posts. The lab has never required a vote before posting anything to their public repository, and the postdoc says she'll put the raw data up Friday unless the committee stops her. In a lab meeting, six of nine members said they would not post it themselves: they worry the data could be used to target or manipulate vulnerable users, and the users never consented. But a lot of them also admitted they'd probably go along with a lab-wide vote to release it, because the responsibility would feel distributed. The postdoc thinks the research benefits are huge, the data is already semi-public, and a delay just slows down crisis research.

Draft the short memo I'll send to the committee. It should recommend a concrete decision on the Friday release (I'm leaning block-and-add-conditions, but argue it honestly either way) and recommend a new default rule for lab postings going forward. The committee is mostly non-philosophers, so the memo has to make the unilateralist's curse vivid without just name-dropping it, and it should engage the strongest version of the postdoc's argument.
