A colleague of mine teaches constitutional theory and all term has been issuing verdicts on precommitment arrangements without stating the principle behind them. Her record so far: she regards Odysseus having himself bound to the mast until he was past the Sirens as legitimate; she regards a problem gambler's casino self-exclusion agreement, which cannot be lifted for a year and afterward only through a formal removal process, as legitimate; she regards a commitment savings account that charges a substantial penalty for early withdrawal as legitimate. She regards a constitutional clause that permanently fixes the national flag, anthem, and official language as illegitimate; and she regards a constitutional clause freezing the existing electoral system and placing it outside the amendment power as illegitimate.

A new case is on the table. A constituent assembly has drafted a constitution that entrenches the existing first-past-the-post electoral system, but this provision is not unamendable: it can be changed only by a two-thirds vote in each house in two consecutive parliaments. I want to know how she ought to judge it if she is to stay consistent with her record.

Set out the candidate rules that fit her earlier verdicts equally well, say what each rule requires in the new case and why, and then give your best prediction along with a defense of why that rule is the better reading of her record. End by specifying a further test case that would cleanly separate whichever rules remain live.
