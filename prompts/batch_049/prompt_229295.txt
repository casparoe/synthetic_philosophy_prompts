I write a small column for a philosophy department newsletter in which ordinary documents — safety cards, warranties, event programs — are rewritten so that some philosophical position is taken literally as operating procedure. The next installment is on the lifeboat-ethics argument: the picture of the rich world as a well-stocked lifeboat with limited capacity and the poor as swimmers in the surrounding water, where taking on more passengers is said to sink everyone, and where feeding or rescuing the swimmers is said only to produce more swimmers later. I'd like you to write the installment for me: the text of a laminated emergency-evacuation card, of the sort posted on the cabin door of a cruise ship, for a cruise line whose official safety policy simply is lifeboat ethics.

Keep it strictly in-world — a guest-facing document, with no mention of the original essay, its author, or the phrase "lifeboat ethics," and no winking at the reader. It should run around 650 to 700 words, in American English, formatted as numbered sections, and remain deadpan throughout: the jokes must come from the precision of the policy details, not from irony markers. The card should include: embarkation priority and the credentials that make a person a "passenger" rather than a "swimmer"; a fixed carrying capacity justified by a technical safety margin rather than by a distributive decision; a rule that crew may throw provisions, flotation aids, or expressions of concern into the water but may not surrender seats; a subsection responding to the suggestion that rescuing people now prevents future drownings, answered by the expected increase in the number of swimmers; and a closing note on each guest's duty to the community of the saved. Throughout, land the standard objections without ever stating them — that the lifeboat frame rigs the arithmetic from the outset, that the line between passenger and swimmer is an artifact of ticket class and birth, that the boat's capacity is treated as a fixed natural fact even though boats can be redesigned and seats redistributed, and that the policy blames the drowning for being in the water while presenting its own refusal as prudent compassion.
