I am a retired geologist who has recently taken up moral philosophy, and I need help adjudicating a dispute that came up in our Kant reading group. Two members argued over whether the first formulation of the categorical imperative — the instruction to act only on maxims one could will to hold as universal law — is a usable test, or whether its verdicts are artifacts of how a maxim happens to be described. Their exchange, as I remember it, went like this:

(1) Martin said the test is hostage to description. Take the canonical example of borrowing money while knowing one will not repay. Described as "I will make a false promise to get a loan when I am in need," the maxim arguably fails, because a world in which everyone in need made false promises would destroy the trust on which promising depends. But described as "I will make a false promise to creditors under thirty on the first Tuesday after a full moon," the maxim seems plainly universalizable: a world running that law is odd but perfectly conceivable, and promising as an institution would survive. Since the verdict flips with the wording, the test alone does not tell us whether the act is right.

(2) Nadia replied that this misapplies the test. The formula says to test the maxim on which the agent actually acts, not a barrister's redescription. If the agent's real policy is to relieve his need by extracting a loan on a promise he never intends to keep, then the day, age, and lunar clauses are not part of his policy; they are added afterward to launder it. Tested as a law of nature for everyone, that real policy makes promising itself impossible, because no one would accept promises made under it. So the original maxim fails, and the embellished version is not the agent's maxim at all.

(3) Martin countered that "the maxim on which the agent actually acts" is an interpretive artifact. Real people act from mixed and partly inarticulate motives, so there is no unique stored sentence; there are many equally true descriptions of the same action. Worse, a clever agent can build his self-exception into the maxim itself: "I may make a false promise when doing so is the only way out of a difficulty for me, given my particular life and character." Universalize that and you get a law by which everyone may make false promises when each sees it as necessary for himself, given who he is. A world like that is not inconceivable; it resembles the world we already have.

(4) Nadia answered that the clause "for me, given my particular life" contains an essential indexical and a bare particularity. You cannot turn a maxim mentioning "me" into a law of nature for everyone without alteration: the law would have to say "everyone may break promises when it suits him," which confers no special exemption on you. The whole point of the universality formulation is to force the agent to abandon the special standpoint and ask whether he could will that everyone, himself included with no exemption, act on the policy. Inserting "given who I am" is an attempt to keep the special standpoint while universalizing a different rule for everyone else.

(5) Martin then said, but notice what this concedes. The test now requires a prior standard for what can count as a law of nature. A statement like "debtors with precisely my sort of history make false promises when cornered" is as universal in form as any law of nature, and a world answering to it is bizarre but conceivable. If you rule it out as gerrymandered, you are already smuggling in a normative restriction on permissible descriptions — and that is exactly the missing criterion. Without an independent account of lawlike description, the test has not been shown to yield verdicts rather than redescriptions.

Please act as a professional philosopher specializing in Kant's practical philosophy. Before reaching a verdict, steelman each of the five moves, including any charitable reconstruction that improves on the words actually used. Then adjudicate point by point: for each move, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and justify the rating in two or three sentences. Finally, say what both speakers are missing about how Kant's test is best understood, and give an overall judgment about who is more right and why. Please evaluate the arguments on their merits, without supplying citations or reading lists.
