I run a small decision-theory reading group — 14 people, mostly philosophers but a few friends from engineering, 90 minutes — and I want a game that makes the absent-minded driver problem hit people in the gut rather than as an algebra exercise. Most of them havent seen the problem. The setup I'd hand them is something like: a driver is going home past two identical intersections; exit at the first and you end up in a bad area (0 points), exit at the second and you're home (4 points), blow through both and you end up somewhere not great but not terrible (1 point). At either intersection the driver can't tell whether its the first or the second one. What I want them to feel is the difference between planning the whole trip before getting in the car — where "exit at the second intersection" feels available and a mixed plan of exiting with probability one-third maximizes expected score — and choosing from inside the car, where the same plan can look like something they should resolutely stick to or something they'd now revise given their beliefs about where they are. Ideally the activity gets them to precommit to a plan first, then execute it over several rounds under a memory-suppression rule, then gives them a chance to revise from inside the run before I name any of the machinery. Points are fine, maybe a small prize for the top scorer. Give me rules, roles, materials (nothing beyond index cards or slips of paper), a timeline, and a debrief that maps what happened — the planning phase, the mid-run revision, the indistinguishability — onto the formal problem. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I'm especially worried about people smuggling info between rounds and about everyone just always-exiting) and how to handle it. And be honest about which feature of the paradox the game can't really produce by itself and that I'll have to supply in the debrief.
