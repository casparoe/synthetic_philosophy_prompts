I got stuck on a question a while ago and I keep coming back to it. A friend of mine who works with neural networks once said that if we ever simulated a mouse brain in enough detail on a computer, the simulation would deserve the same kind of moral consideration as a real mouse, and my cousin said that was obviously ridiculous because it's just numbers being moved around. I don't know who's right, but I've spent a lot of time since trying to figure out what a good answer would even have to get right. The thing I keep noticing is that every answer runs into a different wall. If you say only biological brains matter, that feels like cheating, like you're saying the hardware matters more than the computation. If you say any computation with the right structure matters, then you have to say which structure, and then all these weird cases show up: my gut apparently has a huge number of neurons and does something like information processing but nobody thinks my digestion is morally relevant in itself; a giant lookup table that gave exactly the answers I give would act like me but it seems like there'd be nobody home inside the table; a program nobody ever runs seems not to matter, but if a million people run it at once it suddenly seems like it might, and I cant tell what changed. I also get confused about whether part of a computation can matter without the whole thing mattering, whether a computation that's just written down in the code counts the same as one actually being stepped through, wether speed matters (one step a year versus a billion steps a second), and whether the thing has to be able to notice its own states in order for it to count.

So here's what I'd like, if you can help: I want the checklist you'd use to judge a theory of which computations matter morally, if any do. Not the theory itself, but the requirements an adequate theory has to meet, with the case or consideration behind each one. The purpose would be a theory good enough to actually settle the mouse-simulation debate and the weirder cases downstream of it, not just redescribe them. For each requirement, I'd like to know whether it's non-negotiable or whether it's really just the preference of a particular side of the argument. And I especially want to know whether some of these requirements can't all be satisfied at once, because that would explain a lot about why the argument keeps going in circles, and which single requirement does the most work in ruling out candidate answers. Don't bother scoring the existing answers against the list; I want the criteria themselves. And could you write it as continuous prose, like a letter, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
