Animal welfare law has a weird shape: in most places it is a crime to make an animal suffer — the cruelty statutes — but it is legal, and usually completely normal, to kill an animal, provided it is done humanely. And if someone kills your dog, the wrong the law recognizes is damage to your property, not the loss of a life that mattered to the dog. So the practice seems to presuppose that an animal's pain has intrinsic moral weight, while the animal's future good life has none at all. Reconstruct the theory of value this practice presupposes. Is the practice actully committed to a suffering-focused view like negative utilitarianism, or is it compatible with several different theories once you distinguish what it strictly presupposes from what it merely fits with? And what would have to change in law if we made the implicit theory explicit and applied it consistently — either by taking lost future pleasures seriously, or by going the other way and treating only suffering as morally relevant?
