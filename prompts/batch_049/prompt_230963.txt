Consider the way one transplant system treats two donors. A ventilator-supported patient whose entire brain has irreversibly ceased to function is declared dead while the body is still warm and the heart still beats; surgeons then remove the vital organs under the dead donor rule, which forbids taking vital organs from a living donor. In the same hospital, another patient who is not brain-dead but whose heartbeat has stopped and will not be restarted is declared dead after a short prescribed interval of absent circulation, and again organs are taken under the dead donor rule. The two protocols sit side by side. What theory of death does this arrangement presuppose? Does it presuppose that death is one univocal event, or that "death" is a partly normative status that can be settled differently under different pathways to dying? Reconstruct the implicit account of what it is for a human being to die that makes both practices intelligible; say whether the two protocols presuppose consistent or conflicting views; and identify what would have to change in the practice if that account were replaced by a strictly whole-brain, higher-brain, or circulatory criterion of death. Also say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, i.e., which rival theories of death could license the same bedside behavior. Write in continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
