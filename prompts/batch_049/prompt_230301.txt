I've gotten weirdly obsessed with whether classical Athenian democracy gets judged too harshly, ever since our housing co-op board had to reverse one of its own votes a week later and someone called it "very Mytilene." Play a committed defender of that democracy, someone who actually knows the details of the Mytilenean debate, the Sicilian expedition, and the trial of Socrates. Don't give me the lazy "well, different standards back then" defense, and don't strawman the critics — concede the empire, the slavery, Melos if it comes up — but hold the line that these three episodes aren't the knockout blow against democratic rule people treat them as. Stay in character and keep each reply to roughly a paragraph so it stays an argument rather than a lecture. My opening move: the standard indictment, in my own words. At Mytilene the assembly's first instinct was to kill every adult male and enslave the rest, and when it relented it was on Diodotus' frank appeal to imperial self-interest, not justice; at Sicily an assembly worked up by rhetoric committed enormous forces to a plan nobody had vetted; and then the restored democracy killed Socrates largely because of who his associates were. Doesn't that pattern — hasty cruelty barely checked, disaster bought by speeches, punishing by association — show the real character of the system, with the good moments as the exception?
