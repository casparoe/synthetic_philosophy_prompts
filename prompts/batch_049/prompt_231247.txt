I've been roped into running a hands-on session on Bertrand's paradox for my university's philosophy-and-math club — about 20 undergrads, mixed prep, 75 minutes, a couple of them basically math-phobes so the materials have to be dead simple. I don't want to lecture and draw the three chords on the board; I want them to actually run into the fact that the principle of indifference doesn't by itself pick out what "choose a random chord" means. Design a runnable activity: rules, cheap materials (paper, pencils, string, toothpicks, whatever), a timeline, and a debrief that maps what happened onto why the setup is underdetermined. Use the usual benchmark: a chord counts as long if it's longer than a side of the inscribed equilateral triangle. Ideally groups generate chords under different randomization procedures and only notice the disagreement when they compare tallies — maybe have everyone write down a predicted fraction first so they commit. No lecture up front beyond the minimum rules. Be honest about what the exercise can't show (the deeper question whether there's a privileged measure, not just that people happened to use different procedures) and what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it.
