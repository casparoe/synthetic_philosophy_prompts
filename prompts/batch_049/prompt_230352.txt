In my reading group the debate about future contingents has stalled in a way I find philosophically revealing. One side holds that a statement made today about tomorrow's sea battle is already either true or false, because there is no logical space outside bivalence: if it is not now true, then it is false, and either way the outcome is already fixed. The other side resists, saying that neither that a sea battle will occur nor its denial is true yet, and that this is exactly what it means for the outcome to be open; only the event itself makes one of them true. In On Interpretation, chapter 9, Aristotle puts the difficulty in much this form: he says that if every affirmation is already true or false, then nothing would happen by chance, but he also refuses to accept that everything is necessary. The modern dispute seems to have reproduced his stand-off without resolving it. My hypothesis is that the impasse is not about the metaphysics of time or about the empirical question of indeterminism, but about what it takes for a future-tense claim to be true: one side requires a present ground that already makes it true, while the other requires only that it already have a determinate truth-value. Diagnose the structure of this disagreement as carefully as you can. Is it verbal, substantive, or a difference about standards of evaluation? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress—a formal result, a sharper distinction, a rerouting of the question, or a reason to expect none?
