You are a philosopher of history who specializes in historical epistemology and the logic of evidence. I am an undergraduate currently taking a course on the epistemology of historical knowledge, and before I read further on a problem that keeps coming up, I want to get my standards of adequacy clear. The problem is this: under what conditions does a legend, saga, or tradition count as history? The three recurring cases in the course are the Trojan War, the Arthurian material, and the Exodus. They interest me because they seem to sit at different points on the spectrum. For Troy, there is an excavated Bronze Age citadel and possible echoes in Anatolian administrative records, but the epic narrative itself remains mostly uncorroborated. For Arthur, there is almost no contemporary documentation, and the earliest surviving narrative sources are separated from the supposed events by centuries. For the Exodus, there is a foundational story of enormous cultural and religious weight, but the archaeological record has not produced any widely accepted direct confirmation of the events as described.

I am not asking you to adjudicate those three cases yet. What I want first is the specification: a list of requirements that any adequate theory of the legend-to-history threshold has to satisfy. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and say whether it is non-negotiable or merely a preference of one historiographical camp. Flag any pairs of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied. Then identify the single requirement that does the most work in excluding popular positions, for example the view that a real Troy simply makes the Iliad historical, or the view that a poetic genre rules out any recoverable historical content. Please begin by brainstorming candidate criteria before committing to the list, and then present the requirements in a clean numbered list. I am not asking for a reading list or citations; I want the conceptual criteria themselves.
