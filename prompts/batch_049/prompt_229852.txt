I cover science and ideas for a general-interest outlet, and I am working on a long piece about a standoff in epistemology that has persisted for roughly two decades without much movement. The question is whether a person can rationally hold a belief while also believing that their evidence does not support it—the problem of epistemic akrasia—and, more broadly, what a thinker should do with higher-order evidence: evidence about how trustworthy one's own reasoning is, such as being sleep-deprived, having been selected in a way known to bias, or being told by a reliable source that one has made an error. One side holds that such evidence always requires a person to lower confidence in the first-order conclusion, and that retaining the belief while acknowledging the undermining evidence is always a rational failure. The other side holds that there are cases in which retaining the first-order belief is rational even when one acknowledges that, from the outside, one's performance looks suspect—for instance, when the calculation is transparently before one's eyes and one has no independent grip on the source's assessment. What puzzles me, and what I want to make the centre of the piece, is that both sides seem to agree on the descriptive facts of the stock cases—the checked arithmetic, the disagreement with a peer, the known bias—and yet neither has moved for years. My working hypothesis is that the apparent deadlock is really several disputes running under one label: a verbal dispute about whether "rational" means coherence among one's attitudes or responsiveness to one's evidence; a substantive dispute about whether higher-order evidence is a distinct category of evidence or just first-order evidence about one's own reliability; and a methodological dispute about whether epistemic norms are wide-scope requirements on combinations of attitudes or narrow-scope instructions to follow the evidence. I want a diagnosis, not a survey or a verdict: what kind of disagreement is this, exactly? Is it verbal, substantive, or two fields answering different questions under one phrase? And given that diagnosis, what would count as progress—a formal result, a distinction, a different question, an empirical finding—and should we expect any single resolution to satisfy both sides? I would also like an honest assessment of whether the impasse itself indicates that the original question is ill-posed.
