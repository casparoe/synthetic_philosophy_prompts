I've been thinking about a strange inconsistency in how story-driven games treat their characters, and I want someone to work out what it presupposes. The same game will mark some characters as people: a companion's death triggers mourning, a shopkeeper remembers how you treated her, and the design nudges the player to feel that something was done to them. But it will also let you kill interchangeable guards or bystanders by the dozen, and the game mostly treats that as a transactional problem—a bounty, a lowered morality meter, or a reload that makes the whole episode unhappen. The reload is what puzzles me most: the game offers to erase the act entirely, as if the wrongness belonged to the saved world rather than to what was done to the character. What view of who can be wronged, and of what it is to wrong someone, does this whole practice presuppose? Is it quietly assuming several incompatible things at once? And what would have to change in how these games are designed and played if we replaced that implicit view with a single consistent one—either that fictional characters can genuinely be wronged, or that none of the violence toward them matters morally at all?
