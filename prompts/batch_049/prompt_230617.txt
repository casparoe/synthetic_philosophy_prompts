I am a retired ecologist who has recently started reading classical Chinese philosophy, and I have found myself stuck on a passage in Mencius, Gaozi I (in James Legge’s translation). After the philosopher Gaozi says that life is what we call nature, Mencius presses him with a sequence of analogies:

Mencius added, "Is the whiteness of a white feather like that of white snow, and the whiteness of white snow like that of white jade?" Gao again said "Yes." "Very well," pursued Mencius. "Is the nature of a dog like the nature of an ox, and the nature of an ox like the nature of a man?"

I quote this because it seems to me the compressed joint of the whole Mencius–Xunzi quarrel. Mencius here treats "nature" not as what humans are born with in the sense of bare biological endowment, but as what is distinctly human in a normative sense; if "nature" just meant "what is born with one," he thinks, the dog's nature would be on a level with the ox's and the man's. Xunzi, as I understand him, appears to drive exactly through the opening Mencius is trying to close: he accepts that humans are born with desires and passions that lead to conflict and wants to say that goodness comes from acquired ritual, teaching, and artifice, not from inborn tendency. Yet both of them clearly want to claim they are talking about human nature, not just two different things under one name. My working hypothesis is that the dispute is not fundamentally about what humans are actually like, but about where to draw the line between "nature" and "artifice," and that the two are answering different questions: one a question about developmental tendencies in normal environments, the other about raw inborn material before cultivation. But I am not sure, because sometimes they also seem to make directly conflicting psychological claims about what children or people in the wild would do. I would like a diagnosis rather than a summary of the texts: what kind of disagreement is this? Is it a factual dispute about human psychology, a semantic dispute about "nature," a difference about which cases count as data, or a difference about what becomes of morality if goodness is not innate? And given your diagnosis, is there anything—conceptual, empirical, or interpretive—that could actually move the debate forward? Finally, because I want to keep track of the bottom line, please end with a one-sentence summary of your diagnosis, written in the form \answer{...}.
