I am a retired chemist, and since leaving the laboratory I have been reading political philosophy with a group made up mostly of philosophers and constitutional lawyers. One comparison that keeps coming up in our discussions is the story of Odysseus (Ulysses, in the Latin versions) ordering his crew to bind him to the mast while he is still in command of himself, so that his later self, bewitched by the Sirens' song, cannot steer the ship onto the rocks. In my group this story is used to argue that entrenched constitutional arrangements—a bill of rights insulated from ordinary majorities, a supermajoritarian amendment procedure, an independent central bank—are not failures of democratic self-government but rational self-binding: the polity, while it is thinking clearly, ties the hands of its later self, and that is the whole point. I have heard this deployed so often, and so conclusively, that I want to know exactly how much work the analogy can actually do before I challenge it.

Could you audit the analogy in detail? Spell out what is supposed to correspond to what: who or what plays the role of Odysseus, the mast, the ropes, the crew, the Sirens, the future self, and the original decision to be bound. Then identify where the mapping breaks down, and separate the disanalogies that are merely cosmetic from the ones that matter for the argument that constitutional rigidity is no democratic loss. In particular, which features of the Odysseus case does the analogy quietly import into constitutional politics that a real constitution lacks? Once the disanalogies are on the table, does the conclusion my group draws survive, or was the analogy carrying the argument the whole time? I would also like you to say what would change your mind: which consideration or concrete case would push you toward the opposite verdict, and where in your reasoning you think a mistake is most likely. Lead with your overall verdict in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
