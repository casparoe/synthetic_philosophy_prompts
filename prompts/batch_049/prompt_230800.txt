For several decades, interpreters of Buddhist thought have disagreed about whether Buddhist ethics is best classified as consequentialist, and the argument has settled into a standoff in which the same moves recur, the textual evidence is marshalled in both directions, and almost no one changes position. I want a diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement rather than a survey of the two positions or a recommendation of further reading.

Here is the shape of the dispute as I understand it. One side reads the Mahāyāna material as consequentialist in substance: dukkha and its cessation function as the primary bad and good, the scope of moral concern is all sentient beings, and the bodhisattva's impartial, universal compassion looks like an agent-neutral demand to reduce suffering wherever it occurs. On this reading, precepts are best understood as rules of thumb or training devices that may be set aside when keeping them would cause greater harm, and what separates Buddhist ethics from classical utilitarianism is mainly its axiology and its account of wellbeing, not its criterion of rightness.

The other side reads the path as a form of character- or virtue-based ethics in which the primary object of moral work is the agent's own mind. On this reading, what makes an action karmically significant is principally its intention; training is organized around the elimination of greed, hatred, and delusion in the practitioner; the precepts and monastic rules function as vows or side constraints rather than maximizable norms; and nirvana is not a state of affairs to be produced across a population but the cessation of craving within a particular continuum of experience. The bodhisattva's vow to liberate all beings is, on this view, a regulative aspiration of compassion, not an optimizing principle, and the aggregative, agent-neutral apparatus of consequentialism distorts it. The debate is further complicated by the fact that the two camps tend to weight different bodies of Buddhist material differently, which by itself might explain some of the persistence.

Both camps largely accept the textual facts and the phenomenology of practice; what they disagree about is how to weight those facts and what would make one classification correct. My working hypothesis is that the debate is stalled because the label "consequentialism" is doing several jobs at once — a thesis about value, a thesis about right action, and a thesis about the agent's standpoint — and the Buddhist materials support consequentialism in some of those senses but not in others. If that is right, the impasse is partly unacknowledged equivocation and partly a substantive disagreement about which of those jobs is essential to the label. But I am not sure that is the real structure; the deeper possibility is that Buddhist ethics is not a single theory to be classified, or that the impulse to place it under one Western heading is itself the problem.

So: diagnose this debate. What kind of disagreement is it — verbal, substantive, two parties answering different questions under one heading, or an ill-posed question in the first place? If my hypothesis is wrong or incomplete, say where. And be concrete about what would count as progress given the diagnosis: a distinction, a reformulated question, a restriction of the evidence, or the conclusion that no single Western label should be expected to fit. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble and no restating of the question.
