I keep hitting the same snag in Adam Smith’s account of the impartial spectator, and I would like a diagnosis of where the reasoning goes wrong rather than a survey of possible moves.

(1) Smith explains our judgments of our own conduct as an exercise of sympathy: we examine our motives and actions by imagining how a well-informed, disinterested spectator would respond to them.
(2) The only materials available for constructing this imagined spectator are the sympathetic responses we actually meet in social life — the approval and disapproval of particular people, together with the general rules we abstract from those responses.
(3) But a standpoint built out of those actual responses cannot be more impartial than the social world that supplied them; it will tend to reproduce that world’s partialities, ignorances, and prejudices.
(4) Yet Smith also wants the impartial spectator to correct the verdicts of actual audiences: it is supposed to let us judge our conduct independently of what the people around us happen to approve.
(5) The spectator therefore seems required to be both a product of actual social sentiment and a standard that can overturn that sentiment. If it is nothing over and above the product, it has no independent purchase; if it does have independent purchase, its authority no longer seems to come from the sympathy story.
(6) So Smith’s account looks internally unstable: it can keep the story about the social formation of conscience, or it can keep the claim that conscience is a genuine court of appeal against society, but not both.

I would most like to keep the psychological realism in (1) and (2) — the moral standpoint is acquired through social interaction rather than built in — and I would also like to keep the critical force in (4). I cannot see which step should give way, and I do not want to accept (6). Please take a definite position: identify the step you reject, explain why it looked as though it had to be right, and say what abandoning it commits one to about the impartial spectator.
