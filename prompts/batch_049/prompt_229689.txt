I'm a science and ideas journalist preparing a long-form piece on what historical materialism can still contribute to explanatory reporting, and I want someone to audit the set of commitments I'm planning to work from. The text I keep returning to is Friedrich Engels's letter to Joseph Bloch of 21 September 1890:

"Marx and I are ourselves partly to blame for the fact that the younger people sometimes lay more stress on the economic side than is due to it. We had to emphasise the main principle vis-à-vis our adversaries, who denied it, and we had not always the time, the place or the opportunity to give their due to the other elements involved in the interaction. But when it came to presenting a section of history, that is, to making a practical application, it was a different matter and there no error was permissible."

Against that passage, here is the working creed I'm inclined to defend.

(1) I hold an Engelsian version of the base–superstructure relation: the production and reproduction of real life is ultimately determining, while political, legal and ideological forms exercise reciprocal influence on the course of history and can preponderate in shaping the form of particular events.

(2) I read the quoted passage as setting a methodological bar for case-level work: in a concrete historical exposition, pressing the economic side harder than the evidence can bear is a plain error, not a harmless simplification.

(3) I am persuaded by G.A. Cohen's functional interpretation of historical materialism: legal and political institutions are there because they serve the relations of production, in something like the way a biological trait can be explained by the fitness it confers rather than by a step-by-step mechanical history.

(4) But I also think a functional claim is empty unless one can at least sketch the feedback mechanism — selection, class interest, institutional incentives — that connects the beneficial effect to the persistence of the institution.

(5) For the rise of early modern Brandenburg-Prussia, I do not think class structure and the relations of production explain much; the survival of that state looks geopolitical and dynastic — inheritances, alliances, military-fiscal pressure — with economic conditions operating mainly as background constraints.

(6) Yet when I write about intellectual property, I defend a strongly economic account: the movement toward stronger global IP protection and licensing-based distribution was, in substance and timing, the legal expression of a particular stage of capital accumulation.

What I would like is a consistency audit rather than a lecture on historical materialism. Which of these commitments are in genuine tension, and which only appear to be? Order the genuine tensions by severity, say what the cheapest repair would be in each case and what it would cost, and identify which single revision would reduce the overall strain most. I would also like to know whether keeping Engels's letter as my anchor makes the package easier or harder to hold together.
