I've been collecting verdicts about when my present credence should defer to the credence I expect to hold later, and I want to know whether a single principle captures them. (1) I give 0.8 now that a deal will close, partly on evidence I know I will have forgotten by next year; I expect next-year-me to give it 0.6. I think I should stay at 0.8, not lower myself to 0.6. (2) A doctor tells me a test result tomorrow will move my credence in an illness from 0.5 to either 0.1 or 0.9, with each outcome equally likely now. I think today I should already be at 0.5. (3) I will soon take a drug that reliably produces baseless overconfidence, and I predict my credence in some claim will go from 0.5 now to 0.95 while drugged. I think I should not defer to the drugged credence. (4) Tomorrow I will reread all the evidence and think for hours; I will retain everything I now know, and I predict that by the end my credence will have moved from 0.6 to 0.3. I feel I should move only partway—say to 0.45—because I cannot now see the reason for the drop, yet I cannot dismiss the future me who has thought longer and no less carefully. (5) If I expected a future me who was simply better informed and no less rational than I now am, I would defer entirely. My rough principle is to defer to a future self just when that self is at least as informed and no less rational, and never when the future credence is produced by anticipated irrationality—but that principle either misdescribes case (4) or leaves it without a target. What principle, if any, captures these verdicts? Which of them would a clean principle force me to revise, and is a complicated principle that preserves all of them worth it? Please also say which argument or consideration would most likely change your mind, and where your reasoning about these cases is most likely to fail.
