I spent thirty-five years at the bench before I retired, and this past year I've fallen hard for Peirce — the essays on the fixation of belief and on making our ideas clear. I keep turning over the four methods of fixing belief, and I want it in verse, because I think the form can do some of the arguing for me.

So here's the ask: a sonnet, fourteen lines, with a real turn after the octave and a closing couplet that lands. Let the octave present the first three methods as if they were reasonable ways to keep a belief — tenacity, which just refuses to look at what would unsettle it; authority, which just wants the herd to agree; the a priori method, which settles questions by what feels agreeable to reason. The turn should catch the reader out by showing that all three share the same flaw: they make inquiry a matter of taste, and the world never gets to answer back. The sestet should bring in the method of science as the only method that fixes belief by something outside the mind, and it should make the fallibilist consequence land: precisely because it submits to the world, any settled belief stays overturnable. Work the pragmatic maxim in as more than a label — a belief is a habit of action, its meaning the effects you would expect from acting on it, so the scientific habit is the one that keeps those effects public and checkable. The couplet should say precisely what has to go, which isnt doubt but the idea that a belief is made safe by keeping it insulated from what would test it.

It should be good enough that someone who knows Peirce recognizes each idea without wincing, and someone who doesnt should still find it a good poem. Don't quote Peirce verbatim; bend the line and paraphrase.

After the poem, give me a short plain note on where it takes liberties with the ideas or leans on a contested reading, and say which part of that reading is most likely to fail — what a careful Peirce reader would push back on, and what would make you revise it.
