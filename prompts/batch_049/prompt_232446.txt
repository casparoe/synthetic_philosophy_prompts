I co-run a reading group on Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach, and one running argument keeps resurfacing. Whenever the conversation turns to whether an AI could ever genuinely think, understand, or be conscious, one member — a computer scientist, so the rest of us tend to defer to him — dismisses the whole thing with some version of: Gödel already proved the mind can't be a machine. His argument, as best I can reconstruct it, is that for any consistent formal system strong enough to do arithmetic, there's a Gödel sentence that the system can't prove but that a human mathematician can see is true; so the human mind can always outrun any formal system; so minds can't be mechanical, full stop. I've tried to push back by saying the human only 'sees' the truth by assuming the system is consistent, and that what the system fails to prove is really a conditional (if the system is consistent, then the Gödel sentence), but I can't make the distinction sharp enough to impress him. We've also just read the parts of GEB where Hofstadter ties self-referential statements to the idea of a 'self' or an 'I', and now the slogan has a second life: it's not just about whether AI can be mechanical, but about whether a system that can refer to itself has some ingredient a non-self-referential system lacks, and whether that's what a mind is.

I'd like help teasing the slogan apart. What are the distinct precise claims it could be expressing — claims about particular formal systems, about all formal systems, about what a human can actually survey or know, about Turing machines, about mechanism, about consciousness, about self-reference — and for each one: is it a real consequence of the incompleteness theorems, is it a substantive philosophical claim in need of separate argument, or is it more metaphor than mathematics? Then tell me which of those readings would actually get my group member from Gödel to 'minds can't be machines,' and pinpoint where the slide from theorem to conclusion happens.
