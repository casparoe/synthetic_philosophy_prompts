I recently became the lead maintainer of a roughly twenty-year-old software system at a logistics company, and I have been struck by how often my colleagues invoke Chesterton's fence. Whenever I propose deleting a parameter that is never read, a validation rule that seems to prohibit a harmless case, or a redundant-looking database field, someone senior to me says that before you remove a fence you should first find out why it was put there. The principle now functions as our team's default decision procedure: the person proposing the simplification is expected to reconstruct the original rationale, and if no one can, the change is treated as too risky. I have seen the same move in a civic association I joined, where a bylaw whose original purpose no current member can state is defended on nearly identical grounds. Both groups seem to treat the fence as a compact image of a Burkean conviction: that old rules and institutions store accumulated wisdom that no single present-day observer can fully reconstruct.

I would like a careful audit of the analogy itself rather than a general defense or critique of conservatism. What corresponds to what when the image is run in earnest — the fence, the builder, the road, the passerby who sees no use in it, the act of tearing it down, the landscape around it? Where does the mapping hold, and where does it break? Among the possible disanalogies, I want to know which ones matter for the work the analogy is being made to do — allocating the burden of proof when someone proposes removing an old rule, module, or practice — and which are only superficial. For instance, does it matter that a fence is a deliberately designed artifact with an identifiable maker and a single intended function, whereas the things the principle is used to protect often have no single designer, no recorded purpose, or several conflicting purposes? Does it matter that a fence's function is local and open to inspection, while the function of an old rule can be distributed across a social system and unavailable to any one person? Does it matter that a landscape changes, so that a fence's original rationale may simply have lapsed? And does the physical case actually support the conclusion it is used to draw — that an unexplained practice should be left in place with the burden of proof on the reformer — or does the original scenario support only a weaker duty to investigate before removing?

Please give an explicit mapping (a table of correspondences would be fine), separate the disanalogies that should change the policy conclusion from those that should not, and say whether there is a better analogy or a more precise version of the principle that survives the audit. I am less interested in a survey of the literature than in your own judgment about how much weight the image can really bear. Use American English.
