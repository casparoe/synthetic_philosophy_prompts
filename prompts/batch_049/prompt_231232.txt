Switzerland, Finland, and Sweden each organize their national defense around compulsory citizen service in one form or another: Switzerland around a militia system and armed neutrality, Finland around broadly based conscription feeding one of Europe's largest trained reserves, and Sweden around a conscription system that was suspended for a period and then reintroduced in 2017 on a gender-neutral basis. Until the last few years Finland and Sweden remained outside NATO, and Switzerland still does. The conscription systems themselves are built around territorial defense rather than for projecting force abroad. Yet all three states are liberal democracies that, in nearly every other context, treat compelling someone to work — let alone to kill or risk being killed — as a serious infringement of individual liberty, a thing no state may do merely because it would be useful or efficient. Each of the three also recognizes conscientious objection in some form, typically by assigning objectors to a longer period of civilian alternative service.

I want to know what theory of the state's authority over the person this whole arrangement presupposes, and whether the arrangement is as theoretically unified as its defenders suggest. Specifically: does the practice strictly presuppose a special, non-contractual obligation of citizens to defend the state, an obligation with no analogue in ordinary market relations, or is it fully explicable on a fairness-based view, where defense is a public good that voluntary enlistment would predictably undersupply and conscription merely distributes the burden equitably? Is there an implicit principle that separates what a state may compel from citizens for territorial defense from what it may compel for wars abroad, and what exactly is that principle? What does the conscientious-objection alternative presuppose — a liberal concession to individual conscience, a sincerity test, or an admission that the state's claim is weaker than the draft itself implies? Where does the practice run out, such that several quite different theories would license exactly the same laws? And if the presupposed theory were replaced by a strictly liberal view on which coerced state service is legitimate only as punishment for wrongdoing, or by a libertarian view on which defense is a service that must be voluntarily purchased, what would have to change in these countries' institutions, and what could remain standing?
