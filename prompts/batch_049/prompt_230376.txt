I was in a graduate reading group on Kant’s philosophy of right, and we spent most of one session stuck on the third preliminary article of *Perpetual Peace*. The passage, from Immanuel Kant’s *Perpetual Peace: A Philosophical Essay*, Third Preliminary Article (trans. M. Campbell Smith), reads:

> “Standing Armies (*miles perpetuus*) Shall in Time Be Totally Abolished”; For they incessantly menace other states by their readiness to appear at all times prepared for war; they incite them to compete with each other in the number of armed men, and there is no limit to this. For this reason, the cost of peace finally becomes more oppressive than that of a short war, and consequently a standing army is itself a cause of offensive war waged in order to relieve the state of this burden. Add to this that to pay men to kill or to be killed seems to entail using them as mere machines and tools in the hand of another (the state), and this is hardly compatible with the rights of mankind in our own person. But the periodic and voluntary military exercises of citizens who thereby secure themselves and their country against foreign aggression are entirely different.

Two members of the group, Maya and Dominic, argued about what Kant is actually doing in this article. Maya said the passage has to be read under the essay’s larger architectonic: the preliminary articles are negative conditions of peace, but the definitive articles later supply the positive republican and federal conditions. On her view, Kant’s objection to standing armies is not primarily a prediction about whether they always cause wars but a normative point about the relation between a state and its subjects. Paying people to kill or be killed treats them as machines, and a standing army gives the executive a permanently available instrument that insulates citizens from the costs of war. She argued that from Kant’s standpoint a volunteer professional force in a modern democracy still shares the essential wrong, because it creates a separate caste whose livelihood is lethal force, even if the soldiers consent.

Dominic replied that this exaggerates the normative strand and underplays the empirical strand. He pointed to the first half of the passage, where Kant says standing armies incite arms races and make the cost of peace eventually more oppressive than a short war. He argued that if that empirical dynamic is no longer generally true — and there are long stretches of great-power peace with professional militaries — then Kant’s article loses much of its force. He also challenged Maya’s analogy: if being paid to take risks with one’s body makes a person a mere tool, then paid firefighters, surgeons, and rescue workers would be equally problematic, but Kant plainly did not object to them. He claimed Maya’s real worry is with interstate violence itself, and she is smuggling that into the text.

Maya answered that the firefighter analogy fails because paid killing is not just paid risk; it involves being contracted, in advance, to use coercive force at the state’s direction against other human beings. She also said Dominic was making the typical mistake of treating the preliminary articles as standalone empirical theses when Kant’s whole point is that the preliminary articles only fully make sense once the state is already moving toward a republican constitution and a federation of free states. Dominic responded that, if the article is read that heavily through later parts of the essay, then it becomes difficult to see what independent content it has — and that someone who wanted to defend a professional volunteer army in a democratic republic could accept almost all of what Maya says and still deny Kant’s conclusion.
