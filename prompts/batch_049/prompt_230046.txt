I've noticed something that keeps bugging me and I wonder if it has a name, and more importantly whether I'm drawing a distinction that actually holds up.

On the one hand: there's some anomaly people have known about for ages. A new theory comes along, developed for completely seperate reasons, and it turns out to predict the anomaly just from its other assumptions. Everyone treats that old anomaly as a big point in the theory's favor. The example I keep thinking of is Mercury's orbit being a little off and then relativity coming along and supposedly predicting the offness, but I've seen the same shape in ordinary situations too.

On the other hand: someone has a pile of facts already in front of them, and they build an explanation specifically so that it fits those facts. Everyone says that's cheap, like a story told after the game, and that the explanation "explaining" the facts shouldn't count for much because it was reverse-engineered.

Here's what I can't get straight. In both cases the facts were known before the explanation showed up. So if the facts were already in hand, how can they boost a new theory at all? And if they can boost it in the first case, what principled difference makes the second case cheap? I can feel that the second case is different, but when I try to state the difference it slips away.

Does the general puzzle—old, already-known facts appearing to support a new theory—have a name? And does the distinction between the two cases have a name? I'm not just after labels; I want to understand exactly where the line is, and whether it's as sharp as people act like it is.
