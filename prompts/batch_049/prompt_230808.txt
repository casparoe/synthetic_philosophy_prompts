Last night I sat through a long argument between two friends about the Luring Lottery version of Hofstadter’s Platonia dilemma, and I’m still annoyed because I don’t know who I agree with. The setup is the usual one: a bunch of isolated players, all rational, all in identical situations, and each must decide seperately whether to submit a claim. If exactly one player submits, that player gets a huge prize and everyone else gets nothing; if no one submits or more than one submits, everyone gets nothing. They can’t communicate or agree on a scheme ahead of time.

Maya started by saying that pure rational choice just can’t solve the dilemma. Her line was that any genuine decision procedure available to all the players has to be symmetric, but the winning outcome is asymmetric, so rationality alone cannot pick out the one player. She said the only honest superrational response is to refuse to pretend you can be “the one”—either don’t play, or accept that you’ll all choose nothing and get nothing. Tom pushed back hard and said that’s giving up too early. His point was that the mixed strategy where each player claims with probability 1/N is the unique symmetric equilibrium: if everyone uses it, no one can gain by unilaterally changing their own probability. Maya replied that this just hides the problem: you still need each player to use the same p, and if they’re truly identical superrational players they’ll all compute the same p, but that doesn’t give any of them a reason to believe the actual outcome will favor them, so it’s still arbitrary. Tom shot back that rational choice doesn’t require a guarantee, only that you chose the strategy with the best expected payoff, and since the mixed one is the only undominated symmetric strategy, it’s the rational answer. Then Maya said, if you’re going to accept p=1/N as rational, you should also accept p=0, because it produces the same symmetric expected payoff for everyone: zero. Tom called that nonsense—zero-payoff-for-certain is not the same as a positive chance, and a positive chance is exactly what expected-utility reasoning is for.

I stepped in a few times and got drawn into an argument with my friend about whether the question is about individual rationality or collective rationality, but I honestly couldn’t referee them. Please adjudicate the exchange: who’s right about which specific points, what are they both missing, and who is more right overall? I want a committed verdict, not a tour of possible interpretations—take a definite position on whether Maya’s symmetry objection or Tom’s equilibrium strategy is the better way to think about the Luring Lottery, and give your reasons.
