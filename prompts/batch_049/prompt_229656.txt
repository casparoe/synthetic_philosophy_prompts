You are to draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two groups of researchers who have spent a year arguing about embedded agency. One group, call them the Cartesian-boundary critics, holds that standard Bayesian decision theory and reinforcement learning rest on a picture in which an agent selects actions from outside the state space it models, enjoys effectively unlimited reasoning resources, and is not itself among the things being modelled. On their view this is a foundational defect for any agent realised as part of the world, and the resulting problems—self-referential models, logical uncertainty, self-modification, and reasoning about what one's own reasoning implies—cannot be repaired merely by relaxing idealisations one at a time. The other group, call them the deflationary situated modellers, holds that these are long-recognised features of finite, situated, self-modelling systems; that formalisms such as partially observable Markov decision processes, recursive modelling, game theory, and control theory already handle many of them; and that what the first group calls a crisis is better described as a family of known approximation limits.

For the statement, use this working characterisation, on which both groups have already agreed: a decision problem is embedded to the extent that (i) the agent is a proper part of the world it acts on and models; (ii) the agent is smaller than that world, so perfect self-knowledge and unlimited reasoning are unavailable; (iii) the agent can act on its own hardware or software; and (iv) the agent must sometimes reason about what its own reasoning implies, or about other agents' models of it.

Draft the document they would both sign. It should contain:

1. A numbered section of claims both camps accept, phrased so as to be substantive rather than vacuous and so that neither side regards the wording as slanted.
2. The residual disagreements stated exactly: first in neutral wording each side accepts as fair, then in wording each side would accept for its own position.
3. The cruxes—the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side.
4. For each crux, a concrete case, argument, or observation that both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance.
5. A note distinguishing disagreements that are genuine from those that are merely verbal, with reasons.

Format the document as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table, and keep it self-contained: no bibliography, reading list, or citation apparatus. I suspect one crux will be whether a crisp embedded decision problem can be exhibited for which the received framework gives a provably wrong answer rather than merely no answer, and another will be whether the revisionists' replacements can themselves be stated without reintroducing Cartesian assumptions; treat these only as hypotheses to confirm, sharpen, or discard as the drafting requires.

Finally, append a short drafter's note, no more than half a page, stating (a) which of the agreed claims you are least confident both camps would sign as worded, (b) which apparent disagreement you suspect is most likely to be verbal, and (c) which argument, formal result, or consideration would most change your mind about how to draw the joint statement—for instance, one that would move a crux from unsettled to settled, or reclassify a disagreement from substantive to verbal or the reverse.
