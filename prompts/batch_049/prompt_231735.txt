I'm a philosophy professor, and for the last year I've been attached as the logic person to a small group that does pre-deployment safety reviews for AI systems. A recurring problem: the reviewers keep having to put a probability on claims that are deterministic but unsettled — "this program satisfies its spec on every input", "this protocol has no exploit a bounded attacker would find in a week", "this lemma in the safety case actually follows from the axioms we coded." Right now they make up numbers, or treat the claims like weather forecasts, and nobody can reconstruct the number afterward. Standard Bayesianism is no help here: a logically omniscient agent already assigns probability 1 or 0 to every theorem of arithmetic, and my reviewers are not logically omniscient. I know the logical-induction program is aimed at exactly this — finite reasoners that assign sensible credences to every sentence of a formal language, including sentences whose truth is just a matter of proof, that don't lose too badly to any computably specified bookie (any mechanical betting strategy trying to exploit their incoherence), and that creep toward the truth as they get more compute. But I've never managed to turn it into something an engineer would run.

So turn that idea into a reusable procedure a safety reviewer with an engineering background can actually run. They have about two hours per claim. They can gather bounded evidence — run a static analyzer or a solver for some fixed time, ask one or two domain experts, look at how similar claims resolved in the past — but they cant settle the claim, or it wouldn't be asked. The reviewers will ignore anything that reads like a philosophy lecture, so the theory has to show up as questions, steps, and cutoffs. The principle I want preserved is that unresolved logical claims get treated under much the same norms as empirical uncertainty, with the extra twist that running a computation is itself a form of evidence the procedure can buy by the minute. The procedure should output a credence, a "where should the next hour of checking go" reccomendation, and a stop condition for when to just report the number instead of gathering more evidence. It should also mark every step that is really a judgment call dressed up as a calculation. Please include one worked case where the procedure's answer will annoy the reviewer — say, a tempting informal upper bound on exploit likelihood that the procedure tells them not to round up to 0.999. Use these sections, in this order: (1) The procedure, numbered and runnable, about 500 words; (2) Plain-language rationale for each step, about 500 words; (3) Three worked applications — the spec claim, the exploit claim, and the lemma in the safety proof — about 650 words; (4) Failure modes, where the numbers go wrong, and what to hand back to a human, about 350 words.
