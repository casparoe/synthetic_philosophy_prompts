I retired five years ago after a long career as an experimental cell biologist, and I have lately begun reading philosophy of science, especially work on mechanisms. The reading group I attend has asked each member to write down a set of their own commitments on one question, so that the others can audit the set for internal consistency. The question I have chosen is one that followed me through my working life: when do two processes or two interventions count as involving the same mechanism rather than different ones, and what ought to count as an anomaly for a mechanism one already accepts? Below are the commitments I find myself holding, in approximately the order in which I came to hold them. I genuinely do not know where the strain is, and I would rather not guess.

1. A mechanism, for me, is an organized chain of entities and activities: particular components doing particular things in a particular order. Two systems realize the same mechanism when the same relevantly organized entity-activity chain produces the phenomenon in question.

2. But I do not think the identity of a mechanism is fixed at any single level of granularity. Which parts and activities must be specified depends on the explanatory question one is asking; a description that is complete for one purpose can be too coarse or too fine for another, and what counts as the same mechanism is in that respect relative to explanatory interests.

3. At the same time, I resist the conclusion that mechanism identity is only a matter of descriptive convenience. I want it to be a mind-independent fact, for example, whether two species use the same synaptic mechanism or whether two tumor types progress through the same pathway, a fact that our descriptions can get right or wrong.

4. I also hold that a mechanism can survive the replacement of its particular components. If the same activities and the same pattern of organization are preserved while individual molecules or cells are substituted — an orthologous protein, a transplanted cell population, a side-chain substitution — I count it as the same mechanism. The entities matter, but I do not treat exact molecule-by-molecule identity as required.

5. I find myself classifying downstream convergence as sameness of mechanism for explanatory purposes. When a dozen upstream signals pass through one shared rate-limiting step and a common effector cascade, I say the processes share a mechanism, namely the mechanism of the shared step and its downstream cascade, even though they began at different receptors and differ widely upstream. It would seem pedantic to call those different mechanisms when the reason for grouping them is the shared bottleneck.

6. About anomalies, my rule in the laboratory was this: a single surprising result that appeared to contradict an accepted mechanism was first a reason to check auxiliary assumptions — the specificity of the intervention, the integrity of the preparation, off-target effects — rather than a reason to abandon the mechanism. I would hold the mechanism responsible only if the anomaly persisted across independent methods, independent preparations, and ideally independent laboratories.

7. Yet I have always believed that scientific claims, including claims about mechanisms, are revisable in principle and that no amount of past confirmation puts one forever beyond the reach of evidence. I also believe that when an anomaly directly targets the central activity of a mechanism — the very step that does the explanatory work — that step should not be shielded by the auxiliary-assumption strategy; such an anomaly is evidence against the mechanism from the outset.

Please go through these commitments as an auditor would. For each pair that appears to pull against another, say whether the tension is real or only apparent and how one tells the difference. Where it is real, rank the genuine tensions by severity and give the cheapest repair for each, together with what the repair costs. Then tell me which single revision of the whole profile would relieve the most strain while changing the least of what I actually believed in the laboratory and still find plausible on the page.
