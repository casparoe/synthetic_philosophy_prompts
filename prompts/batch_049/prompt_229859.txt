I have followed the philosophical debate over open borders and the right to exclude for years, and it seems to me the dialectic has been nearly stationary for decades despite very broad agreement on facts and on several values. Both sides grant that border enforcement predictably imposes severe harms on people fleeing violence, poverty, or merely poor opportunities. Both sides grant that coercive restrictions stand in need of justification. Both sides grant that citizens have special obligations to one another. Yet the same moves recur without moving anyone. The open-borders side appeals to freedom of movement, the moral arbitrariness of birthright citizenship, and the fact that exclusion is a standing threat of force against people whose only relevant property is having been born elsewhere. The exclusion side appeals to collective self-determination, freedom of association, and the claim that a state that cannot control entry cannot discharge even its domestic obligations, including those it owes to the worst off among its own citizens. Each side insists the other has not refuted these values but has misassigned their scope.

My working hypothesis is that the real dispute is about the baseline. The open-borders side treats free movement as the default and treats every act of border enforcement as something that must justify itself to the excluded person; the exclusion side treats the bounded political community as the default frame within which self-government, special obligations, and social provision are in part constituted and within which feasibility is assessed. If that is right, much of the first-order argument is downstream of an unargued choice of baseline. But I am not confident, and I am not even sure the baseline question is well formed.

What kind of disagreement is this, actually? A hidden empirical disagreement about what open borders would do to wages, fiscal capacity, or democratic institutions? A merely verbal disagreement about terms such as coercion, self-determination, and association? A difference about which comparisons count as data—marriage law, municipal residence, secession, club membership? Or a bedrock conflict between impartialist and partialist starting intuitions that cannot be argued out? And given the diagnosis, what would count as progress: a distinction, a finding, a model, a reframed question, or a reason to expect no convergence at all?
