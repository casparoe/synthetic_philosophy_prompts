I've been reading about the idea that big political crises come in long, fairly regular waves driven by slow structural pressures—elite overproduction, falling real wages, state debt—rather than by the surface events everyone argues about. It's a compelling picture. But I keep running an argument against it that seems to show the whole approach is self-defeating, and I can't find the bad step. Here it is, numbered:

(1) At least some major political crises are driven by slow structural variables that build over decades and hit in fairly regular waves, the way demographic pressures do.
(2) If the waves are regular and lawlike enough, someone with the right model ought to be able to forecast a major crisis decades ahead, the way demographers forecast an aging population.
(3) A credible public forecast of crisis would change the thing it predicts: elites would get reined in, debts written down, land or positions redistributed, and the crisis would be difused.
(4) So the theory either fails (the predicted crisis never comes) or it only works when nobody believes it. Either way it can't be used openly to guide action.
(5) And this generalizes: any lawlike model of history would have the same problem. So macrohistory either can't be used predictively, or it predicts only what won't happen once you say it out loud.

I'd like to keep (1), because the structural pressures feel real and I don't want to conclude history has no patterns at all. But (4) seems airtight, and the same reasoning would make every public social-science prediction self-defeating, which is obviously not true. Wich step is the wrong one, and what do I give up by rejecting it? If the argument is actually valid, what's left of the attempt to quantify and predict history?
