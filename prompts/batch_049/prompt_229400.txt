In my aesthetics class we keep circling around this weird assymetry I can't get to cohere. Art competitions and photo contests increasingly either ban AI-generated images outright or put them in a seperate category, and the copyright office in the US won't register a picture produced entirely by a text-to-image model because, in their view, authorship requires a human creator. But the same institutions are fine with an artist using really heavy digital intervention: Photoshop's content-aware fill, generative fill (which is literally AI), compositing, filters that basically repaint the image, even a photographer who sets up a camera trap and only chooses where to point it. So the implicit rule isn't that algorithmic help is forbidden, and it isn't that the human has to press the shutter. What are they assuming creativity actually is, such that hours of prompting and curating a model's outputs doesn't count as authorship but installing a camera trap does? I want you to reconstruct the theory of creativity or authorship the practice presupposes, and say honestly whether it's coherent. In particular: what does the practice strictly presuppose versus what it merely happens to fit with; which rival views of creativity (as selection and curation, as novelty in the output itself, as an inner intentional act, as something a community attributes after the fact) does it quietly rule out; and is there a real inconsistency between banning AI-generated images while allowing AI-assisted editing tools? End by saying what contest and copyright practice would actually have to look like if you swapped the presupposed view for one where the meaningful creative act is in the prompt and the curation, not the rendering.
