Ok, I've got this bounded version of the two-envelope thing that I cant stop turning over. The standard answer—no uniform prior over dollar amounts, so the 50/50 step is bogus—seems not to touch it, because the setup is finite and fully specified. Here it is. The possible amounts are $1, $2, $4, $8, $16, $32, $64, $128. My friend picks one of the adjacent pairs (1,2), (2,4), (4,8), (8,16), (16,32), (32,64), (64,128) with equal probability, then hands me one random envelope from that pair. I open it, see x, and can switch once.

(1) If x is $1, the other envelope certainly has $2, so switching gains $1.
(2) If x is $128, the other envelope certainly has $64, so switching loses $64.
(3) If x is any of $2, $4, $8, $16, $32, or $64, then conditional on seeing x, the pair is equally likely to be (x/2, x) or (x, 2x), so the other envelope is equally likely to be x/2 or 2x. Expected value of switching = (2x + x/2)/2 − x = x/4, so switching has positive expected gain.
(4) So after opening, for every x except $128, expected value says switch; only at $128 does it say keep.
(5) But before I open, the chance of seeing $128 is only 1/14; if the list went further up, to bigger powers of two, the chance of seeing the top amount would be even smaller, like 1/200 if the amounts went up to $2^100. So with probability at least 13/14 I'll find myself in a situation where switching looks like the expected-value move.
(6) So before I even open, I should already expect the other envelope to be worth more than mine, and I should just plan to switch no matter what.
(7) But before I open, if I had to choose blind, the envelopes are symmetric: mine and the other one have the exact same expected value, so I should be perfectly indifferent between keeping and switching.
(8) So expected-value reasoning seems to tell me both that the other envelope is better and that the two envelopes are equal, in a finite bounded case where the old 'improper prior' move does no work. My friend says this shows the whole expected-value framework is self-undermining. I can feel something sliding in the jump from the after-opening logic in (4)-(5) to the before-opening conclusion in (6), but I can't say which numbered step to reject, or what a clean expected-value rule would say instead. Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? I'd most like to keep (3) and (7).
