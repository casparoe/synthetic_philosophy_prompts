I'm on the curriculum comittee in my philosophy department, and last week I sat through an argument between two colleagues about an old requirement that every major has to take our "Formal Methods" course. The requirement goes back decades; it was originally put in place because the faculty back then thought the major had gotten methodologically sloppy and that every student should be able to follow a formal argument. As far as I can tell, nobody on the current committee actually believes that rationale anymore — the field has changed and the old course doesn't serve the purpose it was meant to serve. But the requirement is still in the catalog, and a student has petitioned to substitute a newer logic course.

Here's roughly how the argument went.

Rachel said we should approve the substitution, because enforcing a requirement whose rationale nobody on the committee actually holds is just rule worship. Not one of us would defend the old rationale if asked, she said, so we'd be treating a dead justification as if it were our own.

Tom said that's not how institutional rules work. The requirement was enacted by people who had a reason for it, he said, and it stays in force until the committee changes it through the proper process. If we waive it now because we happen not to feel the pull of the old reason, we've replaced governance by rules with governance by whatever this year's committee happens to feel.

Rachel came back that the reason for the requirement isn't in anyone's head anymore. A reason has to be somebody's reason. If no one on the committee holds it, she said, it isn't the reason for anything — it's just a dead sentence in old minutes.

Tom said he doesn't accept that. The reason can be carried by the rule itself, or by the practice of applying it. The whole point of institutional rules, he argued, is that they let reasons outlive the people who had them; if rules stopped binding whenever their original believers left, a university couldn't function.

Rachel replied that this only works while we can still stand behind the rule. You keep saying "the requirement's rationale," she said, but whose rationale is it? If it belongs to no one, it can't justify anything we do today.

Tom answered that the rationale belongs to the rule, or to the office, not to any particular individual — the way a constitutional provision can guide judges who never met its authors. And he added that if we really think the rationale is dead, the honest move is to repeal the requirement through the process, not to pretend it's already dissolved.

The chair moved on before they resolved it. I'm not going to tell you which one I agreed with. Can you go through the exchange and say who is right about which specific points, and what both of them are missing?
