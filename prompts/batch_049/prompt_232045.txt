I'm putting together a short illustrated chapbook on diagrams of self-reference, and the page facing Escher's Drawing Hands needs a poem rather than a prose gloss. I'd like a pantoum: quatrains in which the second and fourth lines of each stanza become the first and third lines of the next, with the final stanza folding the first and third lines of the opening stanza back into its second and fourth positions, usually in reverse order, so the poem visibly ends where it began. Please make the recurrence structural rather than decorative.

The idea to work through is the relation between a tangled hierarchy and an inviolate level. An explanatory chain tends to run down through levels—intentions, hands, pencil, graphite—until it reaches a level beneath which it no longer pays to go, a substrate you simply accept. Drawing Hands refuses that descent: the hand we treat as the higher level, the one doing the drawing, is itself drawn by the hand we treated as the lower level, so no level in the loop can claim to ground the others. Don't let the poem state this thesis in the abstract. Each time a line returns in its new position, the shift of level should change its force, and the final return of the opening lines should make the reader feel the loop tightening rather than announce that it is recursive. Five to seven stanzas; rhyme only if it helps the recurring lines land. It should be precise enough that someone familiar with Hofstadter's strange loops recognizes the idea, and good enough as a poem that a reader with no such background still sees two hands drawing each other into being.
