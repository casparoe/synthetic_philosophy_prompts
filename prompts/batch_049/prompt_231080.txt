I'm working on a short essay for a class on comparative economic history, and I'd like comments on the argument before I tighten it up. The working claim is that the question "why did the industrial revolution happen in Britain rather than China?" is less a single question than a cluster of questions, and that as usually posed it's ill-posed because it smuggles in Britain as the natural/default outcome and treats China as a leftover that needs explaining. My current move is to suggest replacing it with two questions: (1) why did an unprecedented break in the rate of technological change and energy use occur in Eurasia at all in the eighteenth/nineteenth century, given that for most of human history stagnation was the norm; and (2) why did the particular region where it first took hold have features like cheap coal, Atlantic trade, and relatively high wages compared to capital? I then argue the original Britain-vs-China question isn't completely empty—it does force useful comparisons—but it should be understood as a contrastive explanation: it's well-posed only if you specify a contrast class (for example, the Yangzi Delta, not "China" as a whole) and only if you don't presuppose that a single "blocking" factor explains the non-occurrence.

What I'd especially like comments on: Is the essay's central distinction between a well-posed and an ill-posed contrastive question doing real work, or is it just terminological? Is there a risk that by discarding the Britain/China framing I lose the ability to say anything about why China's later industrialization was delayed? And is the two-question replacement too deflationary—does it just relabel the problem without resolving the interesting part? Also, I suspect my second question might be question-begging because "where it first took hold" already presupposes a geographically localized event, which may be an artifact of national histories. Any comments on accuracy or interest appreciated.
