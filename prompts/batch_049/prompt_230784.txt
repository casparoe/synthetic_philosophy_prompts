I've been trying to write down what I actually think about fairness in two-person negotiations after a long argument with my sister about how we'd split the money if we ever sold a jointly owned car, and I ended up with five principles I'd like stress-tested before I go around defending them. Here they are.

(1) Efficiency: a proposed split can't be the right one if there's some other available deal that leaves both of us strictly better off. (2) Symmetry: if the two bargainers are in exactly mirror-image positions — same outside options, and the set of possible deals is symmetric when you swap the two people — they should get equal amounts. (3) Invariance to rescaling: the verdict shouldn't depend on arbitrary units; if one person's payoff gets multiplied by 100 and the other's doesn't, the actual physical outcome should be unchanged. (4) Monotonicity: suppose we start with one negotiation and then expand the set of possible deals, but only my upper end improves while everything available to you stays exactly as it was; I shouldn't end up getting less than I did before. Getting less would be perverse. (5) Independence of irrelevant alternatives: if a deal was fair in the original negotiation, and we then delete some alternatives that were never going to be selected anyway while leaving the original fair deal on the table, it should still be the fair one.

I have a nagging sense these don't all cohere, but whenever I try to pin down the problem I just convince myself each one is obvious. I also half-suspect (2) and (3) pull against each other, though that might just be me confusing the measurement scale with the thing being measured. I don't want a survey of possible fair-division rules. I want you to commit: are all five jointly satisfiable? If not, which pairs are genuinely in tension, ordered by how severe, and which only look that way? What's the cheapest repair, and what does the best overall package look like after the repair, including which of my principles has to be weakened or dropped?
