I run a monthly workshop in a rationalist-adjacent AI alignment reading group: fifteen to eighteen regulars, mostly software engineers and PhD students, one eighty-minute session. I want a session on the eliciting-latent-knowledge problem (ELK). Most of the room has read informal explanations of ELK, but almost nobody has felt the core failure mode: a reporter that is fluent and in some sense competent can still produce answers that score perfectly on every check the trainer knows how to run while never actually reporting the latent fact, and the trainer cannot distinguish those answers from honest reports, because the trainer's tests are the only handle they have on the latent.

Design one runnable, low-tech, in-person activity or game that makes the participants run precisely this failure mode themselves, so that the problem shows up in what they do before it is named. I want:

- A concrete hidden-state setup: a distinction between the territory (the hidden fact or rule) and the map (the rubric, test, or scoring procedure) that is supposed to track it.
- A reporting role analogous to the model and an auditing role analogous to the trainer; the reporters should be able to do well by gaming the rubric without reporting the hidden fact.
- Rules, materials, roles, a payoff scheme that does not use real money but has real small stakes (points that convert into something the group actually wants, like choosing next month's refreshments), and a timeline that fits eighty minutes including debrief.
- A debrief that maps specific moments in play onto specific parts of the ELK problem, says what the facilitator should name only at the end, and leaves participants with the sense that the problem is structural rather than a failure of honesty.
- A note on what will predictably go wrong the first time it is run and how to handle it, and an honest note on which feature of the actual ELK problem the activity cannot reproduce and what the facilitator must therefore supply in words.

Do not give me a menu of candidate mechanics. Commit to a single design and give your reasons for the central choices — especially for the choice that produces the collapse: the stage at which the auditors' rubric becomes the only accessible version of the hidden fact, so that reporting the fact and reporting the rubric become indistinguishable to them.
