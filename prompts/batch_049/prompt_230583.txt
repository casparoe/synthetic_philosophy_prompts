I'm teaching Arrival in a film and philosophy unit, and I want to put it next to Benatar's asymmetry. In the film, Louise learns the heptapod language, sees her future, and still has Hannah knowing she'll get sick and die young. Benatar would say that runs the asymmetry exactly backwards: Hannah's future pleasures would be no loss if she'd never been born, but her future pains are a real harm that never being born would avoid, so procreation can't be justified by appeal to the child's own interests. Does the film actually have an answer to that, or does it just show us the parents' joy and grief and bank on us siding with them? I want a reading that takes both the film and the argument seriously: which scenes do the philosophical work, where does Villeneuve quietly assume the very thing Benatar denies, and what does the film add to the argument that Benatar's prose alone can't?
