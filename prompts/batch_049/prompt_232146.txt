You are an expert in moral psychology and in philosophical debates about the difference between preferences and biases. Work methodically: start by brainstorming the possible rules that could generate my colleague's six verdicts, test each candidate rule against all six cases, and only then form your prediction about the new case.

My colleague, Dr. Asha, a moral psychologist, classifies evaluative tendencies as either "preferences" or "biases." Her settled verdicts on six cases:

1. A wine critic rates wines made from hybrid grapes below traditional varietals. He can articulate his standards (complexity, balance, finish), applies them across regions and producers, and would revise them if a better account of wine quality defeated them. Verdict: preference.

2. An emergency physician prescribes weaker pain relief to Black patients than to white patients with the same injuries. She reports no discriminatory beliefs, is upset by the audit data, and can offer no clinical rationale for the pattern. Verdict: bias.

3. A philosophy instructor grades argumentative essays above narrative ones. She defends the standard as an explicit course aim, announces it on the syllabus, applies it to all students, and would change the grading if she changed her view of the course's aims. Verdict: preference.

4. A hiring officer is friendlier to applicants who attended his own university. He sincerely believes he treats all candidates alike and is surprised when shown the data; the pattern favors graduates of selective private universities over equally strong state-school and first-generation applicants, and alma mater does not predict job performance. Verdict: bias.

5. A vegan moral philosopher judges people who eat factory-farmed meat as morally complicit in serious harm. She can state her arguments, applies the judgment to her own family as much as to strangers, and says she would revise if the arguments were defeated. Verdict: preference.

6. A judge gives shorter sentences to defendants who share his regional accent. He cannot identify any reason the accent should matter, and the pattern shows up in data even though he regards himself as impartial. Verdict: bias.

New case: A department chair strongly favors tenure-track applicants who signal "total availability" — no caregiving responsibilities, willingness to answer late-night emails, no serious outside commitments. The chair explicitly defends this as a legitimate preference for devotion, saying a department is entitled to choose which qualities to optimize for. But the jobs are ordinary 9-to-5 teaching posts with no evening duties; the department's own data indicate that this criterion excludes applicants who are equally productive once hired; and it has a stark disparate impact on women, especially mothers. When shown the data, the chair acknowledges the impact but says the department may still value availability for its own sake.

Answer in at most 650 words, organized under these six headings:

1. Bottom-line prediction: your best guess, in one sentence, about how Dr. Asha classifies the new case.
2. Candidate rules: two or three distinct rules that fit all six settled verdicts.
3. Divergence: what each candidate rule predicts for the new case.
4. Closest analog: which of the six earlier cases is the closest precedent, and why.
5. Reasoning: your grounds for preferring one prediction over the others.
6. Distinguishing case: one further case whose classification would do the most to reveal which rule Dr. Asha is running, and how each rule classifies it.

Do not just define "preference" and "bias"; I want to see the principles generating the verdicts and where they come apart.
