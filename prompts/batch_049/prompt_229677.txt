I chair the residents' association of a converted mill building with forty flats. Some time ago, after a long consultation, a large majority of residents voted to ban short-term holiday lets. Most of us regard that ban as the fair and settled will of the building, because the noise and the coming and going had made life genuinely miserable for those of us who live here permanently. One owner, who runs six holiday lets, has simply ignored the ban. Our formal powers amount to a strongly worded letter, a minute in the association's records, and a further resolution at a general meeting; we have no practical way to fine him or stop him.

One neighbour now argues that we should stop passing resolutions we cannot enforce. On his view, a rule without force is only a wish, and presenting a wish as a rule invites contempt; the honest options are either to acquire real leverage or to drop the ban. Another neighbour argues the opposite: if we drop or water down the ban because one person refuses to comply, we have quietly handed him the power to decide which of the building's rules count, and we might as well have no rules at all. Both neighbours say the authority of the association is what is at stake, but they draw opposite lessons from that fact.

I cannot see that either is wholly wrong, yet together they seem to force me to choose between a rule that is fair but powerless and a rule that is effective but no longer really ours. Is that the actual choice here? Is there a way of thinking about the relationship between justice and force that does not set them against one another, and what would that imply about how the association should deal with the one holdout?
