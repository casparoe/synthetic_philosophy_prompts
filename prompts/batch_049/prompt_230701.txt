You are a philosopher of language with a background in classical Chinese thought. I want you to think carefully before giving your final answer: begin by steelmanning the main interpretive positions, then diagnose the structure of the impasse rather than surveying it.

I have been trying to make sense of the White Horse Dialogue, the Warring States text associated with Gongsun Long and the School of Names. The dialogue's central claim is, roughly, that a white horse is not a horse. The defender supports it with moves like these: if someone is sent to bring a horse, a yellow or black horse will satisfy the request, but if someone is sent to bring a white horse, a yellow or black horse will not; and if the term for horse picks out the shape while the term for white picks out the color, then a white horse is not the same as the horse-shape alone. The objector resists, and the dialogue ends with the defender apparently prevailing on his own stated terms.

Interpretation of this short text has been stuck for a long time, far out of proportion to its length. One camp reads it as a thesis about universals and particulars: the term for horse picks out horseness, while the term for white horse picks out a compound of horseness and whiteness, so the two are different entities. Another reads it as a point about how kind terms work in classical Chinese, perhaps that they behave more like mass terms than like count nouns in European languages, so the real puzzle is about how terms divide and count. Another dismisses the dialogue as a deliberate exploitation of the ambiguity between the 'is' of identity and the 'is' of predication — clever, but not a serious doctrine. Still another treats it as a display of disputation technique rather than an attempt to assert a true thesis at all.

My working hypothesis is that the debate has stalled because each camp imports a modern or foreign theoretical apparatus — universals, the count-mass distinction, strict identity, the assumption that a meaningful claim must be a true-or-false thesis — and then requires the dialogue to be answerable under exactly one of those frameworks. But I am uncertain whether that hypothesis is right, or whether the dispute is instead substantive and definite: about what the terms denote, what the argument's logic actually is, or what the School of Names was trying to do.

So my question is this: what kind of disagreement is this, in fact? Is it verbal or translational, a genuine ontological or semantic dispute, a difference about what counts as charitable interpretation, or a clash of bedrock intuitions that neither side can argue for? And given your diagnosis, what would count as real progress — a distinction, a reconstruction of the argument's moves, a formal treatment, a redescription of the question — or is the honest verdict that this debate cannot be resolved and should be dissolved rather than settled? I want a specific diagnosis with reasons, including an account of which assumption is doing the blocking, and a clear statement of what would have to change for the impasse to break.
