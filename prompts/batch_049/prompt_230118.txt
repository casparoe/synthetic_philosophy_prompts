I sit on the advisory board of my city's wildlife management program. Our settled practice is to intervene heavily in the lives of the local white-tailed deer population for ecological reasons—we administer contraceptives, cull animals to protect native vegetation, and occasionally relocate small herds—but to do nothing when a harsh winter or a disease outbreak leaves deer starving or sick, on the grounds that natural processes should be left to themselves. A colleague put the tension this way: we will shoot a deer for the sake of an ecosystem, but we will not treat one for the sake of the deer. What view of wildness, animal welfare, or the scope of moral responsibility does this practice presuppose, if any? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say what would need to change in our policies if we replaced that view with a rival one.
