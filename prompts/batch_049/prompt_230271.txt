For an upper-level undergraduate course in formal epistemology, I am writing the final examination. Students have completed one course in probability and one in decision theory, and the course has covered precise Bayesianism, the Ellsberg paradox, and the basics of representing belief by a set of probability functions. I want one essay question on imprecise probabilities, to be answered in 75 minutes under exam conditions. The question must not be answerable by reciting the standard motivations for imprecision — ambiguity aversion, the urge to represent ignorance, robustness under sensitivity analysis. The crux should be whether replacing a single probability function with a set of them genuinely solves the arbitrariness problem it was introduced to solve, or merely relocates that arbitrariness: the boundaries of the set look no more determined by evidence than the original single number. Anchor the question in a concrete case — a coin of unknown bias, considered through two agents, one whose credal set is the full unit interval and one whose credal set is the interval [0.4, 0.6] — and have students say which agent, if either, is licensed by the evidence alone. The question should push them to notice that neither interval by itself encodes the symmetry of the underlying evidence, and to address the difference between a set that represents what the evidence supports and a set that represents an agent's total state of belief. Write the question, then a model answer of about 600 words, then a marking scheme built around five must-hit points: (1) a precise statement of the relocation objection; (2) the distinction between evidence-supporting and total-belief-state representations, and why it bears on the objection; (3) at least one candidate constraint on the shape of a credal set — symmetry, convexity, calibration, or robustness — together with its limits; (4) a careful application to the coin example, including what each agent can and cannot claim to know; and (5) a reasoned verdict on whether the objection sticks or merely shows that imprecise probabilists owe an account of where boundaries come from. The marking scheme should assign each point a number of marks out of a total of 20 and give, for each point, a one-line note on how candidates typically mishandle it.
