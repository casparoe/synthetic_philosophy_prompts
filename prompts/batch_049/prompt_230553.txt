I write about science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I am currently working on an essay about a conceptual problem that has long outlived the historical controversy it came from: whether values and culture can be genuine causes of large-scale economic change. The natural point of departure is Max Weber's Protestant-ethic thesis, which claims that ascetic Protestant values — the sense of a worldly calling, methodical self-discipline, frugality, and moral suspicion of idleness — helped bring about the distinctive reinvesting, bookkeeping, profit-seeking rationality of early capitalism. The standing objection, then and now, is that material and institutional conditions such as secure property rights, trade routes, labor markets, price shifts, and state formation did the actual causal work, while values were at best a post hoc story people told about changes driven elsewhere. What I find puzzling is not the historical evidence but the prior conceptual question: if one wants to defend a cultural explanation against that objection, what kind of cause does culture have to be in the first place?

In my reading I keep encountering five different strategies for answering that question. (1) Preference-shaping: values change what agents want and regard as worth doing, and those wants enter directly into economic choices; the disciplined shopkeeper works harder, consumes less, and reinvests more because he has internalized the calling. (2) Channeling: values do not supply the motivational energy, which comes from interests, but they set the switches that determine which track that energy runs along. (3) Equilibrium selection: where material conditions leave several stable institutional arrangements open, culture picks one; it cannot start the engine, but it decides which path a society takes at a fork. (4) Enabling conditions: values such as honesty, trust, thrift, and time-discipline are not triggers but permissive scaffolding that lowers transaction costs and lets markets function, more like nutrients than a billiard ball. (5) Constitutive reading: cultural categories partly constitute what profit-seeking, saving, and efficiency even are as forms of conduct, so treating culture as an external force alongside incentives mistakes the subject matter.

Could you sort these five strategies into three or four tiers according to how well each succeeds in giving culture a genuine causal role without thereby conceding that only material interests do the work? Please state the rationale of each tier, place the five strategies in their tiers, and say in a sentence why each strategy lands where it does.
