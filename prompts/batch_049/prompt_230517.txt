I have come to hold the Dennettian view that the Orwellian and Stalinesque accounts of the color phi phenomenon are not genuinely rival empirical hypotheses. On the Orwellian story, the subject's report of a single moving, color-changing dot results from a post-experiential revision of memory; on the Stalinesque story, the editing occurs before the contents reach consciousness, so the subject never consciously experiences the unrevised sequence. The dispute between them, I take it, arises only because of a Cartesian Theater assumption that conscious experience occurs at a determinate time and place, and there is no fact of the matter about which account "actually" occurred. I want an honest map of what would count against this view. Which observations, experimental designs, or theoretical arguments—including timing data that might distinguish when a content becomes conscious from when it is merely remembered—could establish that there is, after all, a fact of the matter here, or even favor one account over the other? Distinguish defeaters that would be decisive from those that would merely be awkward; indicate which ones a defender of the no-fact-of-the-matter view should concede as counting against it and which they should dismiss; and assess whether the position is well insulated and, if so, whether that insulation is a problem.
