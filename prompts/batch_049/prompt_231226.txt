I've been trying to get clear about something around money and voting, and I keep going around in circles. In the US (and I think most other democracies), buying someone's vote is a serious crime, and so is paying someone to register to vote. But at the same time, campaigns legally spend a ton on get-out-the-vote stuff: they pay canvassers to pester people into registering and voting, they organize rides to the polls for likely supporters, they feed volunteers, etc. And signature gatherers for ballot initiatives are often paid by the signature, which sure looks like paying for participation in direct democracy. So the rule isn't "money has to stay out of democratic participation," because money is all over participation. The rule seems to be narrower: you can't pay for the vote itself, but you can pay for almost everything around it.

What view of the vote — or of citizenship, or of what exactly is being commodified — does that distinction presuppose? I can think of a few candidate stories (the vote is a kind of fiduciary judgment that can't be alienated; the vote is fine to encourage but not to buy; a worry about coercing the poor that get-out-the-vote spending somehow doesn't trigger). But I'd like a careful reconstruction: which of these does the practice actually require as opposed to merely being compatable with, is the line between buying votes and buying turnout actually coherent, and what would election law have to change if we took one of the rival theories seriously instead?

Also, could you flag which parts of this are actually settled and which are genuinely contested among people who work on the topic — I don't want to come away thinking some contested position is the obvious consensus.
