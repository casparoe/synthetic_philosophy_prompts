I work on the technical end of AI alignment, mostly on inferring an agent's values from its behavior, and I have been asked to write down the commitments that actually guide how I set those inference problems up. My collaborators want to see whether the commitments hang together, and I have stared at them long enough that I can no longer tell. Here is the set.

1. By an agent's preferences, I mean the reward function that, together with a model of the agent's planning capacities and of its environment, best rationalizes the choices the agent actually makes. I do not mean the agent's self-descriptions, and I do not mean anything private or introspectable.

2. Under an idealized but realistic setup in which the environment and the action space are fixed, and given enough sufficiently varied choice data, the reward function is recoverable. I treat the familiar complaint that many different reward functions can rationalize the same behavior as a finite-data or narrow-data problem, not a principled limitation.

3. Natural-language reports of values, including someone's careful introspective account of what they want, are one more class of behavior to be explained. They are useful evidence, but they carry no special epistemic authority over the underlying reward function.

4. If an agent predictably seeks resources, protects its own continued operation, or resists modification of its goals, I classify these as instrumentally convergent subgoals rather than as evidence about terminal values, because a wide range of terminal goals would produce the same behavior.

5. None of this inference can get started without an explicit model of the agent's actual planning process and its particular bounds. A preference ascription is always relative to such a rationality model; it is never read directly off raw behavior.

6. Changing preferences, including time-inconsistency, are not a fundamental obstacle. They call for a model whose reward function or discounting varies with time or with state, and with enough data that variation can itself be inferred.

7. The reward function recovered in this way is the only defensible notion we have of what is good for the agent. I do not believe there is an independent, choice-transcendent fact about the agent's true welfare that these methods are imperfectly approximating.

I would like an audit of this list rather than a defense of any particular item in it. Tell me which pairs of commitments genuinely pull against one another, how severe each tension is, and what would have to give in order to resolve it, ordered from most to least severe. For each genuine tension, give the cheapest fix and say what it costs. Then flag any pair that only appears to be in tension and explain why it is not. Finally, identify the single revision to the list that would remove the most tension at the least cost.
