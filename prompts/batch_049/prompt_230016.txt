One of my colleagues works on diachronic rationality, and over several conversations she has defended these three verdicts. First, someone whose all-things-considered preferences at a single time are cyclic — A over B, B over C, C over A — is irrational, because no available option is genuinely best for them. Second, someone who reverses a preference after acquiring new information — for example, dropping a medication after learning about a side effect — is not irrational. Third, someone who, with no new information, shifts from preferring A to B to preferring B to A and back again, paying a small fee each time to swap to whichever option they currently regard as better, is irrational; asked to explain, she says both that such a person is exploitable by a money pump and that the oscillation is unanchored by any change in evidence or values.

Now consider a new case. On Monday a person prefers A to B, where A and B are mutually exclusive one-time opportunities. On Tuesday, with no new information, no change in the features of A or B, and no change in their settled values, they prefer B to A. On Wednesday they make a binding, final choice between the two; the rejected option disappears. Because the options cannot be traded or swapped either before or after the choice, no sequence of exchanges can exploit the reversal. What should my colleague say about the Monday-to-Tuesday reversal if she is to remain consistent with her three earlier verdicts? Identify the two or three most plausible rules that fit those verdicts, say what each rule predicts about this case, give your best prediction of her verdict and the rationale she should offer, and specify one further case whose verdict would most cleanly settle which rule she is actually using.
