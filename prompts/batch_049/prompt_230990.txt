I've landed on a view and I want it stress-tested properly, not given the usual hand-wave. The view: superrationality, in roughly Hofstadter's sense, is the right way to reason in one-shot symmetric games when the other player is the same kind of decision procedure as me — same game, same information, same payoffs, each of us regarding the other as an identical reasoner. So in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma between two such players, you cooperate. Not for reputation, not for reciprocity, not because you care about their payoff: because my action is evidence about what a similarly situated identical reasoner will do, and I can't coherently treat us as the same kind of decision procedure while acting as if our choices are uncorrelated. I already accept the defection-is-dominant point; I'm not confused about that. What I want is the actual map of what would count against the view.

Give me your answer primarly as a table, one row per genuine defeater, sorted so the most serious is first. Use these columns:

- "Defeater": the argument, formal result, possible case, experimental outcome, or observation that hits the view.
- "What it hits": which ingredient of the view it damages — the symmetry claim, the evidential/correlation move, the decision-procedure-identity claim, the step from correlation to what I should do, or something else.
- "Decisiveness": fatal / serious / merely awkward, plus a sentence on why.
- "What a committed superrationalist would do": concede it counts against them, reinterpret it, or dismiss it, and on what grounds.
- "Where it should move me": abandon the view, narrow its scope, add a condition, or hold firm.

Make sure at least one row is about approximate symmetry: same reasoning, but the other player's payoffs are mine plus a little malice or altruism, or one of us has a slightly different prior. Does the view say anything there, or is it silent exactly where real life lives? Also make at least one row about the worry that superrationality just collapses into something already on the table — acting on a common prior, precommitting to a joint plan, rule-utilitarianism — rather than being a distinct standard.

After the table, one honest paragraph: is the position insulated because one-shot games between truly identical reasoners almost never occur cleanly, and is that a bug (unfalsifiability) or the right shape for a normative idealization? The thing that would move me most is a clean case where the identical-reasoning assumption holds and superrationality tells me to do something I can't endorse as rational — not a case where I'd merely dislike the outcome.
