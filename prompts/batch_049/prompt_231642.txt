I'm a PhD student working at the boundary between philosophy and formal social choice, and I've been asked to run a two-hour graduate tutorial on Harsanyi's aggregation theorem for a mixed group of political theory and economics students. Most of them have taken one prior course in decision theory and can manipulate expected utility representations, but they have not studied social choice. I know the theorem myself, but I'm less confident about how to teach it well, so I'd like you to plan the teaching rather than give me the explanation. Please lay out the order in which the pieces must be introduced and what depends on what (for example, why the von Neumann–Morgenstern representation of preferences over lotteries has to be made explicit before the Pareto condition can even be stated), the one concrete example or case that should carry the whole tutorial and what features that example must have and must lack, the misconceptions I should expect, and the exact point in the sequence where each should be defused. I suspect they will treat the individual utility functions as interpersonal measures of happiness, and they will likely overread the theorem as proving utilitarianism with equal weights by itself; there may be subtler confusions I have not anticipated, and I want a map of where each arises.

In particular, the plan should make clear how to head off the idea that the theorem delivers interpersonal comparability, and how to flag that even with the Pareto axiom in place the theorem leaves the weights on individual utilities underdetermined unless some further impartiality or anonymity condition is added. I also want one final check for understanding that would show whether a student has really absorbed the conditional character of the result rather than merely repeating its statement. Please also tell me what I should openly refuse to cover at this level and say so. Do not give me a reading list or suggestions for sources. Please keep the whole plan to at most 900 words.
