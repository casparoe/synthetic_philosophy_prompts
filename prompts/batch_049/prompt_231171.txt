Alright, here's one in the spirit of "what would the dead guy say." Take Hume's defense of suicide — we can skip the bit about whether it's ever rational and take that as read — and run it forward into the modern fight over involuntarily holding suicidal people. In his essay on suicide he more or less sets the question up as whether self-killing violates our duty to God, to ourselves, or to other people, and then argues that it doesn't violate any of the three in the cases he cares about. So now hand him a present-day scenario: someone walks into an ER saying they're suicidal; the hospital puts them on a 72-hour hold, takes their belt and shoelaces, and can keep them there even if they're calm, articulate, and still want to die. Would Hume see that kind of paternalism as a legitimate application of a duty to self, or as exactly the kind of confusion about self-harm and liberty he spent the essay attacking? And would it matter to him whether the person gets a label of "mentally ill" or just seems to be reasoning their way to the very conclusion he defended? Give me an actual verdict, not a both-sides shrug. One caveat: when you lay out his position, mark off the parts that are just straight from the text versus the parts serious Hume readers still disagree about, and be honest about where you're extrapolating past anything he actually wrote. Don't dress up a contested reading as settled.
