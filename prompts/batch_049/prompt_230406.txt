I am a novelist, and I am currently writing a character — a small-time dealer in coins and other collectibles — whose decisions over time drive several plot turns. This has forced me to reopen a question I had long treated as settled: when a person's preferences shift or conflict across time, what exactly makes the shift a failure of rationality rather than a mere change of mind? I have been assembling my own verdicts on small, schematic cases, and I would like to know what principle, if any, captures them — and which of my verdicts would have to be abandoned if no single principle does.

The cases are these. (a) Mo prefers tea to coffee, coffee to cocoa, and cocoa to tea. A trader walks him through a sequence of swaps, each time offering to exchange Mo's current drink for the one he prefers to it in return for a fee small enough not to reverse that strict preference; Mo ends holding the same tea he began with, minus money. I count both the sequence and the underlying cycle as irrational: being a money pump is, to my mind, decisive evidence that rational preferences must be transitive. (b) Same cyclic preferences, but Mo knows his cycle. He refuses, as a package, any set of trades that ends where it began, and he declines any individual trade once he sees that it is the first step of a pump; he is never made poorer. I am torn: it seems wrong to call these unacted preferences a failure of rationality, and yet the cycle still strikes me as a defect of the same kind as in (a). (c) With no new information, Mo prefers tea to coffee on Monday, coffee to tea on Wednesday, and tea to coffee again on Friday; no trades are available during the reversals. I do not count this as irrational at all — it is inconstancy of taste, a possible flaw of character but not of reason. (d) Mo prefers one hundred dollars today to one hundred and ten dollars in a month, and he also prefers one hundred and ten dollars in thirteen months to one hundred dollars in twelve months. No trade is ever offered. I nonetheless count the pair as irrational, because together they are, as I see it, a reversal waiting to happen when the twelfth month arrives. (e) Mo knows he will want cake tomorrow evening, but today he prefers that tomorrow's self abstain, so today he pays the kitchen not to serve it. That is plainly rational commitment. (f) And here is the case I keep going back and forth on, and the one my novel turns on: Mo has resolved to drive home after the party because he knows his later self will want to stay and keep drinking; at midnight he does want to stay, strongly, but he leaves anyway, not because the facts have changed, but purely out of fidelity to his earlier resolution. I want to say this is rational — it is the point of having resolutions — but I cannot see how to square it with (b), where I could not bring myself to call an unacted cycle irrational, and (c), where I refused to treat a mere reversal of preference as a failure of reason.

Is there one account of diachronic rationality that yields all of these verdicts, and what does it say about (f)? If no single principle gets every verdict, which should I overturn, and what does the cleanest defensible principle then predict about (f)? Please state the principle precisely, attaching each clause to the case that motivates it, be explicit about which of my judgments it cannot accommodate, and apply it to one further case of your own construction. Keep the answer to no more than 800 words.
