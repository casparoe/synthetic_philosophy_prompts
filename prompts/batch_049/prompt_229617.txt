For the fourth year running, my reading group on vagueness has ended the term exactly where it began, and I am trying to understand what kind of disagreement we are actually having. The two camps are well rehearsed. The supervaluationists in the room say that a predicate like "bald" is only partially defined: our past use has settled clear cases but simply left the borderline ones open. A borderline sentence, on their view, is true on some admissible sharpenings of the predicate and false on others—where a sharpening is a precise candidate for where the boundary lies, consistent with our use—so the sentence is neither determinately true nor determinately false. The epistemicists deny this. They say that our use of "bald," together with the facts, has already fixed a sharp boundary, some exact number of hairs, so that every borderline sentence already has a determinate truth value; we just cannot know where the boundary lies, because our knowledge cannot resolve such fine-grained differences. The frustrating thing is that the two sides agree on a great deal. Both accept the sorites data—each individual hair seems irrelevant to whether someone is bald, and yet no one can find a cutoff. Both insist on keeping classical logic, and neither camp treats ordinary vague talk as defective or confused. After four years, no one has moved, and the arguments have become ritualized.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not really about language at all. The supervaluationist, it seems to me, is asking semantics to tell us what our shared practice settles—what a competent speaker is in a position to say, given everything the community has done with the word. The epistemicist is asking semantics to tell us what is already true, however inaccessible, and is prepared to posit a boundary we could never locate in order to keep truth fully determinate. If that diagnosis is right, then the two camps are grading semantic theories by different measures of success, and no amount of linguistic evidence will separate them.

I am not confident in that diagnosis, and I am not asking for a verdict on which side is right. What kind of disagreement is this, really: a hidden empirical question, a formal difference, a dispute that is partly verbal, or a clash of starting intuitions about what semantics is for? And given your diagnosis, is there any distinction, formal result, or empirical finding that could genuinely move one side—or should we expect the stalemate to be permanent?
