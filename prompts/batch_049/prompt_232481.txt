I am a novelist currently drafting a story about a character whose well-meaning advice proves quietly destructive, and I keep returning to Jane Austen's Persuasion because it seems to me the most precise novel ever written about the difference between legitimate persuasion and manipulation. I want to read the novel through Aristotle's treatment of the same problem. Near the start of the Rhetoric, Aristotle objects that speakers who arouse anger, pity, or other emotions in a judge are not proving anything about the facts but are corrupting the judge's judgment; such emotional appeals, he says, are like bending the ruler before you measure with it. He does not say all emotional influence is forbidden, but he draws a sharp line between argument addressed to the matter and appeal addressed to the judge's state of mind.

Read Persuasion through that Aristotelian warning. I want a critical reading that is faithful in both directions: it should not reduce the novel to an illustration, and it should not distort the philosophy to fit the novel. Specifically: does Austen treat Lady Russell's influence on Anne as a legitimate exercise of superior judgment, or as a bending of the ruler by someone who simply has the confidence to impose her own class-bound prudence? Where in the novel does advice shade into manipulation? Set Anne's own retrospective verdict—that she was right to submit to duty, even though the advice was mistaken—against the novel's other influencers: Mrs. Clay's flattery of Sir Walter, Mr. Elliot's managed appearances, the Musgroves' affectionate pressure, and Anne's own quieter acts of persuasion. Does Austen dramatize something about manipulation that Aristotle's passage misses, or does she ultimately vindicate his distrust of emotional sway? I am less interested in whether you approve of the characters than in what the novel's emotional geometry teaches that argument alone does not. Please state your verdict with confidence, but also say what would change your mind: which argument from the philosophy or which piece of the novel would most plausibly lead to a different reading, and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
