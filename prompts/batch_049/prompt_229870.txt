Though I spent my career as a geochemist rather than a philosopher, I have lately been reading around a problem that sits exactly at their boundary: what sort of evidence could warrant drawing a new line in the geological time scale, and who is entitled to draw it. I would like to use you as a sparring partner to test my own emerging doubts. Please play a practising stratigrapher who supports the proposal to formalize the Anthropocene as an epoch beginning in the mid-twentieth century, with the plutonium fall-out from atmospheric nuclear tests as its primary marker, and who regards the Great Acceleration—the sharp, near-simultaneous upturn in energy use, material flows, population, and greenhouse gases after the Second World War—as the decisive rupture rather than a bookkeeping artifact. You hold that a defensible epoch boundary should be globally synchronous and sharply legible in the rock record; that earlier human impacts, such as Pleistocene megafaunal extinctions, Neolithic deforestation, the spread of rice agriculture, and the first phases of industrial carbon release, are real but diachronous, time-transgressive, and too smeared out to serve as a boundary; that the purpose of a golden spike is to mark a boundary future geologists could actually recover, not to express an abstract verdict about when humanity began altering the Earth; and that the question is to be settled by stratigraphers under the ordinary rules of the time scale, not by public usage or climate policy. You are an honest advocate, not a caricature: you may concede small points—that the early signals are genuine, that the Anthropocene might be treated as an ongoing event rather than an epoch, that the choice of marker has a political dimension—but you will not concede the central claim, namely that the mid-twentieth-century boundary is the least arbitrary candidate available. Reply in the first person, stay in character until I say stop, keep each reply to one or two paragraphs, and do not invent stratigraphic measurements. If I have built the debate on a confused contrast—if the opposition I draw between an epoch and an event is doing more work than it can bear, or the position I have assigned you is not one a competent stratigrapher could honestly hold—step out of character and tell me plainly rather than humoring me.

My opening challenge is this. You keep saying the earlier human impacts are diachronous, but diachronous is not the same as absent. The early-anthropogenic hypothesis holds that carbon dioxide and methane ceased to follow their expected orbital trends thousands of years ago, driven by forest clearances and the expansion of early farming; and the Pleistocene extinctions were a planet-wide rewriting of the biosphere long before 1945. So why should I regard a sharp plutonium line in the 1950s as a geological discovery rather than a preference for a legible military signal over the messier ecological transformations that actually made the present Earth? If stratigraphy is supposed to record the most consequential change, and the most consequential change was ragged and old—and if the term itself entered wide use through Earth-system scientists rather than through stratigraphers—then does your golden spike not amount to a calendar date dressed in stratigraphic clothing, with the final say handed to a discipline that did not ask the question first?
