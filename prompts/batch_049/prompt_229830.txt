I'm not an academic and have never studied philosophy formally, but I've spent a lot of my spare time thinking about a puzzle that comes from putting Descartes next to the 1968 film Planet of the Apes, and I'd like to see someone qualified work it through properly.

As I understand it, Descartes held that nonhuman animals are essentially machines. They lack language, and because language is the only outward sign we have of thought, we have no reason to believe they think or feel; their cries and struggles are no more evidence of a mind than the sounds a clock makes. The film looks to me like a direct test of that picture. Its apes speak and have an organized society, while the humans they cage, hunt, and experiment on are mute and treated as brutes. The apes, in other words, apply to humans the same style of reasoning Descartes applied to animals — and the audience is shown over and over that the mute humans feel fear, loyalty, pain, and grief.

I'd like you to read the film through Descartes's view of animal minds. My working suspicion, which I want you to test rather than simply confirm, is this: the film does not so much refute Descartes as accept his criterion — language as the gate to moral standing — and show that the criterion is dangerous because it is reversible; whoever uses it can end up on the wrong side of it. But I go back and forth. Sometimes the film seems instead to reject the criterion outright, and sometimes it seems to target a narrower inference, the move from "lacks language" to "lacks feeling," by giving us beings who cannot speak and yet plainly suffer.

Please work as a professional philosopher specializing in early modern philosophy and animal cognition. Think carefully, and before you settle on an interpretation, briefly brainstorm three distinct ways the film could be read against Descartes — as a direct refutation of the language criterion, as a demonstration that the criterion is reversible rather than false, and as an attack on the inference from lacking language to lacking feeling — then say which reading you find most faithful to both the film and the philosophy, and why, and develop that reading in detail. Stay honest where the film resists the lens: the ape society is as much about caste, religious authority, the suppression of evidence, and nuclear war as it is about animal minds, and a good reading should not pretend otherwise. End by saying what the film contributes to the philosophical debate about animal minds that argument alone does not, and where a Cartesian would have a reasonable reply.
