I am a high school junior helping to run a civics club of about fifteen students. We are planning a 50-minute workshop on electoral systems, but the discussion keeps collapsing into people stating whether they are “for” or “against” proportional representation before we have actually separated what they believe about representation, fairness, power, and risk. I want a sorting instrument that forces them to discover their position from their commitments and only afterward attach the usual labels.

Please write a decision-tree handout for this workshop. The tree should split on questions that a high schooler can answer without already knowing the debates: concrete choices like whether a group that wins 49 percent of the vote in a district should elect no one from that district, whether a legislature should mirror the national vote even if it means governing coalitions form after the election rather than before, and whether the ability to throw out a particular incumbent matters more than seat–vote proportionality. Branch on the actual seams: whether the unit of representation is a geographic community, a party, a candidate, or an individual voter; whether local accountability to a specific incumbent is a nonnegotiable feature of a good system; whether the risk of manufactured majorities is worse than the risk of permanent coalition negotiation; and whether simplicity for ordinary voters operates as an independent constraint on which otherwise fair systems are worth having. Leaves should give the position’s name where one exists and one sentence on its most awkward commitment; unoccupied combinations should also be noted, with a brief diagnosis of whether nobody is there because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely underexplored. The final product should be usable as a one-page handout, and its last line should be a one-sentence summary of the core diagnostic principle, beginning “Verdict:”.
