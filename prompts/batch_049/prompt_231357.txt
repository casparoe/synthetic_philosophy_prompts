At a philosophy reading group, two members got into an argument about Fitch's paradox of knowability, and specifically about the principle that every truth is knowable in principle. (1) Amara defended the principle: to call something true while denying that any possible inquirer could ever be in a position to establish it, she argued, is to treat truth as something that floats free of all evidence, and such a notion does no actual work. (2) Dev responded with the Fitch-style objection. Take any truth, p, that is not known, he said, and form the conjunction that p is true and p is not known. By Amara's principle this conjunction must itself be knowable. But no one can know it: whoever knew the conjunction would have to know p (so p would be known), and would also have to know that p is not known (so, since knowledge is factive, p would not be known). So the principle declares knowable something that is unknowable, which is a contradiction unless Amara is prepared to accept that every truth is already known. (3) Amara replied that the objection smuggles in a proposition about our own epistemic state, whereas her principle was meant to apply to substantive truths—claims about the deep past, other minds, the remote future—not to compounds reporting that something is unknown; such compounds were never the target of the principle. (4) Dev answered that this restriction concedes the real fight. Realists already grant that ordinary substantive claims can be verified in principle; what they deny is that every truth can be. If the principle now means only that every truth except those about our own ignorance is knowable, he said, it can no longer support the original idea that truth cannot outrun evidence—it has been narrowed to exactly the cases the other side never disputed. Please adjudicate the exchange. Answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: the first four items should each rate one of moves (1)–(4) from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating, and the fifth item should give an overall verdict on who came out ahead.
