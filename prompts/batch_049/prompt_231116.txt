I have been taking a seminar on early Chinese philosophy, and a disagreement has emerged in our discussion of the butterfly dream passage in the Zhuangzi. In the episode as it is usually told, Zhuang Zhou dreams he is a butterfly flitting about in a way that feels entirely real; upon waking, he cannot tell whether he is Zhuang Zhou who dreamed of being a butterfly or a butterfly now dreaming it is Zhuang Zhou. Our instructor presented the episode as an early form of dream skepticism, comparable in structure to the worry Descartes raises about distinguishing waking experience from a particularly coherent dream, and she argued that it poses a genuine epistemological challenge. A friend in the seminar objected that this framing imports a concern foreign to the text: the passage closes by invoking the transformation of things, and on her reading it is a meditation on the fluid boundary between one form of life and another, or on the equal validity of different perspectives, not a skeptical argument about evidence. She went so far as to claim that treating the passage as dream skepticism turns a point about transformation into a Western-style puzzle about knowledge and gets the text wrong.

Write an essay on this controversy: is the butterfly dream best understood as an exercise in dream skepticism in something like the Cartesian sense, or is the skeptical reading a distortion of what the passage is doing? Consider what the text actually licenses and what it merely leaves open—for instance, whether the uncertainty staged in the episode is best thought of as uncertainty about evidence, about personal identity, or about the boundaries between kinds, and whether the closing line resolves or dissolves the uncertainty rather than leaving it standing. Weigh both readings carefully and engage with the suspicion that the skeptical reading is anachronistic, but do not end on a noncommittal note: defend a specific verdict about the epistemic and philosophical import of the passage, and justify it.
