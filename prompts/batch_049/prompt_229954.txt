I'm a senior eng lead at a consumer software company, and the same argument keeps coming up in every roadmap review: is this a normal competitive decision or are we sliding into a race to the bottom? The usual suspects: aggressive re-engagement notifications, auto-opting users into data collection, matching a competitor's release cadence because they obviously cut QA, endless engagement metrics everyone privately agrees make the product worse. I've read a little about Moloch and multipolar traps — the idea that seperate actors can each make individually rational moves and the group ends up somewhere no one actually wanted, and no single actor can unilaterally stop without losing. I want that turned into something my team can use, not just something I bring up in debates.

Please write me a decision procedure — ideally a set of questions we ask in order — that my team can run in under an hour on any proposed feature or practice to decide whether it's the kind of Moloch / race-to-the-bottom situation we should treat as a trap. The people using it are engineers and one PM; assume no philosophy background. For each question, say what counts as a good enough answer to keep going, where the procedure stops with "this is a trap here" or "this is fine," and where it has to punt to a human because the question is really about values or market prediction rather than something a checklist can settle. The procedure has to distinguish "we're doing this because its genuinely better for users" from "we're doing this because if we don't someone else will eat our lunch," and it also needs to handle the honest case where those are both true at once. It should end in a one-page go/no-go a product lead can sign off on, and flag privacy-adjacent steps for legal as a separate line.

Then run the procedure on three cases:

1. A competitor shipped a "daily streak" notification that spikes retention, and our PM wants to copy it. Our data says most people pulled back by streaks don't end up more satisfied long-term, but it does lift DAU.
2. Auto-enrolling users into telemetry collection for "product improvement," with the opt-out buried in settings.
3. Matching a competitor's two-week release cadence, which our own postmortems say will mean cutting the QA cycle roughly in half.

For the worked examples, be explicit about which step each case trips or passes, and make sure at least one of them comes out "don't do it" even if it happens to be the one I'd personally want to ship.
