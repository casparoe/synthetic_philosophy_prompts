I don't have an academic background in physics, but I have been thinking about Maxwell's demon on my own for a few years, and I run a small "big ideas" discussion group at a public library. We get 12 to 16 adults, none of them scientists, and we meet once a month for 90 minutes. The group is comfortable with the basic ideas of heat, order, and the second law, but not with equations.

For our next meeting I want a hands-on activity rather than a talk. Please design a game in which some participants play Maxwell's demon and sort fast and slow "molecules" between two chambers so that order appears to rise for free. The rules should quietly require the demon to measure each molecule and keep a record of the measurement, and should treat that record as part of the system rather than as a free scratchpad outside it. The game should then force the group to do something with those records — reset them, clear them, or pay for them — and the accounting should visibly consume whatever advantage they had won. I want participants to watch their score do too well and then collapse once the memory has to be reset, so that the second law shows up in the mechanics of the game rather than in anything I say afterwards.

One move should be deliberately anticipated: the players will want to avoid the cost by never erasing the records and just letting the notebook grow. The game should let them try that and see that they have only pushed the disorder into an ever-growing stack of notes, because the demon is inside the box too.

Please give me rules, materials, a scoring scheme that uses no real money, a timeline for the 90 minutes, and a debrief that maps each moment of the game onto the corresponding physical idea — sorting, measurement, the records, the reset, and the second law. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it. Finally, be honest about which part of the real physics the game cannot capture — I suspect that the pretend erasure is just a bookkeeping gesture, whereas real erasure has a physical cost the game can only hint at — and name any other limitations you see.
