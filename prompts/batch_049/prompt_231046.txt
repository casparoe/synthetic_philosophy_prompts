I've been meaning to write one of those think-piece essays for the usual forum crowd and I'd like some comments on the idea before I sink a weekend into it. The working title is something like 'Conceptual slippage is piecemeal, not global', and the microdomain is what counts as having read a book. I want to argue that there isn't one boundary between 'read' and 'didn't read' that technology blurred; there are at least four seperate norms that move independently. (1) Perceptual contact: your eyes actually ran over most of the sentences. (2) Retention: you can later say roughly what the argument was. (3) Earnestness: you put in real effort rather than outsourcing to an LLM or a summary service. (4) Honest self-ascription: you wouldn't mislead someone by citing the book on a date or a CV. Under print these got so bundled that people treated them as one thing, but audiobooks broke (1) off from (2)–(4), speed-listening broke (2) off from (1) and (3), and an LLM summary breaks (3) while leaving (2) and (4) negotiable. The thesis is that when someone asks 'did you actually read it?', they're usually really asking about one of these norms, typically (2) or (3), and the messy disputes dissolve once you see which one is doing the work. I also want to float a small taxonomy of slippage moves — substitution (audiobook for page), compression (a summary instead of the book), offloading (the LLM reads, you don't), and inflation (recounting a book from reviews) — and claim that these are genuinely different kinds of conceptual drift, not just degrees of contact.

The things I keep going back and forth on: I cant tell whether the four-norm bit is cute but empty (is it just relabelling ordinary polysemy?), and whether the substitution/compression/offloading/inflation taxonomy earns its keep or collapses into 'different degrees of contact'. If you were editing me, what would you tell me to cut, which microdomain would make the cleanest version of the same point (I considered 'what counts as cooking' and 'what counts as having seen a film'), and is there an obvious case where all four norms come apart in a way that kills the neat story? Also: is this actually interesting to people who've read the usual Hofstadter-adjacent stuff about fluid concepts, or does it read as 'words are fuzzy, news at eleven'? Please answer in British English, and be harsh about the structure rather than the prose.
