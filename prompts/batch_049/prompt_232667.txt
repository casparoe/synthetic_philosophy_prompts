I am a journalist who writes about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine. I am preparing a feature on the demandingness worry in ethics—the worry that an impartial concern for others might require more of us than morality really can. Rather than survey that debate in the abstract, I want to reconstruct the standard implicit in the long-running advice column of a moral philosopher who answers readers' letters about how much they are obligated to sacrifice for distant strangers.

In her column, she has consistently refused two simple positions: she has never said that a reader's obligations run all the way up to the point where further giving would cost the reader more than it would benefit others, and she has never said that helping distant strangers is entirely optional. All of her rulings below concern annual donations to a well-run global-health charity whose impact she accepts as substantial, and in none of the cases is a special relationship or prior obligation at issue.

Her past rulings:
- A reader earning $40,000 asked whether giving $4,000 (10 percent of income) was required. She said it was required.
- Another reader earning $40,000 asked whether giving $10,000 (25 percent) was required. She said it was not required—admirable, but optional.
- A reader earning $80,000 asked whether giving $8,000 (10 percent) was required. She said it was required.
- Another reader earning $80,000 asked whether giving $24,000 (30 percent) was required. She said it was not required—admirable, but optional.
- A reader earning $200,000 asked whether giving $20,000 (10 percent) was required. She said it was required.
- Another reader earning $200,000 asked whether giving $50,000 (25 percent) was required. She said it was not required—admirable, but optional.

Now a new letter has arrived: a reader earning $70,000 asks whether giving $20,000 a year (about 29 percent of income) to the same kind of charity is required. I want to predict how the columnist should answer if she is to remain consistent with the pattern of her earlier rulings.

Please structure the response in four sections, in this order:

1. Candidate principles (about 200 words): identify the distinct rules that fit her past rulings.
2. What each principle says about the new letter (about 250 words).
3. Closest earlier case and best-guess answer (about 250 words): identify the closest analog under the reading that best coheres with her record, give your best prediction for the new letter, and say which broader principle each possible answer would commit her to.
4. A further case that would separate the live candidates (about 150 words).
