I am putting together an advanced undergraduate reading group on anthropic reasoning, and I would like a piece of sustained satire to use as a discussion text before the students encounter the formal argument. Please write a parody of an investment consultancy's annual 'global outlook' letter — the glossy, faintly self-important macro-strategy note sent to clients — in which the firm's 'anthropic risk' desk has quietly adopted the doomsday argument as the backbone of its long-run forecasting. The humour should come from technical fidelity rather than loose caricature. The memo must handle the self-sampling assumption correctly; it should treat the choice of reference class as the recurring fault line, with the analysts visibly fiddling with it until they themselves no longer fall in the alarming part of the distribution; it should keep distinct the prior probability of extinction and the likelihood shift supplied by the doomsday argument, as well as the difference between the argument and ordinary induction from humanity's having survived this long; and it should include the running joke that the framework cannot survive being applied to itself — every time an analyst recalls that he too is a randomly sampled observer, the fund's own expected longevity collapses, and the memo drifts toward advising clients to diversify into hypotheses under which there will be vastly more observers. A chart labelled 'doom horizon' with a 95% confidence band resting on an undisclosed prior would fit the register. Keep it between 700 and 900 words, format it as a numbered client memo with an executive summary, and sustain the deadpan throughout: a reader familiar with the literature should find the satire fair and technically literate, while a reader who has never met the doomsday argument should still be able to tell what is being ironised.
