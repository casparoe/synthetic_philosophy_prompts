I teach an upper-level undergraduate course in the philosophy of biology, and I'm putting together a lecture on the Gould–Lewontin spandrels critique. A textbook I'm using summarizes the episode this way:

"Gould and Lewontin's famous critique showed that the adaptationist programme in evolutionary biology was deeply flawed. They demonstrated that many, perhaps most, traits are not adaptations at all but spandrels—byproducts of architectural constraints or of selection for other features—and they ridiculed the habit of inventing adaptive just-so stories for whatever trait happens to exist. Their conclusion is that the null hypothesis for any trait should be that it is a non-adaptive byproduct, with the burden of proof falling on anyone who claims it is an adaptation. Since their paper, the field has largely given up treating every trait as an adaptation, and adaptationist explanations are now expected to be tested against non-adaptive alternatives before they are accepted."

I want to know how much of this I can safely present to students. Please audit the passage claim by claim. For each, say whether a careful philosopher of biology would treat it as accurate, as a simplification that loses something important, or as a misrepresentation. I'm especially unsure about: whether Gould and Lewontin actually claimed to have demonstrated that most traits are non-adaptive; whether the "null hypothesis" formulation gets their position right or conflates two distinct burdens of proof; and whether their target was adaptationism as an empirical thesis or adaptationist practice as a research programme. Also flag where the passage treats as settled what is in fact still contested, and give me a compact statement of what is genuinely agreed among people who work on this versus what remains live disagreement—without presenting a contested reading as the consensus.
