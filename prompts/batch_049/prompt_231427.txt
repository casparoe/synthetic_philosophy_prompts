Please act as an expert philosopher of biology, someone who works on the concept of function and on functional explanation, and take the time to work through the following commitments carefully before you answer. I am a philosopher of biology writing up my considered view, and I want a consistency audit before I present it to colleagues. I find myself committed to five theses. First, I hold an etiological, selected-effects account of function: a trait's function is the effect for which it was selected, and selection history is what distinguishes a genuine function from an accidental effect. Second, I hold that a new trait that has not yet been selected can nonetheless have a function: if a structure produced by a fresh mutation pumps blood in a way that keeps the organism alive, then pumping blood is its function from the first generation. Third, I hold that function attributions explain the presence or maintenance of the trait in a population, and that this explanatory role is part of the point of function talk. Fourth, I hold that whether a part has a function should be determinable from the organism's current organization, without first settling its selection history. Fifth, I reject the causal-role account of function, because it is too permissive: it would treat the heart's incidental production of sounds, or its merely occupying space in the chest, as functions on a par with pumping blood. I would like to keep all five theses, but I suspect they do not all hang together. I am not asking for a survey of the literature; I want an audit of this particular position. Please tell me which commitments are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be, rank the tensions by how severe they are, and say what would have to be revised and what each revision would cost. Write your answer in continuous prose, as in a letter or email, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
