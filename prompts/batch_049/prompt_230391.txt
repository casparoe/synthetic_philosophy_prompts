I am working on a feature about a side of the Viennese philosopher Moritz Schlick that tends to be eclipsed by his role in the Vienna Circle: his interwar essay on the meaning of life. His position, in rough summary, is that the demand that life serve some purpose outside itself cannot be met, because purposes confer meaning only as means, and the chain of purposes has to terminate in something valued for its own sake. Meaning, Schlick argued, is therefore not a goal that life reaches but a quality of activity done for its own sake, which he contrasted with goal-directed work and called play. Schlick was killed in 1936, so he never saw the later psychological literature that, quite separately, developed the construct of flow: a state of absorption in which attention and action merge, the sense of time recedes, and the activity is experienced as worth doing for its own sake. Positive psychology now treats such autotelic states as an important component of a satisfying, meaningful life. I want the feature to make a precise, non-obvious claim about how Schlick's account and the flow literature fit together. Suppose Schlick could have read that research: given his stated views, would he have welcomed it as an empirical vindication of his theory, or would he have insisted on differences the psychologists are likely to blur? Work out, as concretely as possible, what he would accept, what he would reject, and what his notion of play included that the empirical notion of flow may leave out.
