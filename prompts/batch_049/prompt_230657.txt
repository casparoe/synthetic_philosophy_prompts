My aunt is retiring at the end of this semester and I've been asked to give the toast at the dinner. She has spent four decades teaching the Enlightenment, and the figure she keeps coming back to is Émilie du Châtelet—not the physics so much as the short, unsentimental essay about happiness she wrote near the end of her life. My aunt's version of it, which I've heard over enough dinners to have half-memorized it myself, goes roughly like this: clearing away prejudice and fear is only groundwork, and it can't by itself make anyone happy; happiness runs on passions, and most of our pleasures are owed to illusion, so the practical question is not how to rid yourself of false beliefs but how to choose illusions that keep you alive and drop the ones that curdle into bitterness. She also insists on the distinction du Châtelet makes between prejudices, which narrow people and should go, and illusions, which make life bearable and should be kept with care.

The toast needs to be warm and funny, and it can't read like a lecture, because half the room is family who have never heard of du Châtelet and shouldn't need to, and the other half is her department, who know the text cold. It also has to stay inside her philosophy. So no "you've earned a rest," no "retirement is a new beginning," no "the best is yet to come." If there is something genuinely good about retiring, I'd like it said in her own terms—something about having finally bought herself long, unbothered hours for the one passion that doesn't depend on luck or other people—but make it land for everyone. And please just continuous prose, no headings, bullet points, bold, or anything like that; write it the way someone would actually deliver it.
