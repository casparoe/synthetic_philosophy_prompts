Next week I'm leading a forty-minute session of my school's economics club, and the students, who have never taken economics, expect me to tell them whether rent control and a higher minimum wage are good policies. The one idea I want them to leave with is that the standard economic case against price controls is not a claim that the underlying goals are worthless; it is a claim that the policy creates hidden costs and misallocates resources—and that uncovering such hidden costs does not, by itself, settle the moral question. Plan how I should teach exactly that idea rather than the whole textbook chapter: the order in which the pieces must be introduced and what depends on what, the one concrete example that should carry the whole explanation and what it must have and lack, the misconceptions they will predictably bring or form (I am expecting something like "economists oppose price controls because they side with landlords" and "if it distorts the market, it must be wrong") and the precise point in the sequence where each should be headed off, one question I could ask at the end to tell whether they have actually grasped the distinction rather than just learned the diagram, and what I should explicitly refuse to cover at this level.
