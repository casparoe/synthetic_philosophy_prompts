I am working on a short piece about the ethics of voting, and I keep getting stuck on a specific passage in Mill's *Considerations on Representative Government* (1861), in the chapter on the secret ballot. I do not have the text in front of me, so here is a close paraphrase rather than a verbatim quotation. Mill argues that the franchise is not something a voter owns and may use for his own private advantage. It is a public trust. A voter's relation to his vote, Mill says, is like a judge's relation to his verdict: the judge is bound to decide for the public good rather than for his own interest, and the voter is bound in the same way. From this Mill concludes that the secret ballot is, in ordinary elections, worse than open voting. Because the vote is a public trust, it ought to be cast in public, and the voter should be answerable to his fellow citizens for how he uses it.

The difficulty I have is this: how is this consistent with Mill's famous defense of individual liberty, rooted in the claim that each person is sovereign over his own life and the only legitimate ground for interference is the prevention of harm to others? Casting a vote looks, on one natural reading, like a self-regarding act: it is my choice, and it may be my private political expression. Yet Mill denies that. Take a definite position: is there a principled distinction between private self-regarding acts and the exercise of a public trust, or does Mill's account of the vote simply sit in tension with the rest of his liberalism? If you think there is a principled distinction, state it as precisely as possible and explain why voting falls on the public-trust side. If you think there is a genuine tension, explain why the tension is real rather than merely apparent.
