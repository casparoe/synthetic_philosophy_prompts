I've been keeping a file on my colleague Mara's snap verdicts about artifact identity—it's the closest thing our department has to case law. Over the years she's said:

- A wooden sloop, the *Merope*, had every hull plank replaced one at a time during a decade of repairs, but the keel and ribs were never touched. Mara says it's still the same boat.
- A delivery van had its doors, hood, seats, wheels, and engine replaced one at a time over several years; the chassis and frame stayed original. Mara says same van.
- A footbridge had every tread replaced as they rotted, while the two original load-bearing beams underneath stayed put. Mara says same bridge.

Now I've got a new one. The *Kalliopi*, an old fishing boat, has been kept in continuous summer service while its oak frame is being replaced rib by rib over three winters. Its hull planks were already replaced years ago in the same gradual way (with the frame then untouched), and after this third winter it won't contain a single piece of original wood—keel, ribs, or planks. None of the removed material was saved. If Mara wants to stay consistent with her earlier verdicts, should she call the *Kalliopi* the same boat as the original? Which precedent is the closest analog, and if her prior cases underdetermine the answer, what principle would she be adopting either way? Also, what further case should I bring her next to finally settle which principle she's actually using?
