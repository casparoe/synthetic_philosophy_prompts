Before I read any further on Sen's liberal paradox, I want the specification I keep failing to find: a full list of the requirements an adequate theory of social choice in a liberal society has to meet, with the consideration behind each, and a clear marking of which requirements are non-negotiable and which are merely the commitments of one camp. I want the desiderata, not another recapitulation of the theorem.

Set the problem up as follows. The theory must take some reasonable input—at least profiles of individual preference orderings over social states—and produce social judgements that can actually guide a decision. Candidate requirements I have seen defended include: (i) unrestricted domain, or something close to it; (ii) weak Pareto, in the sense that a strict preference shared by everyone must be reflected as a strict social preference, so that if everyone prefers one state to another, society must prefer it too; (iii) a minimal liberty condition, under which at least two individuals each have a protected pair of alternatives over which their own preference is socially decisive, in both directions; and (iv) a coherence condition strong enough to ensure that some alternative is always left standing in every relevant finite set, whether that be transitivity or the weaker demand of acyclicity. Keep those four in view, and add any further requirements you think genuinely belong on the list—non-dictatorship, for instance, while keeping it carefully distinct from minimal liberty.

For each requirement, give the case behind it, and say which are constitutive of the very idea of a liberal society and which are parochial commitments of welfarists, libertarians, or social-choice formalists, and why.

Then do what most discussions skip. State precisely which subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, and construct the preference configuration that proves it: two protected pairs and a Pareto judgement that together close a cycle over three states. Use a fresh example rather than the standard one if you can—a protected sphere need not concern reading a controversial book; it might concern publishing one's own medical data, choosing one's form of religious observance, or deciding what appears under one's own name—but make the cycle explicit.

Finally, briefly characterise the main strategies for dissolving the tension—giving liberty lexical priority, giving Pareto lexical priority, reformulating rights as protections over procedures rather than over final outcomes, or restricting the admissible preference profiles—and say which of your desiderata each strategy gives up. I do not need a bibliography; I need the conceptual map.
