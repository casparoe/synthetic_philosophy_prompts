I am a philosophy professor, and each semester I grade roughly forty undergraduate metaphysics essays in which students move from what they can or cannot conceive to claims about what is possible, impossible, or necessary. I want a reusable grading procedure—a checklist I can run in five to ten minutes per essay—that sorts legitimate appeals to conceivability from merely gesturing at a mental image or failing to find a contradiction. The essays determine a substantial part of the final grade, and grade appeals are common, so the procedure must be defensible. The procedure should make precise distinctions where the literature does (for example, positive versus negative conceivability, and prima facie versus ideal conceivability) without requiring me to teach the full theory before grading. It should identify the step at which a student's argument fails, state what would be needed to repair it, and flag any point where the grade must ultimately rest on a philosophical judgment rather than the checklist. Anchor the procedure in this passage from Hume's A Treatise of Human Nature, Book 1, Part 2, Section 6: "Whatever we conceive, we conceive to be existent. Any idea we please to form is the idea of a being; and the idea of a being is any idea we please to form." Then apply your procedure to three typical essay moves: (1) a student argues that because they can imagine a person surviving bodily death, such survival is possible; (2) a student argues that because they can conceive a golden mountain without contradiction, a golden mountain is possible; (3) a student argues that because they cannot conceive water failing to be H2O, it is impossible. Include at least one worked application where the procedure grades the essay more harshly than my first impression would.
