I've been working through the accuracy-first argument for probabilism, and I keep getting stuck on one step. As I understand it, the argument runs roughly as follows: if your credences—your graded degrees of confidence—violate the probability axioms, then there is some coherent credence function that is strictly more accurate than yours in every possible world, according to a suitable measure of closeness to truth. That much I can follow. What I don't understand is why the conclusion is so often stated only negatively, as the claim that incoherent credences are irrational. If accuracy is the sole epistemic value, and some coherent credence function beats my current credences no matter which world is actual, why wouldn't that dominating function count as the uniquely rational credence function for me to adopt? The usual response seems to be that a dominance argument rules out an option without selecting among the options that dominate it, but I can't see why that response is open to someone who has already granted that accuracy is all that epistemically matters. Is it a mistake to read the dominance result as directly recommending a particular replacement credence function, and if so, what exactly does the result establish?
