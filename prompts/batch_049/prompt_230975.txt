Every time I teach philosophy of medicine, this is the bit where I feel like I'm bluffing. Medical systems will happily pay to move someone from below a line up to the line, but not past it. Beta blockers for a heart patient, or for someone with an actual anxiety diagnosis, are medicine; the same drug for a healthy orchestra musician who just wants steady hands before an audition gets filed under enhancement, insurance won't touch it, and a lot of doctors say it isn't their job. Stimulants for ADHD are covered; for a stressed student grinding through finals they're not. Fine. But the line itself keeps drifting: grief becomes clinical depression if it lingers too long, shyness becomes social anxiety disorder, a slightly low testosterone reading becomes a condition to treat. So what theory of health and disease does this whole arrangement presuppose? Is it quietly committed to the idea that disease is biological malfunction, or is it smuggling in value judgments about what a human life is supposed to look like? Can the treatment/enhancement distinction be made coherent on either view? And what would have to change in how medicine is orgainzed if we ditched whatever theory is doing the line-drawing and said the only thing that matters is whether the intervention helps the patient?
