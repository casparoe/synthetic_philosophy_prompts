I teach a course on rational choice and philosophy of social science, and every spring I do a unit on the paradox of voting: one ballot essentially never flips a mass election, and yet people keep voting, so either the standard expected-utility story is incomplete or millions of people are behaving irrationally. The class discussion is always a mess because students describe themselves with whichever label they remember—expressive voter, civic duty, whatever—and then answer the cases in ways that contradict the label. I'd like a diagnostic I can hand out on day one.

Please make a 'which theory of why people vote do you actually hold?' quiz: eight to ten questions, each built around a concrete case, with two or three answer options. The questions should cover territory like a safe-seat election where your candidate cannot lose, a non-binding referendum, paying a fee to vote, voting in a secret ballot where nobody can ever find out, a case where your preferred candidate's win helps a million strangers but costs you something, and a case where you can spend the same amount of time and effort doing something else you find mildly enjoyable. Before writing the questions, work out the branching space that will generate them: whether the value comes from the outcome or from the act itself; if from the outcome, whose welfare counts and whether people assess their chance of being pivotal accurately; if from the act, whether the benefit is expression, duty-satisfaction, or plain enjoyment. Then place the named views on that tree.

The scoring key should sort people onto at least six leaves: the pure self-interested instrumental view (which says voting in a mass election is usually irrational), the altruistic/ethical instrumental view (expected benefit can swamp the tiny pivotal probability because it counts everyone's interests), expressive voting, the civic-duty or D-term view, the consumption/entertainment view, and a rational overconfidence or misprediction view. You may add one or two more leaves if the logical space supports them, but don't pad just so the list looks longer. No question may use the labels or ask what someone identifies as; every question has to force a choice about a case, so the instrument reveals the view rather than inviting people to pick one. Include at least one pair of positions the quiz cannot cleanly seperate, and say exactly what answer pattern would distinguish them and why you left that question out. Also include one combination of answers that none of the real positions occupy, and say whether it's empty because it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Finish with the scoring key and, for each leaf, one sentence on its most awkward commitment.
