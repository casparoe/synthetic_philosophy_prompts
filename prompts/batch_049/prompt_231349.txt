A friend of mine has been following a city council debate about whether to approve a new brominated flame retardant for furniture, and she brought it up at dinner last week. The council can either let manufacturers switch to the new chemical now, or make them wait two years for a long-term exposure study. In the meantime, manufacturers would keep using the older retardant, which is a known thyroid disruptor. The new one looks less toxic in the short term, but there's lab work suggesting it might accumulate in household dust, and nobody has good evidence either way yet.

Mara said the council should invoke the precautionary principle and wait: you don't roll out a chemical that might bioaccumulate on the basis of no evidence. Dev said that's backwards — the status quo is a chemical we already know is harmful, so "waiting for proof of safety" is really choosing a known harm over an unknown one; you can't prove a negative anyway. Mara said "prove a negative" is a dodge: you can require studies with enough statistical power to catch the plausible harms, and the manufacturer just hasn't run them; the old chemical being bad doesn't make the new one good. Dev said if we held every innovation to that standard we'd have blocked vaccines and pasteurization, so she's only applying caution to whatever feels scary. Mara said that's a false comparison: vaccines go through trials and their benefits are direct and huge, whereas this exposure is involuntary, ends up in everyone's furniture, and once it's in household dust it's hard to reverse. Dev said that's status quo bias: the council isn't choosing between risk and no risk, it's choosing between the known risk of the old retardant and the unknown risk of the new one, so a consistent precautionary principle should compare the two, not aim at "no new risk." Mara said there's still an asymmetry: approving a new chemical imposes a fresh risk on people who had no say, while continuing the old one just maintains an existing situation, and the new chemical's harms, if they appear, would land on everyone, including people who might have avoided it if asked. Dev said "new" isn't morally special — the existing situation also imposes risks on people who had no say, so that asymmetry is just conservatism dressed up as principle.
