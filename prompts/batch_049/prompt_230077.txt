So here's the thing I keep going back and forth on. I'm a journalist who covers cognitive science and design, and I've been sketching an essay built around a small puzzle: the single-storey 'a' in a geometric sans and the double-storey 'a' in a classic serif share almost nothing at the level of parts, yet any literate person sees them as the same letter. My working thesis is that the usual feature-list story of categories can't handle this — not because people don't extract features, but because the features are negotiable almost without limit; a good type designer can distort or delete just about any component and the 'a'-ness survives as long as the rest of the glyph context holds. I want to argue, drawing on Hofstadter's Letter Spirit project, that what makes an 'a' an 'a' is a web of roles — a bowl, a stem, a crossbar, a terminal, that second-storey loop — plus a style that constrains how all those roles get filled at once. On this view the identity lives in the roles and the coherence; its more like a division of labor than a checklist of parts, and no particular shape is load-bearing.

Then I want to turn this on current machine learning. Do vision models and multimodal LLMs actually learn anything like letter-level roles and global style constraints, or do they mostly get by on texture statistics and local correlations? And if it's the latter, what would that imply for the hope that such systems could ever get to something like a style, rather than just classifying glyphs? I like the letterform as a test case because it's small enough to reason about but rich enough to break sloppy theories of concept learning.
