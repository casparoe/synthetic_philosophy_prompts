I'm preparing a handout for an upper-level undergraduate seminar in democratic theory, and I want the centerpiece to be a diagnostic decision tree rather than a prose survey. The topic is the boundary problem: democratic procedures presuppose a "we" who is entitled to decide, so the principle that picks out that "we" cannot itself be settled by a vote without circularity. The tree should take a student from that circularity, through a series of concrete cases, to whichever principle of demos-membership they actually hold. Every branch node should be phrased as a question someone can answer without already knowing the names of the positions, so the instrument sorts students by what they say about cases, not by which label they like.

Please build the tree around the following design requirements.

First, the opening node should test whether the respondent thinks the class entitled to participate is fixed by something standing before the policy (shared territory, shared political culture, or subjection to an existing legal order) or by something the policy itself brings about (its causal footprint on people's interests). Use a concrete case to force the choice for anyone who wants it both ways.

Second, use these cases somewhere in the tree; you may add others if the tree needs them:
- a city's rent-control ordinance, and whether long-distance commuters who work in the city but cannot vote there should help decide it;
- an upwind factory whose emissions cross a national border into a neighboring country;
- a carbon tax whose effect on a low-lying island state is large and whose effect on the taxing country's voters is small;
- permanent residents who are not citizens, and citizens living permanently abroad;
- a site for long-term nuclear waste, chosen now, that binds people not yet born.

Third, structure the tree around a distinction students often run together: standing to participate in the decision (a vote or a voice) versus standing to have one's interests count in the justification of the decision. A view can grant the second without the first, and the tree should let that come out. The nuclear-waste case should force the question whether "having one's interests count" can extend to persons not yet alive without collapsing into proxy representation by the living.

Fourth, the tree must end in named leaves at least for: the all-affected interests principle; the all-subjected (coercion) principle; the territorial-citizenship status quo; the cultural or national demos; and a stakeholder principle keyed to the intensity of the interest affected. Where a combination of answers is coherent but unattractive, label the leaf "unnamed" and say in one sentence why nobody is there. Where a combination of answers is occupied by a real position, connect it to the leaf briefly.

Fifth, flag rather than paper over any pair of leaves the tree cannot cleanly separate. My own suspicion is that the all-affected and all-subjected principles converge on nearly every case in the list and diverge only on a thin set, and the tree should say so rather than pretend the distinction does more work than it does. If that is right, say which case, if added, would split them.

Sixth, keep it to something a student can work through in ten minutes: roughly six to eight substantive branching points, with each node labeled by the claim or question that splits it.

After the instrument itself, append a short "confidence note" addressed to me rather than the students. In it, say how confident you are in the tree's classification decisions overall, and single out the one branch point, case, or diagnosis of an empty combination about which you are least sure.
