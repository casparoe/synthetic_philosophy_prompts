I've been going back and forth with a friend about how moral norms actually die, and I keep getting stuck on the same knot. The honor-story about dueling: it didn't end because somebody finally found the knockdown argument — the anti-dueling points (it's lethal, it's absurd, the offence isn't really answered) had all been around for ages — but because what counted as honourable shifted, and dueling started to read as faintly ridiculous and low-status. Similar stories get told about footbinding and, messier, about slavery. But if the change was actually carried by status, honor, and maybe plain economic interest rather than by the arguments, then isnt our current sense that dueling was obviously irrational just a post hoc rationalization of a change that happened for reasons that weren't themselves moral? The arguments sat there for centuries doing nothing; what moved was where the status was. Still, I dont want to land on "we just have different tastes now" either — that feels wrong. So I want help getting clear on what the live options are for holding these together: how can one say that a moral revolution was genuine moral progress while also granting that it was powered by honor, shame, and interest rather than the force of the better argument? Could you lay out the main positions and what each one has to give up? And I'm especially curious whether the same mechanism worry can be reversed — if honor and shame can push norms in good directions, what principled thing stops us from saying every moral panic is just a moral revolution in progress?
