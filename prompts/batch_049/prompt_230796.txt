Something's been bugging me. There's a style of management training, pretty common in sales orgs and startups, that pushes people to take total responsibility for outcomes that were obviously not fully in their control. A branch manager whose store got hit by a hurricane is still expected to stand up in the Monday meeting and say she owns the missed quarter, that she should of planned better, and that saying "the hurricane made us miss" is just an excuse. The training language tends to be things like "if your team fails, you failed" and "there is always something you could have chosen differently." I'm trying to figure out what theory of freedom and responsibility this practice is actually committed to. Does it only make sense if you accept something like Sartre's radical freedom, where we are always free and any "I couldn't do otherwise" is bad faith? Or is it underdetermined, so a compatibilist or even a hard determinist could run the same training for different reasons? And here's the part that really trips me up: if the practice is secretly Sartrean, would it even be consistent with Sartre's actual view, or does it distort his picture by turning freedom into a corporate performance metric and pushing people to deny their facticity rather than own their freedom? The same company also spends a fortune on forecasting and risk modeling, as if it thinks outcomes are largely outside anyone's control, so I can't tell what it actually presupposes. Reconstruct the implicit theory, be clear about exactly wich parts of the practice need it and wich parts don't, and say what would have to change if a company ran the same program on a determinist or compatibilist picture instead.
