I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I'm working on a book that cuts between the decision to drop the bomb and Oxford a little more than a decade later, when they gave Truman an honorary degree and Elizabeth Anscombe threw a fit over it. What I want from you is a careful case analysis of the bombing through her eyes, not the standard saved-lives debate I've heard a thousand times. The way I read her, the charge is that Truman murdered noncombatants on purpose: the bombs were aimed at cities to break Japan's will to fight, and saying the deaths were justified by the surrender they produced is exactly the ends-justify-the-means move she refuses to accept. She also didnt think the alternatives had really been exhausted, since the demand for unconditional surrender and the refusal to tell the Japanese the emperor would be safe made the bomb look like a demonstration rather than a last resort. So do the full mapping: the players, the options, the payoffs, and what her position says Truman should have done instead. Then tell me where the lens earns its keep — the second bomb three days later, the surrender terms, the sheer speed of it — and where it strains: the fog of war, the real uncertainty about whether Japan would have surrendered before the planned invasion, the Soviet entry, the fact that the planners mostly saw the bomb as just another weapon in an already brutal firebombing campaign. Close by turning it around: what does Hiroshima and Nagasaki do to Anscombe's view? If her absolutism has to lean on a contested counterfactual about what Japan would have done, does that make the position more honest or does it show a crack?
