Ok so this week in my metaphysics group we managed to talk ourselves into the view that every human alive caused my friend Cam's fern to die, which cannot be right. I'll lay out the argument:

(1) Omissions can be causes. I forgot to water Cam's fern while she was away, and my not watering it caused the fern's death.

(2) The but-for test is the test of causation: c caused e just in case, had c not occurred, e would not have occurred.

(3) Take a stranger in another town who has never heard of Cam or the fern. Had this stranger not omitted to water the fern — that is, had he shown up and watered it before it died — the fern would not have died. So by (2), his not watering it passes the but-for test, and so it is a cause of the death.

(4) The same goes for any person who could have watered the fern but didn't. So every such person caused the fern's death. But that's absurd: it makes "the cause" of a houseplant's death do no explanatory or practical work, and it makes the stranger's omission no less a cause of the death than mine.

I'd most like to keep (1): it seems crazy to say the fern died without my failure to water it being a cause. But I don't see how to block (3) while keeping (2).

So: which step (or inference) should be rejected, why did that step look so innocent, and what does each of the main ways out cost? In particular, price these three moves: (a) omissions aren't literally causes, at most they figure in explanations or attributions; (b) but-for is fine but only some omissions count as causes, selected by duties, expectations, or what's normal; (c) but-for needs replacing with something more discriminating — and if so, what might that be, and can it still handle the plain fern case?
