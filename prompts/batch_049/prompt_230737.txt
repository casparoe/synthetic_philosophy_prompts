I've been arguing with a friend about the EU and I keep going in circles, so I want someone to lay out the underlying picture for me. Here's what puzzles me. The member states all describe themselves as sovereign — and in the EU's own legal order they are still the ones who can amend the treaties and leave, as Brexit showed. But at the same time, the EU treats its law as supreme over national law, including national constitutional law, and national courts are expected to set aside their own statutes when there's a conflict. So which picture of sovereignty is the EU actually presupposing? Is it that sovereignty can be pooled or divided, or that the old indivisibility idea is just false, or is the self-description a legal fiction with the real story being something else? And a second layer: people talk about a democratic deficit, since the directly elected parliament shares power with a Commission no voter directly chooses, and the Council can legislate by qualified majority so your own state can be outvoted. Yet the same EU keeps pushing subsidiarity and giving national parliaments more of a say, and the treaties keep gesturing toward ever-closer union. What picture of democracy or democratic legitimacy does that structure commit you to? And are those two pictures — the sovereignty one and the democracy one — compatiable, or does one of them have to give? I'm not asking whether the EU is good or bad. I want to know what it's actually committed to, and what it merely happens to fit with.
