I have been defending the standard Bernoulli resolution of the St. Petersburg paradox in a seminar on decision theory, and I want you to play the serious critic rather than explain the resolution to me again. The position is this: a rational person should price a lottery by its expected utility, not its expected monetary value, and the reason the St. Petersburg game does not warrant an arbitrarily large entry fee is that money has diminishing marginal utility. Each additional unit of wealth matters less to the player than the previous one. Bernoulli makes the point early in his 1738 paper Exposition of a New Theory on the Measurement of Risk, when he asks us to compare what a gain of a thousand ducats means to a poor man and to a rich man, and concludes that the value of a gain cannot be fixed by its amount alone but depends on the condition of the person who receives it. I take that observation to be doing the real work in dissolving the paradox.

What I want from you is a map of what would count against this position. I do not mean the familiar objections considered only in order to be brushed aside. I want to know what would have to be true for Bernoulli's resolution to be false, incomplete, or merely a relabeling of the original problem. In particular, distinguish the failures that would be decisive from those that would merely complicate the story. Is the view at risk from the possibility that some rational agents have unbounded utility functions, from the super-St. Petersburg games whose payoffs outrun any given utility function, from the worry that diminishing marginal utility just changes the question from expected money to expected utility without explaining why an infinite expected utility would itself be unacceptable, or from the observation that the resolution says nothing about what a person with a very small but positive chance of some transcendent payoff should do? Which of these, if any, should actually move me? And has the standard resolution been quietly insulated by shifting between two claims, a descriptive one about how people in fact value gains and a normative one about how they ought to? Tell me honestly whether the position as usually defended risks anything at all, and if it does not, whether that lack of exposure is itself a problem for a claim of this kind.
