Two friends of mine, Priya and Marcus, got stuck in an argument last week about epiphenomenalism and I'm hoping you can settle it better than we managed over dinner. Epiphenomenalism here means the view that conscious experiences (pains, the look of red, the ache of regret) are caused by brain states but cause nothing themselves — no behaviour, no speech, no judgments. The fight was over what one of them called the paradox of phenomenal judgment: whether the view can avoid making our first-person reports about consciousness unjustified.

(1) Priya said: if epiphenomenalism is true, then when I say "I'm in pain right now," the pain isn't what causes the belief or the words; some neural state is. So the truth of my belief isn't the reason I hold it. A belief whose truth plays no role in why it's held is unjustified — and since this generalizes to all introspective reports, the epiphenomenalist has to treat the whole practice of reporting how things seem as a lucky accident. That makes the view self-stultifying.

(2) Marcus replied that she's assuming a belief can only be justified when its truth-maker causes it, and that's to strong. A common cause is enough. The barometer reading and the storm share a cause (the pressure drop), and I can be justified in believing a storm is coming from the barometer even though the storm doesn't cause my belief. Likewise, the same neural state that causes the pain also causes my judgment that I'm in pain — so the judgment is still reliable evidence for the pain.

(3) Priya said that only shows the judgment tracks some physical state, not the experience itself. For the judgment to be about the experience, rather than about whatever neural event it covaries with, the experience has to get a grip on the concept somehow. If the felt quality is causally inert all the way down, there's no link between the experience and the content of my thought; the belief ends up being about its physical cause whether I like it or not. So the common-cause story doesn't save the reports, it changes the subject.

(4) Marcus answered that a concept can itself be a physical representation, and its being about the experience can consist in information: the neural state that realizes my concept "pain" is the same state type that reliably accompanies pain, since both arise from one underlying brain event. That covariance can fix content. And, he said, her demand that the experience itself cause the belief is just a causal theory of justification that reliabilists reject; a reliably produced belief is justified, even when the reliability runs through a common cause.

They went around in circles after that until it petered out.

Then give a table with a row for each of the four moves: rate it from flat-out wrong to basically right, with a one-line justification for each. Then, in a short paragraph, say the strongest point neither of them made. Hard cap: 450 words.
