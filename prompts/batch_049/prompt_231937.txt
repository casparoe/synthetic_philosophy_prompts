My lab has been arguing about Gödel on and off for weeks, and one of the grad students has an argument I can't get out of my head. It feels like it proves too much, but every individual step sounds right. He cleaned it up like this:

(1) Suppose someone writes down a finite, consistent safety specification for an AI — a set of rules plus a checking procedure that is supposed to tell us, for any proposed action, whether the rules allow it.
(2) Any serious safety specification has to be able to represent at least basic arithmetic, since it will need to reason about counts, probabilities, payoffs, number of people affected, and so on.
(3) Gödel's first incompleteness theorem says that any consistent, effectively specified formal system that can represent basic arithmetic is incomplete: there is a sentence in its language that the system can neither prove nor refute.
(4) So for any safety specification, there is a true sentence in its own language that the specification cannot prove.
(5) But a specification that can't prove some true sentence in its own language isn't a complete guide to action: when the AI or the checker hits that sentence, the rules just go silent.
(6) In fact, every finite safety specification leaves at least some questions of the form "is this action permitted?" without a verdict.
(7) A single permitted-or-not question without a verdict, at the wrong moment, is enough for a serious accident.
(8) So any finite safety specification is guaranteed to fail in principle, and the whole project of making AI safe by writing down rules is hopeless.

I want to keep believing that writing down a finite set of safety rules isn't automatically hopeless, but I don't know which step to give up or what it would cost. The part I can't get past is that the same argument seems to apply to legal codes, tax codes, and pretty much any written set of norms — so if it's sound, either that's fine because "complete" was never the goal, or the argument is really showing something much bigger than AI safety. Where does this go wrong, why did the bad step look fine, and what am I commited to if I reject it?

One more thing: when you answer, keep clear the line between what's actually settled about the incompleteness theorems and what's genuinely disputed among people who work on this. The Lucas–Penrose-style conclusions especially: don't present a contested reading as the consensus.
