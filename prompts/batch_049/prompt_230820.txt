I have just taken on a yearlong role as the philosopher attached to a consciousness science laboratory, and I need to convert a theory I have long defended in the seminar room into something a working lab can actually run. The laboratory's data are overwhelmingly first-person reports: subjects describing what they saw during masked and binocular-rivalry displays, neurological patients being asked whether anything was present during forced-choice tasks, and dreamers recounting their experience immediately after being woken in the scanner. In principle the lab has signed on to a heterophenomenological approach, understood as follows. A subject's report is a piece of raw data to be explained, neither an incorrigible description of a private realm nor noise to be discarded; the investigator reconstructs from the reports the apparent world the subject seems to be committed to, and then asks what the subject's brain, history, and situation must be like for the subject to produce just those reports, without granting the subject any special authority about what causes the reports.

The director wants a reusable decision procedure rather than a position paper. The people who will use it are research assistants and postdocs with no philosophical training; they must decide within about half an hour per subject whether a report can be entered into the main analysis, how the condition should be coded, and what must be flagged for the principal investigator. These rulings determine which trials are classified as conscious or unconscious, which subjects are excluded, and what the laboratory ultimately claims in its papers, and the director must sign off on any coding that leans on a subject's report to settle a question about consciousness. Please give me a step-by-step procedure: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, where it hands the decision to a person because the issue is one of values or interpretation rather than technique, and what the procedure will not settle no matter how faithfully it is followed.

Then run the procedure on three cases currently on our desk: a blindsight patient who performs at 81 percent correct on a two-alternative forced-choice motion task while insisting he saw nothing and was guessing; a normal observer in a backward-masking study whose post-trial report contradicts the real-time visibility rating she gave during the trial, and who cannot say which of the two responses reflects what she experienced; and an apparently confabulating subject who offers a detailed, coherent description of an image shown during a screen interval that was in fact blank. For each, say what the procedure licenses us to write in the paper, and be explicit about at least one result the laboratory is likely to find unwelcome.
