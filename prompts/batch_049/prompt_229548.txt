I am currently taking an upper-division course on environmental history and the problem of non-human agency, and I need help diagnosing an argument I constructed for a seminar. We have been reading Hippocrates’ *Airs, Waters, Places* as an early example of climatic explanation of human character and institutions. In Part 23, Hippocrates writes:

“For this reason, I think, Europeans are also more courageous than Asiatics. For uniformity engenders slackness, while variation fosters endurance in both body and soul ; rest and slackness are food for cowardice, endurance and exertion for bravery. Wherefore Europeans are more warlike, and also because of their institutions, not being under kings as are Asiatics.”

From this passage I built the following argument, and now it seems to prove far too much:

1. Hippocrates claims that climate directly shapes courage: equable seasons produce slackness and cowardice, while violent seasonal variation produces endurance and bravery.

2. He adds that political institutions also shape courage: subjects of kings become cowards, while free peoples become warlike.

3. But on his own account, institutions are themselves explained by climate. A people made timid by a uniform climate will be unable to throw off or resist despotic rule, while a people made bold by a variable climate will maintain independence and risk-taking on their own behalf.

4. Therefore institutions are not an independent cause of collective character; they are a downstream mechanism through which climate does its causal work.

5. If the same reasoning generalizes, then all large-scale historical phenomena—political regimes, economic systems, religious movements—are ultimately products of climate and other non-human environmental forces.

6. But that conclusion is unacceptable, because it implies that human deliberation, choice, and responsibility are epiphenomenal in historical explanation, and that the reasons people give for their actions are never the real causes of what they do.

7. So Hippocrates’ reasoning, if taken seriously, appears to undermine its own attempt to leave room for institutions and human freedom.

I find the conclusion in (6) impossible to live with, but I cannot see which earlier step is the weak one, or whether the slide from (2) to (3) to (4) is valid. I would most like to keep (1) as a plausible reading of Hippocrates’ text and reject (6), but I do not want to beg the question.

Please act as a professional philosopher of history with a strong background in ancient Greek medical thought and environmental determinism. Think carefully about the argument before answering—perhaps brainstorm several possible diagnoses—then tell me where the argument goes wrong, or, if it is valid, which premise is least costly to give up. Be explicit about what rejecting each major premise would cost, and whether there is any stable middle position that preserves a real causal role for climate without making human reasons merely epiphenomenal.
