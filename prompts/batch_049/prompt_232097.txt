I'm teaching a small undergraduate ethics seminar on ideals of character, and in a few weeks we'll reach the question whether a person who is as morally good as possible — someone whose every choice and attachment is subordinated to moral requirements — is living a life we should admire or want. I'd like a handout I can distribute before the readings so the students encounter the puzzle nose-first rather than through an argument summary.

Please write a deadpan parody of an application packet for the fictional Institute for Moral Optimization, which certifies ordinary people as bona fide Moral Saints. Use the bureaucratic, slightly menacing tone of a real admissions document: biographical fields, a mandatory inventory of the applicant's hobbies, friendships, and family attachments with a written justification for each, a self-reported log of non-moral activity for the previous quarter, character references, and a plan for reallocating whatever time, money, and attention the applicant currently spends on art, humor, idleness, and particularistic love. The target is the genuine position that maximal moral goodness is the correct organizing ideal of a whole life, and the jokes should come from actually understanding it: the recurring worry that a perfectly moral person would have to let wit, taste, play, and deep partial attachments atrophy; the contrast between the saint who does everything spontaneously out of love and the saint who acts grimly from duty; and the monstrousness that creeps in when an institution starts auditing the moral return on every hour of leisure. Stay deadpan — no winking — and let the form do the editorializing, but keep it legible enough that a struggling undergraduate could still see what is being skewered.

Aim for roughly 700 words. It should be funny to someone who knows the philosophical literature on moral sainthood and readable to someone who has never heard of it. At the very end, in a separate author's note of no more than a paragraph, say how confident you are that the parody tracks the actual structure of the debate rather than a strawman, and identify the single point you are least sure of.
