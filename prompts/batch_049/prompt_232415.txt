I teach a final-year undergraduate module on moral responsibility and the reactive attitudes, and I am setting this year's take-home examination. Please draft a single essay question on standing to blame and hypocrisy, together with a model answer and a marking scheme. The question should be answerable in 90 minutes by a strong student, and it should force the candidate to do more than rehearse the familiar slogans that a hypocrite's criticism can still be true, or that hypocrisy 'removes standing'. In particular, the question should:

- provide a concrete case in which two people criticise the same wrongdoing but differ in their own history of similar wrongdoing, so the candidate must say what standing is, and what it is not (not the truth of the judgement, not the offender's desert);
- require the candidate to distinguish at least two accounts of why hypocrisy is meant to undermine standing, such as one that locates the problem in the sincerity or commitment that blame expresses, and one that locates it in unfairness, reciprocity, or a claimed exemption from a shared norm;
- challenge the candidate to test those accounts against a harder case, for example a blamer who avows the norm but violates it through weakness of will, or a blamer who has since reformed and made amends; and
- ask whether the force of the objection 'look who's talking' is best explained by one of these accounts alone, or by both together.

Please set the case, the question and the constraints yourself, but do not make the question depend on any named reading from the literature; it should be answerable from the concepts alone.

Then provide:

1. the examination question, written out in full;
2. a model answer of roughly 900–1,100 words;
3. a marking scheme with five must-hit moves, the marks for each (total 25), and a one-sentence note on how candidates typically go wrong in each.

In the model answer, after the main argument, include a paragraph headed 'What would change this answer', stating which alternative account of standing, if true, would most weaken the model answer's central claim, and where the model answer's reasoning is most likely to fail.
