I'm teaching one seventy-five-minute session on teleosemantics to upper-level undergraduates in a philosophy of mind course, with only a whiteboard and no slides. They've done functionalism and multiple realizability, so they're comfortable with the idea that a mental state is typed by its role, but they've never seen the problem of mental representation or the project of naturalizing content. Don't teach me teleosemantics; plan how I should teach it.

The one idea the session should make land is how etiological function — roughly, what a mechanism was selected to do by its history — lets a teleosemanticist solve the disjunction problem for representational content (the problem that a causal or informational account seems to leave a token with disjunctive content, horse-or-cow-on-a-dark-night-or-glimpse-of-a-horse-shaped-rock, whenever multiple causes can set it off), and why that makes "content is what it's for" more than a slogan. I want the plan built so that by the end the students can see both the work the notion of function is doing and the price being paid for it.

Please give me: (1) the order in which the pieces must be introduced and what depends on what, including the preliminary distinction or setup that has to be in place before misrepresentation can be stated as a real problem rather than a verbal one; (2) the single carrying example the whole session should build on, what features it must have and what features it must lack, and why not the standard candidates such as the magnetotactic bacterium that orients along geomagnetic field lines or the honeybee waggle dance, if you would avoid them; (3) the misconceptions the students are likely to bring or form, in the order they are likely to appear — I suspect at least "proper function means what the trait usually does" and "normal conditions are statistically average conditions" — with, for each, the exact point in the sequence where it should be headed off and how; (4) one question to ask at the end whose answer would show whether they've grasped the function-based solution to the disjunction problem rather than just memorized it; and (5) what I should explicitly refuse to settle or cover at this level, and how to say so. End with a single sentence stating the spine of the whole sequence.
