My best friend is getting married Saturday and I'm the best man. For fifteen years we've had exactly one recurring argument, and its about the ethics of belief. He's the Cliffordian: he treats it as a moral failure to hold any belief on less evidence than you'd demand from a paper in a top journal, full stop. I'm the Jamesian: I keep insisting that some choices are live, forced, and momentous, and that once you've done the evidence-gathering honestly, it isn't irrational to let the rest of you vote too. His now-wife has heard us replay this fight at a hundred dinners and has asked me, only half-joking, to write a toast that does not reopen it at her wedding and that doesn't make a room of non-philosophers sit through epistemology. The room will include his dissertation committee, her Catholic grandparents, a dozen old friends, and a five-year-old niece, so the speech has to land for all of them at once.

Write the toast. It should run four or five minutes, feel warm, be about the two of them specifically, and get as close as it honestly can to the kind of sentence he would call an unsupported beautiful claim without ever stepping over the line into one. The epistemologists in the room should get the joke—that the whole toast is being built under his constraint—without the argument being re-fought; everyone else should just hear a best man who loves his friend. Start with the toast itself, with no preface or commentary.
