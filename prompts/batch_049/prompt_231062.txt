I have been reading John Winthrop's 1630 sermon A Model of Christian Charity on my own, because I keep seeing people use it to claim that the Massachusetts Bay settlers believed they were founding a special community, one that the rest of the world would watch and judge. Most of that discussion skips to the famous image near the end of the sermon, so I read the whole thing and got stuck near the beginning. Here is the passage:

"GOD ALMIGHTY in his most holy and wise providence, hath soe disposed of the condition of mankind, as in all times some must be rich, some poore, some high and eminent in power and dignitie; others mean and in submission.

The Reason hereof.

1 Reas. First to hold conformity with the rest of his world, being delighted to show forth the glory of his wisdom in the variety and difference of the creatures, and the glory of his power in ordering all these differences for the preservation and good of the whole; and the glory of his greatness, that as it is the glory of princes to have many officers, soe this great king will haue many stewards, Counting himself more honoured in dispensing his gifts to man by man, than if he did it by his owne immediate hands."

I am trying to understand what this passage is actually doing. On its face it says that God deliberately arranged human inequality, and that this arrangement of rich and poor, high and low serves a larger good. But later in the sermon Winthrop seems to demand something close to the opposite: the settlers are told to treat one another with a love and mercy so complete that the community becomes like a single body, and its survival seems to depend on everyone's obligations rather than on rank. I cannot tell whether this opening is just standard Puritan teaching that does not matter much for the argument, or whether it is doing real work in setting up what follows. What exactly is Winthrop saying about why inequality exists, how do the reasons he gives differ from one another, and what does the passage commit him to about the kind of society the settlers were supposed to build? Does it support the popular reading of the sermon as an early statement of American exceptionalism, or does it complicate that reading? I am less interested in the historical context than in what the passage itself commits Winthrop to.
