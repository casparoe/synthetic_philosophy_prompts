I just spent a long evening arguing with a friend about what death consists in and when it becomes permissible to take someone's organs, and I came away with a position I would like checked for internal consistency. Here is where I have landed.

(1) Death is a biological fact about an organism, not a social convention or legal fiction.

(2) The correct criterion of death is the irreversible loss of the capacity for consciousness, rather than the integrated functioning of the organism as a whole.

(3) A patient who meets the diagnostic criteria for whole-brain death is unambiguously dead.

(4) All the same, it would be wrong to treat a patient in a permanent vegetative state as dead for purposes such as burial or organ removal; such a patient breathes, has sleep–wake cycles, and seems manifestly alive.

(5) I approve of controlled donation after circulatory death, in which death is declared a few minutes after the heart has stopped, and I do not think this practice violates the dead donor rule, understood as the rule that vital organs may be taken only from a patient who is already dead and that the taking must not itself cause death.

(6) I suspect that irreversible in the definition of death should be read as a practical rather than a metaphysical notion: a function's loss counts as irreversible if it will not be restored because no attempt to restore it will be made, even if restoration is possible in principle.

I realize some of these commitments pull in different directions, but I am not sure which conflicts are genuine and which merely look like conflicts. Audit this set for me. Which commitments genuinely pull against one another, which only appear to, and what is the least costly revision in each case? Rank the tensions by severity and say what each fix would cost. Please write the answer in American English.
