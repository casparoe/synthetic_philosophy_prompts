I am trying to systematize my convictions about how a group's ranking of outcomes should be built up from its members' preferences, and my commitments in a particular case force a contradiction. I would like to know which commitments to revise and whether a single principle—or a small set of principles—captures the ones I ought to keep.

Consider two adult siblings, Priya and Dev, and two sealed letters left by their late father, one addressed to each sibling. Each may open their own letter or leave it unopened. The possible outcomes are:

- N: neither opens their letter.
- P: only Priya opens her letter.
- D: only Dev opens his letter.
- B: both open their letters.

Let "X > Y" mean that the relevant person ranks X above Y. Priya's ordering is N > P > D > B: she thinks the letters would bring only pain, would most prefer that neither open them, but if someone is to be exposed to their contents she would rather it be her than Dev, and she regards both being exposed as worst. Dev's ordering is P > B > D > N: he is convinced that Priya especially needs to know what their father wrote to her, would most prefer that Priya open her letter, would rather see both letters opened than open only his own, and regards opening neither as the worst outcome.

My verdicts on specific comparisons are these:

(1) Between N and P, I think the group should rank N above P. The outcomes differ only in whether Priya opens her own letter; Dev's situation is the same in both. Dev does prefer P to N, but because this comparison changes nothing but Priya's own act, I take her preference on it to be controlling.

(2) Between D and N, I think the group should rank D above N. The outcomes differ only in whether Dev opens his own letter; Priya prefers N to D, but because this comparison changes nothing but Dev's own act, I take his preference on it to be controlling.

(3) Between P and D, I think the group should rank P above D, since both siblings prefer P to D. When every member of a group prefers one outcome to another, the group's ranking should follow that unanimity.

I also hold that the group's "better than" relation should be transitive: if the group ranks X above Y and Y above Z, it must rank X above Z.

Taken together, (1) and (3) give N > P and P > D, so transitivity yields N > D; combining this with (2)'s D > N yields a cycle, and transitivity would then force N > N, which is incoherent. Is there one principle—or a small set of principles—about how individual preferences determine group rankings that captures the pairwise verdicts I find most compelling? If not all of them can be accommodated, which of my commitments should I give up, and what general account does the surviving set add up to? Please state the principle or principles precisely, indicate which of my original verdicts each clause is meant to capture, and be explicit about which of my verdicts the resulting view cannot accommodate. Finally, apply your reconstructed view to a pair I have not listed: B versus N, where Priya prefers N and Dev prefers B, so unanimity is silent.
