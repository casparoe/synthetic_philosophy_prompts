I'm writing a paper on Molyneux's problem, grounded in this passage from Locke's An Essay Concerning Human Understanding (Book II, Chapter IX, Section 8), where Locke publishes the problem as Molyneux sent it to him:

"Suppose a Man born blind, and now adult, and taught by his touch to distinguish between a Cube, and a Sphere of the same metal, and nighly of the same bigness, so as to tell, when he felt one and t'other; which is the Cube, which the Sphere. Suppose then the Cube and Sphere placed on a Table, and the Blind Man to be made to see. Quaere, Whether by his sight, before he touch'd them, he could now distinguish, and tell, which is the Globe, which the Cube."

Rather than simply defend a yes or no answer to that question, I want to extract the principle behind my own verdicts on a range of variants, and then test that principle against the passage and against one further case I cannot settle. Here are my verdicts, going by my gut:

1. The standard case in the passage (cube vs. sphere, same metal, same size, sight restored, asked before touching): No, he cannot identify them.
2. Flat shapes: A man born blind can distinguish a flat square from a flat circle by touch, same material, same thickness; after his vision is restored, both are laid flat before him. I lean yes, he can tell which is the circle and which is the square.
3. Warmth: He can feel that one ball is warm and another is cold, same size and material; after his sight is restored, he is asked to pick the warm one. No.
4. Weight: He can feel that one ball is much heavier than another, same size and appearance; after his sight is restored, he is asked to pick the heavy one. No.
5. Spikes: He can feel that one ball is smooth and another is covered with sharp spikes; after his sight is restored, he is asked to pick the spiked one. Yes, I think he can.
6. Co-occurrence: After his vision is restored, he is allowed to watch his own hand trace the edges of a cube while feeling it, and then he is asked to point out the cube among two seen objects. Yes, almost immediately.
7. The hint: He is told, after his vision is restored, that the two objects before him are the same two he touched as cube and sphere, but he is not allowed to touch them; otherwise the standard problem proceeds as in the passage. I still say No.

I can already see a tension: cases 2 and 5 say yes, while case 1 says no, even though all three involve shape-like information acquired through touch. And case 6 says yes even though cases 1 and 7 say no.

Is there a single principle that gets all seven verdicts, or close? What does that principle say about the case that gives me the most trouble: a newly sighted person who previously knew by touch a sphere and a cube is shown them outdoors, where the cube casts a sharp, angular shadow and the sphere casts a soft, graduated one; could he use the shading to identify the cube? I genuinely go back and forth on that one.
