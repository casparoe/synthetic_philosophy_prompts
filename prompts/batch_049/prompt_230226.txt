I've recently been pressured into running a reading group on whether international criminal courts can ever be legitimate, and over the holidays my brother-in-law kept shutting every conversation down with "Nuremberg and Tokyo were just victor's justice, so international law is basically theater." I can't pin down what the phrase is actually doing for him, because it seems to smuggle several different charges together: (1) the winners staffed the courts and wrote the rules, which is true and maybe not even a criticism by itself; (2) at least some of the charges, especially the crimes against peace count, criminalized things after the fact; (3) nobody on the winning side had to answer for Dresden or Hiroshima or the firebombing of Tokyo, so the real complaint is selectivity; (4) even the court's answers to the "no crime without law" objection were just the victors' own judges ruling for their own side. Those aren't obviously the same accusation once you start pulling on them. Can you seperate the distinct precise theses "victors' justice" can express, mark each one as trivially true, factually shaky, defensible-but-irrelevant, or a genuine problem for treating the trials as law, and say which reading he needs for his conclusion that international criminal law is all theater? I'd also like to know which readings would let someone hold that Nuremberg was defensible but Tokyo wasn't, and whether the slogan overreaches when it treats a complaint about how a tribunal came to exist as if it settled the legitimacy of every single verdict it produced.
