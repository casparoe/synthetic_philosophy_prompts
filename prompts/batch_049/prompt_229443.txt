I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background. I'm designing the "belief" layer of a reasoning system: a component that takes a probabilistic evidence base and outputs the claims the system may treat as settled for downstream planning. Before I commit to an implementation, I want an engineering-style requirements specification for what an adequate theory of rational acceptance has to satisfy. I am not asking for a survey of the literature or citations; the analysis should stand on its own.

The two failure modes that worry me are the lottery paradox and the preface paradox, as I understand them.

Lottery. A fair lottery has one million tickets and exactly one winner. For each ticket i, the evidence makes it 0.999999 likely that ticket i loses. A fixed-threshold rule — accept a claim iff its probability is at least 0.999 — therefore accepts "ticket i loses" for every i. If the system is allowed to conjoin claims it has accepted, it ends up accepting "every ticket loses," which contradicts its certain background knowledge that one ticket wins. So at least one of the threshold rule, closure under conjunction, and the requirement that an accepted body of claims be consistent must give. I want a principled account of which gives and why, not an ad hoc patch.

Preface. An author writes a long, carefully checked nonfiction book. For each sentence, her checking is good enough that she rationally accepts that sentence. But she also has strong inductive grounds, from experience with long books and human fallibility, for accepting that at least one sentence in the book is false — and she says so in the preface. Her total set of accepted claims is inconsistent, and she can see that, yet she seems rational rather than confused. An adequate account must explain this: whether consistency is not a genuine requirement, whether she never really accepts the conjunctions, whether the preface claim is a different kind of acceptance, or something else.

Please write the desiderata: the full list of requirements that a theory of rational acceptance (or of whatever the correct primitive turns out to be — graded credence alone, acceptance plus credence, context-dependent standards, etc.) must meet in order to handle these cases and be adequate for a truth-seeking reasoner. Treat the lottery and preface cases as non-optional test cases, but derive general requirements from them rather than just those two cases.

Keep the scorecard brief; the requirements and their motivating cases are the deliverable.
