I run an interdisciplinary working group on AI alignment and two members have been arguing for months about how a superintelligent system should talk to humans, assuming the alignment problem is mostly solved and the thing is vastly smarter and more persuasive than we are. One side—call it the benevolent-persuasion camp—says that once you've actually aligned it, you should let it communicate as persuasively as it honestly can, because the whole point is to move people away from bias, akrasia, and misinformation and toward what they'd reflectively want. The other side—call it the autonomy camp—says that a system that can model and push every button in our psychology shouldn't be using that leverage on us even for our own good, because then the decision isn't really ours: being talked into something by a mind that outclasses you so completely isn't persuasion, it's programming a human.

I want you to write the document this adversarial collaboration should have produced. Begin with a numbered list of claims both camps can sign: about the difference between informing and manipulating, about why human persuasion isn't a clean analogy, about what honesty rules can and can't fix, about what each side is not claiming. Then do a disagreements table. The rows should cover at least: the definition of manipulation at that scale; whether there's a post-alignment role for deliberately not being maximally persuasive; whether a person can genuinely consent to being persuaded by a system that much smarter than them; whether autonomy is a side-constraint or just one value to be traded; and whether the worry survives if the AI only ever tells the truth. For each row give the exact wording each side would accept for its own position. Then, for the cruxes, give a concrete scenario or argument both sides agree would bear on it, and record what each side predicts about it. Don't render a verdict; if part of the dispute looks merely verbal, say which part and why. No citations or bibliographies—I'll handle that side myself.
