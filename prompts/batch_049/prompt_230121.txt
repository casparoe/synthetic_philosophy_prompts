I am preparing a two-hour seminar for strong first-year undergraduates in a philosophy and economics programme. They can compute equilibria in simple games but have never seen epistemic game theory or formal notions of knowledge. The seminar's target idea is common knowledge of rationality: that rationality is not merely true of each player, nor merely true that each knows the others are rational, but something with a recursive, fixed-point character that changes what players may infer. Give me a teaching plan for the session, not the explanation itself.

The plan should specify: the conceptual dependencies and the order in which the components must be introduced, with an explicit account of why the notion of common knowledge must be in place before students can entertain the difference between "everyone is rational" and "rationality is common knowledge"; the one worked example or puzzle that will carry the idea, and what that example must have and must lack in order to make the gap between finite mutual knowledge and common knowledge vivid; the misconceptions students predictably bring or form—for example, treating "everyone knows that everyone knows..." as the same as a suitably long finite list, or thinking that a public announcement which tells everyone something they already individually knew cannot change what anyone knows—and the point in the sequence at which each misconception should be headed off rather than allowed to harden; a closing check that would show the idea has landed rather than that students can repeat a definition; and what should be deliberately left out at this level and said so. Anchor the plan in at least one concrete game-theoretic payoff, so that students see why common knowledge of rationality matters for what players can infer about one another's actions, not merely as an abstract curiosity.
