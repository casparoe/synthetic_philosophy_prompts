In the years after German reunification, courts in the unified Federal Republic convicted East German border guards of homicide for shooting people who tried to cross the Berlin Wall. The guards had been following written directives of the German Democratic Republic that were in force at the time and that authorized, even mandated, the use of lethal force against fugitives. In rejecting their defense, the appellate courts reasoned that a state directive which violates fundamental principles of justice in an intolerable way never had the status of law, even when the directive satisfied the state's own criteria of legal validity at the time. I want to know what this practice presupposes about the relationship between law and morality. Does convicting the guards strictly presuppose a natural-law thesis — that a sufficiently unjust directive is not genuinely law and therefore confers no legal permission or obligation — or is the practice fully compatible with legal positivism, which separates a rule's status as law from its moral merit? If a positivist can accept the result, what exactly must the positivist say about the earlier directive and about the basis of the later prosecution? Please distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say whether there is a way to describe the courts' reasoning that leaves the positivist/natural-law dispute unresolved.
