I run an undergraduate reading group that pairs a novel with a focused question in moral philosophy, and next month we are doing Mark Twain's Adventures of Huckleberry Finn. I would like you to read the novel through the lens of moral worth and acting for the right reasons. The basic Kantian position is that an action possesses moral worth only when it is done from the motive of duty: the agent must perform the right action because it is right, rather than merely act in accordance with what rightness happens to require. But there is a puzzle, often discussed under the label inverse akrasia: what are we to make of someone who does the right thing while sincerely believing, by their own lights, that what they do is wrong? Huck looks like a case of precisely this. He has absorbed the morality of the slaveholding society around him, and he repeatedly tells himself that helping the runaway Jim is theft and sin and that his obligation is to turn Jim in; at the decisive moments, he helps Jim anyway, at what he himself takes to be the cost of his own salvation. I want a reading that takes both the novel and the moral-psychology debate seriously. Where, if anywhere, should we locate moral worth in Huck's choices — in his sympathy, in an unarticulated perception of Jim's humanity, in his reasoning, in something else — and does any standard theory of acting for the right reasons survive contact with his case? Which scenes would you have the group work through closely, and where does the novel resist a clean philosophical verdict, for instance in the way it makes Huck's motives layered or in the way it distances itself from the moral language Huck has inherited? End by saying what the novel contributes to the philosophical debate about moral worth that a paper in moral psychology alone does not.
