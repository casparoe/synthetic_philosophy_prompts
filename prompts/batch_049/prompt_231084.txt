I teach a graduate seminar on political obligation and civil disobedience, and I am preparing a session on whether the ambition to give a general theory of when one must obey the law is methodologically sound. I want to assign a passage that raises a prior question about what a theory of political obligation could even look like, from David Hume's essay "Of Civil Liberty" in his Essays, Moral, Political, and Literary. The passage is this:

Those who employ their pens on political subjects, free from party-rage, and party-prejudices, cultivate a science, which, of all others, contributes most to public utility, and even to the private satisfaction of those who addict themselves to the study of it. I am apt, however, to entertain a suspicion, that the world is still too young to fix many general truths in politics, which will remain true to the latest posterity.

I want help explaining to my students why this passage matters for contemporary debates about civil disobedience, and I find that I cannot quite articulate the point as sharply as I would like. Is Hume making a claim about the historical variability of political life, such that principles like "there is a defeasible general duty to obey the law" or "civil disobedience is justified only when it is public, nonviolent, and accepts punishment" are premature if they are offered as stable general truths? Or is he making a more limited claim about the empirical study of politics being young, and therefore not yet in a position to supply reliable generalizations to which a normative theory could appeal? I would especially like help with the word "fix" in the phrase "to fix many general truths in politics," and with the question of whether his skepticism should make us lower our ambitions when we theorize about the obligations of citizens under unjust regimes.
