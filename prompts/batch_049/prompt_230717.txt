So I'm a novelist, and next Thursday I'm leading a one-session reading group at the library on why people go out of their way to read fiction that makes them sad. The room will be maybe fifteen heavy fiction readers, no philosophy background. I have about seventy-five minutes, and I've made this mistake before: I jump straight to the catharsis idea and then everyone nods and the whole thing dies. I dont want you to just explain the topic to me — I want a plan for how I should teach it.

What has to come first, and what has to come later because it depends on the earlier bit? I have a hunch the group has to actually feel the puzzle as a puzzle before any theory gets aired, because otherwise whatever explanation they came in with just reabsorbs everything. What should the one carrying example be, and what does it need to have and not have? I'd rather not use a child dying or a beloved character's death, because that drags in real grief and moral horror before we've even got the question on the table — is that the right call, or am I overthinking it? Which misconceptions will they bring — my guesses are 'it's okay because it isn't real', 'it's a purge or release', and 'we're actually just admiring the prose' — and exactly where in the sequence should I head each one off rather than waiting til the end? I also keep tripping over the fact that the feeling during the book and the feeling you have about having read it are different things, and I don't know where in the order to make that split explicit. What's the one question I can ask at the very end that will tell me wether they got the idea itself and not just the vocabulary? And what should I deliberately leave out for this crowd, and say so?

After the actual plan, give me a brief JSON summary I can print on a notecard. Use these fields exactly:

- "sequence": an array of objects, each with "step" (number and short name), "idea" (the move or claim being introduced), "why_here" (what it depends on and why it can't come earlier), "example_or_prompt" (what I say or ask at that point), and "misconception_to_head_off" (which wrong idea this step is aimed at, if any).
