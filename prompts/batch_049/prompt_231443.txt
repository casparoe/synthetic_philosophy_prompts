I run a two-hour session on statistical reasoning for a reading group of graduate students in the humanities: sharp, articulate, comfortable arguing, but with nothing beyond a vague memory of school-level statistics. A few days ago I spent a long evening arguing with a friend about a public-health claim. She had noticed that a study's overall numbers supported one conclusion, while splitting the data by age reversed it, and we could not agree about whether either number was the right one to believe. That argument made me want to turn this exact phenomenon—Simpson's paradox—into the session.

Don't produce the explanation itself; produce the teaching plan for it. I need the dependency order: what has to be in place before the reversal registers as a genuine puzzle rather than an arithmetic mistake—thinking in terms of conditional versus marginal comparisons, the idea of a lurking or stratifying variable, and the way group sizes and group-specific rates combine—and in what sequence those pieces should land, and why. I want one carrying example for the whole session; tell me whether I should build a small invented dataset or lean on something like the Berkeley graduate-admissions tables or the kidney-stone treatment numbers, and what features the example must have and must avoid. Map the misconceptions I should expect from a humanities audience—for instance that the paradox shows statistics can be made to say anything, that the aggregate number is simply wrong, that the subgroup number is automatically the true one, or that the reversal is just an artifact of unequal group sizes—and tell me exactly where in the sequence to head each one off, and what to say at that point. How will I know by the end that they understand the paradox as a real structural feature of pooling rather than having merely been entertained by a counterintuitive trick? And what should I deliberately leave out or refuse to resolve at this level?

Also, separate the settled from the contested. It is settled that an association can reverse when groups are pooled, and that this arises from the arithmetic of how the groups are weighted. It is genuinely contested, or at least not settled by the mathematics alone, how to decide which stratification is relevant in a real case, whether an observed reversal warrants a causal reading, and what "the right answer" is for any particular application. Please keep that line visible throughout the plan rather than presenting a contested methodological verdict as consensus.
