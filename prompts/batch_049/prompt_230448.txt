Two colleagues of mine got into it at lunch after a prime minister's formal apology for a colonial-era massacre was in the news. One of them — call him Dan — said the whole performance is close to meaningless: the PM didn't do it, nobody alive did it, and you can't apologize for someone else's acts; an apology is a confession of wrongdoing by the wrongdoer, and the wrongdoer isn't there to give it.

Maya pushed back: he isn't apologizing as an individual, he's apologizing as the current holder of an office in a state that has persisted through every change of government since the massacre. The state is the same institution that did it. Companies apologize for what previous management did and we don't find it incoherent, so why is a state different?

Dan said the company analogy hides more than it reveals. A company is a going concern whose current members typical inherit the benefits and assets, and can sell their shares. A state is just a changing population and a set of organs; if I don't have to answer for my great-grandfather's crimes, why should citizens have to answer for a long-dead colonial governor's crimes through a state apology?

Maya answered that this misses what a state apology is: it isn't a personal confession of guilt, it's an official recognition of a wrong and of the standing of the people it wronged. The descendants of the victims are alive; the effects — land, language, institutional distrust — are alive; and an apology changes the official story: what gets taught, what gets commemorated, who's account is accepted. That does real work.

Dan said fine, but then it's forward-looking policy dressed up as apology. Everything she just described can be done without an apology — compensation, curriculum reform, renaming. Calling it an apology borrows the moral weight of individual guilt to make a policy feel like more than a policy, and that's exactly the kind of trick we should resist.
