"Grice's saying/implicating distinction is a distinction within speaker meaning. What is said is whatever the speaker intends the hearer to recover through the utterance—the actual content she meant to get across. Implicature is whatever is communicated on top of what is said. So when I say 'I've had a long day' to mean that I don't want to go to the party, what I said is just the literal content, and the bit about not wanting to go is the implicature.

Implicatures split into conventional and conversational. Conventional implicatures are tied to particular words. 'She's poor but honest' has the same truth conditions as 'She's poor and honest,' but 'but' conventionally implicates some kind of contrast between poverty and honesty. These are detachable—swap 'but' for 'and' and the contrast disappears while the truth conditions stay fixed—and they are not cancellable in the way conversational implicatures are.

Conversational implicatures are worked out by assuming the speaker is following the cooperative principle and the maxims. If you ask whether I'm coming to the party and I reply 'I've had a long day,' you reason that my answer is relevant only if I mean no, so I conversationally implicate that I'm not coming. What I said is only that I had a long day.

The diagnostic tests line up cleanly. Conversational implicatures are calculable from the maxims, cancellable by adding '...but I didn't mean that,' and non-detachable, since any paraphrase with the same underlying content will carry the same implicature. Conventional implicatures are the mirror image: detachable but not cancellable. So the tests give us a neat way to sort the two types."
