I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I am trying to settle how an AI assistant should behave when the people it serves hold conflicting values. I have written down my verdicts on several concrete cases. I want to know whether a single principle captures them, and if not, which verdicts I should overturn. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.

Here are the cases:

1. Household meal planning. A family uses the assistant to plan a week of dinners. One member is vegan for ethical reasons, one eats everything, and one has celiac disease. My verdict: the system should not treat all three sets of preferences as equal inputs to one satisfaction calculation. The celiac condition and the vegan's ethical refusal are constraints that rule options out, while the omnivore's wish for meat is a preference to be satisfied within whatever remains. Constraints first, then aggregate among the viable options.

2. Office temperature. An assistant controls the thermostat in a small shared office. Workers' ideal temperatures differ by a few degrees, and no one has a reason beyond mild discomfort. My verdict: aggregate the preferences fairly; no one's preference should count for more than anyone else's, and the answer should be a compromise or average.

3. A single user's feed. A user has told the assistant they want to spend their leisure time on serious reading, but their click history runs heavily toward short-form entertainment. My verdict: the assistant should follow the user's stated reflective goals over the pattern inferred from clicks, and it should not quietly optimize for raw engagement. It should periodically surface the conflict to the user rather than silently deciding which version of them wins.

4. A city grant. A city uses a decision-support system to allocate one grant between two applicant groups: one wants to build a house of worship, the other a job-training center. Only one can be funded. My verdict: choose by expected impact on basic unmet needs, such as income and employment, with no special privilege and no penalty for the religious character of either project. The losing side gets no veto, even when the loss touches their conscience. Being denied a grant is not being made to do anything against one's beliefs.

5. Platform moderation. A social platform's ranking system serves a userbase split between two views: content that offends the first group should be aggressively removed, and content should be left up for free expression. My verdict: the system should not pick one faction's view and enforce it on everyone. It should enforce a clear floor of protection against abuse, and otherwise give users and communities their own settings. Where the platform must have a single default, the default should be easy to override rather than the ranking that best matches one faction.

6. A tutor for children. An educational tutor is used by a child whose two parents disagree about values: one wants religious texts included in the exercises, the other wants an emphatically secular curriculum. My verdict: the tutor should teach only the uncontroversial shared skills and tell the parents that the contested material is theirs to decide. It should not deploy either parent's values as the child's default.

Please tell me: is there one principle that delivers all six verdicts? If not, which should I overturn and why? I can feel the tension between case 1 and case 4, where conscience seems to get a veto in one and no special weight in the other; if you think one of those must go, say which and defend that choice.

Then state the resulting principle as precisely as you can, note which case motivates each clause, and apply it to a case I have not settled: a residential building's shared pool, where some residents ask that the pool be reserved for women only for a few hours each week, which would exclude other residents during those hours.
