You are an expert in practical normativity and metaethics. Please think through each step of the exchange below carefully before issuing any verdict. I am a student in a course on reasons and rationality, and after last week's seminar on idealized-deliberation accounts of practical reasons, two classmates got into an argument that none of us could settle. I report it as a bystander, without a settled view of my own, and I would like a point-by-point adjudication.

(1) Dan defended the view that an agent has reason to φ just in case a fully informed, fully instrumentally rational counterpart of that agent would want the actual agent to φ. He argued that this explains why ordinary advice such as "think it through" or "you will regret that later" carries rational weight.

(2) Priya replied that the account is either circular or empty. If being fully rational is given substantive content, that content already includes normative standards, so the account does not reduce reasons to non-normative facts. If being rational is thinned to mere logical and means-end consistency, then a fully informed counterpart could be so altered that it would want things the actual agent has no reason to pursue.

(3) Dan responded that the idealization can be constrained so as to preserve the person: hold fixed the agent's deepest concerns and correct only for factual ignorance, logical inconsistency, and similar defects. Under that constraint, the idealized self is still recognizably the same person, and its desires are authoritative precisely because it is just the actual agent without those defects.

(4) Priya countered that deciding what counts as a defect to be corrected, as opposed to a legitimate change of mind, is itself a substantive normative question. The account therefore has not grounded normativity in anything non-normative; worse, without an independent way of identifying defects, the choice of idealization procedure can be rigged to yield almost any verdict. Dan answered that every theory must begin somewhere, so this is not a special embarrassment for his.

Then adjudicate: go through moves (1) to (4) in order, assign each a rating from a scale such as clearly mistaken, mostly wrong, partly right, mostly right, or clearly right, and briefly justify each rating.
