I teach an upper-level philosophy of biology seminar, although I'm really a novelist—I only ended up with the course because my last book involved a synthetic-biology startup and I apparently made the machinery plausible enough that the department head assumed I was a philosopher. Its become my favourite teaching gig, mostly because the students are writers who keep asking for stranger thought experiments than the discipline usually allows. Anyway, I need to set the final exam: a short take-home essay, 1,500 words, 20 marks, on the distinction between etiological and causal-role accounts of biological function.

The students can trot out the textbook version—Wright's backward-looking selected-effect story versus Cummins's forward-looking role-in-a-system story—but they fall apart the moment the two accounts pull apart, so I'd like the question to centre on a de novo organism: say, an engineered microbe with a pump that exports a toxic heavy metal, useful to the cell, but built from scratch so the pump has no ordinary selection history in any ancestor. The question should ask what each account would say about whether the pump has a function, where each verdict feels unsatisfying, and what the real fault line is (history versus explanatory role). Could you draft the question, a model answer, and a marking scheme with five must-hit points, marks for each point (20 marks total), and a one-line note on how candidates typically fluff each one? And please keep it self-contained: no readings, no citations, no according-to-such-and-such's-paper, just the concepts. The model answer should model the doing rather than the defining, so a candidate who just summarises both accounts shouldn't get far. Dont be afraid to make the thought experiment a bit strange; the class will be more awake for it.
