Act as a professional philosopher of religion with a strong secondary interest in literary interpretation, and think through the question carefully rather than reaching for the standard answer. I run an online reading group whose regulars mostly come from rationalist forums, and we are about to discuss Ursula K. Le Guin's short story "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas." In every discussion I have seen, the story is treated as a utilitarian thought experiment: one child's abject, ongoing misery is the hidden condition of an otherwise flourishing city, almost everyone knows and accepts the arrangement, and a few walk away. I suspect that framing has crowded out a more interesting reading.

I want to read the story as an exercise in theodicy, or perhaps anti-theodicy. The story has the deep structure of the problem of evil: a world that appears nearly perfect rests on a single known horror, and the inhabitants have settled ways of justifying it — the arrangement is necessary, the child's suffering serves the general good, the child is now too damaged to be helped, the bargain cannot be renegotiated without ruining everyone. Read the story against the standard theodical strategies, including greater-good theodicies, free will defenses, soul-making accounts, appeals to epistemic limitation, and the idea that the sufferer would or should consent. Where does Le Guin's text map onto those moves, and where does it deliberately expose their costs or refuse to be accommodated by them? Does the story take a position on whether the arrangement is justified, and does "walking away" amount to an answer or to a refusal to answer? End by saying what the story adds to the philosophical debate that an ordinary theodicy paper cannot.

Be faithful in both directions: do not reduce the story to a case study, and do not distort the philosophical views to fit it. In particular, note the disanalogies — there is no deity in Omelas, and the child is not free, fallen, or improved by its suffering — but also say which disanalogies matter for the theodicy reading and which are beside the point. Begin by brainstorming several possible readings before settling on the strongest one.
