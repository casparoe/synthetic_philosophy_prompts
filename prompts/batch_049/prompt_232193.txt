Over the last few months I've been going back and forth with a friend who thinks panpsychism is the only way to get consciousness into the natural world. Whenever I bring up the combination problem — how do lots of tiny subjects with their own points of view add up to one unified conscious mind like mine — they wave it off by saying we accept composition everywhere else: individual voices blend into a single chord, pixels make up a coherent image, droplets merge into a pool. They think once you see the analogy, the combination problem stops looking like a problem.

I'm not persuaded, but every time I try to say why I end up just asserting that the little subjects would need to "fuse" or "be somewhere" in the big subject, and my friend says I'm begging the question. So I want a proper audit. Take the chord or pixel analogy (or both) and give me the correspondence table: what maps to the micro-subjects, the macro-subject, the combining relation, the boundaries of the whole, and the point of view from which the unified thing is experienced. Then go through where the mapping breaks. I especially want to know which disanalogies matter for the intended conclusion — that there's no principled obstruction to combining subjects — and which are just irrelevant quirks of the source domain. At the end, tell me wether the combination problem actually survives this analogy, and if it does, whether there's some better analogy the panpsychist should lean on instead.
