I've never taken a philosophy class but I've been chewing on this one for months and can't find where it goes wrong, so I'm hoping someone can sort me out. It's about whether reasons need somebody doing the reasoning.

(1) Calling something "a reason" only makes sense if it's the kind of thing that could actually get a person to do or believe something.
(2) For something to get a person to do or believe something, it has to be available to that person's mind at the time -- they have to at least be able to notice it, or put it together from what they notice.
(3) So if a fact couldn't have been noticed or put together by anyone around, then that fact couldn't get anyone to do or believe anything.
(4) And a fact that couldn't get anyone to do or believe anything isn't a reason, by (1).
(5) Therefore, there are no reasons except the ones some actual mind has picked up on.
(6) But consider surgeons in, say, 1700, before germ theory. They had a reason to wash their hands before operating -- the germs where there and they were killing patients. But nobody around could of put that together; from their point of view, washing was just a ritual.
(7) So either there was a reason floating around with no reasoner to think it, or (5) is false, or we have to bite the bullet and say they had no reason to wash their hands even while infecting patients, which sounds crazy.

I'd really like to keep (6) -- it seems monstrous to say otherwise. But I also don't see how to keep it without admitting these weird unattached reasons. Which step should I give up, and what does giving it up cost?
