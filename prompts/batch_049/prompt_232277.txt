I keep reaching for the same distinction whenever I think about partial belief, and I want a principle that earns it rather than one I have simply absorbed. The distinction is between states of uncertainty that are best represented by a single exact probability and states that are best represented by a range or set of probabilities. I have strong but apparently inconsistent instincts about when each is called for, and I want you to extract the principle behind them — or tell me which of my instincts must go.

Here are the cases as they strike me.

1. A coin I have personally tested and satisfied myself is fair, about to be tossed once: I assign exactly one-half to heads and would bet at even odds with no reluctance.

2. A coin handed to me by a stranger, about whose bias I know nothing, about to be tossed once: I refuse to move from its two-sided symmetry to a probability of one-half. My opinion about heads is more honestly represented as the entire interval from zero to one. Symmetry alone does not force a number on me.

3. A lottery with one hundred numbered tickets and a known random draw: I assign exactly one one-hundredth to ticket thirty-seven winning. No imprecision whatsoever.

4. An urn containing ninety balls, thirty red and the remaining sixty either black or yellow in a wholly unknown mix, one ball to be drawn: I assign exactly one-third to red. But for black I want to say only that my probability is somewhere from zero to two-thirds. One urn, one draw, three possible colors — and yet my opinion about red is exact while my opinion about black is only a range. I cannot yet say what licenses the difference.

5. Two equally credible models, built by equally competent methods, give probabilities of rain tomorrow of thirty percent and seventy percent, and I have no other relevant information: I do not split the difference at fifty. I want my opinion to be the whole interval from thirty to seventy percent. If both models had said thirty percent, I would be at thirty percent sharply. So imprecision seems to arise from unresolved conflict between equally good sources, not from the mere fact that a number falls somewhere short of certainty.

6. Before I have looked at a page I know to be in English, I have no opinion at all about whether the next word on it contains the letter e — not even a vague range. After seeing that it is ordinary English prose, I have a rough interval. After tallying the letter frequencies on the page, I have something close to a point value. This makes imprecision look like a stage on the way to exactness. Yet in the stranger's coin case no purely reflective work sharpens my judgment, while after ten thousand observed flips I would be very nearly sharp. In the two-models case, though, no amount of computation tells me which model is right. So imprecision is not merely unperformed arithmetic, but I cannot state the difference precisely.

7. In small-stakes choices I am content to pick a single number from the middle of my interval and act on it. In a one-off high-stakes decision I refuse to do this; I want the whole interval to constrain my choice, so that a wager must look acceptable everywhere in the interval before I take it. I cannot tell whether this is a genuine normative insight or a rationalization of my own sloppiness.

My rough and admittedly inadequate principle is this: a sharp credence is licensed exactly when either (a) I know an objective chance that fixes the value, or (b) my evidence singles out a unique value to the exclusion of all others; otherwise, my opinion should be represented by the class of probability functions compatible with my evidence.

Give me the principle, or small family of principles, that best captures these verdicts. State it precisely, say which cases motivate each clause, and identify which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and what I should replace them with. Then apply it to at least one case I have not listed, such as this one: a coin is either biased two-to-one toward heads or two-to-one toward tails, exactly one of these biases obtains, and I know nothing about which. Should that be one-half sharp by symmetry, or the imprecise interval from one-third to two-thirds? Finally, answer the question the cases keep pressing on me: is the boundary between precise and imprecise belief a difference in kind or only a difference in degree?
