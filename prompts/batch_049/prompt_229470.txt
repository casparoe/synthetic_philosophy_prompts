I recently had a long argument with a friend about the Catalan, Scottish, and Quebec independence movements, and I came away unsure whether I had been too quick to dismiss the position he was defending. He lent me a chapter from a book on the ethics of secession, and its core summary runs like this. On what the chapter calls the remedial-right-only view, a group has a right to secede only when it has suffered serious injustice, such as systematic human-rights violations, unjust annexation, or persistent discriminatory treatment by the state; where no such grievance exists — as in a wealthy liberal democracy — there is no legitimate right to secede, and the state may permissibly keep itself intact. The chapter then applies this to the present: the Scottish and Catalan movements, it says, are without moral foundation because neither people is oppressed, and their grievances are properly addressed through ordinary democratic politics. It adds that, on this view, a majority vote for independence in a region carries no independent moral weight, since consent of the governed is merely a procedural value rather than a source of rights.

That summary felt persuasive when I read it, and I was ready to concede the argument to my friend, but he pressed it further than the chapter does, and something about the inferences now strikes me as too quick. I want to know how fair the summary actually is to the remedial-right-only position before I accept it. Could you go through it piece by piece: which claims would a careful defender of the view accept, which would she qualify or reject, and whether the conclusions drawn about Scotland and Catalonia, and about the moral significance of a referendum, actually follow from the parts that are accurate?
