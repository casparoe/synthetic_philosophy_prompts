A friend recently sent me a piece from a popular philosophy website that he says finally made Hume's argument against miracles click for him, and he was puzzled that I wasn't quite satisfied by it. The article summarizes the argument in the following paragraph:

"Hume's argument is beautifully simple. A miracle is by definition a violation of a law of nature, and a law of nature is something for which we have uniform, exceptionless evidence across the whole course of experience. Testimony for a miracle, by contrast, can only give us the always-fallible word of some individual witnesses. On one side, then, we have the entire history of experience backing the law; on the other, we have the limited and fallible evidence of testimony. Since the evidence for the law is always the stronger of the two, the probability that the witnesses are lying or mistaken must always exceed the probability that the law was really violated, which means a reasonable person should never accept a miracle story no matter how impressive the testimony is. In the second half of the essay, Hume adds some observations to explain why miracle reports nevertheless circulate: they tend to come from excitable, uneducated people; they flourish in superstitious or backward cultures; and the miracle claims of rival religions cancel each other out. Hume therefore concludes that miracles cannot happen and that belief in one is never reasonable no matter how much testimony is offered."

I can tell the summary is doing something too neat, but I don't know the text well enough to say exactly where it goes wrong. Could you provide an accuracy audit of this paragraph, going through its claims in order and separating what Hume clearly says from what is a simplification a careful reader would wince at and what is just not his argument? In particular, I'd like to know whether the summary ignores the exception Hume builds into his general maxim, whether it slides from a claim about what testimony can establish to a claim about what can happen, and whether the second-half observations are part of the main argument or a separate and weaker supplement. And, finally, does the article's closing claim—that miracles cannot happen and belief in one is never reasonable—actually follow from the parts of the summary that are accurate?
