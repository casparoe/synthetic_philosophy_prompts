Consider, from the outside, the standard scheme of intellectual-property protection in the United States. It treats the same underlying thing — a creation of the mind — in three visibly different ways. For a novel, copyright protects the particular expression but nothing more: the sentences and the shape of a scene, but not the plot, the theme, or the underlying idea; and independent creation is a complete defense, so a second author who somehow writes a nearly identical story without ever having seen the first holds her own valid copyright and owes nothing. For a new chemical process, a patent protects the process itself for a term, even against a rival who independently invented the same process in a separate lab; independent invention is no defense to infringement. For a soda formula held as a trade secret, the right reaches only misappropriation — theft, espionage, bribery, breach of confidence — and a competitor may lawfully reverse-engineer the formula from a bottle bought on the shelf if reverse engineering proves possible, or simply use the formula after independently rediscovering it. A single artifact, such as software, can sit under all three regimes at once, with copyright in the code, patent in the method, and trade secrecy in the undisclosed source.

What view of the thing being owned — and of what it is to "own an idea" — does this pattern presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory as far as the rules warrant, and mark carefully where the rules strictly presuppose something, where they merely fit well with a view, and where they are philosophically underdetermined. Do they commit to the claim that ideas themselves are never owned, and that only expression, disclosed inventions, or confidential information can be? Do they commit to a labor-desert account, on which rights are earned by the work of creation? To a personhood account, on which a work is an extension of its creator's self? Or to an instrumental account, on which exclusive rights exist only because and only insofar as they serve innovation and disclosure? Or does the scheme license several of these equally, with its real commitments lying somewhere else? Then make the test concrete: if a pure labor-desert account were applied consistently across all three regimes, which of the three patterns above — independent creation as a defense, independent invention as no defense, reverse engineering as lawful — would have to be discarded, and what rule would replace it? Answer the same question for a pure personhood account.
