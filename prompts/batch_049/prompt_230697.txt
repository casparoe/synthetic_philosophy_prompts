I'm a retired environmental biochemist, and I've been working though Mill's On Liberty for the first time. Something in chapter two keeps tripping me up. Mill writes:

"But, indeed, the dictum that truth always triumphs over persecution, is one of those pleasant falsehoods which men repeat after one another till they pass into commonplaces, but which all experience refutes. History teems with instances of truth put down by persecution. If not suppressed for ever, it may be thrown back for centuries."

That seems like a pretty dark concession, and I don't see how it fits with the rest of his argument. If truth does *not* always win out, and suppressing ideas can actually work for long stretches, then isn't Mill admitting that the standard reason for tolerating heretical views is empirically shaky? I always thought the point of free speech was that open debate tends, over time, to let the truth emerge. But if Mill himself says history refutes that, why doesn't that pull the rug out from under his case for letting dangerous or false ideas circulate? I feel like he's giving away the whole game and then just proceeding as if he hasn't. Am I misreading what this passage is doing in his argument, or is this a real tension in his position?
