The board of a regional conservation authority will vote next month on whether to authorize an intervention aimed at reducing a large die-off in one of its fenced reserves, and it has asked the authority's ethics advisory committee — of which I am a member — to send a written recommendation before the vote. I am preparing that memo. The reserve's two waterholes are already partly maintained by staff, and the elephant population is substantially larger than rainfall alone would support; in all other respects the authority's policy is non-interventionist. Feeding or treating wild animals is restricted to cases in which a species is threatened with local extirpation, and culling is permitted only in extremis. A severe drought has collapsed the reserve's browse and grass cover; the elephants are losing condition, and some have already died. The proposal before the board seeks authorization for emergency supplementary feeding during the current dry season, followed by a multi-year immunocontraception programme to hold the population below the reserve's carrying capacity. Its case is that the aggregate suffering of hundreds of slowly starving animals, including the slower deaths of dependent calves, outweighs whatever value attaches to an unmanaged baseline — all the more so because the system is already fenced and water-managed, and the authority bears some responsibility for the population's current size. The ecology unit opposes the plan: it argues that feeding now would mainly postpone and sharpen the eventual crash, habituate wild animals to human provision, and set a precedent under which every seasonal hardship becomes a welfare emergency; and that mass contraception is a large, open-ended intervention in a functioning system whose fluctuations are not the authority's to flatten. The board wants a decision document, not a survey of positions. Write the memo I will bring to the board: it should recommend approve, reject, or approve with conditions; set out a general principle for when the authority may intervene to reduce foreseeable wild animal suffering, stated so the board can use it as standing guidance for future requests; and respond to the strongest argument for the position you reject rather than dismissing it.
