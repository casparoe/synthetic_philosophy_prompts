I am teaching an upper-level course on ethics and the novel, and this term we are putting Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day, in both the novel and the Merchant-Ivory film, alongside Bernard Williams's critique of impartial moral theory. Williams argues that a morality which requires deliberation from no one's particular point of view, and which makes each of us responsible for every outcome we could have prevented, leaves no room for the commitments that give an agent's life its shape and that make integrity possible in the first place. His companion thought is the one about the man who saves his drowning wife: the rescue is not improved if, at the moment of choice, the motivating thought must include the impartial rider that saving one's own wife in such a case is permissible. The thought that it is simply his wife should be enough, and the added justification is one thought too many.

I want to read the butler Stevens through this material, and I would like you to work out the reading with me before I face the class. The novel gives us a man of formidable self-command who repeatedly refuses to let personal attachment become a load-bearing commitment: he carries on serving guests while his father is dying upstairs, he will not comfort Miss Kenton when she grieves for the aunt who raised her, and he will not knock when he hears her weeping in her room after she has told him she is leaving Darlington Hall. Only decades later, during his motoring holiday, when he finally visits Miss Kenton, now Mrs Benn, does he allow himself to register that a life with her might have been possible, and that something in him is breaking. I would like you to ask precisely what Williams's framework explains about Stevens and what it obscures. Is Stevens the victim of the ailment Williams diagnoses, a man whose professional impartiality is itself a standing supply of justifications that reach him one thought too late, or is his lifelong cultivation of the ideal of dignity actually a ground project by Williams's own criteria, so that the novel is pressing on a weakness in the account: that integrity, so formally described, might be present in a life that has nonetheless hollowed itself out? And what should we do with the fact that Stevens's projects seem borrowed from his class and his employer, so that his real failure looks less like forfeited integrity than like bad faith, a refusal to own any judgment about Lord Darlington and the political causes he serves?

I want a reading faithful in both directions, not a case study that reduces the novel to an illustration of Williams and not an impressionistic essay that lets the novel's sadness do the philosophical work. Tell me plainly where the book complicates, rebuffs, or extends the integrity objection and the one-thought-too-many point, and conclude with what Ishiguro dramatizes about the psychic cost of impartiality that Williams's stripped-down examples cannot show. The students will not have the text in front of them and I do not want secondary literature, bibliographic suggestions, or quoted passages; I want your own careful reconstruction of scenes and arguments, including the places where an honest critic would have to admit the reading strains.
