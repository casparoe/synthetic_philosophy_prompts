Here's a puzzle I keep coming back to. Say you know the store is open — you can see the lights on and the sign flipped to OPEN. Your friend comes by and says it's actually closed. You don't have to roll over: if you really know it's open, then her claim is misleading somehow, and you can reasonably keep beleiving it's open. Fine. But generalise: if you know p, then anything that would be evidence against p is misleading, so it starts to look like knowing p licenses you to rationally shrug off any counterevidence whatsoever. That can't be right, because sometimes new evidence does defeat knowledge, and a person who never updates is just stubborn, not rational. So there's a tension between two plausible-sounding ideas.

Now imagine two people who've been fighting about this for a year decide to do a proper adversarial collaboration. One is the dogmatist: she says the paradox is basically sound and that's not a bug — when your basis for p is strong enough, you are rationally allowed to dismiss or heavily discount counterevidence, and that's exactly what knowledge does for you. The other is the evidentialist: he says knowledge never gives you a free pass to ignore evidence; the paradox just shows the first step is the one to reject — from knowing p you can't infer that all counterevidence is misleading in a way that lets you set it aside.

Draft the joint document they would sign. I want: (1) a numbered list of claims both accept about how the paradox is set up and which ingredients make it bite; (2) the exact residual disagreement, worded so each side would sign off on the statement of their own view; (3) the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would actually move one side or both; and (4) for each crux, one concrete case or argument both agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Put the disagreements in a table after the agreed-claims section. And if you conclude part of the dispute is merely verbal — say, over what "ignoring evidence" amounts to — say which part and why. Keep the agreed claims non-vacuous; no filler like "both parties agree rationality matters."
