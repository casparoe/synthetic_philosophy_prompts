Draft the joint statement that a substantivalist interpreter of Descartes’s cogito and a deflationary interpreter of Descartes’s cogito could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on the question of what exactly the cogito proves. Begin by characterising the two camps fairly.

The substantivalist camp holds that the cogito does genuine work beyond reporting a thought: it certifies the existence of a thinking thing — a subject that has the doubt and the thought — and that this is a first, demon-proof step toward the res cogitans, even though further argument is needed for the full real distinction between mind and body. On this view, the move from I think to I am a thinking thing is not a mere change of wording.

The deflationary camp holds that the cogito certifies only that thinking is occurring, at the moment at which it occurs, and that the I in I think is either purely grammatical or at most the indexical surface of the thinking episode itself. On this view, the cogito establishes no continuing substance, no owner of thoughts over and above the episodes, and no nature beyond the occurrence of thought; anything stronger is imported from elsewhere.

Take the collaboration’s target claims to be the existence claim Descartes treats as necessarily true whenever he puts it forward or conceives it, and the further claim that he is a thing that thinks. The document should distinguish carefully between these two claims and should not trade in straw positions.

Format the deliverable as follows.

1. Agreed claims. A numbered list of substantive claims both camps can sign, including their joint account of exactly what certainty Descartes secures against the evil demon and what that certainty leaves open. Avoid truisms; each agreed claim should be a concession that at least one side had to work to accept.

2. Disagreements table. Aim for four to six rows. Each row gives: (a) the crux as a precise yes-or-no question; (b) the substantivalist answer, worded so a substantivalist would sign it; (c) the deflationary answer, worded so a deflationary interpreter would sign it. Where two rows collapse into one because the disagreement turns out to have been verbal, merge them and say why; keep the rows that remain genuinely load-bearing.

3. Diagnostic tests and predictions. For each row of the table, state the case, argument, or test that both camps accept as bearing on the crux, and record each side’s prediction in advance. Include at least these three diagnostics:
   - the impersonalisation test: whether the certainty secured by the cogito survives recasting as thinking is occurring rather than as I think, and whether whatever is lost is substantive or merely verbal;
   - the interruption test: whether a thinker who undergoes dreamless sleep, anaesthesia, or another gap in occurrent thought can, on resuming thought, have the same certainty that the I which now thinks is the same I that earlier doubted;
   - the ownership test: what the cogito guarantees in the case of a thought that surfaces unbidden, without any felt activity of authorship, and whether the claim I am thinking remains demon-proof there.

4. What would change our minds. For each camp, state the consideration — a case, an argument, or an analysis of the grammar of first-person thought — that would most likely lead its proponents to revise the view, and identify where its own reasoning is most vulnerable. Make this section candid and specific rather than a perfunctory expression of open-mindedness.

Write the entire document in British English. The result should be a fair record of a live disagreement that both parties could sign: not a verdict on which camp is right, and not a compromise that papers over the residual difference. If, in the course of drafting, you find a place where the two camps have merely been talking past one another, say so explicitly; if you find a genuine crux the table cannot yet resolve, present it as such rather than silently siding with one camp.
