A friend and I spent most of last evening arguing about whether it can ever be rational to prefer never having been born, and what stuck with me afterward was that the real difficulty lay not in stating Benatar's asymmetry but in seeing exactly what follows from it. It made me want to test that skill directly on this week's ethics assignment, so I wrote the following short-answer question. Below are the question and one student's response. I would like you to grade the response as I would, as the TA for the course.

Question (15 points). David Benatar argues that coming into existence is always a harm. His argument turns on an axiological asymmetry. For a person who comes to exist, the presence of pain is bad and the presence of pleasure is good. For a person who never comes to exist, the absence of pain is good, though there is no actual person for whom it is good, while the absence of pleasure is not bad, since no one is deprived of it. Explain why this asymmetry is meant to make non-existence compare favorably with existence, and then give the strongest objection you can to the move from the asymmetry to the conclusion that coming into existence is always a harm.

Student's answer:

"Benatar's asymmetry contrasts two pairs of states. For a person who exists, pain is bad and pleasure is good. For a person who never exists, the absence of pain is good—not good for anyone, but good nonetheless—while the absence of pleasure is not bad, because there is no one to be deprived of it. The key point is that the two absence cases are treated differently: missed pleasures count as neutral, while avoided pains count as positive. Once we accept that, non-existence wins on the comparison of the absence cases, and that is enough. Any life containing even one pain is therefore not worth starting, since its pains could have been absent, which would have been good, while its pleasures would not have been missed, which is not bad. Benatar is careful to add that such lives can still be worth continuing once started; the conclusion concerns only coming into existence. The strongest objection is that we have no obligation to create people who would be happy, so the absence of pleasure cannot count against never existing. But this misses the point, because Benatar is claiming that existing is worse for the child than never having existed, not that anyone has violated a duty. So the asymmetry, once granted, makes non-existence better than existence in every case."

Please grade this answer out of 15. I want a point-by-point assessment rather than a general impression: go through the substantive claims in the answer and mark each as correct, partly correct, or incorrect, with a sentence or two of justification for each. Pay particular attention to whether the student correctly tracks what does and does not follow from the asymmetry, since that is where such answers tend to go wrong.
