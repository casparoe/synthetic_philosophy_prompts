In my reading and in conversations with colleagues, I keep noticing a pattern in how people attribute mental states to computational systems, and I am looking for either its name or a careful definition of the phenomenon. The pattern is a slide between two standards of evidence. People start out insisting, often explicitly, that they are not behaviorists and that what matters is what a system is doing internally. But when they confront a concrete case, the evidence that actually moves them is linguistic output. A system that prints a number, say a high value for a variable labeled pain magnitude, is treated as giving almost no evidence about whether anything is felt, while a system that prints "This hurts" at the corresponding point in the same process is treated as giving real evidence, even when the observer knows that both outputs were generated by the same kind of mapping from internal variables to output strings and that the sentence is just as easy to engineer as the numeral. In debates about large language models I see the same slide in sharper form: everyone grants that a model can be trained to say that it suffers, yet a model's fluent first-person reports are still treated by otherwise careful researchers as among the strongest available evidence, while a silent system that is stipulated to run the same internal computations receives almost no uptake. This is not exactly anthropomorphism, and not exactly the old ELIZA effect, because the people involved typically disavow both.

Does this pattern have an established name? If there is one, how is it distinguished from the ELIZA effect, from anthropomorphism, and from a behaviorist or testimonial criterion of mentality? If no established concept fits well, I would be happy with a coinage, provided it comes with a precise definition and a statement of its edges: in particular, is the slide a fallacy, a bias, a shift in the burden of proof, or a defensible inference forced on us by the problem of other minds? Please write your answer in American English.
