I cover state politics for a newspaper, and in the last year I've watched "the will of the people" end one argument after another: a county supervisor said a ballot initiative's lopsided win gave her a mandate the moment the votes were in; a recall organizer said before election day that the petition signatures already expressed the people's will better than the coming vote could; and a party official waved off a poll showing majority opposition by saying the people's "true" will would emerge once voters had to live with the alternatives. In each case the speaker was defending a move widely described as populist, and the phrase was made to carry the whole justification, yet it seemed to mean something different in each mouth. I want the ambiguity made explicit. Lay out the distinct precise claims "the will of the people" could be asserting — about what a majority actually voted for, about an aggregate of individual preferences, about a collective subject with interests of its own, about what people would want if fully informed, about what serves the common good, about the intensity of a mobilized group — and for each reading say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or false, and what it would actually entitle someone to conclude. Then go back through my three examples and say which reading each speaker needed, and whether that reading could bear the weight they put on it.
