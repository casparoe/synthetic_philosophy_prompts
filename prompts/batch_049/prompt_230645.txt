Below are working notes for an essay I am considering. Before I expand them into a full draft, I would be grateful for critical comments on both accuracy (do the notes misdescribe the phenomenon or the positions at issue?) and interest (would this repay readers who already know the rough outlines of the debate?).

1. I take the familiar target under the label Cartesian theater to be a bundle of at least three commitments:
   (S) Stage: there is a spatially localized site in the brain where contents are "presented" so that consciousness can occur.
   (A) Audience: there is a distinct internal consumer—a viewer or homunculus—for whom the presentation is made.
   (M) Master take: for each event at each moment, there is a single canonical version of what the subject is consciously experiencing.

2. The multiple drafts model is plainly well aimed at S and A. But my interest is in M. The model replaces the single built-in master take with many parallel, content-bearing processes, none of which is intrinsically "the" conscious content; what gets reported depends on how the system is probed, and probing selects or stabilizes some of these processes rather than revealing a preinstalled final draft. I will call this "on-demand canonicity": M is not eliminated but multiplied and made contextual. Once a probe occurs, one candidate becomes the reported content, and with enough probing a retrospective narrative accumulates that looks like a single stream.

3. This yields the tension I want to press. The multiple drafts theorist explains the appearance of a single, continuous stream of experience by saying that this appearance is itself just one more draft or narrative artifact, not a fact that a theory of consciousness must preserve. But that reply carries force only if one has already adopted a methodological policy—roughly, that no first-person appearance is evidence except as one more draft to be explained around or explained away. I suspect this policy, not the neuroscience behind rejecting S and A, is doing the real work, and that it is closer to operationalism than to a discovery about how the brain is built.

4. My constructive suggestion is a "post-hoc marquee": the single-stream appearance would be explained as an after-the-fact convergence of drafts under repeated probing—a coalition of drafts stable enough to be reportable—rather than as a presented sequence in a fixed venue. What I doubt is whether this does enough: it accounts for convergence, but not obviously for the subject's confidence that what they now report is what they were already conscious of before being probed.

I am unsure whether note 3 is an objection or merely a redescription of the view's explanatory ambitions, and whether note 4 quietly smuggles a stage back in.
