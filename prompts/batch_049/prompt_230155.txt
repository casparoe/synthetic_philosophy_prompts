I'm on the policy team at a company that makes voice agents for customer service, and we need to decide which agents must announce up front that they're an AI under a new state disclosure law. The test is behavioral: an agent has to self-identify if a reasonable customer might otherwise believe they're talking to a person, and we can't score "might believe" consistently. Could you write us a reusable decision proceedure that our testers can run while only talking to the agent, not reading its code? It should specify the calls to run in order, what counts as pass/fail/retest, where it stops, and where it hands the decision to a human because the behavioral standard has gone wrong. It needs to handle the cases that keep biting us: an agent that announces immediately but then chats so naturally for ten minutes that callers still treat it as a human colleague; an agent that gives itself away by being too fast at arithmetic and too brittle around ambiguity; and a clearly synthetic voice that still gets callers saying "good girl" to it. Flag the points where a Turing-test-style behavioral standard is the wrong tool and we should override it by policy. Then run the procedure on two agents: one that announces and then uses very human hesitations, and one that never announces but has a slightly synthetic voice your grandmother would recognize. Stakes: a wrong "probably passes as human" call is a legal violation, not just a bad review.
