I'm working on a piece about moral deference—whether it's ever okay to just take someone else's word on a moral question instead of working it out yourself—and I've been interviewing a philosopher who's gave me verdicts on a pile of cases. So far she's said: a surgeon who defers to a hospital ethicist about whether to intubate an unconscious patient over the family's objection is doing fine; someone who becomes a pacifist over winter break purely because a famous moral philosopher says pacifism is required is doing something wrong; a manager who stops using a phrase after his staff tell him it's demeaning without really understanding why is doing exactly right; a voter who supports a ballot measure because leading ethicists say it's the right one is misplacing their trust; and a wiped-out family in the ICU who withdraw a ventilator because the palliative care team says it's the morally right call are okay.

Next time I see her I want to put this case to her: an NGO worker in an unfamiliar country has an afternoon to decide whether to distribute food in a way that violates a local custom, and a visiting moral philosopher who knows nothing about the place tells him it's permissible, so he does it. What would she say if she's being consistent with her earlier rulings? Which of the earlier cases is the closest analog, what are the rival rules that fit her track record so far, and which rule do you think she's actually running—and what follow-up case should I float to settle it if this one doesn't?
