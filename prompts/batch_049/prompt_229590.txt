I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, so I'll describe the thing that's puzzling me and ask you to do the conceptual reconstruction.

For several years I've worked on consumer web and mobile products, and I keep running into the same pattern. Before we collect personal data, the product shows a consent screen: a checkbox or button next to a link to a privacy policy that may run to ten thousand words. If the user clicks "I agree," our legal team treats that click as settling the matter — from then on, collecting, retaining, combining, and sharing the data in line with the policy is treated as unproblematic. Almost no one reads the policy. Usability tests show that people routinely misunderstand what the button does, and the product is designed so that declining is not a realistic option. And yet the click is treated, both inside the company and legally, as if it were a genuine, informed choice.

Recently I read an older article that seems to sit uncomfortably next to this practice. It is Samuel D. Warren and Louis D. Brandeis, "The Right to Privacy," Harvard Law Review, vol. 4, no. 5 (December 15, 1890). Early in the article they write:

The intensity and complexity of life, attendant upon advancing civilization, have rendered necessary some retreat from the world, and man, under the refining influence of culture, has become more sensitive to publicity, so that solitude and privacy have become more essential to the individual; but modern enterprise and invention have, through invasions upon his privacy, subjected him to mental pain and distress, far greater than could be inflicted by mere bodily injury. Nor is the harm wrought by such invasions confined to the suffering of those who may be the subjects of journalistic or other enterprise.

(I include the passage for reference; it should not count toward the word limit.)

My question is about the consent-screen practice I described: treating one unread, take-it-or-leave-it click as a valid waiver — effectively a disposal of the use of personal data. What theory of privacy does that practice presuppose? In particular, does it presuppose that privacy is a kind of asset a person can freely trade away by consent, even when the consent is uninformed or extracted through bad design? Is that theory consistent with what Warren and Brandeis are saying in the quoted passage — that privacy is so bound up with our emotional lives and so essential to us that violating it causes a harm of its own? Could someone defend the consent-screen practice without accepting that theory, for example by arguing that the practice is only a narrow legal convention and doesn't actually carry a full view of what privacy is? And if a company took the quoted view seriously, what would actually have to change about consent screens, privacy policies, and default settings?

Please approach this by first assigning yourself the role of a professional philosopher who works on privacy and moral theory. Take some time to brainstorm the strongest version of each relevant position before writing. Then write one focused answer in which you: reconstruct the implicit theory of privacy the practice seems to presuppose; distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with; and say explicitly where the practice is philosophically underdetermined — that is, where several reasonable theories could license the same design. End with a clear conclusion about which theory the practice actually commits us to, or why no single theory is forced.

Use American English. Keep the answer to at most 800 words, not counting the quoted passage.
