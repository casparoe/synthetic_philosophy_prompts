I keep running into the slogan "information never hurts," and it's starting to bug me because people seem to mean very different things by it. The thing that actually made me want to ask: my PM at work defended a whole extra round of user interviews by saying "more data can't hurt us, it can only improve the the decision." And then a friend told me that refusing to look at your own genetic test results is just irrational, because declining information is voluntarily making yourself dumber. I know there's a decision-theoretic result people call Good's theorem, which I've seen summarized as: the expected value of costless information is never negative. But I don't see how that gets me from "a free signal has non-negative expected value under a fixed prior and a fixed utility function" to "we should pay for more interviews" or "you should always open the envelope." Can you lay out, as a menu, the different precise claims that "information never hurts" could be making? For each, say whether it's the thing the theorem actually proves, a near-trivial restatement, a substantive and defensible claim, or just false. And then say which precise reading my PM's slogan-use would need in order to justify the extra work, and which reading would actually justify my friend's demand that I always open the envelope.
