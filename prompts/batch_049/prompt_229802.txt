I've been fiddling with some small social-choice cases and my gut verdicts won't cohere; I'd like you to find the principle behind them, if there is one. Two housemates, Priya and Morgan, and a salacious novel that's been sitting in the living room. The alternatives are P (Priya reads it), M (Morgan reads it), and N (neither reads it). Priya thinks the book is degrading and would rather nobody read it; but if someone has to read it, she'd rather read it herself than have Morgan read it. Morgan thinks it would do Priya good and would rather she read it; but if she won't, he'd rather read it himself than let it go unread.

My verdicts on the pairwise comparisons:

(1) M vs N: Morgan's own reading, his call — M wins, even though Priya prefers N. Fine.

(2) P vs M: both prefer P to M, so P wins on plain Pareto grounds. Fine.

(3) P vs N: Priya would rather N, Morgan would rather P (he says it'd be good for her). But what Priya reads is her sphere, so N wins; Morgan's preference doesn't count for much here. Fine.

But then (1) and (3) force M > N > P, so M > P, while (2) says P > M. I don't want to give up any of these; I'm least wiling to drop (1) or (3), and (2) feels definitional. Is there a principle that captures all three, and if not, which verdict should go, and what's the least costly repair? Also, what does your preferred principle predict for a nearby case: same two people and same preference structure, but instead of a book it's a vaccine, where 'reading it' becomes 'getting it' and the stakes are slightly higher and more public?
