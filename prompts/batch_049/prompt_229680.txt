I am a novelist, currently drafting a novel whose central figure is a benefactor who has spent years trying to live by a demanding, consequentialist ethic. In the course of the story she arrives at—and, through researching her position, I have come to find myself defending—a specific stance on the problem of consequentialist cluelessness. She holds that cluelessness, the impossibility of tracing the long-run consequences of her choices through their ramifying causal chains, is neither a refutation of consequentialism as a criterion of rightness nor a warrant for quietism or the retreat to simple rules. She regards it rather as a standing feature of decision-making under deep causal uncertainty, to be answered by a frank decision procedure: expected-value reasoning over the consequences that can be modeled, sensitivity analysis around those that cannot, and a deliberate form of moral hedging across plausible empirical and moral hypotheses.

I want the novel to do justice to the strength of the case against her, so I need to know what would genuinely count against this view. Please map its defeaters as you see them: first, the strongest arguments that cluelessness undermines consequentialism in particular rather than merely complicating every long-range practical deliberation; second, the cases, formal results, or developments—actual or constructed—that would most embarrass a committed defender of her position; and third, an honest verdict on how much her view is protected by the distinction between consequentialism as a criterion of rightness and the decision procedure that best copes with our ignorance. If that distinction, pressed hard enough, renders her position empirically empty or insulated from any possible refutation, I want that stated plainly, along with whether such insulation is a defect for a view of this kind. Finally, tell me which objections a sophisticated defender of her position would themselves concede have real force, and which familiar criticisms they would reasonably regard as misfiring.
