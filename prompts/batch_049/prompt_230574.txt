Two colleagues at my software job have been going back and forth for months on an email thread about whether phenomenal consciousness is real in the way we think. I don't have any formal philosophy background, but I got volunteered to put the writeup together. One is an illusionist: he holds that the whole idea of a private 'what-it's-like' with special qualitative feels is an introspective illusion — we have all this globally-available information (call it access consciousness) plus a lot of reactive/representational processing, and we systematically misdescribe those as having feel-y private properties, so once you've explained the access and processing there's nothing left over. The other is a phenomenal realist: she says no, the explanatory gap is real, no functional or representational story will capture why it feels like something, and calling it an illusion just relocates the problem — an illusion has to be experienced by someone, and that experiencing is exactly what's in dispute.

After months of this, they've agreed to coauthor an adversarial-collaboration document instead of continuing to talk past each other. Draft it for them. Include:
- a numbered list of substantive claims both would actually sign, phrased so neither is smuggling in their conclusion;
- a disagreements table: the precise claim each affirms or denies, worded so each side would accept there own row (not a caricature of the other side);
- the real cruxes — the sub-questions where changing your answer would force at least one of them to substantially revise their position;
- for each crux, a concrete case or piece of evidence both agree bears on it (blindsight, change blindness, inattentional blindness, the rubber-hand illusion, a perfect functional duplicate of you — whatever actually works), and what each side predicts that case would show, written down in advance.

If part of the fight is really about words, say which part and why. Don't just summarize the two views — produce the document both could put there names to.
