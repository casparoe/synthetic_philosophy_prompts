Question (20 points). Kripke's skeptical paradox about meaning turns on a thought experiment involving 'plus' and 'quus.' (a) Reconstruct the paradox and explain why it is supposed to generalize beyond arithmetic words to any expression with a rule-like use. (b) Explain what Kripke calls the "skeptical solution" and how it is meant to leave our ordinary meaning attributions intact. (c) Briefly evaluate one serious objection to the skeptical solution.

Student answer:

Kripke's paradox starts from the idea that all my actual uses of "plus" have involved numbers below 57. Define "quus" so that x quus y = x + y if both x and y are less than 57, and x quus y = 5 otherwise. Since I've never computed a sum with numbers that large, every past occasion on which I would have said "125" or whatever is consistent with my having meant quus rather than plus. Nothing about my past behavior settles the choice, and pointing to a mental rule or an algorithm doesn't help, because those can be reinterpreted too — the rule itself is just another sign. So if meaning is determined by facts about me, there is no fact of the matter as to whether I meant plus or quus. The point generalizes because any finite pattern of use can be made consistent with indefinitely many nonstandard continuations.

Kripke's solution is "skeptical" because he doesn't try to find some hidden fact that restores determinacy. Instead he replaces truth conditions with assertability conditions: we are licensed to say that someone means plus when their use agrees well enough with the community's use. The community supplies the standard that makes responses right or wrong, which is also why there's no private language — considered in isolation, a person has no one to count as agreeing or disagreeing with the rule. So on Kripke's picture, meaning reduces to the community's dispositions to approve and correct. A speaker means plus just in case it is true that the community would correct them for deviant responses, and this is what keeps meaning from being arbitrary.

I think the solution gives up too much. If there are no meaning facts, then any talk of correct and incorrect use is just a dressed-up way of saying that the group sides with some speakers and not others, so correctness talk becomes arbitrary. But communities can be wrong about what a word means — think of the way a majority can be mistaken about a term that a minority uses more carefully — so the community's approval can't be what correctness consists in.
