I've been working through the debate between counterfactual and interventionist accounts of causation, and I keep encountering the same master metaphor on the interventionist side: a causal system is a machine, and to call C a cause of E is to say that C is a control knob on E — if you could reach in and wiggle C while holding every other setting fixed, E would move accordingly. The metaphor is not decorative; it is used to argue that interventionist counterfactuals are the only relevant ones, and that a less restricted counterfactual picture overgenerates because it consults counterfactuals that don't correspond to any possible manipulation of the system. I want this metaphor audited. Give me the correspondence table — the knob, the machine, the fixed settings, the act of wiggling, the expected movement — and then the places the mapping breaks. Separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended argument from the ones that don't. I especially want to know what the machine picture quietly imports about causal structure: that causes must be manipulable, that systems decompose into independently adjustable parts, that someone is standing outside the system doing the handling, and that the notion of an intervention can be understood independently of the notion of a cause. Then say whether the interventionist conclusion survives once the metaphor is stripped down, or whether the restriction to interventionist counterfactuals was riding on the picture the whole time. Commit: I want your own verdict on how far the machine analogy can carry the interventionist position, and what, if anything, interventionism should replace it with. A table for the mapping is fine.
