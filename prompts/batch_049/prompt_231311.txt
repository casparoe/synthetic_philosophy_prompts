I spent thirty years in a lab, and the thing I got allergic to was people arguing whether a beaker was half full or half empty: same amount of liquid, same beaker, just two ways of putting it. Now that I'm retired and reading philosophy, I keep seeing that example hauled out to dismiss debates in ontology. Is a statue identical to the lump of clay it's made from? Are there really tables, or just particles arranged tablewise? The reply is always that these are merely verbal disputes, like the glass. I accepted that for a while, but now I suspect the analogy is carrying more weight than it can bear, and I want to know exactly where. Walk me through it: give me the correspondence table between the half-full/half-empty case and, say, the statue/lump or tables/particles-arranged-tablewise debate; then the places the analogy breaks; what it quietly imports about whether theres a fact of the matter underneath our choice of words; and whether the merely-verbal verdict survives once the disanalogies are on the table. And since I keep flip-flopping, tell me what would change your mind here — which consideration would flip your verdict the other way — and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
