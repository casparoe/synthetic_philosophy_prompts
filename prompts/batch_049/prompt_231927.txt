I teach an upper-level philosophy of science seminar, about 14 students (a mix of MA and PhD, plus two neuroscience students), and we have one 2-hour block. We're in the part of the course on mechanistic explanation, and I want a session where students actually feel how slippery it is to say whether two systems have the same mechanism and why that matters for anomaly detection (the problem of deciding whether a weird result is an anomaly for your mechanistic model or evidence that you're looking at a different mechanism entirely). I don't want to lecture first, and the phrase mechanistic anomaly detection shouldn't be said out loud until the debrief.

Please design a runnable classroom activity for this. I'd like it to work on a zero budget: printed cards, envelopes, paper, maybe ordinary classroom objects. It should include rules, roles, materials, a payoff scheme that doesn't use real money, a timeline for the 2 hours, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the conceptual issue. The heart of it should be a forced classification: teams have to judge, under limited information, whether two systems count as the same mechanism or different mechanisms, and whether some new result counts as an anomaly or just a different mechanism. Ideally there's a second phase where they recieve new information that changes the grain of description, so their first classification gets destabilized and they feel the pull of both verdicts.

The students have read general stuff on mechanisms (entities, activities, organization), but they don't know any specific literature on individuating mechanisms, and I want to keep it that way until after the activity. At the end, I want them to come away seeing that same mechanism is a modeling decision with stakes, not something you read off the system itself.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (e.g., teams treating identical outputs as automatically the same mechanism, builders accidentally leaking information, someone demanding a definition mid-game) and how to handle it. And be honest about which feature of real mechanistic anomaly detection the activity can't reproduce and where I'll have to supply it in the debrief.
