In my high school's philosophy and science discussion group, we have been arguing about how far consciousness extends across the animal kingdom. One student offered an argument from analogy: a fish caught on a hook thrashes, flees, and later rubs the injured area against surfaces, much as a human would do when in severe pain. Since the observable behavior is so similar, he claimed, the best explanation is that the fish has a conscious experience similar to pain; he concluded on that basis that fish are conscious and that consciousness probably extends to all vertebrates. Another student dismissed this as resting on a mere analogy, and a third objected that the analogy breaks exactly where it is needed because fish brains are organized very differently from ours and lack the layered cortex that is closely linked to felt pain in humans.

I want to write a more careful evaluation than any of these responses. Could you audit the analogy? Please provide a table of correspondences showing what is being mapped onto what: the stimulus, the observable behavior, the neural substrate, the evolutionary function, and the conscious state itself. Then distinguish the disanalogies that seriously weaken the inference to fish consciousness from those that are probably irrelevant. Finally, state what the analogy can and cannot support: whether the conclusion that fish are conscious survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether there is a better analogy or a stronger line of evidence the student should have used.
