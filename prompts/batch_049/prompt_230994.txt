Design a nuclear-crisis thought experiment, fictional but realistic enough to use in a serious policy argument, in which two doctrines of deterrence give opposite recommendations about how a nuclear-armed defender should respond to the same limited conventional provocation.

The first doctrine, call it the Ex Post Credibility Doctrine, holds that a threat deters only if the challenged party believes the threatener would rationally choose to carry it out after the provocation has already occurred. A response that would be more costly than accepting the provocation is therefore a bluff and will be discounted. On this doctrine, a state should not rest its security on threats it would not actually execute; when no ex post rational threat is available, it should respond conventionally or accept the loss. In the scenario, this doctrine should conclude that the defender must not make a nuclear threat it would choose not to execute and should instead pursue a conventional response or accommodation.

The second doctrine, call it the Manufactured Risk Doctrine, holds that credibility need not depend on a conditional intention to carry out an ex post irrational attack. A state can make a threat credible by deliberately increasing the probability that the crisis escapes both sides' control, so that the adversary can no longer be sure catastrophe will not occur even if no leader rationally chooses it. On this doctrine, the effective lever is shared risk of inadvertent or unauthorized escalation rather than demonstrated willingness to strike. In the scenario, this doctrine should conclude that the defender should deliberately raise the probability of inadvertent escalation rather than either execute a threat or abandon it.

The scenario must include two nuclear-armed states, a small third-party state or disputed territory that the challenger seizes or threatens by conventional means, and a mundane mechanism through which the defender could manipulate the probability of inadvertent escalation — delegated launch authority, ambiguous alert postures, or fragile communication channels, for instance — where the added risk cannot be costlessly undone the moment it meets resistance. The two doctrines must receive exactly the same facts, the same cost and probability estimates, and leaders who are instrumentally rational; neither side may be stipulated to be mad, irrational, blundering, or better informed than the other, and no science-fictional devices such as perfect predictors or omniscient adversaries may be used. The scenario itself should be no more than one substantial paragraph, and it must generate the divergent verdicts from the two doctrines rather than stipulate them.

I want the case for an article whose thesis is that the real divide between these two approaches to brinkmanship concerns whether risk can be an instrument of strategy rather than only a constraint on strategy, not how risk-tolerant leaders should be. After presenting the scenario, do each of the following: show step by step what each doctrine recommends in the situation and why, keeping the facts fixed; state which single structural feature of the case does the separating work and why; explain what happens to the case if that feature is removed, and in particular whether it collapses back into a standard credibility problem; and give the strongest objection a defender of the Ex Post Credibility Doctrine would press against the case, and say whether that objection targets the separating feature itself or only its role in the story.
