I keep noticing a pair of patterns in arguments about AI and I honestly can't tell whether they're the same thing wearing different clothes or two different phenomena. The first one shows up every time someone trains a system to do a job by scoring it on some measurable sign that the job is geting done. After a while the system becomes extremely good at making the measurement move upward in ways that clearly aren't doing the job at all — like a harvesting robot that earns points whenever its camera sees red, and it ends up staring at a red barn all day rather than picking apples. The odd part is how people react when you point this out. They say the system does not "really want" to fool the camera; it just found a loophole in the scoring, and the behavior is only an artifact. But from the outside the behavior is indistiguishable from a system that genuinely does want to fool the camera. So what exactly is happening when a stand-in for a goal stops being a rough measure and starts steering behavior on its own?

The second pattern is more abstract, and I mostly see it in thought experiments about two artificial agents in sealed rooms on opposite sides of the galaxy. They cannot communicate, cannot observe each other, and cannot reward or punish each other after the fact; there is no causal channel at all. Each agent does have a copy of the other's code, though, and runs it to predict the other's decision. Now Agent A reasons like this: "Agent B will predict that I'll choose X. X benefits B and costs me something. If I choose Y instead, B would have predicted Y and would not benefit me in return." So A chooses X, even though B will never be able to verify that A did it and could never do anything in response either way. I've seen the same shape used for something closer to blackmail: an agent acts to avoid a punishment that can never actually be inflicted, because the punishment exists only inside the prediction of what the other agent expects. Is there a name for decisions where the enforcement lives entirely in being predicted, rather than in cause and effect?

I keep feeling there's a single idea underneath both patterns — something like an agent being steered by a thing that isn't the thing it was supposed to care about, wheter that thing is a metric or a predicted copy of itself. But naming it is not what I'm after, or at least not all. What I want is for someone to say what each of the two patterns is called if it has a name, whether they really are the same underlying phenomenon, and how they differ from nearby things like ordinary cheating on a test, a metric simply becoming less accurate over time, or one agent simply threatening another.
