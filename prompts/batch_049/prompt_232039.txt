Okay, I've ended up defending a specific version of Parfit's hitchhiker: in the desert, the rational thing for the hitchhiker to do isn't just to precommit with some external device, but to deliberately make himself into the sort of person who pays the driver when they reach town, even though at that later moment paying has no payoff for him and he can get away with not paying. My claim is that the later payment is irrational judged from that moment alone, while the earlier act of self-transformation is rational judged from the desert, and this isn't a contradiction because the two choices are evaluated at different times.

What would count against that? I want a map of the view's exposure, not just the standard objections I've already heard. Which premise would have to fail, and what would the world or a cleaner version of the case have to look like for it to fail? The threats I half-see are: a rival could collapse the distinction and say it's all one extended action so there's only one rationality verdict; or say the later payment isn't really irrational because it's the act of the character he rationally chose; or say there's no coherent sense in which a disposition can be rationally chosen if the acts it issues in are irrational one by one. I want to know which of those are live, and what I'm missing. Sort the considerations by how decisively they'd count against me, seperate the ones that would only force a qualification from the ones that would kill the view, and say whether the position is insulated — and if it is, whether that's a fatal problem for a claim about rationality over time or just what you'll get with any conceptual claim. Also be honest about what a defender of the view should concede counts against it, not just what a critic would say.
