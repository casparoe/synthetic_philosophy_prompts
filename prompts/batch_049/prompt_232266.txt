Write a Petrarchan sonnet about the difference between indicative and counterfactual conditionals. Put it in a whodunit: a baron is dead and the only two suspects are the butler and the chauffeur. Use the octave to let "if the butler didn't kill him, the chauffeur did" and "if the butler hadn't killed him, the chauffeur would have" be treated as if they said the same thing. The volta should be the moment the reader sees they don't: the indicative is evaluated against what we already know (the baron is dead, one of the two did it), so clearing the butler lands the murder on the chauffeur; the counterfactual goes to the nearest worlds where the butler doesn't kill, even if those are worlds where the baron lives, so the chauffeur inherits nothing. Let the sestet work that out, and have the last two lines state exactly what separates the two. Make it accurate enough that a philosopher of language wouldn't wince, and good enough as a poem that someone who never heard the distinction would still feel it.
