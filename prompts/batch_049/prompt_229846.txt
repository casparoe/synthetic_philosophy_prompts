I'm TAing an undergrad epistemology course and we set a midterm question on the surprise exam paradox. I've pasted the question and one students answer below. Could you grade the answer for me? I'd like a score out of 10 plus the specific comments you'd write in the margins: where it gets things right, where it goes subtly wrong, and why. Please go point by point rather then giving a general impression, so I can justify the grade to the student if the prof asks.

Midterm question: In the surprise exam paradox, a teacher announces on Friday afternoon that there will be exactly one exam next week, Monday through Friday, and that the students will not know the day of the exam before the day it is given. A student argues by backward induction that no such exam can occur: it can't be Friday, because if no exam has happened by Thursday night they would know it's Friday; it can't be Thursday, because once Friday is ruled out, no exam by Wednesday night would let them know it's Thursday; and so on, eliminating every day. State precisely where, if anywhere, this backward induction goes wrong, and explain whether the teacher's announcement can be true.

Student's answer:
I think the induction is unsound, and it fails at the first step. The student says that if Monday through Thursday pass with no exam, then on Thursday night the class would know the exam is Friday. But that inference only goes through if the class still accepts the teacher's announcement—in particular, the part that says there will be an exam at all. Since the whole argument is trying to test that announcement, using it as a premiss in the first step basically begs the question. Once Friday is not eliminated, the rest of the chain collapses, so the announcement is consistent. It can certainly be true: if the teacher picks Wednesday, then on Tuesday night the students don't know it's Wednesday, and both parts of the announcement are satisfied. The fact that the exam felt like a surprise on Wednesday just confirms that. The deeper point is that 'there will be a surprise exam' is true by definition whenever the exam catches you off guard, since 'surprise' just means 'not known in advance.' The students' real mistake is trying to reason with a statement about their own future ignorance as though it were an ordinary premiss; a statement like that cannot be known by the people it is about. The teacher can know it, but the students can't.
