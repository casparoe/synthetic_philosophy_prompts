I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and a university philosophy department has asked me to serve as an outside examiner for an upper-level course in the philosophy of economics. I have a script in front of me and need to return a grade plus written comments precise enough that a curious non-specialist could see exactly what was earned and where the reasoning went wrong. The exam question and the student's answer are below.

Exam question: Milton Friedman famously holds, in what has come to be called the "F-twist," that a scientific model can be good even when its assumptions are unrealistic, and that significant theories tend to have assumptions that are wildly inaccurate as descriptions of reality. Reconstruct the reasoning behind these claims, using Friedman's own examples, and then assess whether this reasoning entitles him to say that predictive success is the only legitimate criterion for judging a scientific model.

Student answer: Friedman's argument is that a theory is not tested by lining up each of its assumptions against reality. The model is tested as a whole, by whether the predictions generated under its assumptions match observed outcomes. His examples are the expert billiards player, who plays as if she has computed the relevant equations of motion, and the leaves of a tree, which are arranged as if each leaf deliberately maximized its exposure to sunlight. Since nobody thinks the player is solving differential equations or the leaves are deliberating, these cases are meant to show that assumptions can be false as descriptions of actual psychology or agency while the model built on them still predicts well. This is why Friedman is standardly read as an instrumentalist about economic models: the realism of the assumptions does no work, and predictive accuracy does all the work. From this it follows that the more unrealistic a model's assumptions are, the better the model is, and that realism in assumptions can never count for anything in deciding whether to accept a model.
