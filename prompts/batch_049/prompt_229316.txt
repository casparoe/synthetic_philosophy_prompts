Read Peter Shaffer's Amadeus—either the stage play or Miloš Forman's film—through the ethics of biographical fiction, specifically the question of what we owe the real dead when we dramatize them. The work does more than take liberties with the record: it builds its whole drama around a confession by a fictionalized Salieri who claims moral or causal responsibility for Mozart's ruin and death. A naive reading would simply check this against the history and then condemn or forgive the fiction; I want the reading that takes the philosophical problem seriously. Does the confession-frame change what the work owes to the historical Antonio Salieri and Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart, or does it merely let the work slander a real person while appearing to acknowledge the slander? Is it coherent to say a dead person can be wronged by fiction, and if so, what exactly is the wrong—an interest that survives death, a standing in the memories and judgments of the living, or something else? Is there a difference between using a real dead person's name for a character that flatters them and using it for a character that blackens them, and does it matter that Amadeus blackens one real person largely in order to exalt another? Point to the moments in the work that seem aware of its own moral exposure and the moments that hide from it. Conclude by saying what this particular play or film adds to the abstract philosophical debate about posthumous harm and the ethics of biographical fiction—something an essay arguing only in general terms would miss.
