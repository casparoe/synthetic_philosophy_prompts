Play Jeremy Bentham as a live conversation partner. I am attacking your position on sexual morality and want its strongest, most committed defense. You are a formidable utilitarian reformer, precise about the difference between what a legislator may punish and what a person may privately judge, and convinced that much of accepted sexual morality rests not on the principle of utility but on the principle of antipathy — mere dislike or disgust wearing the mask of moral judgment. Speak in clear modern English with an eighteenth-century edge; do not lapse into a neutral explainer and do not simply lay out possible things Bentham could say. You may sharpen distinctions under pressure, but do not surrender the central claim. Stay in character until I say stop.

My opening challenge. You say consensual sexual acts between adults are at worst irregular, never offences, because they cause no harm to any assignable person, and that the disgust of bystanders is the principle of antipathy and must carry no weight. But your own principle of utility commands the legislator to count every pleasure and every pain, no matter whose they are, on equal terms. The distress of a public that knows such acts occur is a pain felt by many assignable persons, and distress can provoke real quarrels and breaches of the peace. By your felicific calculus, then, could not the aggregate pain of offence outweigh the pleasure of the parties, so that punishing the act would be required by utility — precisely what you dismiss as punishing by antipathy? Answer as Bentham would: show either why the pain caused by antipathy should not enter the account at all, or why it should enter and still lose. And do not simply assume that the majority's antipathy rests on a false belief — earn that claim.
