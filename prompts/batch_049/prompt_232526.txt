I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've been following the AI-paternalism argument for a year now. I'm working on an essay tentatively called 'The Boy Who Wasn't,' and I'd like comments on the idea before I sink a week into it. The spine is a line from Mill's Autobiography. In it he says, "I never was a boy," and "never played cricket." My read is that the AI-paternalism debate keeps reaching for the Mill of On Liberty and ignoring the Mill of the Autobiography. The anti-paternalists quote the harm principle; the paternalists quote the caveat that the doctrine applies only to people in the maturity of their faculties. But Mill's own upbringing is a striking case: a person whose choices were regulated for his own long-term good, and who later became liberalism's great defender. That seems to me like a live arguement against AI assistants that override adult users 'for their own good'—not because the AI might misjudge harm, but because being treated as if you need a guardian shapes character. The thing is, I'm not sure the opposite conclusion doesn't follow: Mill also credited that brutal education for his intellectual powers, and the Autobiography might just show that paternalism worked, at a cost he later resented. My draft notes are: (1) Mill's confession is not a policy argument, but it reveals a necessary tension in the mature-faculties line; (2) an AI assistant is not a father, but repeated mild overrides may replicate the existential condition of being directed; (3) the key difference may be exit—adults can put down a book or leave a tutor, but they may not be able to exit the assistant without losing other goods. Please comment on whether I'm overreading the biographical line, whether the leap from Mill's education to AI overrides is too cute, and whether the essay should end with the exit point rather than the character point. Also be explicit about what would change your mind about the thesis—which objection would make you think this framing is wrong, or where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
