I work on an applied machine-learning team, and one of our staff engineers, V, has a well-known discipline about the word *believes*. She will not let a design doc or model card say that a system believes something unless the system has earned the word, and over two years I have kept a list of her rulings because I want to reverse-engineer the rule behind them. A friend with a philosophy background says her standard is close to what philosophers call the intentional stance; V insists she just has an engineering standard.

1. **Thermostat.** A domestic thermostat with a single temperature reading and a setpoint that toggles the furnace. She says it does not believe the room is below the setpoint, even though it carries exactly that information; the reading is coupled straight to the actuator, with nothing in between.

2. **Palletizing arm.** A six-axis robot that moves to a taught position and halts when its force sensor trips. She says there is no belief here either; it is closer to a reflex than to a representation.

3. **Chess engine.** A standard engine doing deep search with a learned evaluation function, playing unfamiliar middlegame positions. She says it does believe the opposing king is unsafe, because the only manageable way to predict why it sacrifices material and opens lines is to attribute that kind of assessment to it; predicting its choices line by line from the weights is hopeless.

4. **Autonomous shuttle.** A shuttle whose prediction stack maintains an explicit probabilistic occupancy grid and assigns meaningful probability to a pedestrian stepping out from behind a stopped van. Its braking, lane offset, and request for remote supervision are all conditional on that estimate. She says it does believe a pedestrian may step out.

5. **Generative support model.** A language-model-based support bot with no memory between turns and no database in the examples. It confidently gives one answer and, when the user pushes back, just as confidently gives the opposite. She says it does not believe either answer; we are the ones supplying the coherence.

Now we have a new system, and I want to predict how V will rule. It is an imitation-learned driving policy: a single feedforward network, no recurrence, no explicit world model, no search, no retrievable memory, taking only the current frame plus raw vehicle state. In simulation and closed-course testing, the best way to predict when it brakes is to reason as if it expects a bus, currently occluded at an intersection, to pull into its lane: it brakes in exactly those situations and essentially never in matched situations without the bus. But we cannot point to any layer, unit, or embedding that encodes the proposition that the bus is about to merge; if the representation is in there, we cannot find it.

Does V say this policy believes the bus is about to merge, or does she withhold the word? I am more interested in the rule than the verdict. Please give me: (a) the candidate rules that fit her five rulings, stated precisely; (b) the verdict each candidate rule gives on this new driving policy, with the reasoning; (c) your best prediction of V's actual verdict and which of her earlier cases is the closest analog; and (d) a further case, real or constructed, that would cleanly separate the leading two candidate rules, with the verdict each would assign.
