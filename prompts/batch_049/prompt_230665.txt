I am a novelist, not a philosopher, and I keep noticing a structure in the way a certain kind of person changes his mind that I cannot name. I want to know whether it has an established name in decision theory or the philosophy of rationality, and whether it has been given a sharp definition; if it has not, I want a good working term and a precise account of its boundaries.

Here is the clearest instance. A character — call him Merrick — spends most of his weeks in one frame of mind, valuing quiet, solitary work above everything. But in another frame, reliably triggered by company and praise, he values visibility and influence. Each frame is stable while it lasts, and in each frame his choices are resolute and, to himself, unforced. The crucial thing is that there are only two alternatives in play, and the reversal happens across time, not within a single comparison: at no moment does he prefer one while acknowledging the other as better. In the solitary frame he will pay a facilitator to move him out of a public commitment; in the sociable frame he will pay her to move him back into one. The facilitator never lies, never pressures, and is careful to offer each transaction only in the frame that will welcome it; she charges a modest fee each time. After enough cycles Merrick is back where he began, except for the fees, and when the whole sequence is displayed to him at once he can repudiate no single step as false to the self who took it — yet he can see that all the steps together transferred money to someone who produced nothing.

A second instance: a writer alternates between a minimal, austere method and an expansive, collaborative one, and each conversion entails discarding one kind of equipment and buying back the other at a worse price. Again the reversals are regular, sincere while they last, and individually sensible. A friend who runs a used-goods business handles every exchange and loses nothing.

I keep returning to this because it is neither fickleness nor indifference. It is not weakness of will as I understand it: no step feels like capitulation to something the agent disavows. It is not a change of belief, since nothing about the way the world seems is updated. It is not an epiphany, because the old frame always returns. Between frames, the same person can see the cycle at a distance and would even pay to make both kinds of exchange impossible — but within either frame he would resent the very binding he bought, and the facilitator knows this.

My question has three parts. First, does this configuration — a changing but locally sincere agent whose predictable reversals can be harvested by a neutral third party, always at a net loss to the agent — have an established name, and how precisely has it been characterized? Second, which features are load-bearing? I suspect one can vary the trigger (emotional, social, mnemonic), the stakes (money, time, fidelity, equipment), and the third party's role, and I want to know what is essential to the pattern and what is incidental. Third, how does it differ from the nearby ideas of the weak-willed person, the inconstant or divided self, the person who changes their values after a genuine conversion, and the holder of seemingly inconsistent tastes? If the established term names something broader or narrower than my pattern, I would rather have the carving than the label.

You are an expert on decision theory and the philosophy of rationality, with a secondary interest in how theories of agency surface in fiction. Please think carefully. Before settling on an answer, generate a shortlist of candidate terms, check each against the description above, and say explicitly where each candidate fits and where it fails. I will not be satisfied with a bare name; I need to know exactly what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges are.
