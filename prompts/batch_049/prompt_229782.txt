I’m trying to work out what I think about the timing of conscious experience. I’ve been reading William James’s *The Principles of Psychology*, especially the chapter where he defends the idea that the directly experienced present is not a single instant but a short stretch that includes the just-past and the just-about-to-be. That idea seems right to me, but I haven’t been able to translate it into a rule for cases where the brain seems to edit experience after the fact.

Here are my verdicts on some cases:

1. In the color-phi experiment, two spots (red then green) flash in different positions about 60 milliseconds apart. I think there is a real conscious experience of a single moving spot, and I also think the spot seems to change color around the middle of its path. But I cannot tell whether I experienced the green halfway through at the time, or whether the brain waited until the green flash arrived and then inserted the color into an already-ending experience. I suspect the difference is not a difference in how anything ever seems to me, but I still want it to be a real difference in experience.

2. A person briefly shown six colored letters later says she saw all six, but immediate testing shows she can report only four. I think this later report is not evidence that she actually saw all six: it is a constructed memory, and there is no true yes/no answer about the initial experience.

3. In backward masking, a shape followed very quickly by a mask is reported as unseen. I think the shape may have existed within the James-style short present for a tiny interval, even though it was never reportable, so it was experienced but not available for later thought.

4. In change blindness, when attention is cued to an already-present difference, the difference suddenly seems to appear. I don’t think it had been consciously present all along; the new experience is assembled then, though the person may reasonably say “I see it now.”

5. In blindsight, a person can guess where an object is while saying he sees nothing. I think that is not conscious experience in any ordinary sense; the accuracy belongs to a nonconscious process.

Is there a principle that fits these? I want it to say how much temporal revision is allowed before we stop saying something was actually experienced, and under what conditions the Orwellian story (experience happens, then memory is revised) is better than the Stalinesque story (experience is delayed until revision is done), and whether it makes sense to demand the distinction at all. What does the principle say about the baseball batter who reports seeing the ball hit the bat, even though he started swinging before the ball arrived? And which of my verdicts would such a principle overturn?
