I am a novelist, and for the past year I have been compiling small scenes — some from trial transcripts, hospital notes and planning meetings I have read for a novel, some from ordinary life — in which one person is believed and another is not. I have strong and apparently inconsistent judgements about these scenes, and I would like you to act as a professional philosopher working in social epistemology and help me find the principle behind them. Please think carefully, begin by sorting the cases and testing candidate principles against them, and do not settle for a slogan; I want precision and an honest statement of what will not fit.

Here are the cases and my verdicts. (1) A forensic accountant who is being paid by the defendant's commercial rival gives evidence in a fraud trial, and the court directs the jury to give it little weight; I find that wholly sensible. (2) A man with advanced dementia gives confused, confabulated testimony about a road accident and the police set it aside; I see no wrong in that. (3) A gallery owner dismisses a young woman painter's account of her own canvases as the enthusiasm of a collector's wife without looking at the work or checking her facts; I am certain something unfair is being done to her. (4) A patient reports severe post-operative pain, but the ward doctor assumes he is exaggerating in order to obtain opioids, largely because he is a young Black man and the ward has a standing assumption that patients of his sort exaggerate pain; no analgesia is given, and when the pain proves to be a surgical complication, the notes describe him as a poor historian. That strikes me as a serious wrong and not merely a clinical error. (5) A man with two old convictions for shoplifting reports that two officers beat him in a car park; the unit reviewing his complaint discounts it almost entirely on the strength of that record and does not pull the CCTV that would settle the matter. Here I am genuinely torn: his record seems to me relevant, but refusing to look at available evidence because of it also seems to insult him. (6) A science writer with a stammer gives a careful and well-evidenced account of a flood model to a town council; the chair later says he convinced no one because he could not get the words out, while a fluent oil-company consultant who made several factual errors was judged credible. I regard the chair's response as a clear injustice, though it does not feel exactly like (3) or (4). (7) An uncle of mine, a serial fabulist who has told the same invented story about meeting the Queen for forty years, reports witnessing a burglary; I would give his word almost no weight, and I do not believe I wrong him by doing so.

My working hunch is that a hearer wrongs a speaker only when the hearer lowers the speaker's credibility on account of the social group the speaker belongs to, and not when the discount tracks something about the particular individual — their record, their interests, or their present condition. But I can already see the strain: that hunch seems to excuse the chair in (6) by treating the man's stammer as a present condition of his, and it also seems to excuse the investigators in (5) because they used the man's own record; yet I am sure about (6) and uneasy about (5). Please repair this principle or replace it. I want the final principle stated precisely, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it; I want to know what it predicts about further cases I have not listed, such as a defendant who is doubted because she testifies through an interpreter, or a witness who is believed too readily because she is a titled surgeon; and I want an honest accounting of which of my original verdicts, if any, the principle cannot accommodate and how I should revise them. Please write your answer in British English.
