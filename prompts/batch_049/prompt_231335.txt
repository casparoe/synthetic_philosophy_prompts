I have been teaching the debate over Popper's critique of historicism for years, and it has come to feel like a dispute that persists without actually moving. Popper's side appears to argue that there can be no laws of historical development, because history is a unique, non-repeatable sequence: at best we observe trends, and a trend is not a law. The historicist side replies that this merely stipulates a natural-scientific sense of "law" and thereby misses what historicists actually claim, namely that history exhibits developmental regularities—stages, sequences, structural transformations—that deserve the name of law even if they do not support the kind of exact prediction physics offers. Each side then accuses the other of changing the subject, and the controversy remains exactly where it was. I want your diagnosis of the disagreement itself, not a verdict about who is right. Is this in the end a verbal dispute about the word "law"? A substantive disagreement about whether social and historical systems are open in a way that excludes lawlike regularities? A disagreement about what counts as evidence or refutation? Or are the two sides answering different questions under one heading—say, one about prediction and control, the other about making large-scale historical change intelligible? Support the diagnosis with what each side actually says, and then tell me what would count as progress given that diagnosis, or argue that the impasse is structural and no progress should be expected.
