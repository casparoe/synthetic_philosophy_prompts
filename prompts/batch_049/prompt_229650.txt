After a long argument with a friend about whether video-game characters can be wronged, I find myself with a set of considered judgments I cannot unify, and I would like you to extract the principle behind them, if there is one — or tell me which of my judgments the most defensible principle forces me to abandon.

Here are the cases and how I feel about them:

1. The sandbox rampage. In a sandbox crime game, I sometimes drive onto a crowded sidewalk and shoot pedestrians for a minute or two before reloading. I do not think this is morally wrong, or at least not wrong in any way that concerns the pedestrians. They are shallow, interchangeable, nameless objects with no memory and no interiority.

2. The pleading victim. A story-driven game contains an innocent character who sobs and begs for their life if the player chooses to torture them. Someone I know seeks this scene out because they enjoy the suffering itself. I think there is something morally wrong with that, even though no actual person is harmed.

3. The companion you sell. In a role-playing game, a companion character travels with you for many hours, has a detailed backstory, recalls your shared history, and repeatedly protects your character. The game allows you to sell this companion into slavery for a substantial sum. I could not do it, and I would think badly of someone who did it without hesitation — and not merely because it is poor strategy or makes the playthrough worse.

4. The novelist's child. A serious novel depicts a child's suffering at length. I do not think the author wronged the child, and I do not think the author has a moral reason to make the child's life go better within the fiction. At most, I might question the point of the passage.

5. The persistent pet. A digital pet persists across sessions, learns its owner's routines, shows distress when treated badly, and permanently dies if neglected long enough. I have a real reason — not just a passing preference — to feed it rather than let it die, even though I am fairly confident it is not sentient.

6. The multiplayer betrayal. I promise a friend's character safe passage in an online game, then betray the character to win. That is wrong, but the wrong is against my friend, not against the character.

7. The copy farm. A technician runs thousands of copies of a very simple simulated creature that is trained only to avoid a pain-bearing stimulus; the technician deletes fifty copies a day to test changes. I do not think the technician owes anything to the copies.

My friend's view is that these cases reduce to a simple principle: the characters themselves never matter, the only genuine wrongs are to actual people — other players, developers, oneself — and everything else is a matter of taste, or at most a matter of what enjoying certain fictions reveals about a person's character. That handles 4, 6, and 7 cleanly, and it can recast 2 as a defect of character rather than a wrong done to the victim. But it seems to misdescribe 3 and 5: the companion and the pet feel like the very things I would be betraying. And I cannot see how, on my friend's view, the rampage in 1 and the torture in 2 can differ so sharply when neither has an actual victim.

Is there a coherent account that captures most of these verdicts, and which ones does it require me to give up? Please state the principle precisely, say which case or cases motivate each clause, and then apply it to cases I have not listed — for example: a text-based character with no graphics but decades of generated memories; a character that passes current behavioral tests for sentience and pleads to be spared in real time; and watching someone else carry out the torture in 2 rather than doing it oneself. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
