I'm drafting an essay on the hinge of history hypothesis — the claim that the present era is a uniquely consequential moment for the long-run future of civilization, and not merely in the sense in which every generation feels its own moment to be pivotal. I would like your comments on the argument's structure: where it moves too quickly, where a reader is most likely to resist, and whether the third section's analogy to earlier would-be hinges does the work I think it does. My working notes are below.

The essay will open by laying out two positions. The proponent says: we may be building artificial minds or other technologies that could lock in good or bad outcomes for the rest of human history, so our actions now have a disproportionate share of all future value riding on them. The skeptic says: every era has produced people who believed their own moment was the decisive one, and most of them were wrong; the base rate of such self-assessments is poor, so the present claim needs extraordinary evidence. The essay should present these as two incompatible readings of the same evidence rather than as a debate one side is obviously losing.

My central move is to distinguish two senses of "hinge." A hinge in the dramatic sense is a moment that feels decisive to those living through it. A hinge in the structural sense is a moment at which a small difference in what agents do makes a large, persistent difference to later states of the world. My argument is that the familiar skeptical appeal to past eras confuses these two. That the apocalyptic preachers of earlier centuries were wrong about their dramas tells us little about the structural question, because few or no earlier epochs had comparable feedback loops: a technology that could improve its own design, or a single accident that could foreclose future choices rather than merely delay them.

The strongest form of the skeptic's reply, as I see it, is not the base-rate point but a symmetry point. The same reasoning that tells us our century is structurally special could be run by observers in many future centuries; if the hinge lasts longer than a generation, then most people who correctly identify themselves as living in the hinge are not us. Alternatively, the skeptic can point out that the centuries containing the printing press or the industrial revolution were structurally pivotal too, and those transitions lasted longer than any single human life. The essay needs to answer this cleanly rather than absorbing it as a concession.

The claim I ultimately want to defend is that our moment is distinctive not because it is the most consequential of all moments, but because it may be the first in which the species can deliberately choose among several stable long-run trajectories rather than drifting into one. The hinge, on this view, is not the most important century among centuries; it is the first century that could know it is a hinge and act on that knowledge. That makes the epistemic situation different in kind from that of every previous era that took itself to be the pivot of history.

I would especially welcome comments on four points: whether section two's distinction is carrying too much weight; whether the concession in section three undercuts section two; whether the appeal to a first known hinge in the final section is a real asymmetry or only a motivating thought; and whether the essay overpromises given that I am deliberately not making empirical forecasts. I do not need prose polishing; I need a structural critique.
