I am a staff journalist at a magazine that covers science and ideas. Two or three times a month I write up an empirical finding—an experiment, an observational study, a natural experiment—in which researchers offer an explanation for a pattern in their data. Increasingly I find that the same data arrive with two or more rival explanations attached, each internally coherent and each able to account for the observed numbers. My difficulty is not deciding which explanation is true; it is knowing what my copy is entitled to say.

I would like a reusable procedure I can run on each story, in a few hours, without a statistician and without specialized modeling software. It should tell me when I may write that the evidence supports or favors an explanation, when I may write only that an explanation is consistent with the evidence, and when I must write that the available evidence does not discriminate between the rival explanations. Ground the procedure in what the philosophy of science actually supports about the underdetermination of theory by evidence: that a finite body of data is in general compatible with more than one incompatible explanation, so what advances a claim is whether the evidence discriminates among the live alternatives rather than merely fitting one of them.

Please give the procedure as a checklist or flowchart in words, with the questions in the order I should ask them. For each question, say what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with weak language, and where it permits stronger language. Mark plainly the steps at which a judgment call is required and cannot be eliminated by the procedure, and note the points where a working journalist will predictably go wrong, such as confusing a good story with a discriminating test or treating an explanation's ability to fit the data as evidence for it.

Then run the procedure on the following three situations from my beat:

1. A psychology paper reports that twenty minutes in a park improved working-memory scores in forty undergraduates relative to their own scores from that morning, and the authors claim the natural setting causes a cognitive boost. The same before-after improvement would also be produced by practice effects, regression toward the mean, or simply taking a break, and the study has no comparison condition.

2. Two groups of economists analyze the same administrative panel data on declining labor-force participation among prime-age men. One group says the cause is the automation of routine jobs; the other says the cause is an expansion of disability-benefit eligibility. The broad trends fit both accounts. One group's model predicts a sharper participation decline in high-routine occupations; the other's predicts a sharper decline in counties where benefits became easier to obtain. The paper I am covering reports only the aggregate trends.

3. A randomized trial assigns participants either to a working-memory training program or to an active control game matched for time, contact, and expectation. The training group improves more than the control group on one transfer test that the program explicitly targets, and this is the only one of several measured outcomes on which the groups differ.

At least one of these should produce an answer I will not like, whether because it tells me to drop a causal claim my editors and readers find appealing or because it leaves me unable to choose between the competing explanations. Say plainly which case that is and why. Finally, state what the procedure cannot do: the situations where, even after running it, the honest conclusion is that the evidence underdetermines the choice among explanations, and no amount of journalistic care will remove that.
