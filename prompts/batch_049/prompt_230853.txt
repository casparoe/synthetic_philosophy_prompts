I work as a software architect specializing in identity and access management, and I think natively in the vocabulary of role-based access control: principals, roles, permissions, role hierarchies, sessions with role activation, separation of duties, and mutually exclusive role constraints. I know Confucian role ethics only in broad outline, as the view that a person is constituted by the roles they live out — child, sibling, parent, spouse, friend, ruler, subject — and that the virtues are excellences internal to those relationships rather than traits of an autonomous self standing behind them. The roles interlock: being a father is already a norm-governed relation to a child, and what a role asks of me shifts with the other party and the context. Key terms, as I understand them, are li (roughly, role-governed propriety or performed ritual) and ren (roughly, achieved personhood through cultivating one's roles well), and on this view there is no unencumbered self left over once the roles are stripped away.

Please restate that view, as faithfully as you can, in my RBAC vocabulary. Say what maps cleanly (for instance, contextual role activation as a session, or inherited permissions as a role hierarchy), what maps only with distortion, and what has no expression at all. Then give me a residue report: is the gap mainly an artifact of RBAC's own assumptions — above all, that a principal is logically prior to, and separable from, the roles assigned to it — or does it mark something the Confucian view is claiming that access-control language cannot say? Keep the translation honest: do not quietly turn the person into a principal and call that a translation, and do not flatten reciprocal obligations between role-bearers into permissions over resources. Conclude by saying what RBAC is missing and whether the Confucian view emerges from the translation clearer or more opaque than it went in.
