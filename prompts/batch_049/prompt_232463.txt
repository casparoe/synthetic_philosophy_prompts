I'm drafting a novel where two of my characters have been arguing for a year about tipping, queue-jumping and jaywalking, and I want to get their argument right rather than have them talk past each other. Marla holds what you could call the local-deference view: everyday conventions do create real obligations, so if a place has a custom of tipping or queueing or not crossing against the light, you owe it to the people around you to follow it, and visitors have an especially strong reason to defer to local ways. Idris holds the harm-based view: a convention only matters morally insofar as breaking it would actually harm, deceive, or disrespect someone, and conventions alone are just social pressure—so a visitor who never agreed to a local custom is often not bound by it.

I want the document they would produce if a mediator locked them in an adversarial collaboration and made them write a joint statement. Give me: a numbered list of claims both can sign, kept non-vacuous; then the exact residual disagreement, worded so each of them would accept the description of their own side; then the cruxes, the sub-questions that would actually move at least one of them if settled. For each crux, include a concrete case or observation both agree bears on it, and what each of them predicts in advance. I especially care about the messy cases: a convention that partly exists because of a bad background (like tipping functioning as wage replacement), a local custom a visitor could'nt reasonably have known about, and a convention that isnt morally neutral (say a queue that quietly excludes certain people). If part of their fight turns out to be just verbal, say which part and why.
