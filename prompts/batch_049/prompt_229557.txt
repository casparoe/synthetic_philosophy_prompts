Question: A decision theorist argues as follows: 'Faced with a long sequence of independent, identical favorable gambles, an agent who maximizes expected wealth will wager the entire bankroll each round, courting ruin whenever the stake can be lost; an agent who instead maximizes expected log wealth — the Kelly rule — maximizes the long-run growth rate and will, with probability 1, overtake any fixed-fraction strategy in the long run. This shows that expected-utility maximization is the wrong rule for repeated gambles. The correct rule is geometric rationality: choose the policy that maximizes the long-run growth rate, i.e., bet Kelly.' Evaluate this argument. In particular: (i) assess the mathematical convergence claim; (ii) say whether, and how, a convergence fact can support a normative conclusion about how any agent should bet; and (iii) clarify the relation between maximizing expected log wealth and standard expected-utility maximization with logarithmic utility.

Student's answer: The core mathematical point is correct. For a sequence of independent favorable bets, an expected-wealth maximizer is effectively forced to wager the entire bankroll each round, which courts ruin, whereas maximizing expected log wealth picks the unique fraction that maximizes the asymptotic growth rate; it is a theorem that this Kelly strategy almost surely overtakes any fixed-fraction competitor in the long run. So the argument does show that ordinary expected-utility reasoning fails for repeated gambles. Of course, since log is a concave utility function, maximizing expected log wealth is formally identical to expected-utility maximization with logarithmic utility, but this only means Kelly is the special case of expected-utility theory that happens to be right for repeated gambles. The convergence theorem is decisive: any agent, whatever their risk preferences, should follow Kelly, and someone who deviates because they are more risk-averse than log utility is simply making a mistake about how repeated gambles actually behave. The usual objections — that Kelly betting is volatile, or that some agents are more risk-averse — all reduce to confusing short-run discomfort with long-run optimality.
