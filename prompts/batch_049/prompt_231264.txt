Two of my friends got into it at dinner last weekend, and I've been turning it over since. I've been reading about DNA data storage for work (we're looking at using synthetic DNA for archival backup, which is wild), and I made the mistake of saying something like "DNA is basically a code for an organism." My friend Dana, who's a molecular bio postdoc, jumped in with "not basically—literally." Our other friend Marcus, a software engineer with a philosophy hobby, said it's a metaphor, and one that misleads more than it helps. I mostly just listened, but they kept going:

Dana: "Its not a metaphor. We call it the genetic code for a reason. You have four bases, you read them in triples, each triplet specifies an amino acid. There's a lookup table, with degeneracy and stop signals. Change one base and you can change one amino acid, like flipping a bit changes an instruction. It's a discrete, combinatorial symbol system."

Marcus: "A symbol system implies symbols, and symbols imply something that reads them. In the cell there's no reader. The codon-to-amino-acid 'table' is implemented by tRNA anticodons and aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases: anticodons base-pair with codons, synthetases charge the tRNAs. That's just molecular recognition—hydrogen bonding, shape matching, kinetics. When a key fits a lock, the lock isn't interpreting the key."

Dana: "So the ribosome and the tRNAs aren't readers? The whole translation machinery reads the transcript. It doesn't have to be conscious. A barcode scanner isn't conscious; it still reads a code."

Marcus: "A barcode scanner has an engineer who assigned meanings to its outputs. The cell has no assignment relation; the 'mapping' is just causal dependence. If code just means one physical thing causally constrains another, then sure, DNA is a code—and so is a rock constraining where water flows around it. You've drained the word of the information sense you wanted."

Dana: "Not true. Shannon information is exactly about one physical thing constraining another, and you can measure DNA's information content in bits. People literally write files into DNA and read them back. That's data storage—information—not just causation."

Marcus: "Shannon information is a formal thing; a coin flip carries it, a cloud carries it; it doesn't mean anything. When you store a file in DNA, humans are the interpreters—you've built a system with an intended decoding scheme. Natural DNA didn't get its codon table assigned by anyone. And the synthetic case is you making DNA into a code; the wild case is heredity and metabolism, which is richer than the protein-coding fraction anyway. Most of the genome doesn't code for protein—enhancers, promoters, structural stuff, repeat elements. Calling the whole molecule 'a code' lets a small fraction of it stand for the whole."

Dana: "Okay, but 'coding DNA' is a technical term; nobody claims every base encodes a protein. The coding regions do encode proteins, and that's literally true—there's a stored representation that gets decoded through transcription and translation. And people have literally recoded an organism's genome and it still lives. You can edit the code. That's not a metaphor."

Marcus: "Recoded organisms are, again, our convention imposed on chemistry. The natural case is a frozen accident of evolution, not a convention. I don't deny the mapping exists; I deny it's semantic. It's a metaphor because it borrows the idea of a message standing for something beyond itself, and natural DNA stands for nothing—it's one stage in a causal chain."

I had to leave before they resolved it. Can you go through it point by point and say who's right about which specific claim, and what both of them are missing? Also, when you answer, please separate the parts that are settled (the codon table, the translation machinery, what Shannon information is) from the parts where people who study this for a living genuinely disagree, and don't present one side of the philosophical debate as if it were the scientific consensus.
