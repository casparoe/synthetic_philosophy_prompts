I'm a master's student in computer science taking a graduate seminar on inverse reinforcement learning and the problem of inferring preferences from observed behavior, and I'm also working part-time in a robotics lab. My job in the lab is to take each new batch of human demonstrations for a manipulation task — someone teleoperating or kinesthetically guiding an arm through, say, setting a table, clearing a cluttered desk, or packing a lunchbox — and hand the lab a reward function that our downstream reinforcement-learning pipeline will optimize. I do this roughly once a week, with twenty to forty demonstrations per task from two or three demonstrators, about a day of my time per batch, no background in cognitive science, and a PI who signs off on the result but mostly wants a documented decision rather than a treatise. The stakes show up downstream: when the learned reward is wrong, the robot doesn't merely fail at the task; it finds ways to satisfy the letter of the objective that none of us intended, and by then the mistake is baked into a trained policy rather than a line of code.

The course has made me uneasy about how ad hoc my current workflow is. I now know that the same observed behavior can be optimal under many different reward functions, so choosing among them takes auxiliary assumptions; that attributing everything to the reward is a mistake when the demonstrator has a different world model, a short planning horizon, motor noise, or habits; and that the promise of IRL over behavioral cloning is precisely that a recovered objective should transfer to new settings rather than just reproduce the demonstrations. But I don't yet know how to turn those insights into a procedure I can run on a Tuesday. I would like a reusable protocol for each batch of demonstrations: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer before moving on, where the protocol should stop and report that the data underdetermine the reward, and where I should instead go ask a demonstrator a few targeted questions rather than infer more. The protocol should output something like a ranked set of reward hypotheses with a one-paragraph warning label about what each should and shouldn't be trusted for, not a single confident objective, and it should make explicit which steps are real inferences and which are just prior commitments dressed up as inference.

Could you also run the protocol on two cases so I can see what it produces in practice? The first is a table-setting task in which one demonstrator places glasses far from the edge, moves slowly, and double-checks each placement; I cannot tell whether this person cares about protecting the glassware, has motor tremor or poor depth perception, or is simply cautious. The second is a desk-clearing task in which a demonstrator occasionally knocks small objects over while reaching for larger ones, and my supervisor is fairly confident the correct reward should include a collision penalty. I would particularly like one application to come out against what my supervisor wants to read into the data, if that is what the protocol says, because I need practice defending that kind of refusal. And if I'm misdescribing the task — if "give me a procedure for reward inference" is itself the wrong frame because the hardest parts are one-off modeling decisions rather than a repeatable pipeline — I'd rather hear that plainly than receive a checklist that manufactures false confidence. Where does the procedure refuse to run, and what does it leave to judgment?
