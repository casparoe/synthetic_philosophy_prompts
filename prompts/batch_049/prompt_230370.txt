I retired a few years ago after a long career in physical chemistry, and I have been spending my retirement reading philosophy and literature with the same patience I used to give to a difficult experiment. I have been working through Dostoevsky’s The Brothers Karamazov, and the Grand Inquisitor chapter has stayed with me more than anything else in the book. The Inquisitor’s case against Christ is not, as I first expected, that truth matters more than happiness, or that people ought to be free even if it makes them miserable. It is instead something stranger: that Christ overestimated what ordinary human beings can bear, and that real love therefore requires taking freedom away and replacing it with miracle, mystery, and authority. I would like a poem that makes this argument felt, not merely summarized, and that lets the reader occupy the Inquisitor’s position long enough to see why he thinks of himself as compassionate rather than cruel.

Please write a villanelle in the voice of the Grand Inquisitor, spoken to Christ in the prison cell in Seville. The two refrains should be variants on the poles of his justification — something like, but not exactly, “we have made them happy” and “you would not take the bread” — and they should do structural work: in the first tercets they should read as defiant accusation, by the middle of the poem they should modulate into something more sorrowful or confessional, and by the closing quatrain they should turn against the speaker so the reader sees that he is also describing himself. The key idea I want rendered accurately, and not merely name-dropped, is that the Inquisitor’s position is a rival account of love: he believes freedom is a burden most people cannot bear, and that genuine compassion means removing it. A philosopher of religion should recognize the view, even if they disagree with it.

If you think this request rests on a confused reading of the chapter — for instance, if the Inquisitor’s voice cannot plausibly carry the shift I am asking for, or if the villanelle’s repetitions fight against a progressive self-exposure — please say so plainly and propose the nearest revision rather than forcing it. After the poem, include a single section titled “Notes on the choices,” roughly 200–300 words, explaining what the poem kept accurate, what it compressed or took liberties with, and where the form shaped the interpretation.
