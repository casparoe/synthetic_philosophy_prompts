I'm writing a position paper for a high school civics course about how a country should teach its contested history, and I want a consistency check on a set of views I currently hold. My thinking is shaped by the American argument over whether to center U.S. history on 1619 or on 1776, and by the Japan–Korea disputes about how textbooks describe the colonial past. Here is the set:

(1) The aim of school history is to discover and explain what happened, not to make students proud or ashamed.
(2) Students reach that aim by working through serious competing interpretations of disputed periods, rather than by receiving one approved narrative.
(3) A school system may nonetheless aim at civic formation, so students should leave with a usable shared story of their country, not merely a pile of open questions.
(4) It is wrong to remove well-supported claims about a nation's own wrongs, such as slavery or colonial violence, simply because those claims make the nation look bad or because a foreign government objects to how they are phrased.
(5) But a curriculum need not give equal time, or even a serious hearing, to claims that professional historians regard as settled and false, such as Holocaust denial or ancient-aliens claims.

Please go through these in pairs and say which commitments genuinely pull against each other and which only seem to. Rank the genuine tensions by severity, specify the least costly repair for each and what it sacrifices, and identify the single revision that would do the most to make the whole set coherent.
