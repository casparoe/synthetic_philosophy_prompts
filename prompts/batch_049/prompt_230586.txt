Last night two friends of mine got into an argument I couldn't settle, and I'm still not sure who had the better of it. Our friend Sam had just said, after the heat came back on, that he wasn't sure whether he was still cold: "I think I can still feel it, but it might just be that I expect to." Maya immediately said that sentence can't be literally true. If he still felt cold, then he would know he felt cold. You don't infer your own coldness from evidence — the feeling just presents itself to you. So the only thing Sam could be uncertain about is the word "cold," not the feeling itself. Dan said that's too fast. Feelings fade gradually, and "cold" has a fuzzy boundary. You can know exactly what your body feels like — the temperature, the shivering — and still genuinely not know whether it adds up to feeling cold right now. Sam was just in that borderline.

Maya replied that Dan was confusing the feeling with the label. If Sam had the exact feeling, then he had it, full stop. Whether the word "cold" applies is a question about English, not about his own state. Dan said no: the feeling isn't something Sam can hold still and inspect against a clean category. He's comparing a shifting sensation to a vaguely remembered notion of what "cold" is, and that comparison can genuinely break down. People do discover they were colder than they realized.

Maya came back that those are cases of failing to attend, not failing to know. Once you attend to a feeling, you know it. Dan then asked her to imagine Sam being warmed up very slowly. At noon he's clearly cold; by six he's clearly not cold; and he's asked every minute. If Maya is right, then at every minute where he's still cold, he knows he's cold. But knowing isn't supposed to be this precarious: if he knows he's cold at 3:00, and at 3:01 his feeling has changed only imperceptibly, then his 3:00 belief couldn't have been knowledge unless he was still cold at 3:01. Apply your principle again and he's still cold at 3:02, and so on — all the way to six. Since he clearly isn't cold at six, your principle has to go.

Maya said that turns the arguement on itself: all it shows is that there is no sharp moment of getting warmer, so there's no moment where Sam is cold but fails to know. Dan said that won't save her. Either there's a fact of the matter about whether Sam feels cold at each minute, in which case some minutes are ones where he's cold but can't know which, or there isn't, in which case she's given up on the idea that the feeling is somehow immediately present to him. Neither option fits her starting claim.

They went around like this for ages. Can you go move by move and say who is right about which specific point, where each argument breaks down, and who has the better overall position?
