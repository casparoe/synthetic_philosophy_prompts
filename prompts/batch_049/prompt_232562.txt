Please compare Good's theorem on the value of information with the view that a rational agent can sometimes be better off not knowing. Good's theorem says, roughly, that for an expected-utility maximizer with coherent priors, the expected value of a costless observation is never negative, since the agent may always disregard the observation. Yet one can describe cases—a defendant who knows they will be questioned under oath, a patient deciding whether to take a genetic test whose result cannot be unlearned—where remaining ignorant seems practically rational. I want you to distinguish two ways of reconciling such cases with the theorem. On the first approach, the apparent value of ignorance lies in preferences over one's own beliefs or credences, not in preferences over the world, and the theorem is simply silent about such belief-directed preferences. On the second approach, the cases change the decision problem itself: they introduce strategic constraints, external costs, or later temptations, so that the conditions of the theorem are not met. Which of these two approaches does more philosophical work while preserving the theorem's central lesson? Justify your answer.
