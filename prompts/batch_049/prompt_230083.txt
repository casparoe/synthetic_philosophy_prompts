For a philosophy-and-literature unit on the ethics of impunity, I am pairing the ring of Gyges story from Book II of Plato's Republic with H. G. Wells's The Invisible Man (the 1897 novella, not Ellison's novel). The obvious way to read Wells is as a naturalistic ring of Gyges: give an ambitious man the power to act unobserved and whatever justice he had will drop away. The protagonist begins as a young scientist, makes himself invisible, and moves quickly from petty theft to terrorizing the village where he takes refuge, and by the end he is trying to recruit his old acquaintance Dr. Kemp into a scheme of rule by assassination and fear. Yet I am not convinced the novella actually vindicates Glaucon, because Glaucon's strongest claim is not merely that impunity corrupts. He argues that the happiest life is that of the thoroughly unjust person who keeps a reputation for perfect justice — someone who reaps the gains of wrongdoing while still being honored as good. Wells's protagonist can keep no such reputation: he is cut off from ordinary society, perpetually cold and hungry, hunted once he is discovered, and at least as miserable as he is dangerous.

I want a careful philosophical reading that works out how far The Invisible Man is a dramatization of Glaucon's challenge and how far it alters the terms of the thought experiment. Does the book concede the psychological half of Glaucon's argument — that this man's decency was only ever the product of being seen and of consequences — while undercutting the evaluative half — that the unjust unobserved life is the most desirable one? Does Wells treat invisibility as a corrupting force or as a revealer of a character already disposed to injustice before any experiment? Which scenes would you teach as the points where the disappearance of consequences does the ethical work: the flight after the experiment, his use of the tramp Thomas Marvel, his long confession to Kemp, his response to the mob that finally hunts him down? And where does the philosophical lens stop fitting the book, either because Wells is doing something else — a story about science outrunning morality, or about the despair of someone who can never be recognized by others — or because Glaucon's challenge is about seeming as much as being, and literal invisibility ruins both? I would like a reading that lets each text put pressure on the other, and that is willing to say plainly where the analogy between the ring and the book breaks down.
