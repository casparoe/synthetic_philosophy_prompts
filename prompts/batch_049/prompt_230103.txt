I teach an upper-level philosophy of the social sciences seminar — 14 students, 90 minutes. Design a classroom game that makes the group feel the force of Ian Hacking's 'looping effect' for human kinds: the way a classification applied to people changes those classified, and their changed behaviour or self-understanding then feeds back into and alters the classification itself. The students should generate a small diagnostic-style taxonomy of an innocuous classroom trait, such as how people respond to disagreement, and apply it to one another across several rounds; use no real mental-health labels, since the point is the engine of classification rather than the contents of the categories. The game needs a low- or no-stakes scoring scheme that works without real money, and a re-classification stage each round so label and labelled behaviour co-evolve. The concept must emerge from the activity before it is named: no lecture up front, and participants should not hear 'looping effect' until the debrief, which should map the rounds onto the components of the loop (classifying, identifying with or resisting the label, revised behaviour, revised classification) and mark the difference between a full loop and a mere self-fulfilling prophecy. Give concrete rules, materials, and a 90-minute timeline, say what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it (gaming the labels, the loop stalling, discomfort with being classified) and how to handle each, and be frank about which feature of Hacking's account a 90-minute exercise cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator. Format the response as exactly five numbered sections — (1) rules and materials, (2) round-by-round procedure, (3) scoring and incentives, (4) debrief mapping, (5) predictable failures and the irreducible gap in the exercise — each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
