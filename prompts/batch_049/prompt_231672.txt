I run a small philosophy reading circle at a public library — ten regulars, mostly adults with no formal training in philosophy. We have been reading slowly through David Hume's A Treatise of Human Nature, and last week I got stuck on a sentence in the section "Of the nature of the idea or belief." Hume writes:

"Whatever is absurd is unintelligible; nor is it possible for the imagination to conceive any thing contrary to a demonstration."

I have been wrestling with what this commits one to. If something is genuinely impossible — contradictory, or ruled out by a demonstration — then, Hume seems to say, we shouldn't be able to actually hold it before the mind at all. Yet I can say words like "round square" or "mountain without a valley," and something mental seems to happen. I want to find out with my group whether that mental something is really a case of conceiving the impossible, or just a kind of verbal echo.

Design a 90-minute activity for ten adults in a library meeting room that makes the group feel the force of Hume's claim before they have it explained to them. The activity should be a game or structured exercise in which participants sort proposed scenarios into "can be genuinely imagined" and "cannot be genuinely imagined" — with examples like a round square, a mountain without a valley, a triangle whose angles sum to more than two right angles, a person who is their own ancestor, and a golden mountain. Include the exact rules, any cards or props, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps what happened back to the passage. The debrief should also say what will predictably go wrong when the exercise is run and be honest about which part of Hume's idea a live game cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply — for instance, I've heard there are cases where something feels easy to imagine even though it is impossible, and others where something is possible but stubbornly hard to imagine.
