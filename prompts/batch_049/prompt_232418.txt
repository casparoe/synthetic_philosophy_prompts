Here is an argument I drafted for an environmental ethics seminar. I find the conclusion repugnant, but I cannot locate the step at which the reasoning goes wrong, and I do not trust my own dislike of the conclusion as a sufficient reply.

(1) In high-biodiversity ecosystems such as tropical forests, temperate woodlands, and grasslands, most sentient individuals belong to r-selected species whose young are produced in vast numbers and die before maturity from predation, starvation, exposure, parasitism, or injury.

(2) A life that consists almost entirely of such harms, and that ends in early death before the goods of maturity can be attained, is on balance a negative-welfare life.

(3) So, considered one by one, the typical wild animal life in such ecosystems is net negative.

(4) Clearing such an ecosystem and replacing it with pavement, pasture, or intensively managed row crops usually causes far fewer net-negative wild animal lives to begin in that area in subsequent years.

(5) If an action causes fewer net-negative lives to begin, then, other things equal, it reduces the total amount of animal suffering in the world.

(6) Therefore, clearing a high-biodiversity habitat is, other things equal, a reduction in the total amount of wild animal suffering, and on an impartial welfarist view it is to that extent good.

(7) By the same reasoning, large-scale destruction of the remaining wild habitats would be a moral improvement, and conservation or restoration projects would be, insofar as they sustain dense communities of net-negative lives, systematically bad.

I cannot accept (7), but I also do not want to rescue it merely by rejecting the whole argument as counterintuitive. Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? Identify the exact premise or inferential step that should be rejected, explain why it initially looked innocent, and say what giving it up costs. I would most like to keep (2) and (5), but I am prepared to learn that one of them is where the damage is.
