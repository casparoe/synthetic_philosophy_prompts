I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind and language, and I've been reconstructing my supervisor's view of Moore's paradox from her verdicts on example utterances. Her track record so far: she treats the first-person present-tense form—an assertion of p conjoined with an admission that one does not believe that p—as a genuine paradox. She finds the third-person and past-tense first-person variants unproblematic. She calls the converse form, in which one asserts that one believes p while denying p, defective but is unsure whether it belongs with the original or with a broader class of defective self-reports. She treats an assertion of p conjoined with "I don't know that p" or with "I'm not certain that p" as defective without quite being Moorean, explaining those cases by appeal to the epistemic standard on assertion. She has never commented on graded doxastic attitudes. The next case we want her to assess is an utterance of: "The car is in the shop, but my confidence that it is in the shop is 0.6." What would each of the main accounts that fit her existing verdicts predict, which prediction should I attribute to her if I want the most conservative extrapolation from her track record, and which single further case would best discriminate between that account and its closest competitor?
