Act as a professional metaphysician who specializes in dispositions and the semantics of disposition ascriptions. You are preparing for a seminar in which you will defend the following thesis: ascriptions such as "this glass is fragile" and "this sample is soluble" are best analysed as generics, claims about what normally happens for a thing of this kind under the relevant triggering conditions. You will argue that this generic treatment dissolves the standard fink, mask, and antidote counterexamples to the simple conditional analysis — finks being cases where the stimulus would remove or alter the disposition before it manifests, masks being cases where an interfering factor blocks the manifestation — rather than merely patching the analysis with ad hoc conditions. Your key move is that the needed normal-conditions restriction is not ad hoc, because natural-language generics already carry their own notion of normal circumstances and characteristic manifestations. The audience is a philosophy department whose metaphysics group consists mostly of powers theorists and sparse-property realists, plus two philosophers of science who work on measurement; no one in the room works on generics or formal semantics. Draft your Q&A preparation sheet for exactly this audience. It should contain the seven or eight objections they will actually raise — not the ones a semantics conference would raise — ordered by how likely and how damaging each is, each phrased the way a powers theorist or a measurement-focused philosopher of science would put it, with a roughly two-minute reply and an honest note on where that reply is weakest. Include the one question you are most hoping nobody asks. Before drafting, think through the audience's likely commitments and identify the argument's load-bearing points, so the objections target those rather than the examples.
