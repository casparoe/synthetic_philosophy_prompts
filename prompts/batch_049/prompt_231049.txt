Write a decision tree for a seminar on the foundations of typography and the philosophy of categories. Its purpose is to sort a respondent into one of six named positions on what makes every token of the letter 'a' an 'a', using only that person's responses to concrete glyph cases. The seminar members know their letters but have no prior exposure to the theoretical labels, so the tree must sort by judgement rather than by self-identification; no question may simply ask whether the respondent is, for example, a structuralist or a prototype theorist.

The six leaves the tree must locate and name are these: (1) skeletal structuralism, on which a glyph is an 'a' just in case it instantiates the right part–whole skeleton, with only those alterations that preserve the topology and relations of the letter; (2) prototype theory, on which membership is graded by similarity to a single stored summary representation of the category; (3) exemplar theory, on which the category standard is a cloud of remembered particular 'a's and membership is graded by similarity to those stored instances; (4) functional contextualism, on which a glyph is an 'a' when it plays the proper role in a community's writing and reading practice; (5) normative artefactualism, on which membership rests on the maker's intentions together with the norms of a typographic community, so that not just any functional substitute suffices; and (6) deflationism, on which the sameness across tokens is projected by our sorting practice and there is no further robust essence to capture. If you judge that one of these positions should be split or renamed, do so explicitly and explain why, but preserve at least these distinctions.

Build the tree from the logical space of the debate rather than from the list of labels. The main branch points should include, at least, these commitments: (a) whether being an 'a' is all-or-nothing or admits of degree; (b) whether bare structural or geometric similarity is sufficient for membership; (c) whether the function a glyph performs in a reading practice can, on its own, make it an 'a'; (d) whether the producer's intentions or the typographic community's norms are constitutive; and (e) whether the category standard is a single summary or a set of remembered instances. Each branch should be phrased as a concrete question that someone could answer by looking at or imagining letterforms, without knowing which position it leads to.

Use at least the following probes somewhere in the tree, described verbally rather than shown: a double-storey 'a' set alongside a single-storey handwritten 'a'; an 'a' with its bowl open at the top; a heavily flourished blackletter 'a'; an 'a' rendered only as a dotted or outlined silhouette; a simplified 'a' shaped like an 'o' with a short right-hand stem; and a deliberately novel mark that a small community resolves to use, and thereafter unhesitatingly reads, as the letter 'a'. You may add further probes if they improve separation.

For each leaf, give one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment. After the tree, include a section on logical space: list any combinations of the branch answers that no actual position occupies, and diagnose each as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Include a second section flagging any pair of leaves the tree cannot genuinely separate with the available probes, and say what further case or consideration would be needed to separate them. Write the whole instrument in British English, and do not include any bibliography, reading list, or citation.
