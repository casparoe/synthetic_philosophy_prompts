I am a retired condensed-matter physicist, now spending most of my reading time on philosophy of physics, and I serve on the program committee of a small private foundation that funds experimental work at the boundary of thermodynamics and information. We are deadlocked on a proposal, and I have been asked to draft the decision memo. This is not a request for personal advice; I need a formal document the committee can act on.

The proposal comes from a well-regarded nanoscience group and asks for three years of funding to build an autonomous Maxwell’s demon on a chip. A single colloidal particle would be held in a time-modulated optical trap. A feedback loop would measure the particle’s position quickly enough to resolve individual thermal fluctuations, use that measurement to adjust the trap and extract a small amount of work, and record each outcome on a prepared tape of initially blank bits. A separate set of runs would measure the dissipation required to reset the tape, and the applicants claim the complete accounting will show that the second law holds only if erasing the acquired information is charged at the Landauer bound—roughly kT ln 2 per bit in its simplest form. The project summary states the goal as demonstrating that information is a physical resource and not merely a convenient description.

The committee agrees on the applicants’ competence and on the technical feasibility of the device; the disagreement is about what, if anything, the experiment would establish. Two members see a clean test of the line of argument running from Maxwell through Szilard to Landauer, in which information and thermodynamic entropy are two sides of one currency, and they want to fund it. Two others see a standard feedback-control experiment in stochastic thermodynamics, dressed in the vocabulary of demons and information, and doubt that any experiment can settle whether information is physical. The chair worries about a circularity in the entropy accounting: the proposal seems to presuppose whether the cost properly belongs to the measurement, to the erasure of the record, or to the preparation of the blank bits before the cycle begins—when that allocation is exactly what is in dispute.

Please draft the committee’s decision memo. It should recommend approve, reject, or approve with conditions, with a bottom line the foundation can implement, and it should treat honestly the strongest consideration on the losing side. No external references are needed; the memo should stand on its own reasoning.

Use the following sections, in this order, with roughly the indicated lengths:

1. Summary and recommendation (about 150 words): give the decision up front, including any conditions.
2. The dispute (about 200 words): set out what the committee agrees on and where it divides.
3. What the physics can and cannot show (about 300 words): explain the second-law accounting and what a working demon on a chip would actually demonstrate.
4. The conceptual crux (about 250 words): distinguish a defensible claim that information is a physical resource from an overclaimed one.
5. The strongest case for the alternative decision (about 200 words): present the best argument for the outcome you did not recommend.
6. Recommendation, conditions, and reply (about 200 words): give the actionable bottom line and answer the losing view.
