I'm in a seminar on how moral norms actually change, and after class last week two classmates, Sam and Maya, got into a long argument about the dueling case. I typed up the back-and-forth from memory afterward and I want a referee's ruling, because I could feel them talking past each other but couldn't say exactly where. Here are the moves.

(1) Sam: The dueling case shows that argument basically isn't what kills a practice. The arguments against dueling—it's homicide, it's irrational, it lets a hothead force you to risk your life—had been around for two centuries while dueling went right on. What actually finished it was a shift in honor: refusing a challenge became compatible with being a gentleman, because you could say the challenger wasn't worth the field, and around the same time the duel started looking absurd to the rising middle class. Once declining carried no social cost, the practice collapsed fast. So it wasn't a better argument; it was that honor detached from the duel.

(2) Maya: But "the challenger isn't worth the field" is itself an argument, made in public, giving reasons. And the satire that made dueling look absurd—that's public persuasion too, just in cartoons and farce. What you've actually described is arguments that happened to target honor specifically. That doesn't show argument is powerless; it shows the arguments that worked had to speak the idiom of honor. So you've got a point about rhetoric, not about whether argument does the causal work.

(3) Sam: No, because the mechanism isn't belief change through reasons. The same reply was available to a duelist in 1720 and it would have gotten him branded a coward. What changed between then and the 1840s wasn't that someone finally found the right argument; it's that the audience changed—the professional classes rose, the press started mocking the duel, and status stopped flowing so automatically to that kind of courage. Arguments are downstream of who can confer status. People didn't hear a new argument and update; they noticed that the old option stopped costing them anything.

(4) Maya: But then your mechanism can't generalize. Take chattel slavery: the planters' honor and their economic interests both pointed toward keeping it, and it still took decades of organized argument, sermons, petitions, mass politics, and ultimately a war. Honor didn't do that work by itself—abolitionists had to attack slaveholders' honor from outside, with arguments, for years. So argument looks like the more basic thing, and honor is just one of the channels it uses when it's working.

Can you go through the four moves and rate each one from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two saying why, and then give a verdict on which specific points in each person's view survive? I'd also want to know what both of them are missing about how arguments, honor, and interests might interact rather than compete.
