I teach an upper-level undergraduate metaphysics seminar of 18 students, and I have one 80-minute session on Berkeley's master argument. I want a fully scripted in-class activity — not a mini-lecture — that makes students feel the argument's pull, so that by the end they can state precisely which step they would have to reject to escape it, even if they go on to reject it. The argument, roughly: you cannot conceive of a thing existing wholly unconceived, because the very act of conceiving it makes it the object of a mind. Please design the activity so that the idea shows up in what happens, not in what I say afterwards.

Please design an activity with roles, rules, a timeline, materials, and a debrief. The core should be an escalating challenge: individuals or pairs repeatedly try to specify an object or state of affairs that exists with no mind perceiving it, conceiving it, or thinking about it in any way. Their attempts should be examined on the spot by an umpire or challenger role for where a mind gets smuggled back in — perspective vocabulary, an implied observer, the act of asserting the thing, or the simple fact that the thing is now before someone's mind. Build in a public standoff near the end: the best surviving attempt is presented, and the umpire demonstrates on its own terms why it fails. The emotional sequence should run from "this is a verbal trick" to "the trap is tightening" to "here is the exact step I would reject."

The activity must keep two claims apart so students can feel where the argument lives: (1) Berkeley does not deny that we can think of a thing as unperceived — a tree in a forest with no one watching it is perfectly thinkable; (2) what he denies is that we can think of a thing as existing with no mind involved at all, because the thing-as-thought is always the thought of a mind. Include a designed round in which a student raises the strongest natural resistance — "thinking of a thing is not the same as perceiving it" — and the rules or umpire show how the argument can be restated to survive that move. Also include a round where the room confronts what happens to unperceived things when nobody is looking, and the activity then introduces Berkeley's load-bearing stopgap — an infinite mind that perceives everything at all times — not as theology to be assessed, but as the formal role the position needs.

For the debrief: map specific moments in the activity onto the argument's actual structure, including the move from "no one is looking at it" to "no mind conceives it," the moment when assertion itself starts to feel like a form of conceiving, and the role of the standing perceiver. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it (for example, a student will insist "I can just think of an unthought-about rock" and take that as decisive; another will dismiss the whole thing as a pun on "conceive"). Be candid about the one feature the activity cannot reproduce: the question whether the inference is actually valid — whether a property of my act of thought transfers to what the thought is about — is a logical matter that no amount of felt tightness settles. The activity can only make the premise vivid enough that students see exactly which step a critic must attack. Do not assign readings or rely on secondary literature; everything the students need must be in the activity itself.
