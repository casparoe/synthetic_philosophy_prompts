I'm putting together a handout for an upper-level undergrad course on social philosophy, and my students keep running together three different questions when we talk about unwritten rules: why a group settles into a regular pattern at all, what (if anything) makes that pattern something people can appeal to when criticizing each other, and whether the pattern is actually justified. In a couple weeks we're doing David Lewis on convention and some more recent work on the evolution of norms, and I'd love a decision tree that sorts accounts of how a mere regularity turns into a norm. The branching nodes should be questions a student can answer about concrete little cases without recognizing any jargon. So, for instance, a village where everyone walks on the left side of the path without thinking; a company where an emoji comes to mean 'I accept the meeting' and using a different one now reads as rude; a fishing community that rotates spots every season and treats breaking the rotation as not just unusual but wrong. Maybe throw in a case with mixed motives too, like everyone privately wishing they didn't have to bring a dish to the potluck but doing it anyway because they'd feel awful if they didn't.

Leaves should include at least: a Lewis-style convention (mutual expectations plus common knowledge and a conditional preference to conform, with the pattern being more or less arbitrary), a selectionist/evolutionary account where the pattern is just a stable equilibrium picked out by learning or cultural evolution and the participants needn't represent any 'ought' at all, a social-norm account where people prefer to comply because they believe others expect them to and will sanction or blame them if they dont, and a deflationary regularity view that says there's no extra normative ingredient over and above the regularity and whatever comfort people feel with it. Build the tree from the logical space first—pick the questions that actually separate these views, list which combinations of answers can be coherently occupied, then locate the actual positions—and flag any pairs of leaves the tree cant separate cleanly, with a note on what extra question would split them. Also mark empty cells with a sentence on whether the position is incoherent, just unattractive, or merely never developed. Each leaf should get a short name and a sentence on its most awkward commitment. 7-10 nodes is about right.
