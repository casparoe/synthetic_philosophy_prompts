I'm giving a talk next month at a workshop on anthropic reasoning and self-locating belief, defending the self-indication assumption (SIA). The audience is about a dozen people: eight formal epistemologists who are fluent in Bayesian updating but suspicious of any principle that turns "I exist" into evidence; two cosmologists working on the measure problem; and one decision theorist who thinks the self-sampling assumption (SSA) is the right default and treats SSA's reference-class sensitivity as a feature, not a bug. Most of the epistemologists will know the presumptuous-philosopher literature and will press me on exactly where SIA stops.

The thesis I'll defend: in finite observer populations, SIA is the correct rule for updating on self-locating evidence. Stated precisely, SIA says that, on learning that you exist, your posterior credence in a hypothesis is proportional to your prior credence times the number of observers "like you" the hypothesis implies exist (taking the expectation under the hypothesis when the count is chancy). I am not defending SIA in infinite-population cases; I will explicitly set those aside as a separate problem.

My argument has three moves: (1) refusing to treat your own existence as evidence is not a stable alternative, because SSA quietly smuggles such updating in through the choice of a reference class; (2) SIA is more robust to reference-class choice than SSA, even if not fully immune; (3) the presumptuous philosopher's overconfidence is an artifact of treating observer counts as mechanism-free evidence, and once ordinary physical priors over theories are included, the extreme update does not follow.
