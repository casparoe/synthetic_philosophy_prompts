Two members of my school's mathematics-and-politics club have been arguing about whether a lopsided vote is evidence that the winning side is factually correct. I wrote down their most recent exchange, which started with our student senate's 19–4 vote to endorse the claim that pushing the school day later improves grades and sleep. I could not referee the argument myself, and I would like a point-by-point adjudication.

(1) Daniel said the vote should make us reasonably confident that the claim is true. He appealed to Condorcet's jury theorem: if each voter is more likely than chance to pick the correct answer, and if voters decide independently, then the probability that a majority is correct grows with the size of the group and approaches certainty as the group gets large. With twenty-three senators, he argued, even a modest individual edge over chance would make the majority substantially more reliable than any single senator, so the 19–4 result is real evidence.

(2) Maya replied that the theorem requires independence, and the senators plainly were not independent. All of them attended the same two presentations and read the same summary of the evidence; if that material misled one senator, it would likely mislead nearly all of them in the same direction. That positive correlation, she said, means the vote is not twenty-three independent judgments. It is closer to one biased group thinking once and being counted nineteen times.

(3) Daniel answered that independence is never perfect outside a textbook, and the theorem does not require it to be perfect. Unless the correlation were complete, each senator would still contribute some private information, coming from conversations with different students or from different studies. He claimed that in any such case the majority remains more reliable than the average senator, and that adding voters still helps. He asked Maya how the senators' individual information could fail to show up in the majority, if each of them really knows something the others do not.

(4) Maya said this skates over a second condition. Condorcet's theorem also assumes that each voter is more likely to be right than wrong, and a shared one-sided presentation can make voters systematically worse than chance on the question at hand. If the senators are both correlated and worse than chance, she argued, a larger majority makes the wrong answer more likely, not less. So the margin itself cannot show that the endorsed claim is probably true; the 19–4 result may be evidence only that the whole group consumed the same flawed case.

(5) Daniel replied that the burden is not on him to prove independence and competence from scratch before the theorem can say anything. The theorem identifies conditions under which majority rule tracks the truth, and since no one has shown that the senators were worse than chance or that their errors were perfectly shared, the vote should still count in favor of the endorsed claim over its rivals. He added that Maya's standards would make almost every real-world majority vote useless as evidence, and that is an implausibly skeptical conclusion.

Please go through the exchange move by move. For each numbered move, say whether the speaker is right, overstating, or mistaken, and explain why. Then give an overall verdict on which side is more right, and identify any important consideration neither of them raised.
