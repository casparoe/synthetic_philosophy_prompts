Say I'm putting together a lecture on discounting the future and I want to press students on which arguments for a positive pure rate of time preference are really arguments for *pure* time preference at all. Take the four usual supects: (i) plain impatience — people demonstrably prefer sooner to later; (ii) extinction risk — the world might end, so later consumption may never happen; (iii) growth — future people will probably be richer, so a marginal dollar matters less to them; (iv) nearness — we owe more to people temporally close to us, the way we owe more to kin. Rank them by how strong they are as justifications for a positive pure rate of time preference specifically — the part that would remain after you strip out risk and growth effects — and give each a score out of 10. Where a score is low, say what the argument actually justifies instead. End by saying whether any of the four survives, on its own, as a justification for pure time preference.
