I have been reading Karl Jaspers’s distinction between criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt in connection with a unit on how nations remember wrongdoing, and I keep circling a question that I would like to work through as a poem rather than an essay. The question is whether a person born after an injustice can meaningfully experience guilt for it, or whether that feeling is better described as shame, sorrow, or responsibility. I would like you to write a sonnet, and I would like the poem to take the voice of a student trying to sort through an inherited sense of involvement in her country’s past. I have a specific structural request: the octave should express the common intuition that guilt cannot be inherited, since guilt requires an act that one has committed, and it should let that conclusion feel like a relief. The volta, however, should break that relief by introducing something like Jaspers’s distinction between criminal guilt and metaphysical guilt, so that the turn of the sonnet does the philosophical work of showing that the categories are not as clean as the speaker assumed. I would like the final couplet to leave the tension open rather than resolve it, because the difficulty for me is precisely that neither answer—saying the student is guilty or saying she is wholly innocent—feels adequate. The poem should be accurate enough that someone who knows Jaspers would recognize that it is not simply about inherited guilt but about the difference between being answerable for a crime and being connected to the moral meaning of a past for which no one alive is criminally at fault. Please keep the poem to fourteen lines in iambic pentameter, but do not force the rhymes in a way that makes the language sound strained. This would be for my own study notes, and I want it to end the way the discussion actually feels, which is unresolved.
