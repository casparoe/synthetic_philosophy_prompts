An introductory philosophy of language textbook I am using gives the following account of Kripke's rigid designators. A term is a rigid designator if it refers to the same object in every possible world in which that object exists. Proper names are rigid: "Aristotle" refers to Aristotle himself in any world in which he exists, not to whoever happens to satisfy descriptions such as the teacher of Alexander the Great. Because both names in "Hesperus is Phosphorus" are rigid designators, the identity statement, if true at all, is true in every possible world; it is therefore necessary even though it is known only through empirical astronomy — a necessary a posteriori truth. The textbook then says that for Kripke the reference of a name in the actual world is fixed by an associated definite description, and that this is the only real difference from Frege or Russell, for whom the description supplied the name's sense. From this it concludes that asking whether Aristotle could have been a farmer instead of a philosopher is simply asking whether, in some possible world, the person who satisfies the associated description — the teacher of Alexander the Great — is a farmer. I suspect this final step misrepresents Kripke, but the book is by a professional and I cannot tell whether it is an expository shortcut or a genuine error. Is the textbook's summary fair? Please separate what Kripke would accept, what is a harmless simplification for an introductory audience, and what he would reject, and say whether the conclusion follows from the accurate parts.
