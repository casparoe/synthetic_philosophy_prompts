I'm an undergraduate currently taking a course on decision theory and choice over time, and I also help run a weekly peer-led section for ten first-year PPE students; each session runs fifty minutes. We have just covered money-pump arguments against intransitive preferences, and I find that the argument convinces students in the abstract but not in the gut — they accept an abstract cycle of options on the board without feeling its force. I would like you to design a runnable activity for this section in which the participants themselves fall into a money pump before the term is ever introduced. By the end they should see, from the inside, why a sequence of individually ordinary trades can leave a person worse off through no single bad choice, and what assumptions about memory and foresight the argument depends on.

Could you design the activity in full, including the rules, roles, payoffs, and a minute-by-minute timeline, with one student or the facilitator acting as the bookie and the others as traders using tokens or very small real stakes? The trades should involve three items or gambles arranged so that each pairwise trade looks locally unproblematic from the trader's point of view, while the sequence as a whole returns each trader to where they started minus money. It would be especially useful to run two variants: one in which traders cannot see or do not recall their earlier trades, and one in which a trader is shown the full cycle in advance and may adopt a single global plan. The debrief should then use those two variants to separate the case of genuinely intransitive preferences held at one time from the case of preferences that shift over time, and to show whether simply becoming sophisticated about the cycle protects someone or only relocates the difficulty. Please also include the minimal script for the part of the session that happens before the idea is named, a debrief that maps concrete moments in the game onto the premises of the money-pump argument, the most likely failure modes when the activity is actually run (for example, traders refusing to trade once they sense a trick), and an honest note on which feature of diachronic rationality the activity cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator. Aim the write-up at someone running it for the first time, and keep it to at most 750 words.
