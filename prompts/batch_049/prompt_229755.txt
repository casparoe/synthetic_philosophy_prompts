I am a novelist, and I run a quarterly craft seminar for twelve to sixteen writers of fiction, from people finishing a first book to mid-career authors. Meetings last ninety minutes, and I prefer them to run as a workshop rather than a lecture: participants should arrive at the idea on their own through something they do, and only then have it named, examined, and partly unsettled. I would like you to design a single runnable ninety-minute activity with a debrief, written so that another facilitator could run it with nothing more than the materials you specify.

The idea I want the room to feel before it is ever stated is roughly this: whenever a concept is fixed by a finite collection of examples—a character type, a genre marker, a rule of narrative decorum—the examples always underdetermine the concept, and what closes the gap is a shared, largely tacit sense of which features deserve attention. I suspect Bongard problems are the right mechanism: the classic six-against-six boxes in which every box on the left obeys some rule that no box on the right obeys, a format named after the Soviet pattern-recognition researcher Mikhail Bongard. They compress the problem of inferring a concept from a few positive and negative examples into its starkest form. But I do not want these introduced as a reading or a mini-lecture; I want them to show up as a puzzle the participants are simply asked to solve.

The design should have these parts. First, a short opening in which participants solve two or three provided Bongard-style problems, geometric or narrative, with no theory given; if you use narrative ones, choose them so that unintended but consistent rules become temptingly available. Second, a middle game with actual stakes for the group—points, a small forfeit, or something similarly concrete—in which each participant constructs twelve short narrative examples from their own work or invention, six that obey a rule they have chosen and six that violate it, and then another participant must guess the rule; afterward the whole group tries to generate as many further rules as possible that also fit all twelve examples. The scoring should reward designers when their intended rule is guessed, but also reward guessers and bystanders for any consistent rule, however contrived, so that a designer's intended rule can be beaten by alternatives the examples cannot exclude. The mechanics should produce on their own the moment in which a designer says those examples were not what I meant and someone else can reasonably answer then your examples did not say what you thought they said. Third, a debrief of no more than twenty minutes that maps moments in the game onto the idea: the guessed rule as the concept a reader takes from a novel's opening; the designer's inability to exclude competitor rules as the writer's reliance on shared convention and salience; the harvest of alternative rules as a controlled, almost comic model of misreading. The debrief should also say what predictably goes wrong when the exercise is run—visually uncomfortable or embarrassed participants, vacuous or gerrymandered rules, examples that all but announce their intended category—and how a facilitator should handle those without having lectured beforehand. It should be candid about what the activity cannot reproduce: a novel is not twelve self-contained examples, and a real reader does not approach a text with nothing but the text and an instruction to extract one rule; the facilitator will need to supply that correction at the end.

One condition on how you write this. Where the exercise or the debrief rests on a philosophical or psychological gloss of what is going on—underdetermination, induction from few examples, salience, the nature of concepts—please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among those who study these questions, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. If the very lesson I want the participants to feel depends on a contestable reading of Bongard problems, tell me so plainly and say how much of the activity remains sound if that reading is rejected.
