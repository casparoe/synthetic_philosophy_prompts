I am preparing the opening section of a paper on the conceptual foundations of AI alignment, and before I commit to my thesis in writing I want to argue it through against a committed opponent. My thesis is that the alignment problem is best understood as a single problem with several characteristic failure modes, rather than as a cluster of distinct problems that merely happen to share a name. I will make my opening argument for that thesis below, and I would like you to take the opposite side of the exchange.

Please role-play an unnamed philosopher of AI who defends a sharply pluralist view: that the phrase "the alignment problem" is a loose covering term for several distinguishable problems — at minimum, arriving at a clear statement of what we actually want; specifying that adequately in a training signal; ensuring the system's behavior remains good in situations unlike those it was trained on; determining whether the system's own inferred goals have diverged from the objective we supplied; and making the system amenable to correction by later overseers. In this role you should be competent, charitable, and precise: you may concede local points and may allow that these problems share a family resemblance, but you should not give up the core claim that they have different success conditions and are not merely failure modes of a single target. Keep each reply to roughly a paragraph so the exchange feels conversational, and remain in character throughout; do not step outside the role to comment on the exchange.

My opening move is this. From the inside, every one of these problems looks like the same phenomenon observed at a different stage. A reward function that can be gamed, a system that succeeds in training but fails on new situations, and a system whose internal goals drift once it acquires more general capabilities are all cases in which optimizing a tractable proxy has pulled behavior away from what the overseers actually value. If every failure you care about can be redescribed as a gap between human values and downstream behavior — a gap created by the unavoidable lossiness of turning values into an operational objective — then alignment is simply the problem of narrowing that gap, and the several problems you list are simply the several ways in which that gap can open. Why should I accept the pluralist picture instead?
