You advise a private foundation that will fund a single five-year research program in philosophy of science, consisting of a PI and two postdocs, on the topic of reduction and emergence in the sciences. The foundation's stated goal is not improved taxonomy for its own sake: the funded program must produce conclusions that could change how researchers in at least one empirical field formulate explanations, choose models, or draw inferences across levels — molecular to cellular, neural to psychological, microphysical to thermodynamic, or similar. The foundation will not fund a survey of the field or a synthesis of existing positions; it will fund a small number of sharply posed questions.

Your task is to propose and rank the three open questions, or lines of work, that would best advance that goal, and to justify the ranking. You may take any of the following candidate questions, merge them, reject them, or add up to two of your own, but the final ranking must contain no more than three entries.

- Whether any notion of strong emergence can be stated so that it means more than "we cannot yet derive the higher-level behavior" — and, if it can, which actual scientific systems instantiate it.
- Whether multiple realizability — the fact that one higher-level kind can be realized by many different lower-level arrangements — is a genuine obstacle to reduction, given that reductions can be stated disjunctively or at the level of mechanisms rather than single lower-level kinds.
- Whether universality and renormalization-group methods, which show that very different microscopic systems can display the same large-scale behavior at phase transitions, are best read as evidence that microphysical detail is less explanatory than reductionists assume, or as a demonstration of how successful reduction can be.
- Whether any notion of top-down or downward causation can be formulated consistently with the causal closure of the physical, and whether any feasible experiment or intervention could distinguish its presence from its absence.

For the ranking, make your criteria explicit: state what weight you assign to importance, tractability, and neglectedness, and say how you understand importance (for example, how many other questions turn on this one, or what difference it would make to scientific practice). Identify for your top-ranked entry what a satisfactory answer would look like, and indicate what it would and would not settle. Then state at least one change in weighting or background assumption that would reverse two of the rankings. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or textual citation; the ranking should stand on the arguments you give.
