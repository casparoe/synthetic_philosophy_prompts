For an upper-level undergraduate philosophy of science course, I am making a handout on Hempel's ravens paradox, and I would like it to open with a decision tree that leads a reader from their verdicts on a few concrete cases to one of the main solutions to the paradox, without their having to recognize the solutions by name.

Set the paradox up as a trilemma. The three jointly uncomfortable claims are: the instance condition, that a universal generalization such as All Fs are Gs is confirmed by an observed positive instance of it; the equivalence condition, that whatever confirms a hypothesis confirms any logically equivalent hypothesis to the same degree; and the intuitive judgement that observing a white shoe, which is a non-black non-raven and therefore a positive instance of the contrapositive All non-black things are non-ravens, does not confirm All ravens are black.

Please build the tree from the logical space of the trilemma rather than from a list of names. The eight cells are given by which of the three claims the reader gives up, so the tree should diagnose each cell: name the position when one exists, and mark the cell as incoherent, unattractive, or unexplored when it does not. In particular, the cell that keeps both the instance condition and the equivalence condition while also keeping the anti-confirmation judgement is simply inconsistent, and should be flagged as such rather than assigned to any position.

The positions I want as leaves are: Hempel's satisfaction-criterion view, which accepts that the white shoe really does confirm the ravens hypothesis and blames the sense of paradox on a psychological projection of background knowledge onto a purely logical relation of confirmation; the Bayesian treatment associated with I. J. Good, which keeps the equivalence condition but treats confirmation quantitatively, so that a white shoe confirms the ravens hypothesis by an amount that shrinks with the size of the non-black class and with how the observation was selected, and the intuition is tracking negligible size rather than negative relevance; a Goodmanian projectibility view, which restricts the instance condition to projectible predicates such as raven and black and thereby denies that the shoe is a confirming instance at all; a sparse and technically demanding view that rejects the equivalence condition for confirmation, holding that the two formulations are truth-conditionally equivalent but not equally confirmable because confirmation is sensitive to the structure or the predicates of the hypothesis; and, if you can motivate it, a sampling-procedure view on which whether the shoe confirms depends entirely on how it was collected, so that an indoor ornithologist who selects an object because it is already known to be non-black has tested nothing, while the same object encountered in a genuine ornithological survey can confirm.

Phrase every branch node as a question a student could answer without knowing which leaf it leads to, anchored in concrete choice points: whether a black raven and a white shoe confirm All ravens are black, and whether they confirm it to the same degree; whether a sentence that says the same thing in different words must inherit the very same evidence; whether deliberately selecting an object because it is non-black changes what the observation shows; and whether confirmation is an all-or-nothing logical relation or a matter of degrees of probability. Do not include a question that just asks whether the reader accepts the equivalence condition or the instance condition under those names.

Aim for a two-page handout with six to ten decision nodes and no more than seven leaves. Each leaf should give the position's name, state which of the three claims it gives up, and add one sentence on the position's most awkward commitment. Finally, flag the pairs your tree cannot cleanly separate: I suspect the Bayesian leaf and the sampling-procedure leaf collapse into each other unless the tree includes a question about whether selection can make an observation fully inert rather than merely weak, and that the Goodmanian leaf and the equivalence-denial leaf can be confused when both are motivated by worries about unnatural predicates. Say explicitly where the taxonomy runs out.
