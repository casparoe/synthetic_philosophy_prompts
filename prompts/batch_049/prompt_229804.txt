I'm trying to work out what principle, if any, is actually doing the work in my intuitions about omnipotence, and I want an account precise enough to test against new cases. My verdicts so far:

- A being that cannot make 2+2 equal 5, or cannot make a round square, is still omnipotent; I don't think omnipotence includes doing the impossible.
- A being that cannot create a stone it cannot lift is still omnipotent; as soon as we say the being is omnipotent, 'a stone an omnipotent being cannot lift' describes an impossible object.
- A being that can bring about any logically possible state of affairs except one trivial one — say, making a particular leaf fall at noon rather than a second later — is not omnipotent, even though the excepted state of affairs is possible and harmless.
- A being that cannot lie, even under extreme pressure, is still omnipotent; I read that inability as a kind of strength rather than a gap in power.
- A being that cannot make itself evil, or cannot abandon its perfect goodness, is still omnipotent.
- A being that can do everything logically possible but cannot make a creature that both has genuine free will and is guaranteed never to do wrong is still omnipotent, because that combination is impossible.

I cannot yet see whether one coherent account of omnipotence delivers all six. Please give me the best candidate principle (or a small set of candidates with what each buys), state explicitly what 'possible,' 'limitation,' or 'essential nature' has to mean for each clause to do its work, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts any plausible principle cannot accommodate. Then apply the resulting account to the case I keep going back and forth on: a being that can do everything logically possible except that it cannot choose the second-best option whenever a best option is available. Is that being omnipotent, or does the appeal to 'essential perfection' just collapse into whatever limits I happen to like? Finally, separate what is broadly settled from what is genuinely contested in this area, and flag any point where your proposed principle is a contested move rather than the standard view.
