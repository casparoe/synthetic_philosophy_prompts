I'm a philosophy PhD student working mostly on Sartre's early stuff, and I keep hearing people — including a colleague I genuinely rate — gloss mauvaise foi as just "lying to yourself." The gloss gets used to run one of two arguments: either bad faith is incoherent, because the liar would have to know the truth and hide it from the very same person, or its coherent but basically self-deception, so the cure is just sincerity — stop being dishonest with yourself. From actually working through the bad faith chapter I'm convinced Sartre is doing something much stranger, and that this analogy quietly imports a picture of consciousness and sincerity he's trying to get away from. I'd like you to audit the analogy rather than just restate Sartre. Give me (a) a correspondence map: what plays the role of the liar, the person lied to, the truth known, and the act of concealment; (b) the exact places the parallel breaks, especially the difference between a lie told to another and a lie told to oneself — the deceiver/deceived duality versus the way Sartre distinguishes reflective and pre-reflective awareness, and whether the person in bad faith "knows" the truth the way a liar does; (c) what the analogy smuggles in about sincerity being the healthy alternative, and whether that survives Sartre's own treatment of sincerity. End with \answer{...} containing your overall verdict: exactly one of {neither conclusion survives}, {incoherence conclusion survives}, or {sincerity-cure conclusion survives}.
