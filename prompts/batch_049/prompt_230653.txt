I've been arguing with my roommate, whos in law school, about why courts are so weird about secondhand reports. In normal life we believe people all the time without a second thought—if a friend says her brother saw the car run the red light, I just beleive it. But in court, that same report would usually get thrown out as hearsay unless it fits some exception: the witness can say what she herself saw, but if she says "my brother told me the light was red," the jury isn't supposed to take that as evidence that the light was red. What's strange is that the law then carves out a bunch of exceptions that let in exactly the kind of thing the rule is scared of—excited utterances right after a startling event, statements against the speaker's own interest, dying declarations from someone who can no longer be cross-examined. And the whole time, the opposing side gets to cross-examine the person making the statement, not just the person relaying it, which suggests the court cares a lot about testing the original speaker's perception, memory, and sincerity at the source.

I get that this is supposed to be about trial fairness or whatever, but I'm interested in what the practice presupposes about testimony as a source of knowledge. I gather there's a standing debate between reductionists about testimony, who think believing someone's word is only justified through inference from our past experience of people being reliable, and anti-reductionists, who think testimony is basically a default source of justification like perception or memory—something you're entitled to rely on unless you have a specific defeater. It kind of seems like the hearsay rule is deeply reductionist: it acts like a report doesn't earn its keep until the speaker has been put through cross-examination and the jury can watch them. But then the exceptions don't fit that story, and in-court testimony isn't exactly treated like a default-trustworthy source either; it's more like a piece of evidence that has to survive an adversarial testing procedure. And where I get confused is whether the rule is about the jury's having good epistemic grounds or about something narrower, like preventing juries from being misled—which would be a different kind of commitment entirely.

What I'd like is a reconstruction of the implicit epistemology of the hearsay rule and cross-examination: what view of testimonial justification—reductionist, anti-reductionist, or some hybrid—the practice only makes sense under, which rival views it quietly rules out, and whether its parts actually presuppose consistent things. Then tell me what would have to change in trial practice if you replaced its tacit epistemology with a full-blooded anti-reductionism where testimony is default-justified, and separately what would change under a thoroughgoing reductionism that demanded independent corroboration for every report. I'm less interested in a law-school summary of the rule and more interested in whether the practice is philosophically coherent or just a pile of compromises.
