I've been working through Quine's "gavagai" example in the context of radical translation, and I want to check whether I'm exaggerating the philosophical payoff. As far as I can tell, the indeterminacy of translation just is the familiar thesis that theory is underdetermined by evidence, applied to the special case of linguistics: a finite record of a native speaker's assents and dissents in observable circumstances can always be accommodated by more than one translation manual. Physicists routinely accept that their data leave more than one theory open without concluding that there is no fact of the matter about physical reality. If that analogy holds, Quine's point looks like the modest epistemic caution that we may never have enough behavioral evidence to be certain we have the right dictionary — an uncertainty about knowledge, not about meaning itself. Am I missing a distinction between Quine's thesis and ordinary underdetermination, or is the indeterminacy of translation really just underdetermination dressed up in grander language?
