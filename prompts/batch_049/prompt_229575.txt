I am trying to determine whether a single principle can capture my intuitions about who has the standing to blame whom, and I need help extracting that principle from the cases. Here are six cases, with the verdicts I find myself pulled toward; I have not been able to reconcile all of them.

1. Amara oversaw a hospital billing office and for a decade routinely billed insurers for procedures that were never performed. When she discovers that Ben, a junior colleague, has padded his travel expenses by a few hundred dollars, she files a formal complaint and calls him a thief at a staff meeting. Ben did what she says. I think Amara lacks the standing to blame Ben for this.

2. Cara drove drunk many times in her twenties, stopped after a near-fatal accident, has been sober for twenty-five years, and now counsels people convicted of drunk driving. Her nephew drove drunk last month. She tells him plainly that what he did was wrong and that she holds him responsible. He did it. I think Cara has standing to blame him, even though her hands were once soiled in exactly the same way.

3. Darius spent years helping his business partner hide cash income from the tax authorities. After a bitter falling-out, he publicly denounces the partner as a tax cheat. The accusation is true. I think Darius lacks standing to blame the partner.

4. Elena has spent much of her adult life poisoning colleagues' reputations with self-serving falsehoods; she has never committed anything close to financial fraud. She discovers that a landlord in her building forged gas-safety certificates, and she denounces him at a tenants' meeting. The landlord really did it. I think Elena has standing to blame him, even though she is not a person of clean hands in general.

5. Farid has no relevant past wrongdoing and no stake in the dispute. He buttonholes a colleague whose grant budget he believes was inflated and tells her she is a fraud. She did inflate it. I think Farid does not lack standing; if his blame is objectionable, it is objectionable for some reason other than hypocrisy or standing.

6. Imani, then a junior bookkeeper, once backdated a shipment record under a direct threat of being fired. She later reports the scheme and denounces the manager who ran it for years. I think Imani has standing to blame the manager.

The two pairs that bother me are 1 against 2 and 1 against 4. I can feel the difference in each pair but cannot yet say what it consists in. I am tempted by a principle on which a person loses standing to blame another for a wrong W just in case she is culpably implicated in wrongdoing of the same kind as W and has not repudiated it. But I cannot say how same kind should be typed, whether being an accomplice counts as implication in the way my verdicts require, how coercion affects culpable implication, or why reform seems to restore standing while mere cessation does not.

Please give me the principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that best captures these verdicts. State your preferred principle precisely enough that it yields a determinate verdict on cases I have not described, and for each clause say which of my cases or pairs motivates it. Then apply the principle to two further cases of your own devising: one that discriminates between your two leading candidates, and one that stresses the principle. Since case 5 feels different from the others, say whether your principle treats meddling as a defect of standing at all or as a separate kind of defect. Finally, tell me plainly which of my six verdicts, if any, your principle cannot accommodate, and whether you would overturn the verdict or complicate the principle to keep it.
