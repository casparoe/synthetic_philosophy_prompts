I write the ideas-and-science quiz column for a serious general-interest magazine. I want to run a piece called "How do you actually settle a disputed question?" It should classify readers by the method by which they fix their beliefs, using C.S. Peirce's framework from his essay "The Fixation of Belief" as the underlying taxonomy: the method of tenacity, the method of authority, the a priori method, and the method of science. But the quiz must never name these labels in the questions themselves — readers should be sorted by their answers to concrete cases, not by which self-description they find flattering.

Design an eight-to-ten-question quiz, with a scoring key, that sorts readers into the following leaves: tenacity; authority; a priori; science-fallibilist; and science-infallibilist (the hybrid who adopts the findings and idiom of science but treats them as fixed and unrevisable). Include a "mixed" outcome only if the instrument genuinely cannot avoid it, never as a dumping ground.

Work to these specifications:

- Audience and register. The readers are intelligent non-academics who follow public controversies in nutrition, climate, medicine, and the behavioral sciences. Questions should be concrete, second-person cases with two or three answer options each, none of which mentions the theory labels. Avoid pop-philosophy clichés such as "do you believe what the government tells you?"
- Required cases. At least five of the following six cases must appear, adapted as needed and in any order:
  1. A large new meta-analysis or trial contradicts a dietary or medical guideline the reader has followed for years.
  2. A study in behavioral science the reader has long treated as settled fails a large replication.
  3. A professional scientific body issues a recommendation that contradicts the reader's own longstanding personal experience.
  4. A result reported in a sound-looking study feels "obviously wrong" to the reader on reflection.
  5. A scientific consensus shifts within a decade on a topic the reader cares about; the question should probe whether the earlier consensus counted as knowledge.
  6. A charismatic researcher or public intellectual holds a technically well-argued outlier view that most of the field rejects.
- The diagnostic core. Each answer option should pick out a behavioral commitment, not an attitude. Force choices among: protecting the belief from contrary exposure (tenacity); deferring to a body, community, or credentialed authority (authority); deciding by what seems reasonable, elegant, or plausible on reflection (a priori); and letting publicly available evidence — including the possibility that one is wrong — decide (science). At least two questions should separate fallibilists from infallibilists among science-leaning answers.
- The scoring key. Map each answer option to the nearest method, with a one- or two-sentence justification per option. Then give a rule for combining responses across questions and briefly explain why that rule is appropriate rather than mechanically summing a one-dimensional scale.

Then include a commentary section that treats the instrument's limits as a matter of design honesty:

- Identify which combinations of answers the scoring key leaves unoccupied, and for each empty combination say whether it is conceptually incoherent (for example, a thorough fallibilist who also shields a favored belief from contrary evidence), unattractive but possible, or merely unexplored.
- Flag which position pairs the quiz can barely separate — I expect authority and science-infallibilist to be hard to pull apart, and tenacity and a priori may blur on some items — and say what finer test could separate them even if a short quiz cannot.
- Note where Peirce's fourfold taxonomy strains against modern practice: in particular, deferring to a scientific consensus now sits awkwardly between the method of authority and the method of science. Tell me whether the quiz should force that apart or report it as a hybrid leaf.

End with a short, non-technical paragraph explaining to readers what each result category means once they get their score, without making the categories sound like a ranking of intellectual merit.
