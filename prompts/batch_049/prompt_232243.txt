I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and our desk has begun to notice that we lack a settled line on a question that runs through much of what we publish. In one week we will write that a cosmological model is approximately true or that a new imaging technique lets scientists see how the brain really works; the next week we will run a piece about philosophers who regard the whole vocabulary of representation—language and thought as a mirror of nature—as optional rather than mandatory. I recognize that second view as the neopragmatist, antirepresentationalist position associated with Richard Rorty, but I am not confident about what it implies for the practical business of commissioning, editing, and framing stories about science and ideas.

Please articulate the most important implications of Rortyan antirepresentationalism for science-and-ideas journalism. I want a committed response: I would rather you stake out a definite editorial position, giving reasons, than survey competing readings and leave the choice to me. Please organize the response under these five headings, in this order, with roughly the word counts indicated: “The position I am defending” (about 120 words); “Implications for writing about scientific truth” (about 220 words); “Implications for covering disagreement and controversy” (about 180 words); “Where I would not follow Rorty” (about 180 words); and “Bottom line for the desk” (about 100 words).
