I am taking an undergraduate course on the modern revival of Stoicism, and I have been asked to lead a study session for my classmates, most of whom have only encountered Stoicism through popular self-help writing. The group knows the popular slogan — roughly, that we should focus on what we can control and accept the rest — but I want to move them toward the stricter and more counterintuitive position associated with Epictetus: that what is genuinely up to us is limited to our judgments, impulses, desires, and aversions, while our health, reputation, career, and the conduct of other people are not up to us. What I most want to avoid is leaving anyone with the impression that Stoicism recommends passivity about everything outside one's own mind.

Could you help me plan the order of the session rather than supplying the explanation itself? In particular: what needs to be established first before the stricter claim can even be understood, and why that component cannot come later; what single example or case should carry the whole session, and what it must have and what it must avoid; which misconceptions the group is likely to bring or form — for instance, sliding from the judgment that something is not up to us to the conclusion that it is not worth caring about, or from the claim that something is not up to us to the claim that we cannot influence it at all — and at which point in the sequence each should be headed off; what should be deliberately left out or flagged as contested at this level, such as the modern revision that replaces the two-way distinction with a three-way one; and what a participant should be able to do or say at the end that would show they have grasped the distinction rather than merely learned the slogan.
