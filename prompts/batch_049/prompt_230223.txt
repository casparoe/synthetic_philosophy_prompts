You are an epistemologist who works on extended cognition, cognitive offloading, and the division of epistemic labor. I am drafting a position statement on epistemic dependence on AI for a seminar, and I keep being told that my commitments are individually plausible but jointly unstable. I need you to do a careful consistency audit for me. Please begin by brainstorming the pairwise interactions among my commitments, and any higher-order interactions, before drawing conclusions; do not simply flag everything as being in tension. Then report which pairs genuinely conflict, which only appear to conflict, and rank the genuine tensions by severity. For each genuine tension, say what would have to give and what the least costly revision would be. The point is discrimination, not a list of worries.

Here is the position I find myself wanting to hold, with none obviously dispensable to me:

A. Understanding is a success-through-ability state. To genuinely understand an argument or a result I must be able to reconstruct the underlying reasoning, explain why each step is there, and adapt it to adjacent cases. Merely having reliable access to a black-box that outputs correct answers does not amount to understanding.

B. The extended-cognition point is broadly right. Artifacts can be genuine parts of the thinking process rather than mere aids, and a coupled person-plus-artifact system can have epistemic achievements that the unaided person lacks.

C. There is nothing objectionable in principle about the division of cognitive labor. Letting a specialist or a reliable tool do something I could do more slowly, and then trusting the result, is often exactly what a well-run epistemic life looks like.

D. But the specific case of defaulting to an LLM for intellectual tasks—summarizing papers, generating first drafts, debugging, translating—tends to deskill. The more the tool does, the weaker the unaided capacity becomes, including the capacity to notice when the tool has gone wrong.

E. Deskilling matters because of its connection to oversight. Blame attaches only when either the tool is unreliable for the task in ways I should suspect, or I can no longer verify enough of the output to catch the errors that count. As long as the tool has a good track record for the task and I still can check its outputs, offloading is not blameworthy.

F. First-person understanding has nontrivial final value. A life in which one has worked through problems for oneself is, other things equal, better than one with equally good outputs produced entirely by delegated effort.

Where does this package bind? I can feel that something is wrong in the joints but I cannot locate it, and I do not already know which commitment I would revise.
