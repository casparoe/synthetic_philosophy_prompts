I spent my career as an organic chemist and have only recently begun reading moral philosophy. The dispute between virtue ethics and the situationist critique interests me because it resembles a scientific question about whether a postulated stable disposition actually explains behavior or whether local context is doing most of the work. I would like a reading of Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day through that dispute. Stevens presents his life as an exercise in what he calls dignity, a professional ideal combining loyalty, emotional restraint, and complete absorption in service. Yet the novel seems arranged to test whether this is a stable trait or a performance sustainable only within Darlington Hall: in different circumstances he neglects his dying father, refuses to answer Miss Kenton's clearest overture, and treats Lord Darlington's political errors as matters beyond a butler's authority to assess. I want an honest critical reading that takes both the novel and the philosophical debate seriously. Does Ishiguro show that virtue is more situation-bound than virtue ethicists admit, or does he show that Stevens never possessed the relevant virtues at all and merely occupied a role? Where does the situationist reading overreach, and what would a defender of virtue ethics identify in the book as evidence that genuine virtue and role-performance are not the same thing?
