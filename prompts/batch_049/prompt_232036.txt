I sit on my hospital's ethics committee. A patient in his forties suffered a severe traumatic brain injury six months ago and is now in a minimally conscious state: he cannot speak or communicate reliably, but he sometimes smiles at his wife, follows her with his eyes, and on one occasion squeezed her hand when she asked him to. Years before the injury, he signed an advance directive stating that if he were ever in a permanent vegetative state, he would want all life-sustaining treatment withdrawn. He is not vegetative; the care team agrees he is minimally conscious, and his physicians believe some further recovery is possible over the next year but cannot say how much. His wife and parents read the directive as proof that he would never want to live as he is now, and they are asking that his feeding tube be removed. Several staff members oppose this, arguing that the person in the bed now is not the one who signed the form: he was a different man before the injury, and the present man's responses suggest a quality of life that should be weighed on its own rather than overridden by a document from a former self. I have until Thursday to circulate my written opinion. Please draft it. It should recommend a concrete course of action, give the reasons for it, and engage seriously with the strongest consideration on the losing side rather than merely restating hospital policy or the relevant law.
