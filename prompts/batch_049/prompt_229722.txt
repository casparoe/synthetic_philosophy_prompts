I work as a senior distributed-systems engineer, and I have no formal background in medicine or philosophy. My native vocabulary is nodes, replicas, heartbeats, liveness and readiness probes, crashes, fail-stop and fail-silent failures, graceful shutdown, quiescence, checkpoint and restore, split-brain, and the difference between a process that is merely suspended and one whose state is unrecoverably lost. I would like you to restate, in that vocabulary and as faithfully as you can, the debate between the circulatory criterion of death (death as the irreversible cessation of circulation and respiration) and the whole-brain criterion (death as the irreversible loss of all functions of the entire brain, brainstem included) as rival accounts of when a human being is dead. Keep the translation honest: do not quietly assume that the brain is the control plane and then declare victory for the whole-brain side. The translation should handle the awkward facts, such as that a brain-dead body on a ventilator can keep a pulse and even heal wounds, while a heart can sometimes be restarted after it has stopped if the damage is not yet irreversible.

Then give the residue report. Which parts of the debate translate cleanly into the language of crash detection, quiescence, and unrecoverable state? Which parts could only be carried across with distortion, and which parts have no counterpart at all in how we reason about nodes and services? Finally, take and defend a definite position on what the residue shows. In particular, commit to a view on whether the whole-brain criterion is best understood as identifying the true failure point that the circulatory criterion merely detects by proxy—a legacy heartbeat check—or whether that reading is itself an artifact of the translation. Give your reasons, and do not simply survey the options. Please respond in American English.
