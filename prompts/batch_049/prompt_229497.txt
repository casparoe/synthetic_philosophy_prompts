Draft the joint statement that a committed mathematical platonist and a committed nominalist about applied mathematics could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. The question they have been asked to make progress on is this: when an empirical explanation in science or ordinary life makes essential use of mathematics—an optimality theorem, an arithmetic property, a topological constraint—does that use give us reason to believe the mathematical objects involved exist, or can all the explanatory work be recovered by a paraphrase that carries no commitment to abstract objects?

The statement should use only examples whose structural features are common ground. Include at least these three: a graph-theoretic impossibility used to explain why some task cannot be completed; a geometry-based minimization used to explain why some physical structure takes the shape it does; and an appeal to an arithmetic property such as primality used to explain a biological or physical regularity. If the parties cannot agree on the empirical details of a particular example, they should formulate the example at the level of the structural feature in dispute.

They have already agreed to one methodological constraint: any proposed nominalist reconstruction will be judged not only by whether it preserves truth values, but by whether it preserves the explanatory and inferential role the mathematics actually played—call this the role-preservation condition. The document should state whether this condition is itself part of the agreed framework or is one of the points of contention.

Write the document in American English, in three parts. First, a numbered list of claims both can sign, covering what the examples show about scientific practice, what the role-preservation condition requires, which forms of nominalism and platonism are off the table, and what must be true of a reconstruction for it to settle the dispute. Second, a disagreements table whose rows are the central disputes; for each row give a neutral formulation of the question, a formulation each side would accept as the statement of its own view, and a judgment about whether the residual disagreement is substantive or verbal (and if verbal, in what respect). Third, a list of cruxes: sub-questions such that a settled answer to any one would move at least one side. For each crux, give a concrete case or argument both sides agree bears on it, and record in advance what the platonist and the nominalist each predict about it.
