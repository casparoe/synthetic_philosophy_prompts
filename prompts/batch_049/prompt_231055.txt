Sanjay: "'Fragile' just means that if you dropped the thing, it would break. That's the whole concept."

Priya: "Not if a guardian angel would swoop in and catch the glass just before it hit the floor. It wouldn't break if dropped, but it's still fragile. So 'fragile' can't just mean 'would break if dropped.'"

Sanjay: "That's why the analysis is always understood as 'in normal conditions.' An angel waiting to interfere isn't a normal condition."

Priya: "Okay, but take the sorcerer case. A sorcerer is ready to cast a spell an instant before the glass hits the floor, turning it to steel. Right now the glass is fragile. But if you dropped it, it wouldn't break, because the nearest world where you drop it is one where he zaps it. So 'would break if dropped' is false of a fragile glass. And this time the interference isn't just background conditions — it changes the object itself."

Sanjay: "By the time it would land, it isn't the same glass anymore. The analysis says a fragile thing breaks when dropped while it remains the thing with the structure that makes it fragile. Your sorcerer destroys that structure, the categorical basis of the fragility."

Priya: "But that's circular. If you say 'while it remains the thing with the structure that makes it fragile,' you've built fragility into the analysans. The non-circular version is 'while it keeps its microstructure,' but now you've changed your thesis to 'fragile = would break if dropped, provided its microstructure doesn't change,' which is not the simple analysis you started with."

Sanjay: "Fine, but it's the right sort of thesis. Fragility isn't a spooky power; it's grounded in the glass's actual structure, and the structure tells you what happens under ordinary conditions. Every scientific law is like this — it holds other things equal, and nobody abandons a law just because an interfering factor scuttles a specific prediction."

Priya: "The angel case shows your 'other things equal' is doing a lot of hidden work, though. The glass keeps its structure the whole way down and still wouldn't break, because the angel catches it. So among your normal conditions you'll need the absence of guardian angels. But why is the floor part of 'normal' while the angel isn't? If 'normal' just means 'whatever would let the manifestation happen,' then you're explaining 'fragile' using a notion that already needs 'fragile' defined. That's the circularity."

Sanjay: "I'll grant the crude one-liner fails, but only the crude one. We've landed on a refined version: fragile means having an intrinsic structure such that, holding fixed that nothing interrupts the drop and the thing's structure isn't altered, dropping it results in breaking."

Priya: "But now the angel-exclusion and the sorcerer-exclusion are written into the definition as unexplained stipulations. The sorcerer case was supposed to show something stronger: the very operation by which the disposition is tested can destroy the disposition. That's the finkish case. The angel is a mask — the disposition is still there, but something blocks the manifestation. If both kinds of interference have to be excluded by stipulation, then 'if dropped, it would break' isn't doing the defining work anymore. You've kept the sentence but given up on it as an analysis; it's now a heuristic that holds except when it doesn't."

Sanjay: "I can live with 'a heuristic that holds except when it doesn't,' as long as we agree it's still excellent evidence about what's fragile."

Priya: "I can live with that. But then we agree the simple conditional analysis is false, and the only remaining question is how much of the original claim survives. In my book, what survives is a useful but fallible rule of thumb, not an analysis."
