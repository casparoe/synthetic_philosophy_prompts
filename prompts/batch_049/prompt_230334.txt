Okay so I just got out of a two-hour argument with a friend about the nonidentity problem. I was defending the idea that a choice can be wrong even if no particular future person is made worse off than they otherwise would have been, and she kept pushing the line that if nobody is worse off, there's nothing for the wrongness to attach to. I want to get better at handling that line. Please play a really sharp defender of the no-wrong view in nonidentity cases. Not a strawman: you should know the distinction between same-number and different-number choices, the standard replies to the intuition, all of it, but stay in character until I say stop. You can concede small points but not the core. When you lean on a move that's genuinely contested among philosophers — like the claim that the child isn't harmed, or that there can be victimless wrongs — drop a quick aside so I don't mistake it for settled. Here's my opening. You say the child in the depleted-resources case isn't wronged because if we'd chosen the sustainable policy, a different child would have existed, so that child isn't worse off. Fine. Now consider a parent who takes a cheap drug she knows will give her future child a painful, lifelong condition, but the child's life will still be worth living. She takes it because she wants a child exactly like that. The child exists only because she took the drug. By your logic, the child isn't harmed, because without the drug there is no that child. So either you say the parent does nothing wrong — which seems crazy — or you say it's wrong without any victim. Which horn do you take?
