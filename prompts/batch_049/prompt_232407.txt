I am trying to work out what I actually think about privacy, because the view I once accepted — that privacy is control over information about oneself — keeps delivering verdicts that I do not recognize as my own. Below are concrete cases and my standing verdicts on them. Some verdicts are firm; one is genuinely unstable. I want you to find the principle, or small set of rival principles, that best captures the pattern, state it precisely, and tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to give up or revise. Then apply the principle to the additional cases at the end with definite verdicts, not a menu of options.

Case 1. I read my teenage daughter's diary while she is at a friend's house. I find nothing alarming, and I never mention it or use anything I read. My verdict: clearly wrong, and the wrongness is in the reading itself, not in any consequence that follows.

Case 2. A friend tells me in confidence about a medical scare and asks me not to repeat it. I tell one mutual friend, who keeps it to himself. My verdict: wrong, and not repaired by the fact that the information traveled no further.

Case 3. A supermarket chain to which I belong for discounts logs my purchases, pools them with millions of others, and infers from the pattern that I am likely pregnant, then sends me pregnancy-related coupons. The data are all ordinary transactions I made with the chain. My verdict: wrong, and I think the creepiness tracks a real privacy violation rather than mere surprise.

Case 4. A team of epidemiologists uses de-identified medical records from a million patients, without asking any of them, and discovers a new risk factor for a disease. My verdict: not a violation. The lack of individual consent seems to me fine here, because what emerges is aggregate knowledge about a population and no claim about any named patient.

Case 5. My neighbor builds a low platform on his roof specifically to watch me sunbathe in my fenced backyard, which he can see from that height. My verdict: wrong, even though my yard is visible from his side of the property line.

Case 6. A mapping company photographs every house on the block and publishes a street-level image showing my house's exterior. My verdict: not a violation, though mildly irritating, even though it exposes my home to strangers online.

Case 7. I overhear the couple next door having a loud fight through our shared wall. I listen for a minute or two before deciding I should not. My verdict: I probably should have stopped sooner, but I did not violate their privacy by hearing something that audible.

Case 8. I take a photograph of a busy street fair, and it captures a stranger's face; I later post it on a personal blog. My verdict: not a privacy violation, even if the stranger would rather not appear.

Case 9. The police run automated face recognition across a database of the same kind of public street-fair photographs and identify everyone who attended a political rally. My verdict: wrong, in a way that a single photographer's snapshot is not, even though the underlying images were all taken in public.

Case 10. A colleague observes that I have been avoiding eye contact and leaving early for weeks, and infers, correctly, that I am going through something difficult. He quietly says he is available if I want to talk. My verdict: not a violation; if anything, it is humane. Yet he inferred something I had not told him.

Case 11. This is the one I keep going back and forth on. My partner reads my text messages while I am asleep because she suspects an affair, and the suspicion turns out to be correct. Half of me says this is the same wrong as Case 1: the reading is wrong regardless of what it finds, and the fact that it found something does not launder the intrusion. The other half says live suspicion of ongoing deception changes the situation in a way that nosiness about a diary does not. I want the principle to break the tie.

I can state the tensions that most need explaining. Cases 3 and 4 both turn on sensitive facts inferred without consent from data I let others hold, but only one strikes me as a violation. Cases 5 and 6 both expose my property to someone else's eyes, but only one strikes me as a violation. Cases 8 and 9 both use a public photograph of a face, but only one strikes me as a violation. And Case 10 sits oddly with Case 3: if inference itself were the problem, the colleague would be just as bad as the supermarket.

What I want is this. State the candidate principle or principles that come closest to capturing these verdicts, and for the one you think best, make it precise: what exactly makes an act a privacy violation, and what clause of the principle handles each of the hard pairs I just listed. Then tell me, honestly, which of Cases 1 through 11 the principle cannot accommodate without strain, and why you think dropping or revising that verdict is the right price to pay. Finally, apply the principle to the following with definite verdicts and one-sentence reasons: (a) a health app sells my step-count and sleep data to an insurer without meaningful consent; (b) a teacher reads a note two students are passing in class; (c) a drone hovers outside my apartment and photographs the inside of my kitchen through a window; (d) a city installs microphones to detect gunshots, and they incidentally record a loud conversation near the sensor.

Do not give me a survey of available positions. I am asking for a committed answer: pick the principle you think is best, defend it briefly against the most plausible alternative, and say where you would bet it fails next. Write in American English.
