I have been trying to think through the idea, usually credited to Karl Popper, that what separates science from non-science is falsifiability: a statement belongs to science only if some possible observation could show it false. An argument keeps forming in my head whose conclusion seems to empty that idea out, and I cannot tell which step I should give up.

1. Science is distinguished from non-science by falsifiability: a statement is scientific only if some possible observation would refute it.
2. Any statement can be protected from any observation by rejecting a background assumption instead: the observation was unreliable, the measurement was flawed, or the conditions were not the ones the statement was really about.
3. So an observation never contradicts a statement all by itself; it contradicts the statement together with a network of background assumptions.
4. It follows that when an observation seems to falsify a statement, someone has to decide whether the statement or one of the background assumptions is what failed.
5. If that is right, then falsifiability is not something a statement has on its own; it is something we grant or withhold depending on how much shielding we are willing to allow.
6. But a distinction between science and non-science cannot rest on how much shielding we choose to allow unless we already have a standard that says which shielding is legitimate and which is not.
7. Therefore, falsifiability either fails to draw the line between science and non-science at all, or it secretly depends on a deeper criterion that does the real work.

Where does this chain go wrong? If no step individually goes wrong, which premise is the least costly to reject, and what do I lose by rejecting it?
