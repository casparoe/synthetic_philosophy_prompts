I am working through Descartes’s First Meditation for a course on early modern philosophy, and I keep returning to the following passage from the Veitch translation:

> Though this be true, I must nevertheless here consider that I am a man, and that, consequently, I am in the habit of sleeping, and representing to myself in dreams those same things, or even sometimes others less probable, which the insane think are presented to them in their waking moments. How often have I dreamt that I was in these familiar circumstances, that I was dressed, and occupied this place by the fire, when I was lying undressed in bed? At the present moment, however, I certainly look upon this paper with eyes wide awake; the head which I now move is not asleep; I extend this hand consciously and with express purpose, and I perceive it; the occurrences in sleep are not so distinct as all this. But I cannot forget that, at other times I have been deceived in sleep by similar illusions; and, attentively considering those cases, I perceive so clearly that there exist no certain marks by which the state of waking can ever be distinguished from sleep, that I feel greatly astonished; and in amazement I almost persuade myself that I am now dreaming.

My confusion concerns the epistemic status of the conclusion Descartes reaches here. He begins by noting that dreams can present the same familiar circumstances as waking life, and he observes that waking experience seems more distinct and integrated than dreaming. But then he says he cannot find any certain marks by which waking can be distinguished from sleep, and he ends with the remark that he almost persuades himself that he is now dreaming. I would like help clarifying what exactly the argument at this point is supposed to rule out. Is Descartes claiming that waking and dreaming are indistinguishable in principle, or only that he has so far failed to identify a criterion? More specifically, when he says there exist no certain marks, is that a psychological report about his own inability, or a stronger metaphysical conclusion about the nature of experience? And what work is the phrase “almost persuade myself” doing here? It seems to fall short of the outright doubt he later introduces with the idea of a deceitful God or evil demon; does the dreaming argument already land the sceptical conclusion, or does it leave something that the later doubt must supply? I would be grateful for a careful reconstruction of the moves in the paragraph, rather than a bibliography or summary of scholarship. Please write your answer in British English.
