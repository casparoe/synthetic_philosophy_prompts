I've come to hold a Stalinesque account of the timing of conscious experience, with the color phi display as my central case. In that display a red spot is flashed at one location and then a green spot at a nearby one; subjects report a single spot moving across the gap and changing from red to green at the midpoint, even though no spot was ever physically present in the middle and the only green stimulus arrives later, at the endpoint. On my view the brain constructs the conscious percept only after the later flash has been processed, inserting the color change into the experience before it reaches awareness; no unedited version was ever conscious and then quietly revised. The Orwellian rival, on which the subject first has an unedited experience and then has the memory of it silently rewritten, seems to me less plausible and perhaps not genuinely distinct. Tell me what would count against the Stalinesque account, and against the broader thesis that the important editing of experience happens before awareness rather than after it. Which observations, experimental results, or arguments would favor the Orwellian alternative, and which would show that the two accounts are not merely hard to separate empirically but do the same explanatory work? I want the defeaters ranked from decisive to merely awkward, including what a committed Stalinesque theorist should herself concede as counting against the view, and an honest assessment of whether the position has insulated itself from disconfirmation, and whether that insulation, if it exists, is a problem.
