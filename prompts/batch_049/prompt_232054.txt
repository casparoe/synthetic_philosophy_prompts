Next month I present a paper at a workshop on human behavioral biology. My thesis is that the standard critique of evolutionary psychology is right about the field's practice but wrong about its method in principle: adaptationist hypotheses about human psychology can be genuinely tested, but only if three conditions are met — the selective regime must be characterized independently of the trait it is invoked to explain, the hypothesis must generate at least one novel and risky prediction, and explicit non-adaptationist rivals such as by-products, drift, and phenotypic plasticity must be specified in advance rather than dismissed afterward. My argument is that the field's failures come from violating these conditions, not from adaptationism itself, and I use lactase persistence and the sickle-cell trait as existence proofs that adaptationist hypotheses can satisfy all three conditions when the evidence is available.

The audience will be mostly behavioral ecologists who already work with optimality and game-theoretic models, plus two philosophers of biology who are sympathetic to the Gould–Lewontin spandrels critique.

Prepare my question-period prep sheet: the objections in roughly the order I will actually get them from this audience, each phrased as these people would put it, with an estimate of how damaging it is, what I should concede, what I should hold, a reply compact enough for the time available, and a note on where that reply is soft. Include the objection I am hoping nobody asks.
