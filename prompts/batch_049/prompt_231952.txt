In my Model United Nations club this fall, a delegate moved to alter a voting rule in the middle of the session, and the chair ruled the motion out of order on the grounds that changing the rules while the game is being played is unfair by its very nature — that a rule only binds if everyone knew what it was when they first sat down to play. The analogy between a rule-governed body and a game struck me as doing a lot of quiet work in that ruling. Since then I have come across a game called Nomic, in which changing the rules is itself a legal move: the game begins with a set of rules, some of which spell out the procedure by which other rules may be amended, and players spend their turns proposing and voting on changes to the very rules they are currently playing under. People who discuss Nomic often treat it as a proof that a system whose rules govern their own revision can be perfectly coherent, even elegant.  

I would like a careful audit of the analogy my chair relied on. Please work out what corresponds to what between a game and a legal or legislative system — the players, the rules, the officials, winning, the point of playing, and whatever else seems relevant. Then identify where the analogy breaks down, and separate the disanalogies that matter for the specific conclusion the chair wanted to draw (that changing the rules mid-session is automatically illegitimate) from the ones that are merely incidental. Finally, say how Nomic bears on all of this: does a game in which rule-changes are themselves moves show that self-amendment is coherent and therefore that the game analogy cannot by itself settle the question of fairness? Does the chair's conclusion survive once the disanalogies and the Nomic case are on the table, and is there a better analogy for the kind of self-amendment that actual legal systems carry out?
