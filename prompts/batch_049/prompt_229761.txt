I am drafting a short statement of my current views on self-deception for a discussion group, and I would like a consistency audit before I present it. Here is the package I have landed on:

(1) Self-deception is a real phenomenon, but in paradigm cases it is best explained as motivated false belief: a person ends up believing what they want to believe because their desires bias how they gather, weight, or interpret evidence.

(2) The stronger idea that self-deception requires literally deceiving oneself is incoherent: one and the same system cannot knowingly plant a false belief in itself and then be taken in by the deception.

(3) Still, some findings from split-brain patients suggest that one subsystem can act on information that another subsystem disavows. If that is allowed to count as self-deception, then something like a divided-mind account may be possible after all, and it would be too quick to rule out all partitioned-mind explanations.

(4) In ordinary cases of self-deception, however, the person does not simultaneously believe p and not-p. Rather, the person typically has a false higher-order belief about their own evidence or confidence, or simply fails to integrate information they already have.

(5) People can be morally responsible for their self-deceived beliefs when those beliefs are the downstream result of cultivated epistemic vices, such as systematically avoiding uncomfortable questions or surrounding themselves with flattering sources, even if no single moment of intentional self-deceit occurred.

I want to know whether these five commitments hang together. Which pairs are in genuine tension, and which pairs only look as though they are?
