You have been asked to give the ten-minute opening remarks at a small, invitation-only workshop on the replication crisis, held in a university statistics department. The room includes both frequentist and Bayesian methodologists, several working experimental psychologists who have lived through high-profile replication failures, and a few science journalists. Write the speech. It should be built around a claim R. A. Fisher makes in The Design of Experiments: that no isolated experiment, however striking or statistically significant, can by itself establish a natural phenomenon, because a chance result that ought to occur rarely will still occur with its proper frequency across repeated attempts. Treat this idea as a structural constraint on the speech, not as a passing ornament. The speech must not resolve the replication crisis by simply declaring one statistical school the winner, and it must not retreat into generic calls for rigor. It should give the frequentists and Bayesians in the room something precise enough to argue with, while giving the psychologists and journalists a clear picture of why the crisis is a crisis of inference rather than merely fraud or sloppiness. The tone may be wry but should not be cynical, and the speech should end by orienting the workshop toward what is genuinely hard about replication rather than offering easy reassurance.
