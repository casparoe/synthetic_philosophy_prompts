Yesterday I had a long argument with a friend about the absent-minded driver problem, and it left me less sure of my own position than I would like. We kept circling the same issue: what the driver is rationally in a position to do once he is sitting at an exit that looks exactly like every other exit. I eventually wrote down the commitments I found myself defending in the course of the argument. I want to know whether they actually hang together, because my friend kept insisting they do not, though he never quite located the break.

Here is the version of the problem we discussed, stated so we are talking about the same case. A driver is on a road with two identical exits. Exiting at the first exit leads to a bad neighborhood and yields a payoff of 0. Exiting at the second exit gets him home and yields a payoff of 4. Continuing past both exits leaves him on the highway and yields a payoff of 1. Before setting out, he can adopt a plan, but while driving he cannot tell the exits apart and has no memory of whether he has already passed one. From inside the car, every exit is simply an exit.

My commitments, as they emerged in the argument:

(1) The ex ante optimal plan is to continue at each exit with probability 2/3. This yields an expected payoff of 4/3, and no other plan does better. In particular, continuing at each exit with probability 1/2 would be strictly worse.

(2) The reason the optimal plan is mixed rather than pure is precisely the indistinguishability of the exits. If the driver could tell the exits apart, the optimal plan would be pure: continue past the first exit and exit at the second, for a payoff of 4 with certainty.

(3) When the driver finds himself at an exit, the correct way to evaluate his options is to conditionalize on everything he knows. If the plan is to continue at each exit with probability p, then the probability of being at the second exit, conditional on being at some exit, is p/(1 + p). At p = 2/3, this probability is 2/5.

(4) Under the ex ante optimal plan, continuing and exiting have exactly the same expected value at the information set. Exiting now has expected value 8/5. Continuing now, on the assumption that the plan is followed at any subsequent decision point, also has expected value 8/5. The driver is therefore exactly indifferent at each exit.

(5) Because of this indifference, no consideration internal to the driver's situation at an exit gives him a reason to deviate from the ex ante optimal plan. The appearance that he should change his mind once he arrives is an artifact of quietly handing him information he does not have, such as knowledge that he is at one exit rather than the other.

(6) I also accept a general principle about sequential choice: if a plan is ex ante optimal, then at every information set the plan reaches with positive probability, the action it prescribes there maximizes expected utility relative to the agent's beliefs at that information set. I take this principle to be nonnegotiable; giving it up would mean allowing plans that the agent would, by his own lights, choose against.

Start by brainstorming: go through the commitments pairwise, and for each pair give the strongest case that they are in tension and the strongest case that they are not. Then sort out which apparent tensions dissolve once the commitments are read carefully and which ones are genuine. For each genuine tension, tell me how severe it is, which commitments it puts pressure on, and what the cheapest revision would be. Finally, tell me which single revision would leave me with the most coherent overall package, and what that revision would cost.
