I'm on the collections committee at a mid-size university art museum, and we're deadlocked on something I need to write up before Thursdays vote. Six years ago we accepted a promised gift of a small Dutch Golden Age portrait, tentatively attributed to a minor painter in Rembrandt's circle, and it's been the centerpiece of our little Northern European gallery ever since. Last month a visiting scholar ran pigment analysis and infrared reflectography with the donor's permission, and the results were awkward: the panel's ground is nineteenth-century, two of the pigments weren't available before about 1850, and the brushwork reads as a deliberate imitation rather than an honest workshop copy. The donor still wants to give it to us, but now he's pitching it as "a fine period forgery with its own historical interest" instead of as a Golden Age original.

Half the committee wants to decline. They say the object's value was its authenticity, and accepting it as a curiosity rewards a fraud, or at least launders it into respectability under our name. The other half says the object hasn't changed at all — only the story around it has — and it's actually a better teaching object now, because students can see both how technical analysis works and how much history matters to value. Our curator keeps muttering something about Goodman: in painting, no amount of looking can fully certify an object's history the way a score certifies a performance of a piece of music, so the original/forgery distinction does real aesthetic work. The education curator keeps answering that we've all literally been admiring this thing for years without knowing either way, so whatever work that distinction was doing must have been invisible. I need to circulate a memo of about a page and a half before the Thursday meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action (accept with the new framing, accept but relabel, decline, or some combination), give a timeline, engage the strongest argument on the side the memo ends up recommending against rather than just restating collection-policy boilerplate, and — because the director wants it for the file — say in plain terms what the underlying philosophical disagreement is. No footnotes or bibliography; this is an internal document.
