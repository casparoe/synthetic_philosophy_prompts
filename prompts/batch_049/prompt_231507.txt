Before I read any more on joint action and collective intentionality, I want the full spec instead of the usual move where someone picks one example and waves it at Searle or Bratman and calls it a day. Write the desiderata for a theory of shared or joint action: the list of things it has to handle or explain, the distinctions it can't collapse, the cases it has to get right, and the empirical results (mostly from developmental psych, maybe animal cognition) it has to stay consistent with — with the case or consideration behind each. Mark which are non-negotiable and which are really just the commitments of one camp. And then tell me which single requirement knocks out the most of the standard views (Searle-style we-intentions, Bratman-style shared intentions, Gilbert-style joint commitments), briefly.

Some cases I want to make sure get addressed, though you should use them as pressure tests rather than organize around them: two people carrying a table where one privately intends to quit the project the moment they reach the truck; two strangers both heaving an overturned car off a trapped cyclist without ever agreeing to do it together; one person walking another to the station where one thinks it's a date and the other thinks it's a job interview; a hunting party flushing game where each person just does their own part and nobody issues instructions; kids too young for full theory of mind doing a joint pulling task, like both pulling a lever to get a toy out of a box; a jazz duo who have never played together but still make the tune work; and two people on oppposite sidewalks both heading to the same protest but not going together. I want to know what the theory's account of the 'we' actually is — a special kind of intention, a web of ordinary intentions with a common content, a commitment the participants hold each other to, or something else — and what it can't count as joint.

Flag any pair of desiderata you think can't be jointly satisfied, e.g. full-blown interlocking recursive intentions versus the developmental cases, or requiring a single shared goal content versus handling people who understand the joint action differently. Then briefly, how Searle, Bratman, and Gilbert do against the list. The list is the point, not the scorecard, so spend the space on the requirements and their motivating cases.
