I have recently started organizing a community seminar on the philosophy of the emotions, and the material has become unexpectedly personal. One participant described being terrified of a large but entirely friendly dog: she insists, calmly and with full conviction, that the dog poses no danger, yet the fear returns every time the animal comes near her. That mismatch between her settled judgment and her felt emotion has stayed with me, and it now strikes me as an extremely useful test case. A theory of emotion ought to be able to explain how an emotion can persist, sometimes stubbornly, even when the person experiencing it sincerely rejects the idea that the situation warrants that emotion.

The accounts we have been considering fall roughly into three families. Judgment theories hold that an emotion is, or essentially includes, an evaluative judgment, so that fearing the dog would be a matter of judging the dog to be dangerous. Perceptual theories hold that emotions are perceptions of value or of features of a situation, so that fear is a way of perceiving danger; on this view, an emotion might persist against one's better judgment in roughly the way a visual illusion persists even after one knows the lines are equal. Feeling theories, in the tradition of William James and Carl Lange, hold that an emotion is the felt awareness of a distinctive pattern of bodily changes, so that fear is constituted by the racing heart, the tensed muscles, and the urge to withdraw rather than by any judgment or perception.

Please rank these three families of theories from best to worst with respect to how well they explain such recalcitrant emotions, and assign each one a score from 0 to 10 for this specific explanatory task. For each theory, give me a paragraph explaining its ranking and score, paying particular attention to where it captures the phenomenon of judgment-resistant emotion and where it strains to do so. Please write your answer in American English.
