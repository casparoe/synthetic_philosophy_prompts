Van Inwagen's Consequence Argument tries to show that, if determinism is true, nobody has any choice about what they do. It begins with two premisses: no one has a choice about the state of the world in the remote past, and no one has a choice about the laws of nature. Determinism says that the past plus the laws entails every later fact, including facts about our actions. The argument then applies a closure principle, call it Beta, according to which, if no one has a choice about P, and no one has a choice about the fact that P leads to Q, then no one has a choice about Q. Chaining this together yields the conclusion that no one has a choice about their actions. Since free will is normally understood to require the ability to do otherwise, the argument shows that determinism and free will cannot both be true, which is exactly what incompatibilism says.

The strongest compatibilist reply is Frankfurt-style cases. In a Frankfurt case, Jones makes up his own mind and votes for a certain candidate, and he seems fully responsible. But a neuroscientist has implanted a device that would have intervened and made him vote that way if he had been about to decide differently. The device never actually goes off. So Jones is morally responsible even though he could not have done otherwise. This shows compatibilism is true, because a person can evidently be free and responsible even in a deterministic world. The Consequence Argument, then, is aimed at a requirement we can do without, since moral responsibility does not require the ability to do otherwise. In any case, the Consequence Argument only threatens free will if determinism is actually true, and since whether the actual world is deterministic is still an open empirical question, the compatibilist has no reason to worry.
