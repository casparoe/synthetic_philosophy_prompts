I am writing a novel in which a woman, a composer's widow, is trying to decide how much of her husband is still present in the world after his death. A recurring line in the book, voiced first by a neuroscientist character and later half-believed by the widow herself, is drawn from Hofstadter's picture of the self: a self is not a thing but a pattern, a loop of self-representation and self-reference, and when a brain dies the pattern does not simply go out, because it has already been copied, in coarsened form, into the brains of the people who knew the person. The person persists, the character says, distributed and low-resolution, in those who remain.

The currency in which this idea circulates among my characters is an analogy between the self and software. The brain is hardware; the self is the program running on it. Burn the computer and the program is gone only from that machine; wherever the program was installed, even as a partial or corrupted or outdated copy, it still runs. The intended conclusion is that the husband is not entirely dead: something of his self continues to run, however imperfectly, in the minds of the people who loved him.

I want to know how far this analogy actually holds, because I need to decide whether my novel should endorse the thought, undercut it, or leave it genuinely undecidable for the reader.

Act as a philosopher of mind with a working knowledge of cognitive science and the metaphysics of personal identity, and audit the analogy for me. Before you settle on a verdict, begin with a brainstorming pass: lay out what maps onto what (the hardware, the program, the original machine, the installations, the runtime, the versions, the corrupted copies), and list the candidate disanalogies you are considering. Then separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from the ones that do not, and say exactly which features of software the analogy quietly imports that a self in other brains lacks. After that, tell me whether the conclusion my characters want to draw survives the audit, and if so in what weakened form — what is the difference, put in terms a novelist can use in dialogue and interior monologue, between a weak claim like "other people still carry a rough model of him" and a strong claim like "there is a continuing I"? Finally, tell me whether there is a better analogy for what, if anything, remains after death, and which parts of my novel's emotional argument this analogy can and cannot carry.
