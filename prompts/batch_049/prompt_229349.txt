Act as an expert philosopher of mind who writes demanding but fair final exams. I teach an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar, and I'm putting together the final. I need you to draft one 90-minute essay question, no notes allowed, on epiphenomenalism and the paradox of phenomenal judgment — the worry that if phenomenal properties are causally inert, then our judgments and arguments about them start to look untrustworthy. The question should be written so a student can't just recite what epiphenomenalism says; it has to force them to work through whether someone can consistently believe both that qualia do nothing causally and that the very arguments (like a Mary-style knowledge argument) that lead to epiphenomenalism are still good. Dont make the question depend on citations or particular articles — it should be answerable from the problem's own structure.

Then give me a model answer of about 600 words and a marking scheme out of 20. The marking scheme should be built around five or six must-hit moves: distinguishing the idea that individual phenomenal states do nothing from the idea that phenomenal properties as types do no causal work; explaining how a physical cause of a phenomenal judgment could still make that judgment reliable or justified without the quale itself doing anything; and spotting exactly where the self-stultification charge overreaches. For each move, say how many marks it's worth and add a one-line note on how students typically botch it. Make the difference between an A and a B turn on whether they actually make those moves, not on how polished the essay is. Before you draft, take a minute to brainstorm the two or three sharpest distinctions a strong student could use.
