I have for several years kept a running record of how a colleague in my department—a moral philosopher whose specialty is normative uncertainty—actually deliberates when she is unsure which moral view is correct. I would like to extrapolate from that record to a decision she now faces, and I am less interested in the first-order merits of the decision than in the pattern of her reasoning.

Her record, as I have observed it:

(1) Some years ago she was deciding whether to become a vegetarian. She assigned 80% credence to a demanding consequentialist view of animal welfare that required abstaining from animal products, and 20% to a permissive view on which consuming ordinary animal products is morally acceptable. She became a vegetarian.

(2) When our university hospital sought her advice on ventilator allocation during the pandemic, she placed 40% credence in a view requiring that the ventilator go to whichever eligible patient had the best expected chance of survival, 30% in a view requiring a strict lottery among eligible patients, and 30% in a fairness-based contractualist view that also required the lottery. There was one patient clearly favored by the first criterion, yet she recommended the lottery rather than the maximize-expected-survival policy.

(3) When our department considered a motion to censure a colleague for a demeaning communication, originally sent in private, that had been shared widely, she placed 38% credence in a view holding that what people say in private is not the department's proper concern, which counseled against censure; 34% in a view holding that the resulting climate required an institutional response, which counseled for censure; and 28% in a view holding that the college was obligated to hold members publicly accountable, which also counseled for censure. She voted to censure.

She must now decide how to vote on a proposal that all assessed student work in the department be graded anonymously. Her credences are: 40% in an impartiality-based view of evaluation, which favors anonymous grading; 30% in a view emphasizing the instructor's contextual knowledge of a student's development over a term, which opposes it; 17% in a view stressing the administrative and grade-appeal burdens, which opposes it; and 13% in an instructor-autonomy view, which opposes it.

What should I expect her to do, and what would consistency with her own record require? In particular: which candidate rules about how to act under moral uncertainty fit her earlier verdicts; on which of those rules does this new case force a divergence; what does each rule predict; which rule is best supported by the record as a whole; and what further case would most cleanly discriminate between the remaining candidates?
