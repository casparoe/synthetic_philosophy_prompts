Ok so I just had a long argument with a friend about the Platonia dilemma and Hofstadter’s Luring Lottery thing, and I came out still thinking superrationality is basically right for those perfectly symmetric, no-communication choices. You know the setup: everyone independently decides whether to send in a card, and the payoff only happens if exactly the right number of people send; if too many send, nobody gets anything. My friend kept saying it’s magical thinking, that you can’t just reason as if your choice determines everyone else’s, and that it only works in toy puzzles. I don’t think that’s fair, but I don’t want to be unfalsifiable about it.

So I want the map of what would actually count against superrationality as a decision rule for these cases. Not the standard “rational defect” objection, I’ve heard that. What formal results, possible experiments, real-world analogs, or conceptual arguments would genuinely undermine it? Which of those are live rather than merely logically possible? And is the position insulated by the symmetry and idealization assumptions—like, if you keep saying “only applies to perfect symmetry and ideal reasoners,” does it risk explaining nothing at all? Sort the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me which ones a committed superrationalist would have to grant as counting against them versus which they can rightly dismiss. I’m not asking whether superrationality is mainstream game theory; I know it probly isn’t. I want to know how exposed it really is.
