I cover health and science for a magazine, and right now I'm reporting a piece on whether paying people to join clinical trials amounts to coercion. A bioethicist I interviewed walked me through an argument he says proves too much, and I can't find the hole in it. Heres the argument as I wrote it down:

(1) Consent is valid only if it isn't given under coercion.
(2) A person is coerced whenever their alternative to agreeing is bad enough that no reasonable person could choose it — when saying no isn't a live option.
(3) But then nearly all the consents that matter look coerced. A patient agrees to chemo because the alternative is a painful decline. A warehouse worker agrees to invasive tracking because the alternative is losing income. A student agrees to take part in a study because the alternative is no course credit. And a low-income volunteer agrees to a drug trial because the alternative is going without treatment or the money.
(4) So nearly all consents are coerced.
(5) And by (1), nearly all consents are invalid.
(6) But that conclusion seems crazy — if it were true, medicine, employment, universities, and research itself would be morally impossible.
(7) I can't see which premise to give up.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd like to keep (1) if I can — it's the one my readers would find hardest to let go of.
