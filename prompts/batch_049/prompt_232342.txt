Some friends in my AI safety reading group were going through the debate proposal for scalable oversight, and I can't stop thinking about the epistemology of it. The setup is: instead of trying to get a human to check whether a superhuman AI's answer to some hard factual question is right, you train two copies of the model to argue opposite sides in front of a human judge for a few rounds, and the judge just picks whichever side argued better. The winner's answer is the one you act on. The hope is that if one AI tries to defend a false answer about, say, some complicated protein folding question, the other AI can point to a specific internal contradiction or a bad inference, and a moderately attentive human can follow that move even though she couldn't of produced or verified the answer herself. The background assumption people kept making was roughly: false claims have some local flaw that an honest opponent can expose, while true claims can be defended against any attack, and a human can learn to spot the difference without understanding the whole domain.

That's the part that feels philosophically loaded to me. The whole motivation was that unaided humans cannot judge these answers, but then the proposal hands the judgement right back to a human, just one who gets cross-examination. It seems to presuppose that attacking a wrong answer is systematically easier than producing or verifying a right one, and that surviving adversarial argument is reliable evidence for truth. But true claims can be very hard to defend to a novice, and false claims have been defended successfully in front of very smart audiences for a long time. What picture of truth, knowledge, or good argument does the debate proposal quietly commit you to? Which rival views does it rule out or need to be right? And does the thing hang together, or does it smuggle back in the competence it was trying to do without?
