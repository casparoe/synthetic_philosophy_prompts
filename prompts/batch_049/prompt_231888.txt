I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I keep running into a set of ideas I only half-understand: the claim that categories like race, gender, and money are socially constructed rather than natural kinds. I don't have a settled view on whether that is right, but it keeps coming up at work. I help maintain a risk-scoring service for a lending product, and our schemas are full of fixed classifications: gender, marital status, employment bucket, account tier, and various merged-in third-party segments. In a recent design review, a colleague argued that if these categories are socially constructed, then hard-coding them as enum values is a mistake, because we would be reifying classifications that exist only because of contingent social practices, and we should move toward more flexible, event-based representations. Another colleague replied that this is confused: whether or not a category is socially constructed, it still has real effects on people's lives and on our model's behavior, so deleting it from the schema does not make the underlying social fact go away. I suspect they are partly talking past each other, and I would like an applied treatment rather than a survey of the literature.

What I want from you is a ranked account of the most important implications of the social-construction view, especially for race, gender, and money, for someone in my position: a practitioner deciding how to model categories in software, how to describe them in product and policy documents, and how to weigh the risks of encoding them. Please be specific about which implications should actually change day-to-day engineering decisions, such as schema design, what counts as a breaking change, or how to audit a model's categories, and which implications are mostly about the philosophical status of the categories or about the words we use, with little downstream practical effect. If you think some popular-sounding implications, for example that socially constructed categories are unreal, or that we should refuse to encode them, or that any classification system is arbitrary, do not follow or are overstated, say so explicitly and explain why.

End with a clear verdict on which two or three implications matter most for my situation and why those outrank the others. And please tell me what would change your mind: which empirical fact about how our system is actually used would most shift your ranking, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail, for instance because it leans on a contested reading of the social-construction claim, or because it assumes details about my product that I have not given you.
