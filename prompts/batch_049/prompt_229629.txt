Below is a question from an upper-level undergraduate exam on free will and moral responsibility, followed by one student's answer. Grade the answer out of 10, and then give the point-by-point comments that justify the mark. For each point, say whether the answer is correct, incorrect, or incomplete, and where an inference is drawn, whether the inference is licensed. A bare number or a general impression will not be useful.

Question.

Consider the following case. Dr. M has implanted a monitoring-and-override device in S's motor cortex. The device is calibrated so that it will activate if, and only if, S is on the verge of deciding to press the left button; if it activates, it will cause S to press the right button instead. In the actual course of events, S decides on her own to press the right button, and the device never activates.

Write an essay that does all of the following:

(a) State the Principle of Alternative Possibilities as it figures in this literature.
(b) Explain whether S could have done otherwise in this case, and give the reason.
(c) Explain what the case does and does not show about the Principle of Alternative Possibilities, and say what further premises or arguments would be needed before this kind of case could support the conclusion that moral responsibility is compatible with determinism.

Student's answer.

The Principle of Alternative Possibilities says that an agent is morally responsible for what she has done only if she could have done otherwise. In this case, S could not have done otherwise than press the right button. If she had been about to decide to press the left button, the device would have activated and caused her to press the right button; so pressing the left button was never a genuine option open to her, and pressing the right button was the only possible outcome. At the same time, the device did not actually activate, so S's decision to press the right button was her own, made without coercion or interference. Since S is in the same position, with respect to her actual decision, as she would have been in without the device, and since we would hold her responsible in that ordinary case, S is morally responsible for pressing the right button even though she could not have done otherwise. This refutes the Principle of Alternative Possibilities. It follows that moral responsibility does not require alternative possibilities. Determinism is just the thesis that, given the past and the laws of nature, no one can do otherwise, so the only threat determinism posed to moral responsibility was its conflict with the Principle of Alternative Possibilities; once that principle is rejected, the threat is removed. The case therefore gives us enough to conclude that moral responsibility is compatible with determinism. An objector might say that S still could have formed a different intention or shown a different neural sign before the device intervened, but the Principle of Alternative Possibilities concerns responsibility for the action, not for an inclination or indicator, so this objection does not save the principle.
