Write a triptych poem about Bertrand's paradox and the principle of indifference: three panels plus a closing envoi, roughly eight to twelve lines per panel and four to six lines for the envoi.

Give the three panels three different prosodic forms, and let the change of form carry part of the argument. Use blank verse for the first panel, common meter for the second, and free verse with deliberately irregular line lengths for the third. Each panel should open on the same scene: a circle, its inscribed equilateral triangle, and the question with what probability a chord drawn at random is longer than the triangle's side. But each panel should construe "drawn at random" differently, tracing the three classic procedures: choose two points independently and uniformly on the circumference and join them (probability 1/3); choose a uniform radius and a uniform point along it, then draw the chord perpendicular to the radius through that point (probability 1/2); choose a uniform point inside the disk to serve as the chord's midpoint, drawing the chord perpendicular to the radius through that point (probability 1/4).

The metrical shift should do the structural work: before the envoi names what has gone wrong, the reader should feel that the same question has been asked three times and answered three incompatible ways because the words "random chord" do not by themselves specify a set of equally possible cases. The envoi should make the lesson explicit and precise: the principle of indifference cannot assign probabilities until a partition of equally possible cases has been specified, and a state of ignorance alone does not specify one. The paradox lies in the underdetermination of ignorance, not in the geometry.

Keep it accurate enough that a philosopher of probability would not wince, and good enough as poetry that a reader who has never met the paradox comes away understanding it and why the principle of indifference is in trouble.
