Two months ago I was appointed to the review board of a large public utility, and I have discovered that the question I am now expected to make decisions about is, at bottom, a version of the dust-speck problem. The board must choose whether to keep a safety rule that imposes a momentary, barely noticeable inconvenience on several million people each day, or to suspend it and accept a small number of severe injuries among maintenance staff every year. My colleagues keep invoking the familiar thought experiment — an astronomically large number of people each suffering a single nuisance of a dust speck in the eye versus one person tortured for decades — as if it settled the matter one way or the other, and I find it settles nothing, because it assumes the very thing I need to understand: whether and how such harms may be aggregated and compared.

Before I read any further in this area, I want the full specification of what a theory of the aggregation of very small harms must be able to do to count as adequate. Please write the desiderata, with the case or consideration behind each. It should cover, at minimum, the structural cases any such theory must classify correctly, including harms concentrated in one person versus spread across many; harms repeated over time in the same life versus distributed across many lives; burdens we impose for someone else's benefit versus burdens a policy merely permits; and harms that are individually almost below notice but collectively enormous. It must also state the distinctions such a theory must not collapse, such as the distinction between a harm's being negligible in a single life and its being negligible once aggregated, or among intensity, duration, and number of victims. I also want to know which empirical findings about how people actually experience and adapt to small discomforts the theory cannot contradict, if any, and which requirements are non-negotiable versus merely commitments of specific camps, such as consequentialists who demand additivity or rights-based views that forbid trading small harms for large benefits. Flag any pairs of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which single requirement does the most to eliminate the standard positions, keeping the scorecard brief.
