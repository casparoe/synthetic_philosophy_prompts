I am grading a problem set for an upper-level course in philosophy of cognitive science and would like a second read on one answer. I set the short-answer question below, and a student submitted the answer that follows. Please assign a grade out of 20 and provide point-by-point comments that justify the grade, noting what the answer gets right and where it goes wrong or overstates. The material has recently become personally relevant to me: my research group has begun using Bongard-style few-shot concept tasks to probe abstraction in small models, and I want to be sure I am applying the right standard in my own teaching.

Question (400-word limit). A Bongard problem shows six frames on the left and six frames on the right; the solver must state the rule that separates the two groups. (i) Explain why the examples alone underdetermine the rule. (ii) Identify the resources a solver must bring in order to converge on the intended rule rather than on a gerrymandered alternative. (iii) Discuss whether a Bongard problem has a determinate correct answer. Reference the problem of projectibility where helpful.

Student answer.

Bongard problems are underdetermined by their examples. Any twelve frames, six on each side, admit indefinitely many rules that fit them perfectly, since a rule can always be amended with an exception clause or a disjunct covering exactly those frames. The frames alone therefore never force a unique generalization; this is Goodman's new riddle of induction in miniature.

Solvers nonetheless converge on the intended answer, and the resources they bring are what close the gap. The main one is a preference for simplicity. Among all rules consistent with the twelve frames, there is a simplest rule, and since simpler rules are more likely to project correctly to new frames, the simplest rule is the objectively best hypothesis. A well-designed Bongard problem therefore has a unique correct answer: the correct rule just is the simplest rule consistent with the examples. Naturalness does no independent work here; our sense that some rules are natural and others gerrymandered is simply the same simplicity ordering computed over the predicates we happen to possess.

The problem therefore has a determinate answer in two senses. First, the author has an intended rule, which fixes the target for grading. Second, and more fundamentally, the examples plus a simplicity prior determine a unique best rule, and any competent solver with the same visual vocabulary will arrive at it. That is why Bongard problems are graded pass or fail.

Residual ambiguity arises only when a problem is badly constructed, for instance when two rules of equal complexity fit or when the author intended an ad hoc grouping. But that is a defect in the problem, not a deep fact about underdetermination. The lesson for concept learning is that induction from a few examples works because learners bring a strong simplicity prior; once that prior is granted, Bongard problems are not underdetermined in any interesting sense.
