I've been following the debates about whether we can separate the art from the artist, and I've noticed something strange about how our institutions actually behave. It isn't one consistent policy; it's a patchwork that seems to sort artists by medium, by era, and by the kind of wrong they did. In philosophy departments, Heidegger is taught as a major figure regardless of his Nazism, and the moral question is usually confined to a single lecture or a footnote. In the art world, Caravaggio's murder conviction and Gauguin's relationships with adolescent Tahitian girls don't threaten their place in the canon—the paintings stay up, sometimes with a wall label acknowledging the facts, sometimes not. But when a contemporary film director or musician is credibly accused of sexual assault, the response is often far more drastic: films are withdrawn from festivals, distribution deals are cancelled, and the work becomes effectively unexhibitable, at least for a time. And then there is a fourth category: writers like Louis-Ferdinand Céline or Ezra Pound, whose politics were abhorrent but whose work is still read seriously in literature departments, often with a kind of uneasy caveat.

What implicit theory of the relationship between moral character and artistic or intellectual value does this pattern of differential treatment presuppose? I'm not asking whether the pattern is justified—I'm asking what it would have to assume about the connection between ethics and aesthetics (or ethics and philosophy) in order to be coherent. For instance, the pattern seems to treat the moral stain as more contaminating when the medium is narrative film than when it is oil on canvas, and more contaminating when the artist is alive or recently dead than when they belong to a distant century. Are those distinctions doing real philosophical work, or are they just ad hoc accommodations to public pressure and market logic? Reconstruct the implicit theory as best you can, and then tell me whether the pieces actually fit together. If they don't, which standard philosophical position—autonomism, moderate moralism, ethicism, or some further view—would generate the most defensible consistent practice, and what would institutions have to change to get there?
