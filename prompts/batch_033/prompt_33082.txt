I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I keep running into the same problem — maybe once a month. A source tells me something big: a lab is sitting on a result that would upend a field, or a whistleblower has documents that look damning but I can't verify every piece of. The evidence is suggestive but not conclusive. I want to run the story because I believe it, but I also know Clifford's line about how it's wrong always and everywhere to believe on insufficient evidence. And then there's James arguing that sometimes you have to commit ahead of the evidence when the decision is forced, live, and momentous.

I need a practical decision procedure for when to publish a story I believe but can't fully verify. I usually have a few hours to decide, my editor will push back hard if I can't justify it, and the stakes are high — getting it wrong means retractions, lawsuits, or torching a source's career. Give me the procedure as exactly seven numbered steps, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. Each step should be a question I ask myself, with guidance on what answer means "stop" or "proceed." Flag which steps are judgment calls dressed up as a checklist. Ground it in the Clifford/James debate but make it usable by someone who hasn't read either of them. The procedure should handle cases like these: a source with documents you can't authenticate, a whistleblower with circumstantial evidence but no smoking gun, a lab sitting on a result they won't let you confirm. Make sure the procedure sometimes tells me not to publish a story I really want to publish — if it never says no, it's not doing its job.
