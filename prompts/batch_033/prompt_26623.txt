I recently started working in the policy and communications team at an AI research organization, and I keep encountering the term "the frame problem" in internal discussions, whitepapers, and conversations with our technical staff. It comes up whenever someone wants to explain why AI systems struggle with real-world tasks despite impressive benchmark performance, or why we should be skeptical of certain claims about artificial general intelligence. I realized I needed to understand it properly, so I read a widely recommended popular book on AI and the philosophy of mind. The book gives the following summary, which I have condensed from a longer chapter:

"The frame problem was first identified by John McCarthy and Patrick Hayes in their 1969 paper 'Some Philosophical Problems from the Standpoint of Artificial Intelligence.' The problem is this: when an AI plans an action, it needs to know not only what the action changes but also what stays the same. If you tell a robot to move a cup from the table to the counter, it needs to know that moving the cup does not change the color of the walls, the position of the moon, or the truth of Fermat's Last Theorem. The difficulty is that there are infinitely many such non-changes, and listing them all is impossible. This is why early AI systems kept getting stuck: they could not distinguish relevant consequences from irrelevant ones. The frame problem was essentially solved in the 1990s with the development of non-monotonic logics and the situation calculus, which let AI systems default-assume that things stay the same unless explicitly changed. The deeper lesson of the frame problem is that intelligence requires knowing what to ignore, which is why it remains a central challenge for machine learning systems that lack explicit world models."

I need to know whether I can rely on this account when I write briefing documents and talking points for our organization. Something about the claim that the problem was "essentially solved" does not sit right with me, given how often I still hear it invoked as a live difficulty, and I suspect the summary may be running together the original technical problem with the broader philosophical problem that Daniel Dennett and others later discussed. I would like you to go through this summary carefully and tell me which parts are accurate, which are subtly wrong or misleading, and what someone who works on the frame problem would object to.
