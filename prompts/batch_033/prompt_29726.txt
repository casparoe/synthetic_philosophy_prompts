You are an expert in decision theory and global priorities research, with deep familiarity with the evidential cooperation in large worlds (ECL) literature. Please think carefully and work through the following request step by step.

I'm a senior program officer at a large philanthropic foundation focused on global catastrophic risk reduction. We make about 30–40 major grants per year, typically $500K–$5M. Our grant recommendations go to a board that usually defers to our judgment but expects clear, defensible reasoning.

I've been reading about evidential cooperation in large worlds — roughly, the idea that in a sufficiently large universe, your decision to support or not support a cause provides evidence about what other similarly situated agents will do, and you should therefore act as if you're deciding on behalf of all agents who are evidentially correlated with you. This seems potentially transformative for cause prioritization: a marginal grant to a neglected area might be much more valuable than it first appears if it helps shift the equilibrium across many correlated decision-makers.

But I'm worried about motivated reasoning and double-counting. It's easy to tell oneself an ECL story that conveniently favors whatever cause one already likes. And I've seen people apply ECL-like reasoning inconsistently — treating other funders as correlated when it helps the case for their preferred cause and ignoring correlation when it doesn't.

I want a reusable decision procedure — a checklist or flowchart in words — that my team can apply when deciding whether and how to incorporate ECL reasoning into a grant recommendation. The procedure should:

1. Help us decide whether ECL reasoning is even applicable to a given grant decision (what conditions need to hold?).
2. If applicable, guide us through estimating the relevant multiplier (how many correlated agents? how strong is the evidential link?).
3. Flag the points where we're making judgment calls rather than running a mechanical computation.
4. Include safeguards against motivated reasoning and double-counting.
5. Be usable by someone who has read the basic ECL literature but isn't a decision theorist — roughly two hours of work per grant.

Please work through two or three concrete examples applying the procedure, including at least one case where the procedure recommends against applying ECL reasoning even though it's tempting to do so, and one case where it recommends applying it even though it feels uncomfortable. Also tell me what the procedure cannot do — what kinds of cases it will systematically mishandle or leave unresolved.
