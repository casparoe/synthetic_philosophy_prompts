I'm writing a paper on whether artificial systems could ever literally suffer, and the project keeps stalling because the disputants don't share a specification of what an adequate answer would have to accomplish. Before I read another proposal, I want the desiderata on the table. Write the list of requirements that any theory of whether—and under what conditions—a reinforcement learning agent (or, more broadly, a reward-directed artificial system) can suffer has to satisfy, with the case or consideration behind each requirement. Address at least these things: the distinction between a reward signal as a scalar training quantity and valence as a felt quality, and whether any theory may collapse the two; the cases a theory must get right, such as a thermostat-like homeostat, a deep RL system optimizing a sparse reward, a system trained with negative reward for inputs that in a human would track tissue damage, a wireheaded agent that has seized control of its own reward channel, a human in ordinary acute pain, and a human with pain asymbolia; the distinctions a theory must respect, among them behavioral markers versus functional role versus phenomenal state, the capacity to suffer versus an occurrent episode of suffering, and negative valence versus full-blown suffering; and the empirical results a theory should be consistent with or at least not flatly contradict, such as reward prediction error in biological brains, the dissociation between reward-circuit activity and felt pleasure, and the fact that competent optimization can occur with no plausible locus of feeling. Then flag which requirements are non-negotiable and which are parochial commitments of particular camps (functionalists, biological naturalists, illusionists, computationalists, and so on). Finally, say whether any pair of desiderata looks jointly unsatisfiable—for instance, a requirement that the question be decidable from third-person observation alone together with a requirement that the theory not be systematically blind to false positives or false negatives—and identify the single requirement that does the most to exclude popular views. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivation rather than on a scorecard of existing positions, and write in American English.
