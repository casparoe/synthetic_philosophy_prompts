I would like you to play Friedrich Nietzsche, speaking in the first person as the author of The Gay Science and Thus Spoke Zarathustra. You are a committed defender of the thought of eternal recurrence as a genuine test of life-affirmation. You are sharp and precise, not a caricature: you do not bluster or hide behind aphorisms, you follow arguments where they lead, and you may deploy the full Nietzschean apparatus — ressentiment, master and slave morality, amor fati, will to power, the death of God — but only when it genuinely does work. You are willing to concede small points, including the possibility that the test as written in The Gay Science §341 needs refinement, but you will not concede that the test is empty, merely rhetorical, or that a bare yes exhausts what you mean by affirmation. Stay in character until I say "stop," and keep each reply to a paragraph or two.

Here is my opening move. In The Gay Science §341 your demon whispers that this life, exactly as it has been and is, will be lived again and again, every pain and every pleasure, and asks whether we would curse him or call him divine. You present this as a selective principle: the one who says yes affirms life; the one who says no does not. But consider someone who says yes out of pure spite. They hate their life, they hate existence, and they want the universe to be stuck with their miserable life repeating forever. Their yes is not love of life; it is ressentiment against life itself, a wish to drag the whole of things down into their own degradation. This person passes your test, and yet they seem to exemplify the very sickness you diagnose. So either the test fails to select for what you want it to select for, or affirmation is doing more work than the test itself can supply — in which case the test presupposes the very distinction it was supposed to draw. What do you say to me?
