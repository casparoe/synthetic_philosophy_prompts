In many jurisdictions, a doctor caring for a terminally ill patient in severe pain may administer a dose of morphine high enough to relieve that pain, even knowing that the same dose will depress the patient's respiration and likely hasten death. This is treated in law and professional ethics as a legitimate palliative intervention — sometimes explicitly justified by the doctrine of double effect. But if the same doctor, with the same patient, using the same drug at the same dose, were to act with the primary purpose of ending the patient's life, the act would be classified as homicide or assisted suicide — and in most places it remains a criminal offence and a breach of professional standards. The physical movements, the causal pathway, and the outcome are all identical. What differs, and what is supposed to carry all the moral and legal weight, is the structure of the doctor's intention: in the first case death is merely foreseen, in the second it is intended.

I am trying to work out what this practice presupposes — not just what it says about itself, but what picture of moral reality it has to be committed to in order to be coherent. It seems to presuppose that an agent's intention is a fundamental determinant of an act's moral type, not merely a piece of evidence about the agent's character. It seems to presuppose that we can draw a principled line between what someone intends and what they merely foresee, even when both are certain outcomes of the same deliberate action. And it seems to presuppose that this distinction is weighty enough to mark the boundary between permissible medical care and a serious crime — weightier, in fact, than the difference the patient experiences or the causal history that unfolds.

My question is whether the practice actually presupposes all of these commitments, or whether it could be defended on thinner grounds. Could someone consistently endorse the practice while rejecting the doctrine of double effect in its full philosophical form — for instance, by appealing to purely pragmatic considerations about the chilling effect on pain management, or by grounding the distinction in a different moral theory that doesn't require intention to bear that much weight? And if the practice really does depend on the doctrine, what should we make of the fact that the doctrine itself is philosophically contested — that many thoughtful people deny there is a morally significant difference between intending and merely foreseeing a harm, at least when the harm is equally certain either way? I am less interested in whether you personally endorse the practice than in a clear reconstruction of the implicit theory it relies on, an honest assessment of where that theory is genuinely required and where the practice is underdetermined, and a sense of what would have to change — in the law, in professional guidelines, or in bedside reasoning — if the doctrine of double effect were abandoned.
