Exam question: Explain what is meant by the therapy/enhancement distinction, then discuss one widely discussed hard case and say what it shows about whether the distinction can do moral work. Defend your answer.

Student answer: The therapy/enhancement distinction is usually drawn as the difference between interventions that restore a person's functioning to normal and interventions that raise functioning above normal. On this view, a therapy treats or prevents disease, while an enhancement improves a trait or capacity that is already healthy. The distinction looks objective because it seems to rest on a biological notion of normal functioning. But the biological notion of "normal" is itself partly statistical: "normal" means, at bottom, whatever is typical for a species or within the central range of variation. Since what is statistically normal varies across populations and over time, the line between therapy and enhancement is not fixed by biology alone. This shows that the distinction cannot do any real moral work: if the line is vague and partly conventional, then no defensible moral conclusions can be drawn from it. A hard case is the use of human growth hormone in a short but otherwise healthy child. Whether we call this therapy or enhancement depends on where we draw the line between normal and abnormal stature, and since that line is arbitrary, treating the child is not morally different from treating a growth hormone deficiency. What the case shows is that the distinction is merely a matter of drawing a line, and that moral arguments that rely on it — for example, the argument that insurers should cover therapies but not enhancements — are unsupported.
