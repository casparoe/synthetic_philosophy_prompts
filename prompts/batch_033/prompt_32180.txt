I keep noticing a pattern in discussions of money pumps and I don't know if it has a name. Here's the thing. Suppose someone has genuinely cyclic preferences: they prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A, and lets say these aren't mistakes — this is just how they value things. The standard story says they're vulnerable to being turned into a money pump: they'll pay to swap B for A, then pay to swap C for B, then pay to swap A for C, and end up back where they started but poorer. But here's what I notice: if the person is smart and knows their own preferences, they can just refuse to make the first trade. They look ahead and see that if they accept the swap from B to A, they'll later accept C for B, and then A for C, and they'll lose money. So they say no thanks at the first step. But now their behavior — the trades they actually accept and refuse — is indistinguishable from someone who has perfectly transitive preferences. So in what sense do they still "have" cyclic preferences? The preferences are real in their head, but they never show up in action.

I saw a version of this in a faculty meeting last month. We were ranking three job candidates, and one colleague's preferences were clearly cyclic — he preferred candidate X to Y on research grounds, Y to Z on teaching grounds, and Z to X on collegiality grounds. When someone pointed out the cycle, he said "fine, then I'll just abstain from the pairwise votes and we'll go with whatever the committee decides." But does abstaining mean his preferences stopped being cyclic? They didn't. He just refused to act on them.

Is there a term for this gap between the preferences someone introspectively has and the preferences that govern their choices when they're being strategically smart? It's not quite akrasia, and it's not self-deception — they're not fooling themselves about what they prefer. They just know that acting on their preferences would lead to a bad outcome, so they don't act on them. I'm trying to figure out whether the money pump argument actually needs the person to be naive, and if so, what work the argument is doing for someone who isn't.
