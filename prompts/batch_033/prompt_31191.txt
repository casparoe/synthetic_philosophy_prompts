You are a professor of bioethics with a side practice in clinical ethics consultation. Please think carefully and work through the following step by step.

I direct a hospital ethics committee, and we are revising our protocol for cases where a patient's currently expressed wishes conflict with their own advance directive. We need a decision procedure our consultants can follow — not just principles, but an operational sequence: what questions to ask, in what order, what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with a recommendation, and where it must hand the decision back to a human because the question is irreducibly one of values.

The procedure should be grounded in the philosophical literature on precedent autonomy, personal identity over time, and the authority of the earlier self — but it must be usable by someone who has not read that literature. Be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and flag the cases the procedure cannot settle.

Then apply the procedure to two cases:

1. A 68-year-old with early-stage Alzheimer's, still competent, has an advance directive refusing antibiotics for pneumonia. She now has pneumonia, is mildly confused but still oriented, and says she wants treatment. Her daughter says she has become more religious in the last year and now believes life should be preserved whenever possible.

2. A 42-year-old with a severe spinal cord injury wrote an advance directive three years ago refusing life-sustaining treatment if he were ever quadriplegic and ventilator-dependent. He is now in exactly that condition, fully cognitively intact, and consistently says he wants to continue living. He acknowledges the directive but says he simply changed his mind about what kind of life is worth living.

For each case, note where the procedure gives a clear answer and where it doesn't.
