I'm taking an aesthetics seminar this semester on art and morality, and we've been going back and forth on the whole "can you separate the art from the artist" question. My friend — not in the class, but she's read a ton of art criticism and has really strong, consistent views on this — has been my unofficial sounding board. Over the last few weeks I've thrown a bunch of cases at her, and here's where she's landed:

- Caravaggio: murdered a guy in a brawl in 1606. She says his paintings are unquestionably great art. "He's been dead four centuries, and the paintings don't depict the murder — there's nothing to separate because the wrong doesn't enter the work."
- A living singer-songwriter whose whole brand is vulnerability and emotional honesty, whose lyrics are all about treating people well, and who was recently exposed for emotionally abusing multiple partners over a decade. She says the music is ruined: "The songs are lies now. The persona was the art, and the persona was a fraud."
- Leni Riefenstahl: she says *Triumph of the Will* is not great art, because "the aesthetic achievement is inseperable from the evil it was designed to serve — you can't evaluate the filmmaking independent of its propaganda function."
- A 19th-century novelist who held virulently racist views but whose novels (I've read two) contain no racist content or themes whatsoever — just intricate family dramas. She says: great novels, the racism doesn't touch them.
- A contemporary rapper whose lyrics graphically depict violence and who has actually committed violent crimes. She says not great art, but when I pressed her she clarified: "That's because the lyrics themselves glorify violence — the crimes just confirm it's not a persona. If the same guy made instrumental jazz I'd feel differently."
- A mid-century abstract expressionist painter, dead since the 80s, who was by all accounts a cruel husband and a deadbeat dad. His paintings are pure color field works — no figures, no narrative, no emotional confession. She says: great art, no question.

Now here's the case I want to predict her on before I bring it up with her, because I'm trying to figure out what principle she's actually running. A living filmmaker has just released a critically adored coming-of-age drama — no violence, no political message, just a tender story about adolescence. It comes out that eight years ago, before film school, they embezzled a large sum from a community theater nonprofit they were volunteering for. The film has nothing to do with money, nonprofits, or crime of any kind.

What will she say about this one? More importantly: what are the candidate rules that fit all six of her verdicts so far, do they converge or diverge on this new case, and if they diverge, which one do you think she's actually running and why? If you had to design one more test case that would definitively tell the candidate rules apart — something I could casually bring up next time we talk — what would it be?

I'd love it if you'd approach this as a professional philosopher working in aesthetics. Think carefully, lay out the possible principles she might be operating with, and don't just jump to a conclusion — the interesting part is seeing which rules survive her full set of verdicts and where they come apart.
