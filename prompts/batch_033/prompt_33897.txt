I'm a high school junior and I've been sitting in on my school's programming club, where the older members sometimes drift into game theory. Last week two seniors, Leo and Maya, spent an hour on an argument I found hard to referee. They were discussing what Leo called program equilibrium: a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma in which each player submits a computer program instead of a simple action, and before the program outputs cooperate or defect it receives the other program's source code as input and can make its move conditional on what it reads. Leo proposed a program that cooperates if and only if the other program is exactly identical to itself. If both players submit it, he argued, both cooperate and earn the cooperative payoff; if either deviates, the program sees the difference and defects, so the deviant earns at most the defection payoff. Thus mutual cooperation is a stable equilibrium, and the standard claim that defection is the only rational move in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma stops being true once players can condition on each other's code.

Maya replied that this proves very little. The program cooperates only with byte-for-byte identical copies, she said, so any harmless difference such as a comment, a renamed variable, or a different compilation breaks the cooperation, which makes the construction irrelevant to any realistic setting. Moreover, always-defect is still an equilibrium in the same setup, so Leo's result does not show that cooperation is the rational outcome, only that it is one of several outcomes the setup permits. She added that the equilibrium concept itself gives no reason to select Leo's program over always-defect or over other conditional programs that also cooperate with their own kind and defect against outsiders; under those conditions, she said, calling cooperation rational is close to saying only that it is not impossible. Leo answered that Maya was conflating separate questions: whether the program is robust to small changes, and which equilibrium would be selected by designers or evolution, are different from whether cooperation is compatible with rational self-interest in the idealized setting. The relevant comparison, he said, is between programs in the actual matchup: against itself his program earns the cooperative payoff, which strictly dominates what always-defect earns against itself, so the cooperative program does better when it meets its own kind. That many equilibria exist does not make any particular one less real; it just means the game has become a coordination problem, and conventions such as driving on one side of the road are arbitrary yet rational once established. Maya countered that the road-convention analogy is misleading, because a driving convention is stable through shared expectations and penalties, whereas Leo's result depends on exact syntactic identity, which no realistic process selects for. If the set of equilibria is that large, she said, calling cooperation rational has no content beyond saying it is not impossible; the interesting question is which programs would actually be selected in a population or by designers, and there a defector may still win by exploiting whatever cooperators are not so narrowly conditional.

I could not tell which of them had the better case, and I would like an adjudication. Please go through the exchange move by move, say who is right about which specific claims, identify anything both of them are missing, and then say who is more right overall.
