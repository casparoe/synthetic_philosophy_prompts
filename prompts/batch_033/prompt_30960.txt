I'm trying to sort out what I actually think about deferring to my future self, because van Fraassen's Reflection principle -- roughly, that my current credence in a proposition should equal my expected future credence in it -- feels like it gets some of my gut reactions right and others badly wrong. Here are my verdicts on cases (for simplicity, all the propositions are ordinary factual ones, not indexical ones like 'it's Monday'):

- Tomorrow I'm meeting the founder of a startup I've been evaluating; I'm at 0.3 that it succeeds. I don't know whether the meeting will push me up or down, but I know my post-meeting credence will be based on new information and I'll weigh it sensibly. Gut: I should set today's credence equal to my expected post-meeting credence. Fine.

- Tonight I'll have three drinks and, I know from long experience, I'll become 90% sure my neighbor is stealing my packages. I'm at 15% now, and the drinks don't come with any new evidence; they just make me suspicious. Gut: I should not raise my credence to 90%. I'd rather stay put and warn sober-me not to act on drunk-me's view.

- I'm on a jury. After this morning's testimony I'm at 0.7 that the defendant is guilty. But I know the sedative I take at lunch makes me forget the single most incriminating detail, and by evening my credence will be back around 0.4. Gut: I should not now drop to 0.4; I should trust my current, better-informed self and write down my reasons before the sedative hits.

- I'm about to spend the weekend reading a book that will give me a lot of new evidence about climate sensitivity. I don't know what the book says, but I know I'll update carefully on whatever it says. Gut: my current credence should just be my expectation of my post-reading credence. Fine.

- Tomorrow I'll be asked to re-evaluate the same evidence I have now, but I'll be well-rested with two uninterrupted hours, whereas now I'm exhausted and distracted. I don't know what conclusion rested-me will reach. Gut: I should defer to rested-me's expected credence, even though there's no new evidence. Yes.

- After I give a successful talk, I know I'll become wildly overconfident about my own abilities: my credence that I'm in the top 5% of philosophers will jump from 0.2 to 0.8, and it's a predictable ego boost, not new evidence. Gut: I should not now raise my credence to 0.8.

So my gut says: defer to the better-informed future self, the future self with new evidence, the future self who's more careful; don't defer to the drunk self, the sedated self, or the ego-inflated self. The obvious pattern is something like: defer to future-me exactly when future-me will be at least as well-informed as I am now and at least as rational. But I'm not sure that's a real principle rather than a restatement of my verdicts, and it seems circular -- I'd have to use my current judgment to decide whether future-me is rational and informed, which is the very judgment Reflection was supposed to constrain.

What I want: a precise principle (or small set of candidate principles) that captures all or almost all of these verdicts. Finally, tell me honestly which of my verdicts the principle can't accommodate.
