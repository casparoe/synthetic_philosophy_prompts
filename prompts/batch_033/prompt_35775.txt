I'm an undergraduate in a distributive justice seminar, and we've been reading the luck egalitarian literature and its critics. I find that when I try to sort out what I actually think, I keep drifting away from the labels and want to work from my own verdicts first. Here are the cases as they strike me, not as I think I'm supposed to judge them. Two people begin with equal resources and equal abilities; one works long hours and saves, the other declines work and spends down what they have. The resulting inequality does not strike me as something society must correct. A person born with a serious congenital condition that requires expensive lifelong care strikes me as entitled to that care as a matter of justice, and a child born into a poor family strikes me the same way. Two equally prudent people hold identical jobs, and one is struck by a rare disease that leaves them unable to work; I think the unlucky one should be compensated. But now take a person who freely chooses a dangerous, well-paid job — say, working on an offshore oil rig — and is later injured on the job. I want to say he chose the risk, so he has less claim to full income support than the rare-disease case, yet I cannot bring myself to say he should receive only minimal medical care; the injury itself still feels like something we owe him treatment for. A person who gambles away their savings also seems to me to have chosen their losses, and I don't think society owes them restoration to their former position, but I also don't think they should be left destitute. A person who deliberately cultivates expensive tastes — a fine-wine palate, say — and then claims they need extra resources to be equally well off gets no sympathy from me. But a person who develops clinical depression and cannot work feels completely different: I want to treat the depression like the congenital condition, not like the expensive taste, though I'm not sure I can say what makes the two cases different in a principled way. And the case that most unsettles me: someone born with exceptional natural talent earns far more than someone born with no such talent who works just as hard. Part of me says the talent is pure luck and the gap is unfair; another part says the talented person's effort and choices are still theirs, and I don't think I want to equalize the whole gap.

The seminar keeps suggesting a principle something like this: disadvantage that is unchosen is the concern of justice, while disadvantage that is chosen is not. But my verdicts don't line up cleanly with that, and I want to know whether there is a more precise principle — or a small set of candidate principles — that does. Please state it exactly, say which of my cases motivates each clause of it, and then apply it to cases I haven't listed: a person who knowingly chooses a low-paying career they love and later resents their richer peers; a person who made a risky choice but made it under conditions of severe childhood disadvantage; and a person born with a talent who had to train for years to develop it. Please be explicit about which of my verdicts each candidate principle cannot accommodate, and which of my verdicts I would have to give up under each. Finally, please separate what is settled in this area from what is genuinely contested among people who work on it, and don't present a contested view as if it were the consensus.
