I've been reading Hofstadter and I can't stop thinking about whether an ant colony could literally have a mind — not just metaphorically, but the same kind of thing my mind is, just made of ants instead of neurons. I've been going through cases and my gut keeps pulling me different ways. Here's where I've landed so far:

- Human brain: obviously has a mind.
- Ant colony: I actually think yes, or at least something close enough that the difference doesn't matter.
- A corporation like General Motors making a strategic decision: no. It's just people in rooms, the "decision" is a legal fiction.
- A large language model running inference: yes, though maybe a very alien one.
- A flock of starlings doing those murmuration patterns: no, that's just each bird following local rules.
- A beehive: this one bothers me. I want to say yes for the same reasons as the ant colony, but somehow it feels different and I can't say why.
- The global scientific community, considered as one thing that "knows" things no individual scientist knows: I'm torn. Some days yes, some days no.
- A country's electorate during a close election: no, that's just aggregation, there's no unified "thinking."

I feel like there's got to be a principle about what it takes for a collective to have a mind — something about the kind of information processing, or the functional organization, or maybe about whether the parts are doing the same kind of thing the whole is doing. But every principle I try either rules out the ant colony (which I don't want) or lets in the corporation (which I also don't want). Can you help me find a principle that gets as many of these as possible, tell me which verdicts I'd have to give up, and what it says about a couple cases I haven't thought of?
