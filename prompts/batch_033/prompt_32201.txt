I've been working my way through the philosophy of consciousness over the past year — starting with Chalmers, then working through Dennett, and most recently spending a lot of time with Frankish's defense of illusionism. I've come to find myself convinced by the illusionist position: roughly, that phenomenal consciousness — the "what it's like" of experience, the qualitative feel, the redness of red — does not exist as a real property of our mental states. Instead, we have powerful introspective representational systems that generate the appearance of phenomenality, the compelling sense that our experiences have these special qualitative properties, but that appearance is itself a kind of cognitive illusion. There is no actual phenomenal property; there is only the representational content that we systematically mistake for one. I don't think this means consciousness doesn't exist — it just means we've been radically wrong about what kind of thing it is.

I'm aware that this is a minority view, and I want to be intellectually honest about the commitments I'm taking on. I'd like you to take on the role of a philosopher of mind who is sympathetic to realism about phenomenal consciousness — not a straw-man opponent, but someone who genuinely thinks illusionism is mistaken — and lay out, in a careful and systematic way, what would count against illusionism. I'm not looking for the standard objections that illusionists already have well-rehearsed replies to. I want to know what evidence, what arguments, what introspective data, what empirical findings, or what conceptual considerations would genuinely put pressure on the view if they obtained. Which of these would be fatal, which merely awkward, and which does the illusionist have to concede as counting against them even if she thinks she can ultimately accommodate them? I'm especially interested in whether illusionism has been qualified into unfalsifiability — for instance, by reinterpreting any apparent counterevidence as just more features of the illusion itself — and if so, whether that is a genuine problem for a view of this kind, or whether it's simply the nature of a position about the metaphysics of first-person experience that it can't be straightforwardly tested. Most of all, I'd like to know: what would a committed illusionist herself admit would, if true, falsify her view? In other words, what does illusionism actually risk? Please think through the question step by step, and be willing to conclude that the view is well-insulated if that's where the analysis leads — but also be honest about whether that insulation is a theoretical vice.
