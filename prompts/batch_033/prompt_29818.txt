In a graduate moral philosophy seminar last week, someone dismissed a consequentialist argument about special obligations by saying, "That's just Williams's point — it's one thought too many." The room nodded as if that settled the matter. The case under discussion was whether a doctor who arrives at an emergency room to find her own child among the patients should deliberate impartially about whom to treat first, or whether the very act of weighing her child against the others would itself be a moral failure — the thought that does the weighing being the "one thought too many."

I can feel the intuitive force of the slogan, but I suspect it's doing more atmospheric work than argumentative work, and I want to go to office hours with a structured question rather than a vague complaint. Can you lay out the distinct precise claims that "one thought too many" could be making? I'm interested in claims about the nature of deliberation (must a virtuous agent not deliberate at all, or only not deliberate in a certain way?), about the relationship between justification and motivation (does having a justifying reason to save your child mean you shouldn't need to reach for it?), about the place of impartiality in ethical life (is the problem that impartiality is the wrong stance for intimate relationships, or that even if it delivers the right verdict it corrupts the agent who applies it?), and about the psychology of virtuous agents (is the claim descriptive — virtuous people don't deliberate — or normative — they shouldn't?).

For each reading you identify: is it a platitude that no one would dispute, a substantive and defensible claim, or something false or overstated? And crucially, which of these readings would actually license using the slogan to dismiss the doctor's deliberation in the case we were discussing, as opposed to merely expressing discomfort with it? I want to know whether the slogan, once precisified, does the work my classmate was asking it to do.
