I serve on my university's institutional review board, and we have a new protocol that is dividing us. I want to work out what we are committed to given our own past decisions. Here are the consent-relevant rulings from our precedents:

- We approved a study that concealed its hypothesis — it told participants the research was about visual perception when it actually measured implicit racial bias — but accurately described every task the participant would perform (computer-based categorization tasks and questionnaires). Full debriefing was provided.
- We rejected a study in which a confederate, posing as a fellow participant in a group problem-solving task, was scripted to make repeated mistakes that visibly frustrated the real participant. The consent form described the ostensible task but did not mention the confederate, and no debriefing was planned.
- We approved a study that withheld from participants which of three short stories they would read, telling them only that they would read "a short story" and answer comprehension questions. Full debriefing followed.
- We rejected a study that offered individuals receiving public assistance a payment equivalent to one month's rent for participating in research involving sleep deprivation and painful stimuli, even though the consent form was otherwise complete and accurate.
- We approved a study that told participants they were interacting with another participant in an adjacent room via computer, when in fact the other participant's responses were generated by a simple computer program. The interaction was limited to choosing numbers in a coordination game, and full debriefing followed.
- We approved a study that used a confederate posing as a fellow participant in a group discussion about favorite films. The confederate followed a script designed to be unremarkable — expressing commonplace opinions, neither dominating nor withdrawing. The consent form described the discussion but did not mention the confederate, and full debriefing was provided.

The new proposal is a study on social perception. Participants would be told they are having a video call with another participant in a different room. During the call, each person takes turns answering questions about their hobbies and interests. In reality, the other participant is a pre-recorded video of an actor; the actor's responses are scripted to be warm and engaged but are not responsive to what the participant says. The questions the participant answers are mildly personal — what they like to do on weekends, what kind of music they enjoy, and so on. The consent form accurately describes the video call procedure and the question topics but states that the other person is another study participant. Full debriefing would occur immediately after the session. The study recruits from the general adult population and excludes anyone with a history of social anxiety.

What should we decide if we want to be consistent with our own precedents? Which of our past decisions is the strongest analog for this case, and which pulls most strongly in the opposite direction? If our precedents genuinely underdetermine the answer, what principle would we be adopting with each choice, and what further case would cleanly distinguish between them?
