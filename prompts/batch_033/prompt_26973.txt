I've been working on a short essay about Gödel's argument from rotating universes to the ideality of time, and I'd appreciate detailed comments. The essay is below. I'm particularly interested in comments on the central interpretive claim — that Gödel's argument contains two distinguishable strands, one Parmenidean and one Kantian, and that the standard reconstruction tends to conflate them. But I'd also welcome comments on any other aspect: the philosophical substance, the textual basis, the clarity of the exposition, or whether the argument I attribute to Gödel actually works. Please be frank about weaknesses.

When Kurt Gödel turned his attention to general relativity in the late 1940s, he produced a solution to Einstein's field equations that describes a rotating universe — one in which the matter of the cosmos as a whole rotates relative to the local inertial frames defined by the gyroscopes of an observer. The Gödel metric has several striking features, but the one that captured Gödel's philosophical imagination was the existence of closed timelike curves: paths through spacetime along which an observer could, in principle, travel into their own past. Gödel took this to have profound implications for the metaphysics of time.

In his 1949 paper "A Remark About the Relationship Between Relativity Theory and Idealistic Philosophy," published in the Schilpp volume on Einstein, Gödel argued that if a Gödel-type universe is physically possible — and he believed there were empirical reasons to think our own universe might be rotating in the relevant sense — then time cannot be objectively real in the way we ordinarily suppose. The argument, as he presents it, is meant to lend support to a broadly Kantian idealism about time: time is a feature of our mode of intuition rather than a feature of things in themselves.

The standard reconstruction of Gödel's argument runs roughly as follows. If closed timelike curves exist, then there is no objective fact about which events are past, present, or future simpliciter, because for any two events on a closed timelike curve, each is in the other's past and future. But if there is no objective passage of time — no objective becoming — then time as we ordinarily conceive it does not characterize the world as it is in itself. It must instead be a contribution of the experiencing subject, which is precisely what Kant claimed.

This reconstruction, however, conflates two distinct argumentative strategies that are worth pulling apart. The first strategy is what I will call the Parmenidean argument: if temporal relations form closed loops, then the very idea of an objective flow or passage of time collapses, because passage requires an asymmetric, directed structure that closed curves cannot supply. The second strategy is the Kantian argument: because we cannot even visualize or make intuitive sense of a world without temporal passage, the fact that the world as described by physics lacks it shows that time is a form of our intuition rather than a feature of reality.

The Parmenidean argument does not require any Kantian machinery. It is, at bottom, the argument Parmenides made in his poem "On Nature": change and becoming are unintelligible because they would require something to come from nothing. Gödel's version replaces Parmenides' purely logical considerations with a physical model, but the structure is the same: show that the world as described by our best theory lacks objective temporal passage, and conclude that passage is not real. The argument stands or falls on the physics and on the conceptual analysis of what passage requires, not on any claims about the nature of intuition or the transcendental conditions of experience.

The Kantian argument, by contrast, makes a further claim: not merely that passage is unreal, but that we are constitutionally incapable of representing the world without it, and that this very incapacity explains why we mistakenly take time to be real. This is a claim about the structure of our cognitive faculties, and it is not entailed by the Parmenidean argument. One could accept that closed timelike curves show that passage is not objectively real while rejecting the Kantian account of why we believe in it. Perhaps we believe in passage because of the local thermodynamic arrow of time, or because of the asymmetry of our memories and anticipations, or because of the forward light-cone structure of our local region of spacetime — all explanations that do not require transcendental idealism.

Gödel himself seems to have held both arguments simultaneously, and his prose does not clearly distinguish them. But distinguishing them matters for assessing how much of Gödel's conclusion survives if the Kantian framing is set aside. The Parmenidean core of the argument — that closed timelike curves are incompatible with objective temporal passage — has force independently of any idealist commitments. The Kantian superstructure — that this shows time is a form of intuition — requires additional premises that are far more controversial and that Gödel does not adequately defend.

What I find puzzling is why Gödel chose to present his argument in Kantian terms at all. One possibility is biographical: Gödel had a longstanding interest in Kant and may have seen his cosmological result as vindicating a philosophical view he already held. Another is strategic: by linking his argument to Kant, Gödel could present it as a contribution to a recognized philosophical tradition rather than as an idiosyncratic metaphysical speculation. But a third possibility is more interesting: perhaps Gödel believed that the Kantian framing was genuinely required to make sense of the situation, because without it we would be left with the seemingly paradoxical claim that time is both unreal (as the physics suggests) and inescapable (as our experience attests). The Kantian distinction between phenomena and noumena resolves this paradox in a way that a purely Parmenidean eliminativism about time does not.

If this third reading is correct, then Gödel's Kantianism is not an optional add-on to his argument but its essential completion. The rotating universe shows that time cannot be a feature of the noumenal world; our inability to think without temporal concepts shows that time must be a feature of the phenomenal world; and Kant's transcendental idealism provides the framework for holding both claims together without contradiction. On this reading, the Parmenidean and Kantian strands are not two arguments but two premises of a single argument whose conclusion requires both.

I am not fully convinced by this reading, but I offer it as a way of taking Gödel's Kantianism more seriously than the standard reconstruction does. I would welcome comments on whether this reading is defensible, on whether there are textual grounds in Gödel's writings for distinguishing the Parmenidean from the Kantian strands, and on whether the argument from closed timelike curves to the ideality of time can be made to work without relying on Kantian premises that most contemporary philosophers would reject.

When you comment, please separate what is reasonably settled among scholars who work on this material from what is genuinely contested or open to interpretation, and do not present a contested reading as though it were the consensus. Also, I'd ask that you write in continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way you'd write an email or a letter.
