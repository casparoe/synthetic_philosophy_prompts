I've been going through Bernard Williams's critique of utilitarianism with my reading group — the integrity objection and the point about one thought too many — and I've ended up pretty convinced by it. The way I understand it: Williams says that utilitarianism forces us to think about our deepest commitments (our ground projects, the people we love) from an impartial, universal standpoint, and that this demand alienates us from our own agency. The famous example is the man who has to choose between saving his wife and a stranger from drowning. If he pauses to run the justification — "in situations of this kind it's generally permissible to favor one's spouse, and this woman is my spouse, therefore..." — then, Williams says, that pause itself is one thought too many. The very need to justify the partiality from an impartial perspective shows that something has gone wrong with the agent's relationship to his own reasons. The broader claim is that a moral theory that requires us to hold our ground projects at arm's length, always ready to sacrifice them if the impartial calculus says so, is a theory that undermines integrity — the coherence of a life organized around commitments that are, in the right way, non-negotiable.

I'm not a philosopher (I work in product management), and our group has been going back and forth on whether this objection is as strong as it feels. I want to know what would actually count against this position — not just the standard replies I've already read about (like the distinction between a decision procedure and a criterion of rightness, or the idea that sophisticated consequentialism can just absorb the point). I mean: what evidence, what arguments, what kinds of cases should genuinely move someone who holds this view? Which parts of the objection are the most vulnerable, and which are the hardest for a consequentialist to answer? Are there things in the litterature since Williams that have made the objection look weaker — or stronger? And I want an honest assessment of whether the objection has been insulated over time, qualified in ways that make it unfalsifiable, and if so, whether that's actually a problem for a view of this kind or just par for the course in ethics.
