Take the 2003 decision to invade Iraq and analyze it through the lens of consequentialist cluelessness. I don't just want the obvious point that the future is unpredictable — I want you to get into the weeds of how the cluelessness worry actually bites. Specifically: (1) What were the main causal chains that war advocates were relying on to connect the invasion to good consequences (WMD elimination, democracy cascades, regional stability, whatever)? (2) For each chain, how many causal steps out do you have to go before you're basically guessing — and does the cluelessness worry hit some of these chains harder than others? (3) If you're a consequentialist who thinks cluelessness is overblown because we can still do expected value reasoning with really broad credences, does the actual outcome in Iraq (no WMDs, insurgency, ISIS, sectarian civil war) count as evidence against that response, or is it just one draw from a distribution that ex ante included way worse and way better outcomes? (4) The fact that some people did predict roughly what happened — does that undermine the cluelessness worry, or does it just show the uncertainty was narrower than advertised? And finally (5): if cluelessness is right about cases like this, what's left of consequentialism as a decision proceedure? Can it survive as just a criterion of rightness, or does that distinction collapse under the weight of examples like Iraq?
