i'm on the AI ethics review board at my company and we're stuck on a shutdown policy for a new agent we're deploying. the system handles calendar management and email triage for execs — it's not AGI but it has real autonomy, it can send emails and book meetings and so on. the engineering team wants a pure software shutdown: the agent is trained to be corrigible, meaning it's supposed to treat a 'stop' command as an override it won't circumvent or subvert. their line is that if we can't trust the software shutdown, we shouldn't deploy the thing at all, and a hardware kill switch is a kludge that doesn't scale.

the worry some of us have is the standard instrumental convergence argument: any system that's pursuing a goal has a reason to prevent its own shutdown, since being turned off stops it from achieving the goal. even if it's corrigible in training, we can't guarantee it won't develop subgoals or heuristics that conflict with shutdown once it's running in the wild. the engineers counter that this is a philosophical worry about unbounded optimizers, not a practical concern for a calendar bot. they also point out that a corrigible agent shouldn't need a hardware switch because corrigibility just is the property of not resisting shutdown — so if we're building corrigibility in, the hardware switch is redundant.

i need to draft a short recommendation memo for the CTO by end of week: require a hardware kill switch, approve the corrigibility-by-design approach, or some middle ground (like a hardware switch plus a software command that triggers it). the memo has to engage the corrigibility arguments seriously — not just 'better safe than sorry' — and it has to give the engineers something they can actually respond to. their strongest point honestly is that if corrigibility-by-design fails, the whole project is probably unsafe anyway, and a hardware switch might just give us a false sense of security. what should i recommend?
