I'm in this philosophy of action seminar and we've spent the last three weeks on choice without preference — the Buridan's ass problem and all its descendants. My professor is one of those people who never gives you the theory directly, she just keeps throwing cases at us and expects us to reverse-engineer the rule. I've been taking notes on her verdicts and I think I've narrowed it down to two rules, but I can't tell which one she's actually running.

Here's everything she's said so far:

– On the original Buridan case (donkey exactly halfway between two identical bales of hay): "The donkey doesn't starve. It picks one and eats. The whole puzzle rests on a mistake — rationality doesn't require a preference when there's nothing to prefer. The will can determine itself."

– On someone at the grocery store faced with two identical cans of soup, same brand, same price, same everything: "She grabs one without deliberating. Nothing irrational about that. She doesn't need to stand there comparing them — there's nothing to compare."

– On a job candidate with two offers that are identical in every respect (salary, location, prestige, colleagues, you name it): "He flips a coin. That's one way to handle it. Reasonable." When someone asked whether he needed to flip a coin or could have just picked, she said "we'll come back to that."

– On someone choosing between two identical routes to the same destination, same distance, same scenery, same traffic: "She picks the left one. She doesn't need a reason to favor left over right. The symmetry means either choice is fine."

– On an investor choosing between two index funds that track the same market with the same fees: "He spends an hour trying to find a difference. That's the mistake — he's looking for a reason where there isn't one. He should have just picked one and moved on."

Next week we're doing a case I'm supposed to present: a judge who has to sentence someone, and the guidelines say 3 to 5 years. In this particular case, all the usual aggravating and mitigating factors genuinely cancel out — the judge has no reason to prefer 3, 4, or 5 years. The judge thinks for a moment, says "4 years," and moves on. No coin flip, no rule like "always pick the middle of the range," no consultation of sentencing data for similar cases (there aren't any on point). Just an arbitrary selection.

The two rules I think might fit her verdicts are:

(A) When options are identical in all choice-relevant respects, arbitrary selection is perfectly rational. The will is sufficient to break the tie — no procedure, randomization, or further reason is needed.

(B) When options are identical, you need some kind of decision procedure to break the tie — a coin flip, a rule like "pick the first one I notice," or whatever. Without a procedure, the choice isn't fully rational because it's just a groundless impulse.

Under (A), the judge is rational. Under (B), the judge isn't fully rational — she should have used a procedure. Which one do you think my professor is running, based on what she's said so far? And if her existing verdicts actually underdetermine it, what's the case I should ask her about that would settle it?
