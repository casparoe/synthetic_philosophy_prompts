Two colleagues of mine, Elena and Marcus, have been going back and forth for weeks about a case one of them encountered in the contractualist literature, and when they finally asked me to referee I found I couldn't. The case is this. A technician is trapped in a room full of live transmission equipment. If the equipment stays on, he will be killed; if it is shut down, he can be rescued, but the broadcast of a World Cup final will be interrupted, and an enormous number of viewers — say, a billion — will each lose a short stretch of the match and suffer a mild frustration. Should the broadcast be shut down?

(1) Elena says obviously yes: a person's life is not outweighed by any number of people's mild irritation at missing part of a game. This, she says, is what Scanlonian contractualism gets right. A principle must be justifiable to each individual, and no individual viewer has a complaint that comes anywhere near the technician's complaint against being killed. To sum a billion small frustrations into something that outweighs a life is to treat "the viewers" as if they were a single subject who suffers the sum of those frustrations, but no such subject exists.

(2) Marcus says that cannot be the whole story. If no quantity of lesser harms may outweigh one greater harm, then the same reasoning requires saving one person from a broken arm rather than preventing a billion people from each suffering a broken finger — indeed, rather than preventing a billion people from each suffering a harm only slightly less severe than a broken arm. That, he says, is absurd. At some point numbers must count, and a view that cannot say where has simply drawn an arbitrary line and called it principle.

(3) Elena replies that the broken-arm conclusion is not an embarrassment, because justification is owed to persons one at a time. Compare the technician's complaint with any single viewer's complaint, and the technician wins every time; that the comparison comes out the same way a billion times over changes nothing. She concedes that numbers matter in one place: when individual claims are equal, as when one must choose between saving one person and saving five from the same harm, numbers act as a tie-breaker. But a tie-breaker cannot make a weaker individual claim stronger than a stronger one.

(4) Marcus answers that the tie-breaker concession only deepens the problem. Suppose one must choose between saving one person from death and saving five from a harm only barely less severe — permanent quadriplegia, say. Each of the five has a claim nearly as strong as the one person's claim to life, yet on Elena's rule the single death wins, because each pairwise comparison favors the person who will otherwise die. If Elena now says the harms are close enough for numbers to count, she has reintroduced aggregation and still owes a principled threshold, which is the very thing she has not supplied. If she will not say that, she is committed to letting five people become quadriplegic in order to prevent one death.

(5) Elena responds that the threshold objection cuts both ways. Marcus's own view has to say how small a harm may be before it becomes irrelevant when weighed against a life, and any aggregative position faces its own version of the line-drawing problem. The real disagreement, she says, is not about where to draw a line but about whether harms to different people may be added together at all, and Marcus keeps presupposing that they may.

They then turned to me. I could not tell who was right about what, so I would like a careful adjudication. Please go through the exchange move by move and say, for each move, whether it is right, wrong, or partly right and why; then say who is right about which specific points, and what, if anything, both of them are missing. If one of them is more right overall, say so, but only after the point-by-point analysis.
