I keep noticing this thing in how people explain behavior and I wonder if there's a name for it in philosophy or psychology. Whenever someone does something bad, people immediately reach for character words — "she's selfish," "he's dishonest" — as if the person has a stable trait that explains the whole thing. But the more I pay attention, the less this seems to hold up. The coworker who's generous at the office turns out to be cold to his own kids. I've caught myself being patient with strangers and then snapping at my partner an hour later. Situations seem to pull behavior around way more than traits do, but we keep talking as if traits are the real explanation.

And it's not just everyday stuff. I read about this study where seminarians were told to go give a talk — some about the Good Samaritan parable, some about job prospects — and on the way they passed someone slumped in a doorway. Whether they stopped basically came down to whether they'd been told they were running late. Being in a hurry swamped everything you'd expect about their character. Then there's the one where ordinary people administered what they thought were dangerous shocks to a stranger because a guy in a lab coat told them to. Again, the situation seems to overpower whatever we'd call "character."

I'm not saying character is fake. But there seems to be this deep mismatch between how we talk about people — in terms of stable inner traits — and how they actually behave, which looks wildly sensitive to context. Does this mismatch have an established name, and is there a literature on it? If there's a debate, what are the rough sides? I'm less interested in a one-word label than in getting clear on what exactly the tension is and what its edges are.
