I'm trying to get clear on what I actually think about the Meno problem — why knowledge is supposed to be better than mere true belief, and whether it always is. I've been going back and forth on a bunch of cases and I'd like someone to extract the principle behind my verdicts, or tell me which ones I have to give up.

Case 1: The classic. I'm trying to get to Larissa and I have a true belief about the right road based on a lucky guess. My friend knows the way because she's driven it before. Knowledge is obviously better here.

Case 2: I believe my flight leaves at 3pm because I misread the confirmation email (it said 3pm but I thought I saw it in the subject line, when actually I was looking at the wrong email entirely). It does leave at 3pm. A colleague knows the departure time because she checked the airline website this morning. Knowledge is better.

Case 3: Trivia night. I guess that the capital of Burkina Faso is Ouagadougou — pure luck, I just like the sound of the word. My teammate knows it. Knowledge wins, no question.

Case 4: I have a true belief that my partner isn't cheating on me, based on a vague feeling that I can't articulate. My friend has the same true belief about her partner but based on years of actual evidence — shared finances, consistent stories, no late nights unaccounted for, etc. I want to say her state is epistemically better than mine. But here's the thing: if the belief is true either way, and the relationship is fine either way, what exactly is better about her situation? The outcomes are identical. I still feel like knowledge is better, but I'm not sure I can say why.

Case 5: I believe a mushroom is safe to eat because I like its color. It is safe. You know it's safe because you can identify the species. Knowledge is better, and here the difference feels massive — your state is action-guiding in a way mine isn't, because if we encounter a different mushroom tomorrow, you can discriminate and I can't.

Case 6: This is the one that messes me up. Suppose I have a terminal illness and there's a small chance that believing I'll recover will trigger a placebo effect that gives me extra months. I genuinely believe I'll recover, based on a complete misreading of my test results. My belief is true — I happen to be in the placebo-responsive subgroup. My doctor knows I'm in that subgroup (she ran the genetic test) and knows the placebo response is real. But here's the weird part: in this case, my true belief seems better for me than the doctor's knowledge, because the doctor's knowledge doesn't come with the belief that I'll recover — it comes with the belief that a placebo response might happen. The actual recovery-promoting belief is mine, and it's a mere true belief. So is knowledge better only ceteris paribus, and the ceteris aren't paribus here?

Case 7: I believe truly that my investment will go up, based on a tip from a cab driver who "has a feeling." It goes up. My friend knows it'll go up based on her analysis of the company's balance sheet. I want to say her knowledge is epistemically better than my true belief, but I made the same amount of money. Is the value of knowledge over true belief ever purely epistemic, or does it have to cash out in practical terms eventually?

Case 8: A student believes the answer is 12 because she copied it. Another student knows it's 12 because she did the proof. Knowledge is better — but here the value seems to be about the student, not the belief. The first student can't answer the next question; the second can. So is the value of knowledge really about the belief-state itself, or about the capacities it's connected to?

So here's what I want: give me a principle (or a small set of candidates) that captures which of these verdicts hang together and which don't. In particular, tell me whether Case 6 forces me to admit that knowledge isn't always better than true belief, or whether I'm just confusing epistemic value with prudential value there. And if the principle says something about cases I haven't thought of, lay those out — I want to see if the principle predicts my verdicts before I have them.
