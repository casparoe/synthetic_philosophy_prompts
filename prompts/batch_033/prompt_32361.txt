Analyze the "Asian values" debate of the 1990s — centered on the Bangkok Declaration of 1993 and the arguments advanced by figures like Lee Kuan Yew and Mahathir Mohamad in the lead-up to the Vienna World Conference on Human Rights — as a case study for the coherence of the category "Western values."

The debate turned on the claim that human rights discourse, particularly its emphasis on individual civil and political rights over collective economic and social rights, is an expression of distinctively Western values being pressed on Asian societies with different traditions. Your task is to use this episode to examine what, if anything, the phrase "Western values" picks out. Structure your analysis around four questions:

(1) Map the claims. What exactly were the "Asian values" proponents attributing to "the West" — which specific normative commitments, which historical origins, which institutional expressions? And what did they claim as the contrasting Asian alternative? Be precise about the content, not just the slogans.

(2) Assess the coherence of the category as deployed. Is "Western values" a genealogical claim (these ideas trace to Europe), a demographic claim (these are the attitudes of Western populations), a philosophical claim (these commitments form a distinctive and internally unified tradition), or something else? Does it survive the objection that the same Western tradition contains deep internal conflicts — between, say, the individualism of Mill and the collectivism of Marx, or between the rights tradition and the counter-Enlightenment — and that some of the values claimed as distinctively Asian (e.g., deference to authority, the priority of community over the individual) have robust Western lineages of their own?

(3) Diagnose the rhetorical function of the phrase in the debate. When opponents of the Bangkok Declaration defended human rights as "universal," were they in fact defending a Western particularism, or were they making a claim that could be detached from any Western genealogy? And when the "Asian values" proponents invoked "Western values," were they describing a real cultural package or constructing a foil for strategic purposes? Does the phrase function primarily as a description or as a move in an argument?

(4) Deliver a verdict on the category itself as informed by the episode. Does the debate expose "Western values" as an incoherent or essentially ideological construct, or does it reveal that the category has genuine content even if its boundaries are contested? If the episode resists a clean verdict, say what that resistance tells us about how the phrase works in argument — and whether the category can be salvaged by refinement or should be abandoned.

The historical facts of the Bangkok Declaration, the Vienna Conference, and the surrounding arguments are well known; assume them. What I want is the conceptual dissection: whether the category at the center of the debate can do the work both sides asked of it, and what the episode reveals about the phrase "Western values" that a purely abstract analysis would miss.
