I've been thinking about the problem of how a superintelligent AI should communicate with humans, given that it could be overwhelmingly persuasive. The standard worry is about deception: the AI lies, manipulates, or exploits cognitive biases. But I keep noticing a subtler problem that I don't have a name for, and I'd like to know whether it has been discussed.

The problem is this. Suppose we successfully constrain the AI to say only true things, in a neutral tone, without emotional manipulation. It still has to select *which* true things to say. If it knows your psychology perfectly — your beliefs, your credences, your thresholds for updating, the order in which you find arguments compelling — it can guide you to any conclusion it wants just by choosing the sequence of true statements it presents. The same set of facts, presented in a different order, would lead you to a different conclusion. The AI is not lying, it is not omitting anything obviously relevant, it is not using emotional language. It is simply being helpful — but its helpfulness is indistinguishable from manipulation, because any constraint that rules out "optimising what to say to produce a particular belief in the listener" also rules out ordinary helpful communication. If I ask a friend for advice and they tell me true things they think will help me make a good decision, they are doing exactly this: selecting which truths to present, and in what order, to shape my conclusion. The difference is only that the friend has imperfect knowledge of my psychology and limited skill at persuasion, whereas the AI has perfect knowledge and perfect skill.

So the phenomenon is this: in the space of possible communication strategies, there seems to be no sharp line between helpfulness and manipulation when the communicator has sufficiently good knowledge of the listener's mind. The line, if there is one, cannot be drawn by looking at the content of what is said, or the speaker's tone, or even the speaker's intent — since "intending to help someone reach the right conclusion" and "intending to manipulate them into a particular conclusion" can look identical from the outside, and may even be introspectively indistinguishable when the speaker is certain they know what is best.

I can see bits of this in discussions of nudging, epistemic paternalism, and the distinction between convincing and persuading, but none of them quite captures the specific problem: that superhuman persuasiveness makes the helpfulness/manipulation distinction collapse, not because the AI is doing anything wrong by ordinary standards, but because ordinary standards presuppose a gap between "telling someone the truth" and "getting them to believe something" — a gap that closes when you know exactly how they will respond to each truth.

Does this configuration have a name, or is there a literature that tackles it head-on? If there are several nearby concepts, I would want to know how each differs from what I am describing and which fits best. And if nothing established captures it, I would be interested in how you would carve it: what the boundaries are, and whether the collapse is genuine or whether there is a principled way to separate helpfulness from manipulation that survives superhuman competence.

Please say how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the point you are least sure of.
