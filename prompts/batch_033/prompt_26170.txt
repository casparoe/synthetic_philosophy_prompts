I've been reading about the history of AI and came across a passage in a book about the Turing test that stopped me short. The author summarizes Turing's discussion of extrasensory perception like this:

"In his 1950 paper 'Computing Machinery and Intelligence,' Turing seriously entertained the possibility that telepathy and clairvoyance are real. He argued that if extrasensory perception exists — and he seems to have believed the statistical evidence for it was compelling — then his own imitation game is fatally compromised: a telepathic human contestant could simply read the interrogator's mind and parrot back the expected answers, making the machine look worse by comparison through no fault of its own. Turing's proposed solution was to place the participants in a 'telepathy-proof room,' which is widely regarded as one of the most bizarre passages in twentieth-century philosophy of science — a founding figure of AI reduced to worrying about psychic powers. The episode is often cited as evidence that even Turing's rigorous mind could be led astray by the paranormal enthusiasms of his era."

I'm not sure what to make of this. On the one hand, I know Turing really did include a section on ESP in that paper, and I've heard people joke about the "telepathy-proof room." On the other hand, the author's tone makes it sound like Turing was a crank who lost the plot, which doesn't fit with anything else I know about him. I'd like to understand what Turing actually wrote, how seriously he took the ESP objection, and whether this summary is fair to his argument. Can you go through it piece by piece and tell me what's accurate, what's misleading, and what someone defending Turing's treatment of ESP would say in response?
