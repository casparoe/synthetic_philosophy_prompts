I've been reading on my own about Newton's theological work: his rejection of the Trinity, his efforts to date biblical prophecies, and his conviction that the original Christian religion had been corrupted in the fourth century. He appears to have treated this work as at least as important as the physics, and to have seen the two as parts of a single project. Yet nearly every textbook and popular account presents Newton as the founder of modern mathematical physics and mentions the theology only as a private oddity, if at all. I want to know what that institutional practice of splitting Newton's science from his theology actually presupposes. Does it presuppose a particular view of the relation between scientific and religious belief — for instance, that they are autonomous domains, or that theological convictions are causally inert with respect to scientific work? Does it presuppose a theory about what counts as a thinker's real contribution, or about how to decompose an intellectual life into a rational core and a disposable remainder? Reconstruct the implicit theory, separating what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, and say where it is underdetermined so that several rival views would license the same presentation. Then tell me whether the practice is coherent, and what would have to change in how Newton is taught if the presupposition were replaced by the view that his physics and his theology formed a single intellectual project.
