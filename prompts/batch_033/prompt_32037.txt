I teach a graduate seminar on distributive justice, and we have been stuck for two weeks on whether prioritarianism actually solves the leveling-down objection or merely relabels it. I would like your credence on the following proposition:

**"Prioritarianism is a genuinely distinct theory from telic egalitarianism, and it succeeds in avoiding the leveling-down objection in a way that telic egalitarianism cannot."**

Please decompose your credence into the sub-questions below, assigning a rough probability to each with a sentence or two of reasoning. Then state how you combine them into an overall number, and tell me which term your credence is most sensitive to — that is, which sub-question, if you changed your mind about it, would shift the overall number the most.

Sub-questions:

**(a)** Is the distinction between "equality is intrinsically valuable" and "benefits to the worse off have intrinsically greater weight" a distinction with a real normative difference, or are these two ways of describing the same underlying structure? (Your credence that they are genuinely distinct, not merely notational variants.)

**(b)** Conditional on (a) being a genuine distinction, does prioritarianism's way of avoiding the leveling-down objection — roughly, leveling down benefits no individual, and the prioritarian evaluates outcomes only by weighted individual well-being — succeed without tacitly relying on egalitarian intuitions that would themselves be vulnerable to leveling down?

**(c)** Does prioritarianism face a residual leveling-down-type problem of its own — for instance, when we consider choices that affect the size or composition of the population, or when we compare outcomes where the identities of the worse off change — and, if it does, is that residual problem less severe than the original leveling-down objection is for egalitarianism?

**(d)** Setting aside the leveling-down debate entirely: even if prioritarianism handles that objection well, is it the most plausible view among those that give extra weight to the worse off? (Your credence that it beats sufficientarianism, leximin, and hybrid views on other desiderata such as the separateness of persons, the demands of interpersonal comparability, and sensitivity to the absolute level of well-being.)

If you think I have omitted a sub-question that belongs in the decomposition — for example, something about the relationship between the currency of distributive justice and the structure of the weighting function, or about whether the whole debate presupposes a kind of impersonal goodness that some views reject — please add it, justify its inclusion, and fold it into your number.
