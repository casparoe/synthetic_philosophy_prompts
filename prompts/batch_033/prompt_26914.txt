Draft: "What Thomson's Lamp Teaches Us About Determination"

Thomson's lamp is standardly presented as a puzzle about the physical possibility of supertasks. A lamp is switched on and off infinitely many times in a finite interval: on at t = 0, off at t = 1/2, on at t = 3/4, and so forth, with each switching taking half the time of the previous one. At t = 1, the series of switchings is complete, and we ask: is the lamp on or off? The natural answer is that the description simply fails to determine a final state — the lamp's condition at t = 1 is not just unknown but indeterminate relative to the specification of the supertask. Thomson himself took this to show that supertasks are impossible, but I want to suggest that the real lesson is different and more general.

The lamp's final state is underdetermined because the sequence of states (on, off, on, off, ...) has no limit. But notice what this reveals: a complete specification of what happens at every moment before t = 1 does not, in general, determine what happens at t = 1. Determination across a limit requires convergence. This is not a quirk of infinite sequences; it is a structural feature of how descriptions relate to the worlds they describe. The same point arises in classical mechanics, where a particle may reach a singularity in finite time and the equations of motion fail to determine its subsequent trajectory — Norton's dome being the best-known example. In both cases, we have a complete specification of the state at all times before some boundary, and yet the state at the boundary is not forced by that specification. The temptation is to treat this as a defect of the description and to demand a more complete one. But I argue that the demand is misplaced: the descriptions are complete, and the world itself can be indeterminate at such boundaries. The lesson of Thomson's lamp is not that supertasks are impossible, but that determinism is a substantive claim about the world — one that can fail even when the laws and the initial conditions are fully specified — and that the failure is not a failure of description but a genuine metaphysical possibility.

I am unsure whether the analogy between Thomson's lamp and Norton's dome is as tight as I want it to be. In the lamp case, the indeterminacy arises from the non-convergence of a discrete sequence; in the dome case, it arises from the non-uniqueness of solutions to a differential equation at a point where Lipschitz continuity fails. These are different mathematical phenomena, and I worry that calling both "indeterminacy at a boundary" papers over a distinction that matters. I'd be grateful for thoughts on whether the analogy can be defended or whether I should recast the argument without it.
