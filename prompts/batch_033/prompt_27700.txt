I cover the mind and brain beat for a general-interest science magazine, which means I receive a steady flow of papers, press releases, and book pitches claiming, in one way or another, that conscious experience is causally inert: that the feeling of deciding, intending, or attending is produced by the brain after the fact and does no work of its own. My editors want me to handle these claims responsibly rather than either dismissing them out of hand or repeating them credulously, and I am not a philosopher by training. I need a reusable editorial procedure I can run against any such claim in about half an hour before I commit to covering it, with clear points where I should stop with a no, and clear points where I should hand the question to someone with philosophical training because the issue is conceptual rather than empirical.

Please turn the epiphenomenalist position, together with the paradox of phenomenal judgment, into a decision procedure for my beat. The questions should be ordered so that each later question depends on the earlier ones; for each question, say what counts as a sufficient answer. In particular, I want the paradox of phenomenal judgment built in as an explicit test: if the claim were true, would it undercut the reliability of the very judgments, introspective reports, and experimental self-reports on which the claim rests, and how should that affect the coverage decision? Then run the procedure on three cases: a neuroscience finding that measurable brain activity precedes a person's reported awareness of a decision; a philosophical argument that phenomenal properties are epiphenomenal because every physical effect has a sufficient physical cause; and a popular-science claim that the self is a story the brain confabulates after acting. Make sure at least one of the cases yields a verdict I will find inconvenient, and end by stating plainly what the procedure cannot decide.
