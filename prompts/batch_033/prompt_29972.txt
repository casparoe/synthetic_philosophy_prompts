And here's the student's answer (about 500 words):

"The illusionist position, as articulated by Frankish, holds that phenomenal consciousness — the kind of consciousness that involves subjective, qualitative 'what-it's-like' properties — does not exist. According to illusionism, when we introspect, we mistakenly take ourselves to be detecting private, intrinsic, non-physical qualia, but in reality these are misrepresentations generated by our cognitive architecture. Illusionism should be distinguished from eliminativism: eliminativists deny that consciousness exists at all, whereas illusionists grant that we have experiences in the functional sense (access consciousness, reportability, etc.) but deny that these experiences have the special phenomenal properties we intuitively attribute to them. Dennett's work on consciousness is often read as a form of illusionism, though he himself prefers the term 'illusionism' for only some aspects of his view.

The meta-problem of consciousness, introduced by David Chalmers, is the problem of explaining why we think there is a hard problem of consciousness in the first place. That is, why do humans have such strong intuitions that phenomenal consciousness exists as something over and above the physical? The meta-problem is supposed to be tractable by standard cognitive science: we can investigate the psychological mechanisms that generate our sense of a phenomenal 'given.'

Now, the meta-problem clearly supports illusionism. If we can fully explain why we believe in phenomenal consciousness without ever appealing to phenomenal consciousness itself, then we have no reason to posit its existence. The belief can be explained away as a kind of cognitive illusion. This is essentially an inference to the best explanation: the best explanation of our intuitions about phenomenality doesn't require phenomenality to be real, so we should conclude it isn't. This is why illusionists like Frankish have spent so much time on the meta-problem — solving it would essentially prove illusionism correct.

One might object that explaining why we have a belief doesn't show the belief is false. But in this case, the belief in question is about a special kind of property that is supposed to resist physical explanation. If the meta-problem shows that our reasons for believing in such properties are generated by cognitive mechanisms that would produce the belief regardless of whether the properties exist, then the belief is undercut. It's like explaining why someone believes in ghosts without ghosts existing — once you've done that, the belief no longer carries evidential weight.

There is a worry, however, that illusionism can't distinguish between conscious and unconscious perception. If there's nothing phenomenal about conscious experience, what makes a consciously seen stimulus different from an unconsciously processed one? Illusionists can respond that the difference is functional: conscious states are those that are globally available for reasoning, verbal report, and action guidance, while unconscious states lack this availability. This is essentially the global workspace account, which fits naturally with illusionism."
