My cousin served two tours in Iraq, and for years we have been circling the same argument: whether he did something wrong by fighting in a war that he now agrees should not have been fought. I recently read a book on the ethics of war to try to think through it properly, and I came across a passage that summed up a view it called "the moral equality of combatants":

"Just war theory has traditionally kept two questions apart: whether a war is just in the first place (the jus ad bellum question) and how it may be fought (the jus in bello question). On the standard view, these two questions are independent in the strongest sense: the justice or injustice of the war itself has no bearing whatsoever on the moral situation of the soldiers who fight it. This is the doctrine of the moral equality of combatants. A soldier fighting for an unjust cause has exactly the same moral rights as a soldier fighting for a just one, because individual soldiers cannot be expected to judge whether their country's cause is just, and in any case they are only following lawful orders. Soldiers on both sides are therefore permitted to kill enemy soldiers and forbidden to kill civilians, and neither side's soldiers may be held personally responsible for the war's crimes. This is why we can condemn a war as aggression while still honoring the aggressor's soldiers for fighting honorably."

I am inclined to accept the passage, partly because it would let me stop fighting with my cousin, but I worry the book is telling me what I want to hear.
