I'm a PhD student in behavioral genetics and a teaching assistant for an upper-level undergraduate course on interpreting heritability. Below is the exam question and one student's answer. Please grade the answer as I would for this course: assign a mark out of 10 and, more importantly, provide point-by-point comments identifying exactly where credit is earned and where it is lost, including any subtle errors a hasty grader might overlook. The comments should be specific enough that I could hand them to the student.

Question: A well-conducted twin study in a particular population estimates the narrow-sense heritability (h²) of a cognitive trait at 0.6. A popular article about the study reports that genes are 60% responsible for a person's IQ and the rest comes from the environment. Assess this interpretation. Your answer should say what h² does and does not license about (i) an individual's trait, (ii) the likely effects of environmental interventions, and (iii) mean differences between groups.

Student answer: Heritability is the proportion of phenotypic variance in a population attributable to genetic differences among individuals, so the article is wrong to say genes are 60% responsible for any particular person's IQ. Heritability is a population-level statistic: it partitions variance across individuals, not causes within a single person. A high h² also does not mean the trait is fixed or that environmental interventions cannot work. Height is highly heritable, yet average height has risen with improved nutrition, so a trait can be heritable and environmentally malleable at once. The estimate also says nothing about why group means differ: a trait can be highly heritable within every group while the between-group difference is entirely environmental, because the sources of within-group and between-group variation need not be the same. Finally, since h² is the ratio of genetic to total variance, it will tend to fall if environments are equalized; equal environments leave less environmental variance, so the genetic proportion drops. The 0.6 estimate is specific to this population, environment, and time, and cannot be exported to other settings or treated as an upper limit on how much an environmental intervention could change the trait.
