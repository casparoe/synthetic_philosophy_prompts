I keep encountering a line of argument in discussions of the many-worlds interpretation that goes roughly like this: the wavefunction branches like a tree, and just as a tree's branches have different thicknesses, the branches of the wavefunction have different "weights" given by the squared amplitude. The Born rule — that the probability of finding yourself on a branch is proportional to its squared amplitude — is then supposed to follow naturally: of course you are more likely to end up on a thicker branch. The analogy is deployed to argue that the Born rule is not an extra postulate tacked onto many-worlds but is already implicit in the structure of the branching itself.

I want you to audit this analogy. Give me:

(a) A correspondence table: what in the quantum formalism maps to the trunk, to a branch, to the thickness of a branch, and to the act of "finding yourself" on one branch rather than another.

(b) An account of where the analogy breaks down. In particular, address the following worry: in a real tree, thickness is a physical magnitude that exists prior to anyone's epistemic situation — a branch either is thicker or it is not. But in many-worlds, the squared amplitude is not obviously a magnitude of the same kind. The formalism gives you a number attached to each branch, but the claim that this number should govern credence is precisely what is at issue. Does the analogy do any work beyond redescribing the problem in arboreal language?

(c) An assessment of whether the conclusion the analogy is used to support — that the Born rule requires no additional postulate — survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If it does not, say what argumentative work the analogy was doing in making that conclusion seem plausible.

(d) If you think the tree-thickness analogy fails, briefly say whether any alternative analogy (from decision theory, self-locating uncertainty, or elsewhere) fares better at making the Born rule look natural in many-worlds, and what that better analogy would have to capture that the tree analogy misses.
