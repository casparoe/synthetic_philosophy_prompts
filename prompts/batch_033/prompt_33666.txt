My mother was diagnosed with Alzheimer's two years ago, and I have been trying to get clear about what makes someone the same person over time — or what makes someone a self at all. I started writing down my spontaneous reactions to different cases, and I would like to know whether there is a coherent principle behind them, or whether some of my verdicts need to go.

Here are the cases and my verdicts:

- Myself: a self, obviously.
- My mother now, even though she no longer remembers my childhood and sometimes does not recognize me: still a self, and still the same self she was, though diminished.
- A split-brain patient whose left hand unbuttons a shirt the right hand is buttoning: I genuinely do not know whether to say one self or two.
- Google or the Catholic Church: not a self, even though each acts in ways that look intentional and maintains a kind of identity over time.
- A future AI that keeps a detailed autobiographical record and tells a coherent life story about its experiences: I am reluctant to call it a self, though I cannot quite say why.
- Someone in a fugue state who has lost all autobiographical memory and adopted a new identity: one self, the same one as before, just radically disconnected from their past.
- A human mind successfully uploaded to a computer, where it continues without interruption: a self, and the same self as the biological original, as long as the transfer was seamless.
- An eight-month fetus: I lean toward saying not yet a self, but I am uneasy about it.

Is there a principle of selfhood that captures most or all of these? If no single principle gets them all without becoming ad hoc, which verdicts should I reconsider, and why? Finally, what does your proposed principle say about a case I have not listed — someone who has been in a minimally conscious state for ten years?
