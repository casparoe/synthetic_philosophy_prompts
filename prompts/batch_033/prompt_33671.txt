I am drafting a position statement on the debate between democracy and epistocracy, and I would like a consistency audit of my current commitments before I commit them to print. Here is the set I hold:

(1) The justification of democracy is instrumental. Universal equal suffrage is worth retaining only if, on balance, it produces better political outcomes — decisions that more reliably protect basic rights, promote welfare, and avoid serious injustice — than the feasible alternatives.

(2) The empirical case for voter competence is weak. Most citizens in large modern democracies are rationally ignorant: because the chance of casting a decisive vote is minuscule and political information is costly, most voters know little and rely on party identification, group loyalties, and low-information cues.

(3) If a feasible epistocratic arrangement — for instance, plural voting weighted by a test of basic political knowledge, or a limited veto for a well-constructed expert body — could be shown to produce reliably better decisions than equal suffrage, it would not be objectionable merely because it distributes formal political power unequally.

(4) Unequal political power is not unjust in itself. An arrangement that gave some citizens more votes than others would be permissible in principle if it predictably served the interests and standing of everyone, including those with fewer votes.

(5) Nevertheless, I reject weighting the franchise by education, income, or occupation, and I reject disenfranchising any competent adult. Those proxies are epistemically crude, and their use would entrench the very inequalities of class and status that democratic politics ought to be able to scrutinise.

(6) I also hold that every competent adult citizen has a claim to equal political standing. A citizen subject to laws she had no equal say in authoring is dominated unless the unequal arrangement can be justified to her in terms she could reasonably accept — and not merely by showing that the arrangement produces better aggregate outcomes.

(7) As a practical matter, I oppose introducing epistocratic institutions in any contemporary state. Given the actual distribution of political knowledge, any workable test or expert body would shift power towards the already advantaged, invite capture, and entrench the preferences of a narrow professional class; the likely result would be worse for the worst-off, not better.

I would like an audit of this particular set, not a survey of the literature. Go through the seven commitments and analyse which pairs are in genuine tension, which pairs merely appear to be in tension, and which single revision would do the most to make the whole set hang together. Rank the tensions by severity, and for each say what would have to give and what the cheapest repair would cost. Please also say what would change your mind: which argument or empirical finding would most alter your ranking or your overall verdict, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail. Please write your answer in American English.
