[Exam question]
Kripke, in Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language, presents a skeptical paradox about meaning and rule-following. Explain (a) what the paradox is, (b) why dispositionalist accounts fail to resolve it, and (c) what Kripke's Wittgenstein proposes as a "skeptical solution." Does the skeptical solution refute the skeptic, or does it do something else? Be precise.

[Student answer]
The skeptical paradox Kripke attributes to Wittgenstein is that no fact about a speaker's past can determine what they meant by a word or rule. Kripke illustrates this with the "quus" example: if I've only ever computed sums with numbers smaller than 57, there's no fact about my past usage that makes it the case that I meant addition rather than "quaddition" (where x quus y = x + y if x, y < 57, and 5 otherwise). Any candidate fact — my past applications, my explicit thoughts, my dispositions — can be reinterpreted to be consistent with either plus or quus. So meaning seems to vanish: there's no fact of the matter about what anyone means by anything.

Dispositionalist accounts try to ground meaning in what I'm disposed to say or do. Kripke's Wittgenstein rejects these for several reasons. First, my dispositions are finite: I'd make mistakes or die before computing very large sums, so my dispositions don't determine the correct answer for all possible cases. Second, and more fundamentally, dispositions are descriptive — they're about what I would do — but meaning is normative: it's about what I should do. Dispositions can't capture the distinction between making a mistake and following a different rule.

The skeptical solution accepts the skeptic's conclusion that there are no meaning-constituting facts about the individual in isolation. But instead of concluding that meaning-talk is nonsense, Kripke's Wittgenstein argues that we can legitimize meaning-talk by looking at the social context. What makes it correct for me to answer "125" to "68+57" is the fact that my response matches what my linguistic community would do. The community provides the standard of correctness. When someone deviates systematically, we stop attributing competence to them. This is a skeptical solution because it concedes the skeptical point (no individualistic facts ground meaning) while showing that our ordinary meaning-attributing practices are justified on different grounds — namely, social agreement.

The skeptical solution does refute the skeptic in a sense, because it shows that meaning-talk is still legitimate. But it refutes the skeptic on the skeptic's own terms by changing what counts as a solution — it's not a straight solution that finds the fact the skeptic says is missing, but it does restore our right to talk about meaning.
