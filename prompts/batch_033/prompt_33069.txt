I'm taking an upper-level philosophy of language seminar this semester, and we've been working through Quine's "Two Dogmas" and the early chapters of *Word and Object*. Before the course, I'd read a popular introduction to twentieth-century philosophy — one of those well-reviewed books that tries to make difficult figures accessible to a general audience — and I now realize that my memory of Quine's indeterminacy thesis was shaped almost entirely by that book. I went back and found the passage. Here it is, from the chapter on post-positivist philosophy of language:

"Quine's famous 'gavagai' thought experiment shows that when a field linguist tries to translate a newly encountered language, there is simply no fact of the matter about what its words mean. Imagine a linguist observing a native speaker who says 'gavagai' whenever a rabbit scurries past. The linguist provisionally writes down 'rabbit' as the translation, but Quine argues that 'gavagai' could equally well mean 'undetached rabbit part,' 'temporal rabbit-stage,' 'rabbitness,' or anything else that happens to be present whenever rabbits are present. No amount of pointing, nodding, or further questioning can settle the matter, because any additional evidence the linguist gathers will itself have to be interpreted through the very translation scheme that is in question. Quine's conclusion is that meaning is radically indeterminate — there is no such thing as what a word really means, and all translation is ultimately a matter of practical convenience rather than correctness. For this reason Quine is often described as a meaning skeptic: he showed that communication is, at bottom, impossible, and that we can never genuinely know what anyone else is talking about."

Now that I've read some Quine firsthand, something about this summary unsettles me, but I'm having trouble separating the genuine Quine from the summary's gloss. I know that Quine was a behaviorist about meaning — he thought the only admissible evidence for a translation is publicly observable behavior — and that he emphatically did not think he was showing that communication fails. He repeatedly says we get along fine. But I'm not sure whether the summary's errors are merely matters of emphasis, or whether it's systematically misrepresenting the argument and its upshot. Could you go through this paragraph sentence by sentence and tell me what a careful Quine scholar would accept, what they'd wince at, and what is simply wrong? I'm especially interested in whether the final sentence — that communication is impossible — follows from anything Quine actually argued, or whether the summary has drifted from indeterminacy into something closer to a caricature. And if the summary is indeed unfair, what would a fair, one-paragraph popularization of the indeterminacy thesis look like?
