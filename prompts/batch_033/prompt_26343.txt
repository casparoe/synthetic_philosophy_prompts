I'm on the ethics committee at a mid-sized hospital, and we get maybe three or four cases a year where an advance directive says one thing but the patient, now cognitively impaired (dementia, usually, sometimes a TBI), seems to want something else. The classic case: a woman writes an advance directive refusing antibiotics for pneumonia if she ever has severe dementia, then ten years later she has severe dementia and gets pneumonia, and she seems perfectly content, smiles at visitors, enjoys pudding. The directive says let her die; the family says she's clearly not suffering. Our committee has to make a recommendation, and honestly we're just winging it each time — different members invoke different principles, we split along the same lines every meeting, and there's no consistency across cases. Last month we had two cases that were structurally identical and we went opposite ways, which nobody felt great about.

I've been reading some of the philosophy on this — Dworkin on precedent autonomy, Dresser on experiential interests, Jaworska on the capacities that survive in dementia — and I want to turn whatever the best philosophical account is into an actual procedure we can run. Not a policy exactly, more like a structured decision tool: a sequence of questions we ask about each case, with guidance on what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure says "follow the directive," where it says "override the directive," and where it says "this is a judgment call, here's what's at stake and who should make it." The procedure has to be usable by a committee of clinicians, social workers, a chaplain, and one community member — most of whom haven't read any of this stuff. It should take maybe 45 minutes to run. And it should be honest about which steps are doing real normative work versus which are just clarifying facts. If the procedure sometimes tells us to do something that feels wrong, I want to know that too — test it on at least two cases, including one where it gives an answer you wouldn't personally endorse. Also, one thing I've noticed is that a lot of the philosophical debate turns on whether the demented patient is "the same person" as the one who wrote the directive, and I'm not sure our committee even has a working view on personal identity, so the procedure should either avoid that question entirely or give us a way to answer it without doing metaphysics.
