Im a novelist, not a philosopher, and the thing I keep tripping over in my work is that my characters' inner lives are mostly failure: a woman who believes her husband is alive when he isn't, a man whose memory of the fire is largely confabulated, a kid who calls the raccoon a dog. I want to understand, in a strictly naturalistic way, what it is for a physical system like a person to have states that are about something — where "about" is allowed to be false, empty, or never-yet-encountered. I've heard the names Dretske, Millikan, and the teleosemantics crowd, plus the asymmetric-dependence camp, but before I read any of them I want the spec I should be grading them against.

So: write the desiderata for an adequate naturalistic theory of mental content. Give me the requirements list — the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it cant collapse, the empirical constraints it has to respect — with the case or thought experiment behind each. At minimum make sure the list confronts: misrepresentation; the frog and its fly-detector (it snaps at flies, but it will also snap at BBs); why the content is "fly" rather than "small dark moving thing" or "retinal stimulation"; thoughts about things no ancestor ever encountered (quarks, unicorns, next Tuesday); and the Swampman-style question whether a molecule-for-molecule duplicate with no history has the same content. But I want you to tell me which of those are genuine requirements and which are red herrings, not just rubber-stamp them.

For each requirement, say whether it is non-negotiable or a taste of a particular camp. If some pair of them can't be jointly satisfied, say so — that is the part I most want to see, because a desiderata list is only interesting if it might be impossible. Also tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude the popular theories. End with a very short scorecard: a sentence or two each for indicator/causal accounts, teleosemantics, and asymmetric-dependence accounts against your list. No citations, no reading list.

Purpose: adequacy means good enough to be true of actual evolved brains rather than a toy model, and good enough that the aboutness it assigns to a person matches, at least roughly, what a careful reader would say the character is thinking. If you need a table, use one. Hard cap: 900 words, scorecard included.
