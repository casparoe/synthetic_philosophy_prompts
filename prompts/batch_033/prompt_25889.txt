I've gotten pretty convinced by the Dennett line that Darwin and Turing each pulled off a "strange inversion of reasoning" — showing that competence (design, intelligence) can exist without comprehension (a designer who understands, a mind that knows what it's doing). Natural selection builds eyes without knowing what eyes are for; a transformer generates coherent paragraphs without understanding the propositions. And I buy the bigger claim that this isn't just two cool examples but a deep structual fact: comprehension is a late, expensive, fragile thing that rides on top of oceans of blind competence.

But I've noticed I can't easily say what would make me change my mind, and that bothers me. So: what would count against this view? Not "what would show comprehension is everywhere" — that's a strawman. I mean things like: what if the competence/comprehension distinction itself can't be drawn in a principled way? What if there are domains where you genuinely can't get the competence without the comprehension, and those domains turn out to be central rather than peripheral? What if the historical claim is wrong — if the pre-Darwinian natural theologians already had something like the inversion, or if Turing's inversion was already implicit in earlier work on formal systems? What if the whole framing just collapses into "complex things can arise from simpler things," which nobody ever denied?

I want the defeaters sorted from fatal to merely awkward, and I want to know which ones a committed Dennettian would themselves say count against the view (not just what critics lob at it). And if the view has been insulated by qualifications — if every challenge can be met by saying "well, I didn't mean it that strongly" — tell me that honestly, and whether that kind of insulation is a problem for a philosophical thesis of this sort, or whether it's fine.
