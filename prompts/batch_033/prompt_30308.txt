So I'm in this political philosophy seminar and we read Sen's thing about the impossibility of a Paretian liberal, and I've been turning it over in my head for like a week because the argument seems airtight but the conclusion is obviously insane. Let me lay it out the way I understand it:

(1) Any decent social choice procedure should satisfy two principles. (a) Pareto: if literally everyone prefers X over Y, society should pick X over Y. (b) Minimal liberalism: each person should get to decide at least one thing about their own private life—formally, for each individual there's at least one pair of alternatives where their preference alone determines the social ranking, because it's nobody else's damn business.

(2) Now take two people, Prude and Lewd, and one copy of a scandalous book. Three options: Prude reads it, Lewd reads it, or nobody reads it. Prude's ranking, best to worst: nobody reads it, Prude reads it, Lewd reads it. (Prude thinks the book is corrupting but would rather suffer it himself than let Lewd be corrupted.) Lewd's ranking: Prude reads it, Lewd reads it, nobody reads it. (Lewd thinks Prude would really benefit from being shocked out of his prudishness, but if Prude won't bite, Lewd will at least enjoy the book rather than let it gather dust.)

(3) Minimal liberalism gives Prude the right to decide between "Prude reads it" and "nobody reads it" (since that's about his own reading), so society ranks nobody over Prude-reads. And it gives Lewd the right to decide between "Lewd reads it" and "nobody reads it," so society ranks Lewd-reads over nobody.

(4) By transitivity, society ranks Lewd-reads over Prude-reads.

(5) But now check everyone's actual preferences between those two: Prude prefers Prude-reads over Lewd-reads, and Lewd also prefers Prude-reads over Lewd-reads. Both want Prude to be the one who reads it. So by Pareto, society must rank Prude-reads over Lewd-reads.

(6) We've got a cycle: Lewd > nobody > Prude > Lewd. The procedure spits out no best alternative.

(7) So you can't have both Pareto and minimal liberalism. But Pareto seems basically right—how do you justify picking an option that everyone agrees is worse? And minimal liberalism seems like the absolute floor of respecting people as separate persons.

My classmates mostly want to blame Pareto by saying the preferences in the example are "meddlesome" and shouldn't be fed into the Pareto principle. But that feels like gerrymandering: if we start filtering which preferences count as legitimate inputs, where's the principled stopping point? And Prude's preference isn't even that nosy—he's willing to bear the cost himself (reading the book he hates) rather than impose it on Lewd. That seems almost decent. Meanwhile if we try to fix things by restricting liberalism to preferences only about your own actions, doesn't that just define the domain to make the paradox impossible by fiat, without actually resolving the underlying tension?

I can't tell if this is a genuine impossibility result showing two attractive values are fundamentally incompatible, or if there's a bait-and-switch in how liberalism gets formalized at step (3). Where exactly does the argument go wrong, and what do I have to pay to get out?
