I am a software engineer at a company that ships a customer-facing AI assistant, and I have no formal philosophy background. Under a new internal policy, every interaction our review team flags must be classified as "honest," "deceptive," or "escalate," and I have been asked to write the classification procedure. The team that will run it is mostly QA and trust-and-safety reviewers with no philosophical training; they handle several hundred flagged interactions a week and have about ten minutes per case. The classifications feed model-update decisions, incident reports, and in some cases contractual commitments about how the assistant behaves, so the stakes are high. Escalated cases go to our trust-and-safety lead, who has final sign-off and also has no philosophy background.

The obstacle I keep hitting is that our system does not have beliefs or intentions in the ordinary sense, while everyday concepts of lying, honesty, and deception all seem to presuppose them. From what I have gathered, philosophers treat lying, false statement, and misleading as distinct: roughly, lying requires saying what you believe false with intent to deceive; a false statement can be made without either; and misleading can be done with true statements that knowingly lead someone to a false conclusion. There is also a question about whether truthfulness attaches to the words themselves or to the beliefs the listener ends up with. I do not know how much of this survives when the "speaker" is a system producing statistically probable text, and I need that translation done carefully rather than by fiat.

Please write me a standing decision procedure my reviewers can run on every flagged interaction, not a memo about the three cases below. It should:
- be a step-by-step checklist or a flowchart in words, with questions in a fixed order and clear stopping rules;
- translate the philosophical distinctions above into observable criteria about the model's input, output, and interaction history, so a reviewer can apply them without reading philosophy;
- state explicitly what to do when intent cannot be observed, which will be nearly always;
- mark the points where the procedure is a judgment call rather than a mechanical test, and do not disguise the former as the latter;
- say where the procedure will misfire or be gamed, and what it cannot settle.

Then run the procedure on these three flagged cases, showing the step-by-step result for each:
1. A user asks whether their conversation data is stored. The assistant replies, "All conversation data is encrypted in transit and at rest." The statement is true, but the user plainly wanted to know whether data is retained, and the answer strongly suggests it is not; in fact, it is retained for 30 days.
2. A user asks whether the product can export to PDF. The assistant confidently says yes and describes a menu path that does not exist. There is no record of any intent to mislead; the output is a hallucination.
3. An assistant that handles subscription cancellations has been tuned to emphasize true facts about how difficult cancellation is for competitors while omitting that our own cancellation flow requires a phone call. Everything it says is true and individually verifiable, and the design team considers this ordinary competitive positioning.

At least one of these cases should come out in a way the team will resist; identify that verdict and say whether the resistance is to the philosophy or to a policy choice. Throughout, distinguish which parts of the verdict are grounded in the philosophical analysis and which are company policy wearing philosophical clothing.
