So I've been playing this game called Nomic with some friends from my philosophy club, and it's honestly been keeping me up at night. For those who don't know, Nomic is that game Peter Suber invented where the whole point is that you can change the rules — including the rules about how to change the rules. It starts with an initial ruleset, and players take turns proposing rule amendments which get voted on. What's wild is that the self-amendment mechanism isn't just a feature, it's basically the entire game.

Anyway, I got kind of obsessed with this and wrote up a short essay about what I think is the deepest puzzle in Nomic. I'd love it if you could read it and give me comments — specifically, I'm worried I might be missing something obvious, or that my argument has a hole I'm not seeing. Also curious whether you think the conclusion is actually interesting or just kind of trivial once you think about it. Here's the essay:

---

*On the Impossibility of Total Self-Amendment*

Nomic presents itself as a game where any rule can be changed. But I want to argue that there is at least one rule — or something rule-like — that cannot be changed without destroying the game, and that this reveals something important about the limits of self-amending systems generally.

Consider the initial ruleset of Nomic. Rule 101 says something like: "All players must abide by the rules." Rule 108 or thereabouts governs how rules are amended: a player proposes a change, it gets seconded, there's debate, a vote is held, and if it passes, the rule is changed. Now, players can and do amend Rule 108 itself — they might change the voting threshold, or eliminate the seconding requirement, or whatever. So far so good.

But here's the puzzle. Suppose the players vote to amend Rule 108 so that it says: "Henceforth, rules may be amended by any player unilaterally, at any time, without a vote." Has this amendment been validly adopted? Well, if the old Rule 108 was still in force at the moment of the vote, and the vote met its requirements, then yes — the amendment is valid, and now the new rule governs. But what if someone objects after the fact and says the amendment was invalid? Under the new rule, they could just unilaterally declare it invalid. And someone else could unilaterally declare *that* declaration invalid. The system seems to collapse into either chaos or a situation where raw power (who can shout loudest? who controls the shared document?) determines what the rules "are."

One might respond: well, this just shows that some amendments are stupid, not that they're impossible. But I think it shows something deeper. What makes the amendment procedure *itself* binding in the first place? In a normal game, the rulebook is backed by something external — the publisher, a governing body, social convention. In Nomic, the rulebook is supposed to be self-contained: the rules tell you how to change the rules. But this creates a regress problem. If I ask "why is the current amendment procedure the correct one?", the answer is "because it was adopted according to the previous amendment procedure." And why was *that* one correct? Because it was adopted according to the one before it. This chain traces back to the initial ruleset. But what grounds the initial ruleset? Nothing within the game — it's just stipulated by Suber, or agreed to by the players before play begins. So the self-amendment is never truly total; there's always an unamendable foundation that isn't itself justified by the system's own procedures.

This, I think, is a version of the problem legal philosophers talk about with constitutions and basic norms — Kelsen's Grundnorm, Hart's rule of recognition. But Nomic makes it vivid in a way that real legal systems don't, because in Nomic you can actually *try* to amend the amendment rule all the way down, and the game lets you see where it breaks.

So my questions for you: (1) Is the regress argument actually sound, or am I conflating two different senses of "grounding"? (2) Does the fact that players can just agree to stop playing and start a new game of Nomic with different initial rules dissolve the problem, or does that just push it back one level? (3) Is any of this actually interesting or am I just rediscovering Hart in a more convoluted way?

Thanks — really appreciate any thoughts, even if they're just pointing out where I've gone wrong.
