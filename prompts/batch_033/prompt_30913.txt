I've been teaching a grad seminar on late nineteenth-century philosophy and the rise of professionalized psychical research, and I want to give my students a puzzle about Sidgwicks evidential standards — not the metaphysics, just the way he treated cases. No sources needed, just think it through.

Here's the track record, as I've reconstructed it. I'm not quoting anyone, just summarizing what the man actually did, according to the standard story anyway.

- In 1863, Sidgwick and a friend messed around with table-turnings and got raps and movements that Sidgwick was convinced weren't caused by anyone present. He thought the phenomena were real but didn't take them as evidence of spirits.
- In the mid-1870s he and Eleanor sat with a bunch of professional mediums and came away largely disillusioned: most of it looked like fraud or self-deception, and they rejected those particular sittings.
- But he was impressed by Crookes's reports on D.D. Home, who produced strong physical phenomena under Crookes's observation; Sidgwick seems to have thought at least some of Home's stuff was probably genuine.
- When William Barrett and others started reporting thought-transference experiments, Sidgwick took them seriously and ended up convinced that telepathy between living persons is real, at least under some conditions.
- At the 1895 Cambridge sittings with Eusapia Palladino, the phenomena were caught out as trickery under tightened controls, and Sidgwick judged her fraudulent.
- With Leonora Piper, the American trance medium, he thought the data were genuinely impressive and hard to explain by ordinary means — she kept producing veridical information about dead people known to the sitters — but he still wouldn't infer survival, because telepathy from the sitters (or from living sources nearby) seemed like a live alternative.

Now suppose a new case lands on my desk. A trance medium gives a sitting to a sitter who never met the deceased and knows almost nothing about them. The medium produces a detailed statement about a specific event in the deceased's life. The event was never told to anyone while the deceased was alive, and no living person knew of it. The day before the sitting, the deceased, while alive, had written the memory down and sealed it in an envelope, but the envelope stayed in a safe deposit box until after the sitting, when it was opened and the details matched. The medium had no access to the box, and fraud seems out.

My question is: given Sidgwick's earlier rulings, what should he say about this? I'm less interested in the final answer than in the implicit rule he's running. What other rule fits his verdicts just as well? What does each rule predict about the sealed-envelope case? Give me your best guess about which rule is really his and why, and then give me a further case — a hypothetical or real one — that would cleanly distinguish the two.
