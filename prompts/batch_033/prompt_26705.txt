A couple weeks ago I was at a cabin weekend with some friends, and two of them got into an argument that basically ate the whole evening. We'd been talking about Newcomb's problem, which led somehow to Parfit's hitchhiker scenario — you know, the one where you're stranded in the desert, someone offers you a ride but says they don't trust you to pay them once you're safe, because at that point you'd have no self-interested reason to follow through. The hitchhiker insists he'll pay, but the driver points out that any rational agent, once rescued, would just keep the money. The question is whether the hitchhiker can do anything about that, rationally.

Here's roughly how the argument went between them. I'll call them Dan and Mira.

Dan kicked it off by saying the hitchhiker is just screwed: he can't sincerely bind himself, because he knows that once he's safe, the best thing to do is stiff the driver. And the driver knows he knows that. So no deal, no rescue. Tragic but airtight.

Mira said that's too narrow. She said the hitchhiker can rationally cultivate a disposition to keep promises even when it's locally against his interests — being a promise-keeper pays off across a life, because other people can then trust you. So the hitchhiker isn't being irrational; he's being globally rational by being locally "irrational."

Dan came back and said: wait, but the hitchhiker in the desert right now can't just *decide* to have that disposition. Dispositions aren't things you can adopt at will in the moment you need them. If he doesn't already have the disposition, he can't credibly signal it. And if he does already have it, then he's not really making a strategic choice — he's just acting out of character, which isn't the same as *rationally* choosing to pay.

Mira said that's actually the whole point: the rational thing to do is to *already be* the kind of person who keeps promises, and you can work on becoming that person over time. The rationality is in the character-engineering project, not in the moment-of-decision calculation. She said Dan was smuggling in the assumption that rationality only evaluates acts one at a time.

Dan said no, he's not smuggling anything — he's pointing out that "become a different person ahead of time" doesn't help the guy who's *already in the desert*. The scenario is about what that guy can rationally do *now*. If the answer is "nothing, he should have thought of this years ago," then the scenario is doing its job: it shows that act-level rationality sometimes traps you.

Mira got a bit frustrated and said Dan was treating the hitchhiker like a utility-maximizing ghost that can step outside its own psychology and reprogram itself, which is exactly the picture of agency she thinks is broken. She said real agents have identities, commitments, and self-conceptions that aren't just levers for expected utility.

Dan said that's all very nice but it doesn't solve the problem — it just relabels it. If the hitchhiker's "identity" makes him pay, then he's not acting on reasons, he's acting on programming. And if the programming happens to produce good outcomes, great, but that's not the same as the action being *rational*. A thermostat isn't rational.

At that point they looked at me and asked what I thought, and I honestly didn't know how to sort through it. Can you adjudicate this? Go through their exchange move by move and say who's right about which specific points, and whether there's something important that neither of them is seeing. I'm especially interested in whether Dan's last point — that acting from a cultivated disposition isn't the same as acting rationally — holds up or collapses under pressure.
