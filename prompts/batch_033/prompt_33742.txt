Two early-modern scholars have been arguing for a year about the right way to characterize Kepler's relationship to astrology. The first, S., defends a narrowing reading: Kepler thought the inherited astrological tradition was mostly superstition, and his project was to replace it with a small, physically respectable residue, namely planetary aspects understood as real geometrical and harmonic configurations acting through light on the soul and the sublunary world. On this reading, his continued horoscope-casting is a professional compromise or economic necessity, and his criticism of zodiacal signs' influence, houses, and rulerships is his considered verdict. The second, P., defends a reform-from-within reading: Kepler practiced astrology throughout his life, wrote in its defense against both credulous practitioners and wholesale despisers, and sought natural causes not because he wanted to eliminate astrology but because he thought natural philosophy would make it more reliable. On this reading, his rejection of specific doctrines is selective and internal, and his astrological practice remains part of his broader empirical program.

Draft the joint statement these two could co-sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Structure it as follows:

1. Numbered agreed-claims section: the biographical, textual, and conceptual claims both can accept, phrased neutrally and non-vacuously.
2. Disagreements table: each residual disagreement formulated so that each side would accept the wording of its own position.
3. Cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would genuinely move at least one side. For each crux, identify the kind of evidence or argument both would agree bears on it, for example how he handles his own predictive uncertainties, how much zodiacal and house-related language he retains in his annual calendars, how he justifies aspects in his optical and harmonic writings, and how his presentation differs when addressing patrons versus fellow scholars. Record in advance what each side predicts the evidence will show.
4. A closing note identifying which parts of their dispute are verbal rather than substantive, if any.

Do not cite secondary literature, and do not put any phrase in quotation marks as if it were Kepler's own words; paraphrase instead.

End with one sentence summarizing the deepest residual disagreement, as the final line, beginning "Summary:".
