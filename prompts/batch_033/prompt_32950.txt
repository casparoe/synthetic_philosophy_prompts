Read Charlie Chaplin's *Modern Times* (1936) through Marx's analysis of machinery and automation, with particular attention to the "Fragment on Machines" in the *Grundrisse*. In that text, Marx argues that under capitalism the worker does not employ the means of production; the means of production employ the worker. This reversal reaches its fullest expression in the automatic factory, where the worker becomes a mere "conscious linkage" between machines, and the accumulated skill and knowledge of social labor confront the worker as an alien power housed in fixed capital. At the same time, Marx insists that this very development contains an emancipatory potential: the general intellect, embodied in machinery, could eventually reduce necessary labor to a minimum and expand the realm of freedom — but only if the relations of production are transformed.

Chaplin's film dramatizes the first half of this argument brilliantly. The assembly line accelerates beyond human capacity, the feeding machine disciplines the worker's body during the one moment it might otherwise be his own, and the Tramp's nervous breakdown literalizes the idea that machinery under capitalist command does not serve the worker but drives him to collapse. But does the film ever gesture toward the second half of Marx's argument — the possibility that the forces of production themselves, decoupled from capitalist relations, might be liberating? The factory set-pieces are the film's most famous sequences, but the Tramp's arc also includes the department store scene (where he playfully inhabits the space of consumption after hours, rollerskating blindfolded through a world of commodities), the shack by the water (a return to precarity outside the wage relation), and the final image of the open road.

I am writing a paper that resists the common reading of *Modern Times* as a straightforward anti-technology film, and I want to take the film's comic set-pieces seriously as philosophical arguments rather than treating them as mere slapstick. I would like a careful analysis of specific sequences that asks: where does the film distinguish between machinery as such and machinery under capitalist command, and where does it fail or decline to make that distinction? Does the film offer any image of what a non-alienated relation to technology might look like, or does all machinery in Chaplin's world collapse into a disciplinary apparatus regardless of who owns it? And what should we make of the ending — the Tramp and the Gamin walking away down a country road toward an uncertain future — is this a Marxian refusal of the existing terms of industrial society, or does it concede too much to the idea that technology is irredeemable and that the only free life is one outside it?
