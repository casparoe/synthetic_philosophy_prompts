I work in the model risk group of a midsize consumer lender, and several times a year a business unit brings us a scoring model — built in-house or by a vendor — and asks us to sign off that it is fair across racial and other protected groups. Our sign-off is what allows the model to go into production, and we would be the ones defending that choice to regulators. I have no academic background in this area, but I have read enough on my own to understand that there are impossibility results in algorithmic fairness: roughly, a single score cannot generally be calibrated, have equal false positive rates across groups, and have equal false negative rates across groups at the same time when the outcome rates differ, so the instruction to make the model fair is not a specification anyone can simply satisfy. What I do not have is a way to turn that fact into a repeatable review procedure.

Please write a decision procedure my team could actually run each time a model comes to us for sign-off. It should tell us, in order, which fairness properties to check, how to determine whether the impossibility result applies to the data in front of us, and when the review has to stop being a technical check and become a choice among trade-offs that our model risk committee — which includes legal and the business owner — must make. The procedure should be usable by people with no graduate statistical training, take no more than a few hours of analysis per model, and mark clearly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as computation. Then apply the procedure to two hypothetical models: a credit approval score where group approval rates differ, and a fraud score where the fraud base rates differ sharply. Make one of the applications a case where the procedure gives the business owner an answer they will not like, and end by saying what the procedure cannot settle. Please write the answer in American English.
