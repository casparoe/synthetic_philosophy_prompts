I'm a journalist working on a longread about the hidden rules of everyday conversation — like, when do you actually have the right to say something, and when should you just shut up? I've gotten pretty deep into this philosophy debate about the "norm of assertion," and I'm torn between two camps: the knowledge norm (you shouldn't assert p unless you *know* p) and the truth norm (you shouldn't assert p unless p is *true*, full stop).

For my piece, I want to include a short dialogue between two philosophers who each defend one of these views. Make them equally sharp and charitable — no strawmen, no cheap shots. I want real back-and-forth where they press eachother on the hardest cases: lottery propositions (can you flat-out assert "my ticket lost" before the numbers are drawn, just based on the odds?), Gettier-style situations where someone has a justified true belief but no knowledge, and what actually seperates lying from just being wrong about something you reasonably believed. The dialogue should help readers see why this isn't just philosophers splitting hairs — it matters for how we think about blame, trust, bullshit, and what we owe eachother when we open our mouths.
