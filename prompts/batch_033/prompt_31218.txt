In my philosophy of mind seminar we read Dennett's "Quining Qualia," and the professor walked us through the Chase and Sanborn coffee tasters. The setup: Chase and Sanborn are professional tasters for the same coffee company; the blend itself hasn't changed (the chemists have checked), both used to love it, and both now dislike it. Chase says the coffee tastes exactly the same to him as it always did, but his preferences have changed — he's become a more sophisticated drinker and no longer likes that taste. Sanborn says the opposite: his preferences haven't changed at all, but the taste experience itself has changed, because his taste system is now doing something different with the same chemical input. Both are sincere, both are experts, and neither is obviously confused about his own mind.

Here's the reconstruction of the paper's argument that I cant shake:
(1) Chase and Sanborn agree on every publicly available fact and differ only in their after-the-fact gloss: one locates the change in his evaluation of an unchanged taste, the other in a changed taste with an unchanged evaluation.
(2) There is no further evidence — memory tests, blind tastings, recordings of taste receptors or reward circuitry, anything — that could, even in principle, settle which gloss is right. Any candidate evidence can be absorbed into either story by moving the line between "the experience itself" and "the reaction to it."
(3) If two descriptions cannot be told apart by any possible evidence, then there is no real difference between what they describe; the difference is only verbal.
(4) So there is no fact of the matter about whether Chase's experience changed or only his evaluation of it changed.
(5) But the distinction between "the experience changed" and "my evaluation of the experience changed" is exactly the distinction qualia were supposed to mark — between the way something appears and how I take it. If that distinction is only verbal in the coffee case, it's only verbal everywhere.
(6) Therefore qualia, as a posit, are incoherent: there is nothing in the world answering to the idea of an intrinsic way-tastes-seem over and above our judgments and reactions.

My problem is that the same pattern of reasoning seems to show there's no fact of the matter about whether someone is in pain or merely disliking a stimulus, whether two people mean the same thing by "red," or whether a memory is accurate or confabulated — basically any case where the evidence can be redescribed to fit two glosses. That's a lot more than "qualia are confused," and it feels like the argument has to go wrong somewhere, but each step looks plausible on its own. I'd like to keep the idea that there's a real difference between being in pain and merely disliking a sensation, and I'd also like to keep a broadly naturalistic picture of the mind — so which premise do I reject, and what exactly does rejecting it cost? If there's a subtler reading where the conclusion is true but doesn't generalize to pain and memory, tell me where the generalization gets blocked.
