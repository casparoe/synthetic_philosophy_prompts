I am a sixth-form student, and most of my serious thinking outside school happens in the vocabulary of competitive programming: objective functions, constraints, feasible sets, search spaces, global and local optima, and the question of whether a given algorithm can be guaranteed to reach the optimum at all. I have recently been reading about Leibniz's doctrine of the best of all possible worlds — the claim that God surveys every possible world and chooses to actualise the best one, and that this choice is meant to explain why the world contains the particular evils it does. I keep wanting to formalise this as an optimisation problem, and I suspect that doing so would reveal which parts of the doctrine are doing genuine work and which parts collapse into metaphor.

Please restate Leibniz's position in that vocabulary as faithfully as you can. I am fluent in the programming terms, so do not explain them; use them precisely. Say what the search space is, what the objective function is, what the constraints on candidate worlds are, why the chosen world counts as the global optimum rather than a merely local one, and where evil sits in the picture. Then give me the residue report. Which claims translate cleanly, which translate only under distortion, and which have no counterpart in this vocabulary at all? Finally, I want your judgement on the residue: is it a sign that my optimisation vocabulary is missing something Leibniz actually meant, or that his position was less precise than it appears once it is forced into this form? I already have suspicions — for instance, that reducing divine goodness to a scalar objective quietly assumes that the goodness of worlds is comparable and additive in ways Leibniz may not have intended, and that God's choosing out of goodness is not obviously the same thing as running an optimiser — but I want you to test those suspicions rather than simply confirm them.
