I've been turning over an argument about procreation that seems to show one of my firmest beliefs has to go, and I can't tell which premise is doing the damage.

(1) It's wrong for a couple to conceive a child they know will have a life of near-constant suffering.

(2) It's not wrong for a couple to decide not to have a child, even when the child would have had a happy life.

(3) The best explaination of why both of those are true seems to be: an act is wrong only if it's bad for some particular person. Creating the suffering child is bad for that child. Failing to create the happy child is bad for no one, since the child never exists.

Now here's where it slips. (4) A couple doing IVF has two viable embryos. One would develop into a child with an exceptionally good life. The other would develop into a child with a merely okay life, though still clearly worth living. They implant the second on a whim, because they prefer its predicted eye color or some other trivial reason.

(5) I think this is wrong. (6) But it's not bad for the child they create: that child gets a life worth living and woudln't exist if they'd picked the other embryo. (7) So by (3), it shouldn't be wrong. (8) But if I drop (3), I need some impersonal reason that can explain (5), and any impersonal reason strong enough to make (5) wrong seems like it would also make (2) wrong — it would give us a reason to create happy lives, and I don't think we have one of those.

So I seem to have to give up either (2), (3), or (5), and I'd really like to keep all three. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Is there a way to keep (2) and (5) together that doesn't just gerrymander a principle to fit?
