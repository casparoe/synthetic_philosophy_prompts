I'm preparing a seminar on the metaphysics of natural kinds, and I want to use Jorge Luis Borges's "Funes the Memorious" as a narrative thought experiment to test the usual positions. After his accident, Funes perceives and remembers in such exhaustive detail that he cannot tolerate general terms: it bothers him that the word "dog" covers a dog seen in profile at three-fourteen and the same dog seen from the front at three-fifteen, and his private numbering system gives each number a unique name rather than grouping them. Read the story against the debate over whether the sciences carve nature at its joints. Does Funes's world of pure particulars show that kinds are merely cognitive shortcuts imposed by a finite mind — and if so, why does the story treat his condition as a paralysis and a curse rather than a gain in fidelity? Or does his paralysis indicate the opposite: that abstracting to kinds is what lets us grasp real commonalities and projectable structure, so that a mind that refuses all generality is blind to something genuine in the world? Where would a defender of natural kinds — about chemical elements, biological species, or even the everyday kind "dog" — have to grant that the story puts real pressure on the realist picture, and where is the pressure merely apparent? I would like a reading that treats the story as making a philosophical intervention in its own right, not simply illustrating a textbook view, and that is willing to say where Borges dramatizes something the kind-realism literature does not quite anticipate.
