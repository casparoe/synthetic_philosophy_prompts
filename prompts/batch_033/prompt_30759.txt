I am a graduate tutor for a second-year philosophy of religion seminar, and we have just finished the unit on Pascal's wager. I want to give my students a handout that sorts them into positions by what they actually commit to rather than by which label they have encountered. In discussion I notice the same fault lines recurring: some students think the wager only works if the payoffs are finite and the choice is framed as a problem about managing risk; others think the many-gods objection is decisive; others think the deepest difficulty is that one cannot simply decide to believe; and a few object that wagering is self-interested in a way that would defeat its own religious point. I would like a decision tree that takes a student from a single starting commitment to a named position, with each branch node phrased as a question or short test case that a student can answer without already knowing which position it leads to.

Please build the tree as follows. The starting commitment should be: 'I accept that, when the evidence does not settle whether God exists, considerations about how one's life would go — including what one risks gaining or losing — may legitimately bear on whether to commit oneself to a theistic way of life.' The branch questions should be the claims on which the positions genuinely come apart, and should include, though need not be limited to: whether infinite utilities are coherent enough to figure in deliberation; whether a definite probability can be assigned to a particular god-hypothesis; whether there is exactly one live god-hypothesis or many; whether one can adopt a belief or only act as if one believes; and whether the character of the reason for wagering could itself make the wager self-defeating. Aim for roughly eight to ten branch questions and no more than a dozen leaves, and keep the decision-theoretic apparatus to expected utility, dominance, and infinite value, explaining any technical term.

The leaves should include at least the following, under whatever names you think clearest: the classical Pascalian wager; the many-gods objection or the retreat to a generic theism; an evidentialist rejection; a Jamesian or pragmatic view that allows such considerations only under epistemic parity; a decision-theoretic critique of infinite utility; a voluntarist view that one can cultivate belief indirectly; and an anti-wager theist view that self-interested wagering is the wrong sort of reason. First set out the logical space of answers, then locate these positions in it, and note any unoccupied combination with a one-sentence diagnosis of why it is empty — incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate. Refer to positions by their argumentative structure rather than by citation; I will add references when I adapt the handout.
