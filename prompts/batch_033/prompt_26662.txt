I serve on an incident-review team that investigates failures in an assistant built on a large language model and used to advise patients about medications and scheduling. In the case currently before us, the system told a patient that a medication she had stopped taking was still active. The team has spent two meetings divided over whether the report may use the word believed, and I have been asked to write a short statement of my own position before we meet again. The matter has become more than stylistic for me, and I want to know whether the views I actually hold are jointly tenable before I commit them to paper.

Please treat what follows as a consistency audit rather than a defense of any single item.

1. I accept the intentional stance as the right starting point for explaining the assistant's behavior: we attribute beliefs and desires to a system when doing so lets us predict it reliably, and such attribution does not require the system to share our internal architecture.

2. I also think that current large language models, unlike a thermostat or a lookup table, have internal states that are content-bearing, causally integrated, and updated in response to new information. Because these states play enough of the belief-role, I am prepared to say the model has beliefs in a literal, not merely metaphorical, sense.

3. In the case under review, I think the most accurate description of the failure is that the assistant believed the patient was still taking the medication. What went wrong was a false belief, not a random output.

4. I do not think the assistant is a moral agent, and I do not think that believing something false makes it the kind of thing that can be blamed or held responsible. Responsibility lies with the developers and the deployers.

5. As a matter of documentation practice, I intend to write the report so that it says the system generated or produced the claim rather than that it believed it. Official documentation should not encourage readers to model the system as an agent, even when that modeling is accurate.

6. I also hold that whether a system has beliefs is not settled by predictive convenience alone. There is a fact of the matter about whether a system's internal states occupy the belief-role, and that fact is what makes a belief attribution true or false.

7. I draw the line at desires and intentions. The assistant had beliefs, but it did not want or intend anything, because wanting and intending require a persistent, self-generated direction that these systems lack.

I would like to know which of these commitments pull against one another, which pairs are only apparently in tension, and how the genuine tensions should be ranked by severity. For each genuine tension, what is the least costly revision, and what would that revision cost? I would also like to know whether the set as a whole commits me to anything I have not listed here.
