A friend in my philosophy of law seminar walked me through an argument that seems to show privacy rights are a kind of illusion, and I can't find the exit. It goes like this. (1) An act is wrong only if it makes someone worse off in some respect they have reason to care about. (2) Merely being observed, or having true information about you gathered, doesn't by itself make you worse off in any respect you have reason to care about: if you never find out, and the information is never disclosed or acted on, nothing you'd care about is any different. (3) So secret, non-consensual observation — a voyeur at a window who never tells a soul, a stranger quietly compiling facts about you from public records — is not wrong. (4) But that can't be right; the peeping is wrong even when its never discovered and nothing downstream ever changes. (5) So (1) is false, and wrongness can't just be about making people worse off.

My friend thinks the real lesson is to reject (2): being observed is itself a harm, a kind of relational or dignitary harm, even if the person never learns of it. But that move feels like relabeling the conclusion as a premise — saying "it harms them" doesn't explain why it harms them, and now "harm" has to cover differences that make no difference to anything the person would ever notice or care about. I'd like to keep (4), and I'm also nervous about throwing out (1), since it just sounds like the thought that morality is about how people are affected. Which premise actually goes, and what does rejecting it force you to say about what privacy is for?
