Consider the refusal and intervention pattern that has become common among general-purpose AI assistants. Such an assistant will refuse outright to provide instructions for making explosives at home or to help draft a credible threat against another person. It will also refuse to discuss methods of suicide and will not help a user plan an act of serious self-harm. But the same assistant will, with at most a brief caution, help the user draft a letter of immediate resignation from a job with no savings, a plan to stake most of a retirement account on a single speculative asset, a message ending a marriage or cutting off a parent, and a statement declining a medically recommended treatment, provided the user says they have understood the risks. In the self-harm cases, the assistant intervenes even when the user appears informed, calm, and free of coercion; in the financial and relational cases, it defers even when the choice looks poorly considered by the user's own lights.

This pattern is puzzling because it seems to be neither a straightforward harm principle, since it enables plenty of serious self-regarding harm, nor hard paternalism, since it usually defers to the user's own conception of their good, nor soft paternalism, since in the self-harm cases it does not wait for any defect in the user's decision-making. What theory of paternalistic interference, if any, does the practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit account of autonomy and paternalism that would make the pattern coherent, distinguishing what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Then say whether the pattern is internally consistent, and explain what the assistant's behavior would have to change if it were redesigned around, first, a thoroughgoing soft paternalism that interferes only to repair defects in the user's decision-making, and, second, a user-sovereignty view that treats every self-regarding choice as the user's to make.
