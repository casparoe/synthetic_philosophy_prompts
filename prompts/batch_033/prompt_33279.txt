I spent forty years in atmospheric chemistry and retired a couple years back, and I've been spending my time in a philosophy reading group on obligations to future people. We hit an argument I can't get out of, and it seems to prove too much, which makes me suspect one of the steps, but I cant tell which. Here's the setup. Compare a green policy — rapid decarbonization — with a burn policy where we keep emitting for another century. In 2200, under burn, the world is hotter, poorer, and hungrier, but most people still have lives worth living. The wrinkle is that the particular people alive in 2200 under burn wouldnt have existed under green at all: different migration, different marriages, different timing of births, a whole different set of people. So the argument runs: (1) A policy is wrong only if it makes some particular person worse off than that person otherwise would have been. (2) No particular person living under burn is worse off than they would have been under green, because under green they never would have existed. (3) So burn is not wrong, from (1) and (2). (4) The same reasoning holds however bad the outcome, so long as the resulting lives are still worth living — even if burn leaves almost everyone in 2200 with lives barely worth living. (5) But a policy that leaves nearly everyone with barely-worth-living lives, when a different policy was available, can't be morally indifferent. (6) So (1) is false.

The trouble is I find (1) about as close to self-evident as anything in ethics, and I also can't give up (5), so if I have to choose I'd rather keep both of those and reject whatever else has to go, but I can't see what's left that I'm allowed to give. I want a committed diagnosis, not a menu of options: start with your bottom line in the first sentence, then walk me through the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating the question back to me. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
