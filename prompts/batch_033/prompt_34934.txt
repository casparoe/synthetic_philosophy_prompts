I serve on my country's Law Reform Commission. We are reviewing the criminal prohibition on sexual relations between consenting adult siblings. The conduct is currently a felony carrying a substantial custodial sentence. The question before us is whether to recommend repeal, retention, or retention with a modified penalty. The commission is divided. Some members argue that the state has no business criminalizing private conduct between consenting adults that causes no direct harm to non-consenting parties; they point out that concerns about genetic risk can be addressed by contraception and that the availability of contraception makes a blanket prohibition overbroad. Others argue that even if no individual third party is harmed, the criminal law may legitimately enforce a shared moral conviction that certain conduct is intrinsically degrading, and that a society is entitled to preserve its moral character through its criminal code. A third group argues that the prohibition can be justified on harm-based grounds after all — that the family as an institution is harmed when the relationships that define it are blurred in this way, or that consent within a family structure cannot reliably be free of the coercive pressures the prohibition exists to prevent. The chair has asked me to draft a written opinion for circulation before our next meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action and engage seriously with each of these positions. The opinion should identify the strongest consideration on the losing side and explain why it does not, in the end, carry the day. The chair has also asked that the opinion not simply invoke labels like "legal moralism" or "the harm principle" as though they settled the matter, but instead work through the underlying reasoning so that commissioners without philosophical training can follow the argument.
