I'm an undergraduate in an upper-level philosophy of language course that has just finished Quine's discussion of radical translation and the 'gavagai' example, and I want to write a short paper predicting how Quine would have responded to modern neural machine translation, which he did not live to see. Suppose a system is trained on a large bilingual corpus, with no explicit semantic rules or translation manual, and it reliably maps the native's 'Gavagai' onto 'rabbit' rather than onto 'undetached rabbit part' or 'rabbit stage.' Would Quine take this as evidence that translation is not indeterminate after all—that there is a fact of the matter about which manual is correct—or would he treat the system's output as simply one more empirically adequate translation manual whose apparent determinacy comes from the training data and the evaluation metric rather than from any fact about meaning? Please reconstruct the part of Quine's position that is at stake, predict what he would most likely say, and separate the points where he would have to grant that the technology has changed the terms of the debate from the points where his original argument would still apply.
