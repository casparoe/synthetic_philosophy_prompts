I teach a grad seminar on decision theory and one of my sharper students keep deploying the line "if you violate the axioms of expected utility, you're a money pump" as a conversation-ender whenever someone questions the normative force of the axioms. I've heard versions of this for twenty years — "you'll be Dutch-booked," "your preferences are exploitable," "you're literally burning money" — and I'm starting to think the phrase does a lot of work by being vague enough that nobody has to specify what they mean. So I want a menu of precisifications. What are the distinct claims someone could be making when they say this? Distinguish the literal claim (someone can actually extract your wallet), the diachronic claim about sequences of choices, the synchronic claim about your current preference structure, the behavioral claim about what you'd actually do, the normative claim about what's wrong with your preferences even if no one exploits you, and any other reading you can pull out. For each, tell me whether it's plausible on its own terms and whether it actually licenses the conclusion that you should conform to the expected utility axioms. And tell me which reading is doing the actual argumentative work when the slogan is used to dismiss someone who, say, prefers a sure $900 to a 90% chance of $1000.
