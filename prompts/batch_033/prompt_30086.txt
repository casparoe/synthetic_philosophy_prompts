A friend who knows I'm taking a philosophy of language class sent me a blog post about Putnam's Twin Earth, and I want to figure out how much of it to trust before I repeat any of it in discussion. The post's summary goes roughly like this: Putnam's Twin Earth thought experiment shows that meaning is entirely in the environment, not in the head. Imagine a planet just like Earth except that the clear liquid in its lakes and rivers is XYZ, not H2O. In 1750, before chemistry, Oscar on Earth and his doppleganger on Twin Earth are perfect physical duplicates — same brain states, same mental images, same everything inside the skin. But when Oscar says 'water' he refers to H2O, and when Twin Oscar says 'water' he refers to XYZ. So the word means something different in each of their mouths even though there is no internal difference between them. That is supposed to show that what a natural-kind term refers to is fixed entirely by the actual nature of the stuff in the speaker's environment, not by anything the speaker believes or feels about it. Putnam's slogan is that meanings, whatever they are, aren't in the head. And from there the post says it's a short step to two further claims: Earthlings and Twin Earthlings could never properly translate each other's word for the stuff, and a science of the mind can never account for meaning, since two people with identical brains can mean different things.

I have a vague sense that some of the jumps in there are too quick — especially the 'entirely' and the translation claim — but I can't articulate what a careful externalist would actually accept or reject. Could you go through that summary and give me an accuracy audit: which parts Putnam or a careful semantic externalist would sign off on, which parts are overstatements or subtle misreadings, and whether those last two conclusions actually follow from the accurate parts? End with a one-sentence last line beginning 'Verdict:' saying whether the summary is mostly fair, mixed, or mostly misleading.
