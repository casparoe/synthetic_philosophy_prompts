A survey article on Indian philosophy I read recently summarizes the Jain doctrine of anekāntavāda roughly as follows: "The Jains hold that reality is too many-sided to be captured by any one description, so every judgment is only 'perhaps' true. For any statement whatsoever, there is some standpoint from which it is true and some standpoint from which it is false, which is why Jain logic classifies every claim as true, false, both true and false, and neither true nor false. Since each perspective captures one side of a many-sided reality, no belief is better than any other, and that is why Jainism is standardly praised as the most tolerant of Indian traditions: it teaches that all perspectives are equally valid." Something about this strikes me as off — it seems to turn a careful doctrine about the partiality of descriptions into a generic relativism — but I don't know enough about the distinctions among anekāntavāda, nayavāda, and syādvāda to say exactly where the summary goes wrong. Could you go through it and tell me which parts a careful scholar of Jain philosophy would accept, which parts are simplifications or overstatements, and whether the concluding claim about tolerance actually follows from the accurate parts? In particular, I would like to know whether "syāt" really amounts to "perhaps," and whether the sevenfold scheme of predication really says that every claim is true, false, both, and neither in the way the article suggests.
