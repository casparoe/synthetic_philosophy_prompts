Analyze the United Kingdom's 2017 notification of its intention to withdraw from the European Union under Article 50 of the Treaty on European Union through the lens of speech act theory, as developed by J. L. Austin and John Searle. The facts you need are these. In June 2016 a UK referendum produced a narrow majority for leaving the EU. After domestic legal and parliamentary authorization, on 29 March 2017 the Prime Minister delivered a formal letter to the President of the European Council stating the UK's intention to withdraw. Article 50 requires a Member State that decides to withdraw to notify the European Council of its intention, and provides that the treaties cease to apply either upon the entry into force of a withdrawal agreement or, failing that, two years after the notification. In December 2018, in the Wightman case, the Court of Justice of the European Union ruled that such a notification could be revoked unilaterally before the withdrawal took effect, provided the revocation was unequivocal and followed national constitutional requirements.

Treat the notification letter as a candidate performative utterance. I want a careful mapping rather than a verdict in one sentence. Was the letter a declaration in Searle's sense, an utterance that changed institutional reality by representing it as changed; a commissive, committing the UK to a course of action; an assertive, reporting a decision that already existed; or something that does not sit comfortably in any one category? Then run it through Austin's felicity conditions — the appropriate persons and procedure, correct and complete execution, the requisite intentions, and uptake — and say where the case fits cleanly and where it strains. Pay particular attention to uptake: had the European Council refused to treat the letter as a notification, would the act have failed as a performative, or does the treaty make uptake automatic once the letter is properly delivered? Then use the revocability ruling to test the theory: if the effect of a performative can be undone unilaterally by the speaker, does that show the notification was never a true declaration, or does it reveal that the category of declarations is too rigid for legal speech acts that initiate reversible procedures? Finally, step back and say what the episode as a whole shows about the Austinian framework when applied to institutional legal speech — whether it is a confirmation, an anomaly, or some of each — and whether any modification of the framework would handle the case better.
