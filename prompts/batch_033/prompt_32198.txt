I'm running a discussion group on collective action problems—the inefficacy objection has come up repeatedly on the forum over the years, but we've never really mapped out where people actually stand. I'd like to start with a diagnostic sorting quiz.

The inefficacy objection: an individual's contribution to a large-scale harm (buying one factory-farmed chicken, taking one avoidable flight, casting one vote) seems to make no perceptible difference to the outcome, so it's unclear whether there's an obligation to refrain. The literature has splintered into several responses, and I want a quiz that sorts people by what they actually believe about cases, not by which label they prefer.

Please write a 10-question sorting quiz. Each question should be a concrete scenario—not a statement of principle—with 2–3 answer options. Design the scenarios to pull apart the following fault lines:

- Whether expected utility from a tiny probability of triggering a harm threshold can ground an obligation.
- Whether "what if everyone did it?" or universalizability reasoning works or is question-begging.
- Whether integrity or complicity gives reasons independent of causal impact.
- Whether the obligation is individual or collective (and if collective, what follows for the individual).
- Whether there's a relevant normative difference between market-mediated harms (meat, flights) and voting.

Build the scoring key from the logical space of answer combinations first, then locate the following positions within it: straightforward causal-inefficacy skepticism (no obligation), expected-utility threshold-triggering (obligation through tiny chances), Kantian/rule-consequentialist universalizability, integrity-based obligation, complicity-based obligation, and collective-obligation views. Flag any unoccupied cells in the logical space and briefly note whether the vacancy is surprising. Also flag any pairs of positions the quiz cannot cleanly separate, and be honest about where the quiz's design forces a false choice.
