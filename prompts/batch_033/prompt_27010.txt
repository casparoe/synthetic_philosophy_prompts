I was at a department happy hour a few weeks back and got cornered into listening to two of my cohort argue about Williams's "one thought too many" objection for what felt like the whole evening. I've been turning it over since and I honestly can't sort out who had the better of it. Here's the shape of the thing, as near as I can reconstruct it.

The setup, which they both agreed on, is the drowning-wife case from Williams's "Persons, Character, and Morality." A man can save either his wife or a stranger, but not both. He saves his wife. Williams says the trouble starts when the man explains himself. Here's the bit they were fighting over—I snapped a photo of the passage one of them had pulled up on her phone:

"This construction provides the agent with one thought too many: it might have been hoped by some (for instance, his wife) that his motivating thought, fully spelled out, would be the thought that it was his wife, not that it was his wife and that in situations of this kind it is permissible to save one's wife."

Anyway, the argument went like this.

**Megan** (she's doing a Kant-heavy dissertation) said Williams is overrated on this point. She said: look, nobody is telling the husband to actually run through a moral principle while his wife is drowning. The principle—"it is permissible to save one's wife"—is a retrospective justification, not a deliberation guide. Williams is attacking a strawman who consults the moral law in real time, and no Kantian or utilitarian actually thinks that's how practical reasoning works. So the objection has no bite.

**David** (virtue ethics guy, writing on Murdoch) said she was missing the point. Williams's objection isn't about real-time psychology. It's about what the agent would say if asked to fully spell out his reasons. Even retrospectively, if the full story of why he saved her has to include "and the impartial moral law permits partiality toward spouses," that's one thought too many *at the level of justification*. The wife should be the whole answer. If you need a principle to license the rescue, you've already let the theory into a place it doesn't belong.

**Megan** came back: that begs the question. The impartialist can perfectly well say that his reason was "she's my wife" and that this is a perfectly good reason—but the reason *why* it's a good reason is that impartial morality permits partiality in close relationships. The justificatory ground is impartial, even if the motivating reason isn't. That's not alienating; it's just being clear about what makes reasons reasons. You're confusing the fact that the husband doesn't need to *think about* the ground with the claim that the ground isn't there.

**David** said that's exactly the problem. If the justificatory ground is impartial, then in the final analysis his love for his wife matters because it's the kind of thing an impartial system approves of. That's backwards. His love matters because it's *her*. The impartial grounding inserts a principle between the husband and his wife at the level of justification, and that's the alienation Williams is talking about. It's not about what the husband thinks; it's about what the theory says about his reasons.

**Megan** (this was her third move, I think) said: fine, but if you reject impartial grounding, what's your alternative? That "she's my wife" is a basic reason with no further justification? That's just abandoning the project of giving a systematic account of morality, which is what Kantians and utilitarians were trying to do. Williams isn't refuting impartialism; he's just rejecting the whole project. And that's not an argument—it's a preference.

**David** said: exactly, rejecting the project *is* the argument. Williams is saying that the project of systematizing morality from an impartial standpoint is itself what corrupts our understanding of our own lives. Any theory that requires you to step outside your own projects and relationships to justify them misunderstands what a life is. That's not a preference; it's a substantive claim about the shape of practical reason.

They went back and forth a bit more but that's the core of it. I keep going back and forth on who's got the better of the exchange. Megan seems right that Williams is a bit unfair to the distinction between deliberation and justification, but David seems right that the alienation point survives even once you make that distinction. And I'm not sure whether Megan's final move—"you're just rejecting the project"—is a fair characterization or a cop-out.
