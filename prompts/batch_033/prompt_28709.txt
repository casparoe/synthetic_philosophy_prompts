I'm trying to get clear on what I actually think about reasons for attitudes — belief, admiration, fear, resentment — and I've been writing down my gut reactions to cases. I know this is the sort of thing people call the wrong kind of reasons problem, but most of what I've read doesn't quite fit my own reactions. Most of my verdicts point one way, but one pair keeps tripping me up, and I'd like to know if there's a principle that gets all of them, or close, and which of my verdicts it forces me to drop.

Here's where I land.

1. A demon will torture me unless I genuinely admire him, even though he has no admirable qualities at all — he's petty, cruel, and vain. I think I do have a reason to admire him; only a fool would ignore a threat like that. But it's a weird reason. It doesn't make him admirable. If someone asked "is he admirable?", the threat would be beside the point. It feels like a reason to make myself admire him, not a reason that bears on whether he merits it.

2. A billionaire offers me $10,000 to be genuinely afraid, for one minute, of a harmless spider in a jar across the room. I think I have a reason to be afraid — ten grand is ten grand — but the spider still isn't scary, and the money has nothing to do with whether it is.

3. A god offers infinite bliss to anyone who genuinely believes he exists, and infinite torment to anyone who doesn't. I think I have a reason to try to get myself to believe, but the offer gives me no more reason to think he exists. The deal just doesn't bear on whether the belief is true.

4. My closest friend is accused of embezzlement, and the evidence is honestly pretty damning. I also know that if I believe he's innocent, I'll be a more loyal and supportive friend through the trial. I think the loyalty gives me a reason to hope he's innocent, to double-check the evidence, even to wish I could believe it — but not a reason to believe he's innocent. The belief has to answer to the evidence.

5. A colleague is genuinely fair, patient, and kind. I think those traits are reasons to admire her, and they feel like a completely different sort of reason from the demon's threat. They're the kind that actually bears on whether she's admirable.

So far I have a tentative principle. Something like: their are two kinds of reasons for an attitude. Object-given reasons bear on whether the object has the property the attitude presents it as having — admirable, dangerous, true, whatever. State-given reasons bear on whether it would be good to hold the attitude. Only object-given reasons are real reasons for the attitude itself; state-given reasons are just reasons to bring it about that I hold it. That fits 1 through 5.

But then there's this:

6. I'm ill, and a doctor I trust tells me that patients who believe they'll recover have significantly better recovery rates. I find myself thinking that this is a reason to believe I'll recover — not just a reason to get myself to believe it, but a reason for the belief. Yet it looks like it has exactly the same structure as the demon case: a benefit attaches to holding the attitude. Why does the health case feel like a reason for the belief while the demon case feels like only a reason to induce it? Is it because the belief is self-fulfilling — believing it makes it more likely to be true — and admiration isn't, since admiring the demon doesn't make him admirable? Or is the health case just another wrong-kind reason, and I should stop trusting that intuition?

So my question is: is there a principle that gets all six, or nearly all six? If you have to drop or revise one of my verdicts, which one, and why? And what does your principle say about these:

- a drug that would cause me to believe my spouse is faithful, when I have no evidence either way, and taking it would make the marriage happier;
- someone who offers me money to be angry at a person who is genuinely blameworthy, so the anger is already fitting and the money is just extra;
- someone who resents a real slight, but the resentment is slowly ruining their life — do they have a reason not to resent, and is it the same kind of reason as their reason to resent?
