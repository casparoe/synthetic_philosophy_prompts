I have been working through what I actually think about the timing of conscious experience, and my verdicts on particular cases keep pulling in different directions. The background contrast is the familiar one between Orwellian and Stalinesque revisions. As I understand it, an Orwellian account says that a content enters consciousness and is revised afterward, so that what I now take myself to have experienced is a later edit of an experience I really had. A Stalinesque account says that the editing happens before anything reaches consciousness, so the experience I have is already the finished product and there is no earlier conscious version to be revised.

Here are my cases and verdicts.

1. Color phi. Two dots are flashed in quick succession at nearby locations, the first red and the second green. I report seeing a single dot move from the first location to the second and change from red to green roughly at the midpoint of its path. I am strongly inclined to say that the color change is part of the experience itself, not something I misremember afterward, though I cannot say how the midpoint color could have been experienced before the green dot appeared. Verdict: Stalinesque, if the choice is forced.

2. Cutaneous rabbit. A tap on my wrist is followed quickly by a tap near my elbow. I feel a series of taps running up my forearm through intermediate spots that were never touched. I want to say the intermediate taps are in the experience itself, not merely in a later memory of it. Verdict: Stalinesque.

3. Metacontrast masking. A disk is flashed and then immediately surrounded by a ring. I report seeing only the ring, although the disk demonstrably influences my later responses. I see no reason to posit a brief conscious perception of the disk that was then erased. Verdict: the disk never became conscious; Stalinesque if anything.

4. Flash-lag. A dot moves across a screen and a second dot is flashed directly in line with it. I see the moving dot ahead of the flash. This does not feel like a revision of the past at all; it feels like the visual system predicting where the moving dot should be. Verdict: neither Orwellian nor Stalinesque, but anticipatory.

5. Libet-style timing of intention. Watching a rotating clock, I report the moment I first feel the urge to flex my wrist; the recorded time is about two hundred milliseconds before the movement, while the readiness potential begins several hundred milliseconds earlier still. I trust the physiological data, but my introspection is adamant that the urge occurred when I said it did. Verdict: I am torn. Either my conscious intention is itself constructed late, after the decision process has begun, which would be Stalinesque, or I had an earlier conscious intention that has been re-dated or forgotten, which would be Orwellian. I cannot settle it.

6. Change blindness across a saccade. A scene changes during my eye movement, say a person's shirt changes color, and I fail to notice. I have no memory of the pre-change color. I am inclined to say there was no detailed conscious experience of the pre-change color to be revised; my impression of having seen a complete scene is itself the construction. Verdict: Stalinesque.

7. Ordinary misremembering of a just-seen stimulus. In a rapid serial visual presentation, I am asked what word appeared, and I confidently report a word that was not in the list; when the list is then replayed slowly, I can recognize that the word I reported was not there. Here I want to say that I did have a conscious experience of the actual sequence, and my report of the absent word is a later confabulation laid over it. Verdict: Orwellian.

The tentative principle I keep reaching for is this: consciousness only ever receives a final draft; there is no first draft in consciousness to be revised, so every apparent case of retroactive influence is really pre-conscious construction. But that principle sits poorly with case 7, and it seems to have nothing to say about the anticipatory character of case 4, which I do not want to collapse into retroactive construction.

Is there a single principle that captures all seven verdicts, or close to it? If not, which verdicts should I give up, and what does the best principle say about a case I have not listed: chronostasis, where after I saccade to a clock the first tick seems to last longer than the later ticks, and I am inclined to say the extra duration is genuinely in the experience rather than added later in memory? I am less interested in being told that these are all illusions than in being shown how a precise account of the relation between neural timing and conscious timing sorts them. A good answer will state the principle precisely, show how each clause is motivated by one of my cases, apply the principle to the chronostasis case, and say plainly which of my seven verdicts it cannot accommodate.
