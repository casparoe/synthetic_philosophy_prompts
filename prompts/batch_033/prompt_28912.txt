im the faculty speaker at this year's commencement at a small liberal arts college, and I've run into the problem that everything a commencement speech is supposed to say sounds to me like bad faith. I teach Sartre — radical freedom, no fixed human nature, the idea that existence comes before essence, all of it — and I can't bring myself to stand up and tell two hundred graduates to "discover who they really are" or "be true to yourself" or that their degree has turned them into something. Those are exactly the moves I spend my seminars teaching students to distrust. But I also can't give a Sartre lecture to four hundred parents in folding chairs in the sun.

Can you write the speech? The Sartrean commitments should do the actual structural work — they should determine what the speech can and cant promise — rather than being dropped in as decoration. It has to land as encouragement, not as a philosophy lecture, and not as a bleak "you're on your own" downer. And I don't want the fix to be just swapping "destiny" for "choice," telling them they're free to be whoever they choose, as if that were a one-time decision. On the view I actually hold, that's its own kind of bad faith, because you go on choosing — including when you pretend you've stopped — and the speech should get that across without making the parents panic. It also can't pretend freedom is a consolation prize; part of the view is that freedom is closer to a burden than a gift, and the speech has to be honest about that while still feeling like a commencement address. Maybe one small joke for the handful of students who actually read Sartre, but don't lose everyone else. Aim for roughly eight minutes when read aloud.
