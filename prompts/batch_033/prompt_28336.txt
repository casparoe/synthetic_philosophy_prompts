I did thirty years in physical oceanography and only started reading epistemology after I retired, so I'm still finding my feet. Lately I keep getting tangled up in the peer disagreement debate — conciliationism vs. steadfastness — and I've realized I don't actually have a settled view, because every paper I read seems to shift me a bit. I'm not quiet sure why I find it so slippery. So I want to force myself to commit.

Here's the exercise. In each case below, I start with credence 0.8 in some proposition, and the person I disagree with is someone I'd antecedently have called my epistemic peer — same training, same evidence, same track record — unless the case says otherwise. For each case, tell me what credence I should end up with after learning of the disagreement, as a number between 0 and 1, and give a sentence or two for why. Then rank the cases from "I should move the most toward the other view" to "I'm entitled to move the least." Don't lay out the options and leave it to me: pick numbers and defend them. If you think different cases call for different amounts of movement, that's fine — the ranking is where that shows up.

The cases:

1. The proposition is that a long column of figures I've just added sums to a particular number; I've checked the arithmetic twice. My peer, equally careful with the same column, announces a different sum.

2. The proposition is a substantive philosophical thesis. We've both read the same papers and argued for hours, and we still disagree.

3. The proposition is a claim about whose career would benefit from some policy, and I know — though my peer doesn't see it — that my peer has a serious financial stake in the opposite answer.

4. Ten people I'd antecedently regard as my peers, working independently on the same evidence, all reach the opposite conclusion and report it to me in a batch.

5. My peer was up all night with a sick child and is running on no sleep; they still argue sharply for the opposite view.

6. I have a vivid apparent memory that supports the proposition; my peer, who was there for the original event, has an equally vivid apparent memory that contradicts it.

7. The disputed proposition is itself about our peerhood: I start at 0.8 that we're epistemic peers on this topic; the other person, whose training and track record look equal to mine by my lights, is at 0.2 that we're peers.
