I've been reading about AI alignment on and off for about a year, mostly blog posts and a few papers, while I wrap up a philosophy MA. I'm hoping to do a PhD on the conceptual foundations of alignment — not the ML side, but the part where you figure out what the problem even is. And that's exactly where I'm stuck. I keep encountering different framings of "the alignment problem" and I can't tell whether they're different angles on the same thing or genuinely different problems. You've got the classic "don't let the AI kill everyone" formulation, then there's intent alignment (make it do what we intend), value alignment (make it share our values), corrigibility (make it non-resistant to being shut down or corrected), and then there's inner vs outer alignment, which seems to be about whether the problem is in the training signal or in what the model ends up optimizing internally. And then separately there's the whole thing about specifying human values in the first place, which some people treat as part of alignment and others treat as a seperate problem entirely.

What I'm trying to figure out is: if I have to pick one of these framings to spend four or five years on, which one is most upstream of the others? I don't just mean "which is most important" in some abstract sense — I mean, if you made genuine progress on one of these framings, which one would do the most to make the others easier or even unnecessary? And as a secondary question, which of these is most suited to someone coming from philosophy rather than ML? I want to work on the conceptual stuff that ML people aren't going to sort out on their own, not try to compete with them on their home turf. I'd rather hear your reasoning about the dependencies between these framings than get a list of papers to read.
