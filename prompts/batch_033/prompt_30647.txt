I keep noticing a pattern in arguments about sunk costs and I'm trying to figure out whether it has a name, or whether it even is a single thing. The standard line is that you shouldn't keep throwing money at something just because you've already spent money on it. But I keep running into cases where past spending seems to matter in a way that isn't the usual fallacy, and I don't have a word for the difference.

One case: a friend pays for a whole year of gym membership up front, and then keeps going on evenings when he's not really feeling it. The obvious read is that he's trying to get his money's worth and falling for the sunk cost thing. But his actual reasoning is different. The annual fee means each visit now costs nothing at the margin, so the calculation for any given evening has genuinely changed: the fee is sunk, so it shouldn't count, but precisely because it's sunk, going is cheaper than it would be if he paid per visit. The sunk cost is doing real work by changing the future price structure, not by being a reason to recover the past expense.

Another case: a company keeps funding a clinical trial that looks like it's failing. The naive read is the same fallacy. But the people running it say that killing the trial now would signal to investors that the science was bad, which would raise the cost of capital for the next project. Here the past spending matters because abandoning it sends a signal about quality, not because the money itself needs to be made back.

These two don't feel like the textbook fallacy, but their not unrelated to it either. Is there an established term for this distinction — sunk costs that are genuinely irrelevant to a decision versus sunk costs that matter because they change future options, incentives, or information? Or is the right answer that these are still just the fallacy with rationalizations bolted on? I'd rather have the carve than a label: what exactly makes the difference, and where are the borderline cases?
