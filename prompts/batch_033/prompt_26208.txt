I've been revisiting Harry Frankfurt's classic 1969 attack on the Principle of Alternative Possibilities — the thesis that a person is morally responsible for what they have done only if they could have done otherwise — and I find myself genuinely uncertain about whether his argument lands. The standard Frankfurt-style scenario, as you will recall, involves an agent (call him Jones) who decides on his own to perform some action, whilst a counterfactual intervener (Black) stands ready to force Jones to perform that very same action if Jones shows any sign of wavering. Because Jones goes ahead unaided and Black never lifts a finger, many share Frankfurt's intuition that Jones is fully responsible — yet, given Black's standby readiness, Jones apparently could not have done otherwise. If this intuition holds, PAP collapses.

What has been troubling me is the so-called flicker-of-freedom response, developed in various forms by philosophers who want to rescue something of PAP's spirit. The flicker strategist grants that Black's apparatus forecloses Jones's ability to *refrain from the action*, but insists that some residual alternative possibility survives — a different neural precursor, a moment of hesitation, a shift in the manner in which the intention is formed. The crux of the debate is whether any such flicker is robut enough to bear the weight of moral responsibility, or whether these are merely trivial differences that give the incompatibilist nothing worth wanting.

I would be grateful for a sustained comparison of Frankfurt's original challenge and the flicker-of-freedom reply. Specifically, I would like you to examine whether the flicker strategy genuinely preserves PAP by refining our understanding of what counts as a morally relevant alternative, or whether it in effect concedes Frankfurt's central insight whilst retreating to a purely verbal defence. Which side of this dispute do you find more compeling, and on what grounds? Kindly ensure your answer is written in British English throughout.
