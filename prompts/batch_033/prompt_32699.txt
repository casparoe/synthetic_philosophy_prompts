I've been trying to get clear on what I think about hard choices — the kind where you're not just trading off more of one thing for less of the same thing, but where the things you're choosing between seem to be different in kind. I've got a set of cases and my gut reactions to them, and I want to know if there's a principle that captures the pattern, or at least most of it.

Case 1: A job pays more but the company does work I think is harmful. I don't feel torn at all. The money just doesn't register. It's not that the money is outweighed; it's that it seems irrelevant. Easy no.

Case 2: Same job, but instead of more money, the work itself is deeply fascinating — the kind of thing I'd lose myself in. Now I'm torn. The intellectual value seems to compete with the moral cost in a way the money didn't. Hard choice.

Case 3: Two career paths. One is socially valuable but dull (something like policy admin). The other is thrilling but selfish (something like professional surfing). I'm torn, and I don't think either choice is wrong. But I also don't think you can put them on a single scale and say which comes out ahead. It's not a tie; it's a different kind of thing.

Case 4: I'm on a hiring committee. One candidate is technically brilliant but impossible to work with, the other is solid and reliable and a joy to be around. I'm torn, and I think it's fine to go either way. But when someone on the committee says "just weigh the brilliance against the collegiality" I want to say that's not how it works. They're not on the same scale.

Case 5: My friend asks me to lie to their partner about something small. Saying no will damage the friendship; saying yes damages my integrity. I'm torn, but I also think — and this is where it gets interesting — that there's a right answer here (don't lie) and the difficulty is in doing it, not in knowing what to do. That feels different from the career cases.

Case 6: I've got to choose between two treatments for a chronic condition. Treatment A gives me longer life but with significant daily pain. Treatment B gives me fewer years but much less pain. I'm torn, and I think the values are genuinely incommensurable. But I also think I have to choose, and whatever I pick I'll be able to give reasons for it.

Case 7: I'm deciding how to spend a charitable donation. I can give to a charity that saves lives in a developing country, or to a local arts programme that enriches my community's cultural life. I'm not torn. Saving lives is just more important. But I can't explain why this isn't a hard choice when the career case (socially valuable vs. selfishly thrilling) was.

So my verdicts, in a nutshell: Some apparent hard choices don't even get off the ground because one value is silenced (Case 1). Some are hard because the values compete but can't be put on a single scale, and there's no uniquely right answer (Cases 2, 3, 4, 6). Some are hard in a different sense — the difficulty is in doing what you know is right, not in not knowing what's right (Case 5). And some choices that look like they should be hard aren't, and I can't say why (Case 7).

What principle, or set of principles, captures the difference between cases where values genuinely compete to make a hard choice, cases where one value is silenced or excluded from consideration, and cases where the difficulty is about weakness of will rather than about the values themselves? And what does your principle say about a case I haven't resolved: deciding whether to have children, where the values at stake — freedom, love, meaning, exhaustion, finanical strain — seem to belong to entirely different categories of life, and I can't even tell whether the choice is hard in the first sense (no right answer) or the second (I know the answer but find it hard to act on)?

Oh, and when you reply — please don't use headings or bullet points or bold or any of that. Just continuous prose, like you're writing me a letter. I find formatted stuff hard to read when I'm trying to think.
