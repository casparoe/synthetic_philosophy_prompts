For a graduate seminar handout on the zombie conceivability argument, write a decision tree that sorts a reader from their answer to a single opening question into one of the standard positions on the argument against physicalism. Start from the zombie scenario as usually stated: a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a conscious human being, physically and functionally identical but with no phenomenal consciousness at all. The tree is meant to be used before students read the papers, so it must sort people by what they actually accept about the scenario and the explanatory gap, not by which label they recognize.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names. The natural first split is whether a complete physical description of a subject leaves a genuine further question about what it is like to be that subject: the epistemic gap. From there, branch on the moves that actually separate the positions: whether the gap, if it exists, is conceptual, a matter of current ignorance, or ontological; whether the ideal conceivability of zombies entails their metaphysical possibility; whether phenomenal consciousness, if fundamental, is causally efficacious; and, where a position denies that conceivability entails possibility, what it says makes zombies impossible. Introduce the distinction between prima facie and ideal conceivability only where the tree needs it, and phrase every node as a question or short claim with two or three answer options that a student could answer without knowing the taxonomy. For example, an option should read like "a completed physics would settle what it is like, and anyone who knew all the physical facts could not coherently wonder about it," rather than "type-A physicalism." State the opening question in full.

The leaves must include at least: type-A physicalism; type-B physicalism (the phenomenal concept strategy); type-C physicalism (the view that the gap is closable in principle but only by future knowledge we now lack); interactionist dualism; epiphenomenalism; Russellian monism (type-F); and illusionism. You may add a strong-necessity position (a brute a posteriori necessity with no conceptual or structural explanation) and a global modal skeptic position if the tree's logic requires them. Aim for eight to ten leaves. For each leaf, give one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Where the branching generates a combination of answers that no live position occupies, say so and give a one-sentence diagnosis of whether the combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or a view someone could hold but nobody prominent does. Finally, flag any two leaves the tree cannot cleanly separate with the questions you used, and state what further question would be needed to split them; I expect type-B and type-C, and Russellian monism and the strong-necessity view, to be the likely trouble spots. No bibliography or citations.
