I retired a few years ago from a career in computational neuroscience — modeling motor control, reinforcement learning, that sort of thing — and I've been spending my newfound free time working through the philosophical literature on free will, a topic that always hovered in the background of my scientific work but which I never had the occasion to engage with systematically. I've now drafted a short essay (about 1,500 words) that tries to bring some conceptual resources from complex systems theory and control engineering to bear on the question of which varieties of free will are actually worth wanting. The core idea is that the traditional debate has been dominated by what I'll call the "prime mover" model of agency — the intuition that a free act must be initiated by the agent in a way that is not fully determined by prior causes — and that this model looks increasingly implausible once you take seriously the hierarchical, multi-scale structure of biological control systems. I sketch three alternative varieties of free will that emerge naturally from thinking about agency as a control problem: (1) reactive flexibility, the capacity to respond to a given stimulus with a wide repertoire of behaviors rather than a fixed or stereotyped response; (2) temporal depth, the capacity to act on the basis of representations of distant futures and counterfactual pasts rather than only the immediate environment; and (3) recursive self-modeling, the capacity to represent one's own decision processes and adjust them in light of higher-order preferences. I then argue that these three varieties jointly capture what matters to us in ascriptions of moral responsibility, and that the "prime mover" variety is a metaphysical red herring — we don't actually want it, and we shouldn't miss it if it turns out we don't have it. The essay closes by suggesting that incompatibilist intuitions are, at bottom, a kind of vestigial dualism that persists even in people who have explicitly rejected substance dualism, and that a proper understanding of how biological control systems actually work can help dissolve those intuitions.

I would be grateful for comments on this draft, particularly from philosophers who know the compatibilist literature better than I do. I have three specific worries I'd like feedback on. First, I'm not sure whether my three varieties genuinely carve the space at the joints or whether they collapse into each other under pressure — for instance, does recursive self-modeling just reduce to temporal depth plus some extra cognitive machinery, and if so, is the tripartite scheme doing any real work? Second, I worry that I'm simply rediscovering distinctions already well-known in the literature — Frankfurt on higher-order volitions, Fischer and Ravizza on guidance control, Dennett on the varieties of free will worth wanting — and repackaging them in systems-theoretic language without adding anything new; I'd like an honest assessment of whether the control-theoretic framing actually earns its keep or is merely decorative. Third, and most importantly, I'm uncertain whether my argument that we don't actually want the "prime mover" variety of free will is convincing, or whether it simply sidesteps the deep metaphysical unease that motivates incompatibilism in the first place. I'd appreciate any suggestions for tightening the argument, flagging objections I haven't anticipated, or identifying places where my scientific analogies are being stretched beyond what they can bear.
