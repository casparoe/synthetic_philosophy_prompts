I spent my career in computational neuroscience—modeling neural circuits, building simulations of cortical columns, the whole thing—and since retiring I've been reading philosophy of mind. One thing that genuinely puzzles me is how the debate over computationalism seems to have been frozen since at least the 1980s. By computationalism I mean the claim that the brain literally is a computer, that mental processes are computational processes, not merely that computation is a useful metaphor or a modeling strategy. The moves are well-rehearsed: on one side, neurons transform inputs into outputs according to rules that can be captured on a Turing machine, and we routinely simulate neural circuits; on the other, you have Searle's Chinese Room, the frame problem, arguments from embodied and situated cognition, and the charge that computation is observer-relative—that we project it onto the brain the way we project it onto the orbits of the planets or the flow of a river. Both sides seem to agree on the empirical facts about neurons, and yet nobody moves.

My working hypothesis is that this isn't an empirical dispute about brains at all. It's a dispute about what "computation" requires: does it require a system whose states have original intentionality and a user who assigns semantic content, or does it merely require causal processes that admit of a computational interpretation? If I'm right, the impasse is conceptual, and more neuroscience won't help. But I'm not confident in this diagnosis, and I'd like a professional philosopher who works in this area to assess it.

Before you answer, please do the following: first, map out the strongest versions of the computationalist and anti-computationalist positions as defended by their most careful proponents—not the straw men one finds in online debates. Then, diagnose the disagreement: is it verbal, is it a clash of bedrock intuitions about what counts as an explanation, is it a hidden empirical question neither side has formulated clearly, or are the two sides actually answering different questions under one label? Give your reasoning. Tell me honestly what, if anything, could break the impasse—a sharper definition of computation, a new experimental paradigm, a formal result, or a reframing of the question. Finally, please separate what is genuinely settled among people who work on this from what remains contested, and do not present a contested position as if it were the consensus.
