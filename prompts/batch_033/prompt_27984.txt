I've been going back and forth on this with some people in my local ACX meetup and I think we've talked ourselves into a corner. The question is whether there's a principled difference between counterfactual mugging (where the FDT/UDT consensus says you should pay) and XOR blackmail (where it seems obvious you shouldn't), or whether accepting one forces you to accept the other.

Here's the argument we keep coming back to:

(1) In a standard counterfactual mugging, Omega flips a fair coin. If heads, Omega asks you for $100 and pays you $10,000 if you give it. If tails, Omega just gives you $10,000 iff you would have paid in the heads case. The coin comes up tails, Omega asks whether you'd have paid, and you get $10,000 if you answer honestly that you would have. Standard FDT/UDT says you should pay, because the decision procedure "pay if asked" gets you $10,000 in both branches, while "don't pay" gets you nothing in both. Your answer in the tails branch doesn't causally affect anything — Omega already knows what you would have done — but your decision procedure acausally determines what happens in both branches.

(2) Now consider XOR blackmail. A blackmailer sends identical letters to a million people: "I've committed a serious crime and I'm going to frame exactly one person for it. Pay me $100 and I'll remove your name from the pool of candidates." The blackmailer can credibly commit to this policy: exactly one person will be framed, chosen at random from those who don't pay. Everyone knows the policy is real. If everyone pays, no one gets framed and the blackmailer walks away with $100M. If no one pays, exactly one person gets framed and the blackmailer gets nothing.

(3) From the perspective of any individual recipient, the structure looks a lot like counterfactual mugging. If you pay, you're safe and you lose $100. If you don't pay, you face some probability of being framed (1/k, where k is the number of non-payers) and the expected cost of being framed is enormous. So paying seems to dominate. And the reasoning has the same flavor: you're not just asking "what happens if I personally pay or don't pay," you're asking "what decision procedure do I want to be running," and the procedure "pay when threatened this way" keeps you out of the framed pool.

(4) But paying in XOR blackmail is obviously wrong. If everyone pays, we've collectively handed a blackmailer $100M for nothing of value, and we've made the world safe for blackmail as a business model. The right move is to not pay and to encourage everyone else not to pay. A decision theory that tells you to pay here is giving bad advice.

(5) So either the reasoning in (3) is wrong (and we need to explain exactly why the cases are disanalogous), or FDT/UDT gives the wrong answer in counterfactual mugging, or FDT/UDT gives the wrong answer in XOR blackmail and we should bite the bullet that paying is actually correct.

I feel like the disanalogy has to be somewhere in the difference between Omega (a perfect predictor who is basically running a philosophical experiment and is, if not benevolent, at least not actively malicious) and the blackmailer (an agent whose decision procedure we actively don't want to acausally cooperate with). But FDT is supposed to be agent-neutral — it doesn't care whether the other agent is nice, it just asks what decision procedure you want to be running, and whether the outputs of that procedure logically control the other agent's behavior. So if the decision procedure "pay when faced with this kind of acausal trade" gets systematically exploited by blackmailers, that seems like a real problem for FDT, not just a "well don't run that procedure then" patch.

Which step is wrong, and what do you have to give up to reject it? If you think the cases are disanalogous, what's the precise difference that does the work — and does that difference generalize, or does it just carve out a special exception for blackmail?
