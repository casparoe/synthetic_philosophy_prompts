I'm a software engineer at a company that makes a budgeting app, and I keep getting put on "copy review" right before release. A few times a week I have to sign off on the exact wording of push notifications, settings screens, and paywall text. We already have a legal review for outright false advertising, but the legal team only cares about what a sentence literally says. I keep seeing stuff that is technically true but feels gross — like a notification that says "Your premium features expire tomorrow" when the user can still use the app read-only, or a toggle that says "Protect my data" but actually opts them into sharing it with partners. I have no formal philosophy background, but I know there's a whole debate about whether misleading someone is morally different from lying to them, and I want that turned into something I can actually use.

Please write me a decision procedure for a piece of proposed copy, something I can run in about 15 minutes before the release goes out. The output should be a verdict like "lie," "misleading but not a lie," or "clean," plus a flag/rewrite/don't-care recommendation. I want the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to move on, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it explicitly hands off to a human because the step is a values call rather than a fact about the copy. It should use whatever philosophy actually knows about the lying/misleading distinction — for example what the speaker intended, what a reasonable user would infer, and what the sentence strictly says — but translated for someone who hasn't read the literature. Be honest about the points where the distinction can't be drawn cleanly or where the procedure will misfire.

Then run it on three real-ish cases from my app:

1. A push notification: "Your free trial ends in 2 days — subscribe to keep budgeting." The truth is that after the trial the user can keep using the app in read-only mode, just not edit budgets.

2. A settings screen: "We never sell your data." The company shares user data with a data broker under a "partnership agreement" and receives payment for it.

3. A referral prompt: "Invite friends and you'll both get $10." Actually the inviter gets a $10 credit, and the friend gets $10 off their first paid month, not a $10 credit.

I'd like at least one of those to come out as something I'd personally of let through, so I can see the procedure earning its keep.
