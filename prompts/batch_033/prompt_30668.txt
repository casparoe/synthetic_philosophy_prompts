Write a sestina about the paradox of fiction. Choose the six end-words so that a philosopher of aesthetics would immediately recognize the vocabulary of the debate — 'believe,' 'real,' 'grieve,' 'page,' 'pretend,' and 'know' would serve, but you may adjust them as long as the same six concepts stay in view. The form should do the philosophical work: a sestina keeps the same six words while rotating their order, and that fixed permutation should make the reader feel how each attempt to dissolve the paradox only rearranges the same unresolved terms. The background inconsistent triad is this: (1) we have genuine emotional responses to fictional characters and events; (2) genuine emotional responses of the relevant kind require belief in the existence or occurrence of their objects; (3) we do not believe that fictional characters or events exist or occur. Across the six stanzas, voice distinct attempts to escape this triad — giving up the first premise, giving up the second, giving up the third, accepting the inconsistency outright, and nearby variants — without naming any philosopher or technical label. The envoi should gather all six end-words in the traditional way, three at line ends and three within the lines, and it should end with the paradox still open rather than resolved. A specialist in aesthetics should be able to see exactly which premise each stanza is trying to abandon, and a reader who has never encountered the problem should still be left with a good poem rather than a lecture.
