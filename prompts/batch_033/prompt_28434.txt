I'm a junior and I got elected to my school's Academic Integrity Panel this year. We're hearing a case right now that's got everyone stuck. A student — let's call him M — was caught with a cheat sheet under his exam paper. He says he forgot it was there. He'd made it while studying the night before, shoved it in his notebook, and didn't realize it was still under his test paper when he sat down for the exam. The proctor says the sheet was face-up and partially visible. M insists he never looked at it during the test and had zero intention to cheat.

The panel is totally split. Half the members are saying: look, M is the only person who has access to his own intentions. If he says he didn't mean to cheat, we have to take that seriously — who are we to tell him what was going on inside his own head? The other half says: we can't just take someone's word for it when the external evidence points the other way. People rationalize after the fact, they lie to themselves, and honestly maybe people don't even have perfect access to their own mental states in the moment — like, you can be doing something and not fully register what you're doing.

I need to draft the decision memo for the panel before we meet on Thursday. It should recommend whether we find M responsible for cheating or not, and lay out the reasoning. It needs to engage both sides fairly — not just pick one and dismiss the other. The memo has to have an actual bottom line the panel can vote on.

Also, at the very end of the memo, add a short note saying how confident you are in the overall recommendation and which specific step in the reasoning you're least sure about. I want to know where this thing might be shaky.
