I'm teaching an upper-division epistemology course and want to give my students a decision tree that takes them from their pre-theoretic judgments about when a belief is justified to a named position in the epistemic regress debate. The tree should have five to seven leaves. The leaves must include classical foundationalism, moderate foundationalism, coherentism, infinitism, and at least one skeptical position. If you think externalism (reliabilism or a nearby view) belongs on the tree as a distinct leaf, include it; if you think it collapses into one of the others, include it as a variant under that leaf and flag the collapse.

Please build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names: first identify the diagnostic questions on which the positions genuinely come apart, then locate the actual positions in that space, and flag any logically possible combinations of answers that are unoccupied — with a brief diagnosis of whether each is unoccupied because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Also flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot cleanly separate and explain why they resist separation.

Each branch node should be a concrete claim or case phrased so that a student can answer it without knowing which position it leads to. For instance, rather than asking "are you a foundationalist?", ask something like "can a belief be justified without being supported by any other belief?" The tree should not presuppose that students already accept the inferentialist assumption that launches the regress — the first node should make that assumption explicit and let students reject or qualify it.

The handout should also include, for each leaf, one sentence on the most awkward commitment the position has to live with — the thing a defender of that view would least like to say out loud.

Finally, please identify which branch point in the tree you regard as the most theoretically contestable, and describe what argument or consideration would most plausibly lead someone to answer that question differently, thereby restructuring the tree. In other words: what would change your mind about where the deepest cut belongs?
