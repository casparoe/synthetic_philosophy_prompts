In a discussion about why bad ideas persist, someone made the following argument: cultural evolution is Darwinian through and through. Ideas — memes — are the replicators, just as genes are in biology. They vary (through misunderstanding, recombination, creative elaboration), they are inherited (through imitation, teaching, and media), and they differ in fitness (some spread more effectively than others). And just as the "purpose" of a gene is only to replicate itself, not to benefit the organism, the "purpose" of a meme is only to get copied, not to be true or useful to its host. Therefore, we should no more expect widely held beliefs to be true than we expect common genes to be good for us in some absolute sense — truth is at most one possible contributor to memetic fitness, and often irrelevant. They used this to explain the persistence of conspiracy theories, pseudoscience, and political misinformation.

I find this seductive, but I'm unsure how hard the analogy can be pressed. I'd like a systematic audit:

1. A correspondence table mapping the biological terms (gene, organism, mutation, selection, fitness, inheritance, population, environment) onto their cultural counterparts, with a brief note on each mapping where it's contentious.

2. A list of disanalogies, sorted into two categories: (a) those that matter for the specific conclusion that truth is generally irrelevant to a belief's spread, and (b) those that are real disanalogies but don't threaten that conclusion.

3. Your verdict on whether the conclusion survives the disanalogies that matter, and if it doesn't, what the most defensible restatement would be.

Keep the response to no more than 1,000 words.
