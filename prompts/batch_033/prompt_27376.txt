At a reading group, two students got into an argument about whether dreams are experiences during sleep. (1) Mara opened with a Malcolm-style line: since a person sound asleep cannot make judgments or give reports, and since the concept of dreaming is tied to the waking inclination to tell a dream narrative, it is conceptually confused to posit a private experiential episode occurring in sleep. (2) Leo replied that Dennett's cassette theory is a coherent alternative to the received view: an entirely unconscious process during sleep could lay down a memory trace that is first composed into a narrative during recall, so the dream would be experienced only upon waking. If that alternative is coherent, Leo argued, the question whether dreams are experienced during sleep is empirical, not conceptual. (3) Mara answered that Dennett himself treats the cassette theory only as a specimen designed to make a philosophical point, not as a defended position; that point is compatible with Malcolm's separate claim that the concept of dreaming is fixed by waking reports, so we cannot get behind the report to an inner experience. (4) Leo responded that she was now pressed against a dilemma: if the received view is an empirical hypothesis, then Malcolm cannot rule it out by conceptual analysis, and if Malcolm's argument is only about our everyday criteria, it cannot settle what happens during sleep. (5) Mara replied that this conflates evidence with criterion: Dennett may show that two empirical stories would feel the same from the inside, but Malcolm's point is that the waking narrative is not evidence for a hidden episode; it is the very criterion for having dreamt. Please adjudicate the exchange: evaluate each move individually, identify where the argument slides between conceptual, epistemic, and empirical claims, and say whether Malcolm's denial of dream experience survives Dennett's challenge.
