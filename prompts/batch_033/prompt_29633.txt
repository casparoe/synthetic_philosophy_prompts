I'm giving a department talk next month defending a verificationist line: roughly, that any true statement is the sort of thing we could in principle come to know — no truth is permanently closed off from inquiry. A collegue just showed me Fitch's argument and I've been rattled every since. Here's the version he gave:

(1) Every truth is knowable: if p is true, then it's possible for someone to know that p.
(2) Take any truth p, and let q be the proposition that p is true and nobody ever knows p.
(3) If q is true, then by (1) q is knowable — there's a possible situation where someone knows q.
(4) But knowing q is impossible. Knowing q would mean knowing both conjuncts: you'd know p, and you'd know that nobody ever knows p. Since knowledge is factive, knowing the second conjunct would require that nobody ever knows p to be true. But your knowing the first conjunct already makes it true that somebody knows p. So knowing q would force "somebody knows p" and "nobody ever knows p" to both be true — a flat contradiction.
(5) Since knowing q is impossible, and (1) says any true proposition is knowable, q must not be true after all.
(6) So for any p, it's false that p is true and nobody ever knows p — i.e., every true proposition is known by somebody at some point. But that's absurd: some truths are just lost, and nobody ever has or ever will know them.

I really don't want to give up (1), since that's the whole point of the talk. Which premise or inference do you reject, and what does rejecting it actually cost? If the right move is to restrict (1) to some subclass of truths, what makes the restriction principled rather than just gerrymandered to dodge the counterexample?
