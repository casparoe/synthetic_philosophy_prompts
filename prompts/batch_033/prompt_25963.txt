ok so we've been doing acausal trade in my decision theory class and i built this argument that's been bugging me for like a week. it seems to show that if you accept the basic acausal trade reasoning at all, you're on the hook for cooperating with basically everyone who might be reasoning about you, including people who don't even exist yet or who are in other branches of the wavefunction or whatever, and this just seems completely unmanageable. like it proves too much. but i can't find where the step goes wrong.

here's the argument:

(1) If you and another agent can each benefit the other at some cost to yourselves, and you each have a sufficiently accurate model of the other's decision procedure, then it's rational for each of you to do the beneficial thing — this is the core acausal trade intuition (the "trading with simulators" kind of case).

(2) The "sufficiently accurate model" condition is met whenever you can predict each other's behavior well enough that the expected benefit of cooperating exceeds the expected cost.

(3) Given (1) and (2), you should cooperate with any agent whose decision procedure you can model well enough to make the expected-value calculation come out positive.

(4) You can model the decision procedures of a vast number of agents — past civilizations that might have run simulations of future agents like you, future civilizations that will run simulations of past agents like you, agents in other branches of the Everettian multiverse, agents in other Hubble volumes, Boltzmann brains, etc. — well enough to know that they would be reasoning about whether to benefit you acausally, and that their reasoning would have roughly the same structure as yours.

(5) By (3) and (4), you are rationally required to cooperate acausally with this vast set of agents.

(6) But the set is so large and the possible trades so numerous and mutually interfering that you cannot actually compute what you should do — the decision problem is radically intractible. A theory of rational choice that demands the uncomputable is not a usable theory.

(7) Therefore, by reductio, the core acausal trade intuition in (1) must be rejected or at least severely restricted.

the thing is i actually want to keep (1) — the basic cases like trading with a perfect simulation of you seem genuinely compelling. but i can't see a principled place to draw the line between those cases and the explosion in (4). if you say "only cooperate with agents who actually exist" then you have to say what "actually exist" means in a way that excludes Everett branches but includes the simulation case, and that seems hard. if you say "only cooperate when the decision problem is tractable" then you're making rationality depend on your own computational limits which seems weird. my prof mentioned something about logical counterfactuals and how you need the right kind of decision-theoretic connection, not just prediction, but i'm not sure i see how that blocks the argument without also blocking the original cases we want to keep.

so: which step do i reject, and what do i lose by rejecting it? or is there a way to restrict (1) that doesn't feel ad hoc?
