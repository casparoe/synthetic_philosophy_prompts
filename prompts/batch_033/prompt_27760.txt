I am drafting a position statement for a seminar on practical reason, and before I circulate it I would like a consistency audit of the package of views I find myself defending. Over the past term I have settled, tentatively, on the following six claims about hard choices and incommensurable values, and I want to know whether they can all be held together or whether some of them quietly pull against the others.

(1) Value is irreducibly plural, and many goods are incommensurable in the sense that there is no single cardinal scale on which their contributions to choice-worthiness can be measured. I do not take this to mean that options are usually incomparable; I think most pairs of options can be compared in one respect or another.

(2) In a genuine hard choice — for example, between two careers that draw on quite different values — the options are often on a par: neither is better than the other, neither is worse, and they are not exactly equal in value. Parity is a fourth positive value relation, not merely the absence of any comparative relation.

(3) I find the small-improvements argument convincing. If two options are on a par, a slightly improved version of one of them is better than the original option and yet still not better than the other option; this is difficult to explain if the only possible relations are better than, worse than, and equally good.

(4) I am committed to a reasons-first picture of rational choice: an option is rationally permissible only if it is not less well supported by one's reasons than some available alternative, and where neither option is better supported and no further consideration breaks the tie, picking one rather than the other is arbitrary rather than fully rational.

(5) Nevertheless, I do not think choices between on-a-par options must be arbitrary. I am inclined to say that an agent can rationally commit to one of the options, and that the commitment itself creates new reasons that were not antecedently in play, so that the eventual choice is supported by reasons the agent herself brings into being.

(6) I also think it can be rational to feel regret after such a choice — not the regret of having chosen wrongly, but regret for the particular value of the unchosen option — and that this regret is fully compatible with having chosen well.

I would be grateful if you would audit this package rather than simply expound the positions. In particular: which of these commitments are genuinely in tension, and which tensions are merely apparent once the relevant notions are disambiguated? Please rank the genuine tensions by severity, say for each what would have to give and what the least costly revision would be, and tell me whether there is a single revision that would do most to make the whole set cohere. Please write your answer in British English.
