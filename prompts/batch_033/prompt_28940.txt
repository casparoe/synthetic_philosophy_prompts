I am a novelist who teaches an occasional graduate seminar on philosophical problems about fiction, and I am now grading a take-home examination. Below is the question I set and one student's answer to it. Please grade the answer and give me the specific, point-by-point comments that justify the grade. I want an assessment tied to particular claims in the answer — places where a distinction is conflated, where a view is rendered imprecisely, where an inference does not go through, and where a correct point is made too loosely to carry the argument — rather than a general impression. Please assign a letter grade as part of your response.

Question. Explain the paradox of fiction as it arises for readers' emotional responses to fictional characters. State the assumptions that jointly generate the paradox, then critically discuss two proposed resolutions: the suspension-of-disbelief response and the make-believe account of reader emotion. For each, say what it concedes, what it preserves, and what remains problematic.

Student answer. The paradox of fiction begins from three claims, each of which seems true. First, readers have genuine emotions toward fictional characters and events: we pity a doomed protagonist such as Anna Karenina, fear for the victim in a horror film, and feel grief when a beloved character dies. Second, it is part of the concept of emotion that one can have an emotion toward a person or state of affairs only if one believes that person or state of affairs is real. Third, readers do not believe that fictional characters exist or that the depicted events really occurred; they know throughout that the novel is a work of fiction. Since the three claims cannot all be true, at least one must be rejected.

Radford's response is to accept all three claims and conclude that our emotional responses to fiction are irrational or inconsistent: we are moved by what we know does not exist. This preserves the data but leaves the paradox unresolved in any satisfying sense.

The suspension-of-disbelief response rejects the third claim in practice: readers temporarily set aside their knowledge that the events are not real, and that temporary loss of the reality-distinction is what allows emotion to arise. The attraction of this view is that it explains why our emotions recede when the spell is broken. But it faces a serious difficulty: readers do not actually forget that the work is fiction; if asked during the experience whether the character exists, they will answer no. So the response either overstates what readers do or simply relabels the phenomenon.

Walton's make-believe account goes further. Walton argues that reader emotion is itself make-believe: the reader's fear is not a real emotion but a pretend one, like a child's fear in a game of monsters. Because the emotion is only make-believe, the first claim is false, and the paradox dissolves. This is really the same problem as the paradox of tragedy, since both concern how we can be moved by what we know is not real.
