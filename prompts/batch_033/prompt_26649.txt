I've been covering the intersection of political thought and popular culture for years now, and a question keeps coming up in conversations with readers and colleagues that I'd like to explore more systematically. The question is whether *Breaking Bad* — a series that, by now, has been analyzed from nearly every angle — yields something new when read through the lens of Machiavelli's distinction between *fortuna* (fortune, chance, circumstance) and *virtù* (not virtue in the moral sense, but the combination of skill, resolve, adaptability, and sheer force of will that allows a political actor to impose himself on events). The surface reading is obvious enough: Walter White is a man who transforms bad fortune (a terminal cancer diagnosis, a stalled career, financial precarity) into an arena for the exercise of virtù, building a drug empire through intelligence, ruthlessness, and an almost preternatural ability to read and manipulate others. But the surface reading may also be too neat, because Machiavelli's own view of the relationship between fortune and virtù is more unstable than the cliché of "the Machiavellian schemer" suggests. For Machiavelli, fortune is not merely an obstacle to be overcome; it is a force that can undo even the most virtuoso prince, and the prince who believes he has mastered fortune is often the one most vulnerable to its reversals. The famous image in Chapter 25 of *The Prince* — fortune as a river that can be dammed and channeled by foresight, but also as a woman who must be beaten into submission — carries tensions that Machiavelli never fully resolves, and that may map onto Walter White's arc in ways the standard "rise and fall" reading misses.

I'd like a reading of *Breaking Bad* that takes both the series and the Machiavellian framework seriously, without reducing either to an illustration of the other. Specifically: at what points in the narrative does Walter's virtù genuinely master fortune, and at what points does fortune reassert itself in ways that virtù cannot anticipate or control? Is the series' finale a vindication of Machiavelli's claim that fortune "shows her power where virtù has not been put in order to resist her," or does it complicate that claim — perhaps by suggesting that Walt's virtù was always partly a product of fortune (the luck of his particular talents, the historical accident of the meth market, the improbable survival of Jesse Pinkman as both ally and conscience)? And does the series add something to the philosophical debate that *The Prince* alone does not capture — for instance, by dramatizing the psychological cost of living as though fortune can be subdued, or by showing how the exercise of virtù transforms the agent in ways that make him progressively less able to judge when fortune has turned against him? I'm not looking for a thesis that tidies everything up; I'd rather an honest reading that notes where the Machiavellian lens illuminates a scene or a character choice, and where the series resists it and the critic has to say so. The piece would run in a longform ideas section, so assume an audience that knows the show but may not know Machiavelli beyond the adjective. And please write in American English.
