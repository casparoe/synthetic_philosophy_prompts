I've been following the constitutional reform debates in my country with more attention than I used to, and I keep coming back to a puzzle that I cannot shake. Our constitution contains what the courts call an eternity clause — a provision stating that certain articles, on fundamental rights and on the federal structure, are permanently unamendable. The amendment procedure itself requires a two-thirds parliamentary majority and a referendum, but the eternity clause says that even that is not enough: those particular articles are simply off the table, forever. I am not asking about the political wisdom of this arrangement. What bothers me is something more basic, almost a logical knot at the centre of the whole thing.

If the constitution is a set of legal rules, and one of those rules is the amendment procedure, then the amendment procedure seems to be the highest rule — it tells you how to change any other rule. But the eternity clause appears to claim a status above even the amendment procedure: it says the amendment procedure cannot be used on certain provisions. Yet the eternity clause was itself enacted through some procedure — perhaps the original constitution-making process, or perhaps an earlier amendment. So what happens if a future parliament, following the amendment procedure to the letter, votes to repeal the eternity clause and then amend the protected articles? Have they amended the constitution or have they broken it? And if the constitutional court strikes that down, what authorises the court to treat the eternity clause as binding when the amendment procedure — which is supposed to be the constitution's own rule about how to change itself — was followed to the letter?

I am trying to work out what theory of legal rules, or of constitutional authority, a practice like this one presupposes. It seems to require a picture where some rules are more constitutional than others, or where the identity of the legal system is tied to certain substantive commitments such that changing them amounts to replacing the system rather than amending it. But I am not sure whether that picture holds up under pressure, or whether the practice is ultimately just a political convention dressed in legal language — and if it is, whether that is a problem. I would like you to reconstruct the implicit theory as carefully as you can, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and tell me where the practice is philosophically underdetermined — where several rival theories of legal validity could license it equally well. Please also say how confident you are in each part of the analysis and single out the point you are least sure of.
