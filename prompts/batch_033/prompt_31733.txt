I'm a graduate student in philosophy of biology, and for a paper I'm writing I've been keeping a careful record of how one of my committee members, an evolutionary biologist with strong views about adaptationist reasoning, classifies particular traits. She sorts every trait into one of three categories: adaptations; exaptations, by which she means traits co-opted for a function other than the one that originally shaped them; and byproducts, traits with no selected function of their own. Her verdicts so far: the giant panda's "thumb" is an adaptation for stripping bamboo, even though it is a modified wrist bone rather than a true digit; the red color of blood is a byproduct of hemoglobin's oxygen-binding chemistry; male nipples are a developmental byproduct of a shared early developmental program; the human chin is a structural byproduct of facial retraction and dental reduction; feathers are an adaptation for flight, though she grants that they first arose for thermoregulation and were only later co-opted and reshaped by selection for flight; and the vertebrate eye is an adaptation for vision.

Next week she has to classify the human capacity for learning to read: adaptation, exaptation, or byproduct? I care less about the bare label than about the rule she is implicitly running, because I suspect her record so far underdetermines it. What are the candidate rules that fit all six verdicts? Which of them diverge on the reading case, and what does each predict? Give me your best prediction of her verdict on reading, with the reasons for preferring the rule behind that prediction. Then propose one further case, one she has not yet ruled on, whose classification would do the most to tell the surviving rules apart, and say exactly how her ruling on that further case would discriminate between them.
