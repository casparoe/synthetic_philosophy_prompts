I'm taking a metaphysics course this semester and we've been doing dispositions for the last couple weeks. I think I get the basic picture: the simple conditional analysis says something is fragile iff it would break if struck, soluble iff it would dissolve if placed in water, and so on. Then we hit finks and masks and honestly I'm having trouble keeping them straight in my head — or rather, I can state the definitions, but I'm not convinced they're really two different things.

The way my professor laid it out: a fink is something that removes the disposition when the stimulus condition occurs. So the sorcerer waits for the glass to be struck, and right at the moment of impact he casts a spell making it not fragile — the glass doesn't break, but not because it wasn't fragile beforehand; the fragility got finked away. A mask, by contrast, is something that blocks the manifestation without touching the disposition itself. The glass is wrapped in bubble wrap, you strike it, it doesn't break, but it was fragile the whole time — the bubble wrap just masked the manifestation.

Here's what's bugging me. If you take the mask case and describe the bearer of the disposition as the glass-plus-bubble-wrap system, then striking that system doesn't produce breakage, and so the system isn't fragile, and it looks like the disposition got removed — which is what a fink does. Conversely, if you take the fink case and insist the glass is fragile simpliciter, you could say the sorcerer's spell is just a weird kind of mask that blocks the manifestation at the last instant, rather than genuinely removing the fragility. So it seems like whether you call something a fink or a mask depends entirely on how you're individuating the object that bears the disposition, and what you're willing to count as "the disposition itself" versus its manifestation conditions. Is there a principled way to draw the distinction that doesn't bottom out in these kinds of redescription games? And if there is, what's the cleanest case where finks and masks genuinely come apart — one where no reasonable redescription can turn a fink into a mask or vice versa? My worry is that the literature might be marking a distinction that's more about how we talk than about anything in the world, and I'd rather figure that out now than get to the final paper and realize I've been building on sand.
