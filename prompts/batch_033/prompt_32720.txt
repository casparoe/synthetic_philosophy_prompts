I'm trying to get clear on what I actually think about the morality of violence, going by my raw reactions to cases rather than a principle I've committed to in advance. I'd like you to look at my verdicts and tell me whether there's a coherent principle that captures them — or most of them — and what it says about the cases I'm still stuck on.

Here are the cases:

1. **Home intruder.** Someone breaks into my house at night carrying a weapon. I have a gun in the nightstand. I shoot and kill him. Verdict: permissible, though I wish I'd had a non-lethal option.

2. **Tackling the bomber.** I'm in a crowded market and see someone about to detonate a bomb. I tackle them hard, breaking their arm, and the bomb doesn't go off. Verdict: clearly permissible — the force is proportionate and necessary.

3. **Shooting the fleeing shoplifter.** A store owner sees a teenager steal a phone and run. The owner shoots the teenager in the back as they flee. Verdict: not permissible. The harm prevented doesn't come close to the harm inflicted.

4. **The torturing regime.** A revolutionary movement uses violence — including targeted killings of officials — to overthrow a regime that systematically tortures political prisoners and has killed tens of thousands. The revolutionaries have tried nonviolent resistance for years and been met with massacres. Verdict: I lean permissible, but this is the one that keeps me up at night. I'm not confident.

5. **The pipeline.** Activists sabotage construction equipment for a pipeline that, if completed, will lock in emissions that contribute to climate-related deaths, displacement, and ecosystem collapse. No one is physically harmed. Verdict: permissible. Property destruction feels categorically different from violence against persons, and I want a principle that respects that difference.

6. **The resisting spouse.** A woman is being physically attacked by her husband. She grabs a kitchen knife and stabs him, killing him. She had no other way to stop the attack. Verdict: clearly permissible.

7. **The defiant pacifist.** It's 1942. A twenty-year-old in Britain refuses to enlist, believing all killing is wrong, even in the war against Nazi Germany. Verdict: I can't bring myself to say this person is doing something *wrong*. I admire the stance, even though I also think the war is justified. This is the other one that keeps me up.

8. **Police beating a confession.** Officers beat a suspect they're certain is guilty of a child abduction to get him to reveal where the child is. The child is still alive but running out of time. Verdict: not permissible. I want to say the state especially should not do this, but I'm not sure my objection is only about the state.

9. **The brawler.** Two men get into a bar fight over an insult. Both are willing participants, neither is seriously injured. Verdict: wrong, but less wrong than most of the other cases on this list. I'm not sure why I feel that way.

10. **The sanction.** A country imposes economic sanctions on another country, knowing they will cause malnutrition and excess deaths among civilians, in order to pressure the regime to stop developing nuclear weapons. Verdict: not permissible. But I struggle to articulate why this is worse than the revolutionary case (4), given that the harm is similarly indirect and the goal is arguably less urgent.

I have a few themes I keep circling but can't turn into a principle: proportionality (but not just consequentialist calculation), imminence of the threat (but that doesn't explain why I'm okay with the pipeline sabotage), the difference between force and violence (but I can't define it), and something about whether the violence is deployed by a state or by a person. I also notice that some of my verdicts seem to treat killing — even justified killing — as a genuine moral loss, not just a neutral act once the justification is met, while others (like the bomber case) don't carry that weight.

What I'm asking for: is there a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures the pattern across these cases? For each candidate, tell me which of my verdicts it has to overturn, which ones it handles easily, and what it predicts for a case I haven't listed — ideally one that would let me test the principle against a fresh intuition. If no single principle gets all of them, I want to know which of my verdicts are the most expensive to keep and what the cleanest trade-off looks like.

Please separate what you take to be reasonably settled in the philosophical literature on the ethics of violence from what is genuinely contested among people who have worked on this, and flag any point where you're presenting a contested view as if it were consensus.
