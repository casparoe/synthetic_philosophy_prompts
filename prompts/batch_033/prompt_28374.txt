I cover the science-and-ideas beat, and for years I've been watching the Bayesian–frequentist dispute in statistics without seeing it move. What I find striking is that the replication crisis seemed to hand the Bayesians a powerful empirical exhibit: widespread failures to replicate look like exactly what you would expect if you accept that most published p < 0.05 results are weak evidence against a null that is usually false, and that low base rates of true effects combine with low power to produce a literature full of false positives. And yet the debate hasn't shifted in any decisive way. Practicing scientists still reach for null-hypothesis significance testing by default; reform efforts have mostly produced within-paradigm fixes (lower alpha thresholds, preregistration, corrections for multiplicity) rather than a turn toward Bayesian methods; and frequentist statisticians offer their own account of the crisis — p-hacking, researcher degrees of freedom, and publication bias — that doesn't require abandoning their framework. After a decade of this, I want a diagnosis of the impasse itself, not another round of adjudication. Is this a substantive disagreement about the nature of inference, so that pointing to replication failures genuinely cannot settle it because each side interprets the same evidence within a different conception of what a probability statement means? Is it partly verbal — two senses of "probability" generating an appearance of conflict where the real disagreement is about which questions are worth asking? Is it at bottom a practical dispute about which toolkit produces fewer errors in the hands of typical working scientists, which would make it an empirical question that nobody has run the right comparative trial to answer? Or is it something else — a sociological equilibrium in which the switching costs for journals, curricula, and grant committees exceed any theoretical advantage either side can demonstrate? I am not asking you to resolve the debate. I am asking you to characterize what kind of disagreement it is, drawing on what each side actually argues, and to tell me what, if anything, would constitute genuine progress given that characterization.
