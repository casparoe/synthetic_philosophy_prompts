I'm drafting a novel whose protagonist, a retired cartographer, becomes convinced that certain landscapes possess a kind of normative structure — that a river bend or a mountain pass does not merely sit there but somehow calls for a particular human response: a bridge here, a shrine there. She is not religious, and she does not think anyone placed these demands there. Her thought is that the reasons are free-floating: they do not belong to any agent, human or divine, yet they are genuinely reasons — considerations that count in favor of doing one thing rather than another. I want her to be articulate about this, and I want the novel's antagonist, a philosopher of action, to push back hard.

So I need to understand the actual philosophical controversy here. The debate, as I have gathered from my reading, turns on whether all reasons are internal — necessarily tied to some agent's existing desires, projects, or concerns — or whether there can be external reasons that apply to an agent regardless of what she happens to care about. But the free-floating-rationales idea seems to go a step further: it suggests reasons that are not tied to any agent at all, not even hypothetically. The antagonist would say this is incoherent — that a reason is always a reason for someone, and that talk of agentless reasons is a category mistake, like asking what is north of the North Pole. The protagonist would reply that we already accept something like this in other domains: mathematical truths do not depend on mathematicians, and perhaps normative truths do not depend on norm-followers either. She might also point to the way we talk about the natural world as having a grain or a logic that makes certain interventions fitting and others clumsy, even when no one's interests are at stake.

I would like an essay that weighs the case for and against the existence of free-floating rationales — reasons without reasoners. The essay should engage the internalist challenge seriously, consider what theoretical costs each side pays, and reach a definite conclusion. Do not just map the terrain; take a position and defend it. This will directly shape how I write the central philosophical conversations in the novel.
