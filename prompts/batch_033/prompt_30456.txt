I am a professor of philosophy and chair of my university's Grounds and Sustainability Committee. Each month we review proposals for insecticide use across the campus: aphids on the ornamental lindens along the main quadrangle, mosquito control around the stormwater wetlands, drywood termites in the nineteenth-century library, scale insects in the glasshouses, ants in the student kitchens. At present we decide case by case, and our discussions lurch between members who regard insects as beneath moral consideration, members who would ban all chemical control on precautionary grounds, and members who simply defer to the pest-control contractor's recommendation. I want to stop relitigating first principles every month, and to give the facilities director, who signs off on every application, something more principled than whichever argument prevailed at the last meeting.

Please write a decision procedure the committee can actually run on a proposal. It should be grounded in what environmental and animal ethics can defensibly say about harming and killing insects, yet remain usable by non-specialists in about half an hour per proposal, on the strength of evidence the proposing unit can reasonably supply. Specify the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure returns a no or attaches conditions, and where it must hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. Pay particular attention to the hard cases: uncertainty about insect sentience, the difference between nociception and pain, the difference between harms to individual organisms and harms to populations or ecosystems, the value-ladenness of the category 'pest', and the question of who bears the residual risk. Be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and about what the procedure cannot settle.

Then run the procedure on three proposals the committee will actually see this term: (i) a neonicotinoid soil drench for aphids on the ornamental lindens, where the damage is purely cosmetic; (ii) a larvicide application to the campus wetlands following two locally acquired cases of mosquito-borne disease; and (iii) whole-structure fumigation of the library to eliminate a drywood termite infestation threatening the timber frame and the special collections. I expect at least one of these to yield an answer the committee will find unwelcome; make that explicit rather than softening it. Please write the answer in British English.
