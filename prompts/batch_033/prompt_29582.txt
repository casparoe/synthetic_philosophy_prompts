I just spent two hours arguing with a friend about whether an author's intentions determine what a text means, and I've come away less sure of my own view than when I started. I'm going to lay out my gut reactions to a series of cases, and I'd like you to tell me what theory of textual meaning — if any — captures the pattern, and which of my verdicts I might need to give up.

Here are the cases:

1. A poet writes a couplet that readers find haunting and profound. Years later, in an interview, the poet says: "Honestly, I just liked the sound of the words together. I wasn't trying to express anything." My verdict: the couplet still means what readers find in it. The poet's denial of intent doesn't erase the meaning.

2. A novelist dies. Among her papers, scholars find a letter in which she states that a major character was meant to be gay. Nothing in the published novel clearly indicates this; the character's sexuality is never addressed. My verdict: the character is not gay in the world of the novel. The intention didn't make it onto the page.

3. A legislature passes a statute. The text as written plainly prohibits activity X. But the legislative record shows that every single legislator who voted for it believed it permitted activity X, and they all stated this during debate. My verdict: the statute prohibits X. The text says what it says.

4. A poet uses the word "silly" in a serious poem about a saint. Unbeknownst to the poet, "silly" in Middle English meant "blessed" or "innocent." The older meaning fits the poem beautifully — far better than the modern one. My verdict: the older meaning is not part of what the poem means, however much it fits. The poet didn't know it.

5. Two parties sign a contract. The text says the buyer must pay "within thirty days of delivery." Both parties understood this to mean thirty business days, and both acted on that understanding for years. A dispute arises, and a literal reading would mean thirty calendar days. My verdict: the contract means thirty business days. The shared understanding at the time of signing controls.

6. A 1930s novel describes a Jewish character using language that, at the time, was common and not widely regarded as antisemitic by gentile readers. Today the language is unmistakably antisemitic. My verdict: the language is antisemitic in the novel. The meaning of the words has changed, and the novel now carries that meaning.

7. A comedian tells a joke that she sincerely intends as a playful tease. The audience hears it as cruel. My verdict: the joke is cruel. What the speaker intended doesn't override how the words reasonably land.

I can feel that these verdicts are pulling in different directions. Cases 1, 6, and 7 seem to side with the reader or audience over the author. Cases 2, 3, and 4 seem to side with the text over the author's private intentions. But case 5 sides with the parties' shared intentions over the text. And case 2 and case 1 seem to be in direct tension: why does the poet's denial of intent not matter, but the novelist's assertion of intent also not matter? Am I just privileging whatever outcome lets me ignore the author?

Is there a single principle that makes sense of all seven verdicts? If not, which ones should I reconsider, and what's the best overall account of the relationship between authorial intention and textual meaning that fits the cases I should keep?
