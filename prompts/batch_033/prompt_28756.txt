ok so I've been going down a rabbit hole about constitutions and whether they actually make sense. The idea is that a constitution is like a Ulysses contract — you tie yourself to the mast because you know future-you is gonna do something stupid. A country writes a constitution to stop future majorities from doing dumb stuff in a panic. That all sounds fine. But then I started thinking about it and now I'm stuck with this argument that seems to prove constitutions are basically illegitimate, and I can't find the exit.

(1) If a constitution has genuine moral force, then the people who wrote and ratified it had the authority to bind everyone who came after.
(2) If they had that authority, then a group of people at one point in time can make rules for people who don't exist yet and who never get a say.
(3) If a group can make binding rules for people who never get a say, then "consent of the governed" is not actually required for legitimate government.
(4) If consent of the governed isn't required, then there's no principled difference between a constitutional democracy and a dictatorship that happened to be popular for one generation and then just stuck around.
(5) But there obviously IS a difference — like, a real one, not just a vibe.
(6) So either constitutions don't have genuine moral force (they're just habits we've gotten used to), or consent of the governed doesn't matter as much as we thought.

I really don't want to give up (5). But I also feel like giving up (1) means the whole idea of constitutional government is just a polite fiction. And giving up consent of the governed seems even worse. Where does this argument actually break? Is there a step where I'm sliding between two different meanings of something without noticing?
