Friedrich Nietzsche died in 1900, well before the procreation asymmetry was formalized in analytic ethics by David Benatar and others. The asymmetry, in its canonical form, runs as follows. The presence of pain in a life is bad for the person who experiences it, and the absence of that pain is good — even if no one exists to enjoy that absence. The presence of pleasure is good for the person who experiences it, but the absence of that pleasure is not bad — because there is no one who is deprived of it. From this, the asymmetry's proponents argue that coming into existence is always a net harm: the pains of existence count against it, while the pleasures we would have missed by never existing cannot count in its favor, since no one would have been around to regret them.

Your task is to predict, with careful textual reasoning, how Nietzsche would have responded to the procreation asymmetry had he encountered it in its modern form. Address the following in your response.

First, identify the specific Nietzschean commitments that bear on whether he would accept the asymmetry's core value judgments. Consider in particular his views on the role of suffering in human flourishing, his critique of hedonism and of utilitarian calculation, his doctrine of the will to power, and his conception of life-affirmation. For each commitment you invoke, explain how it cuts — does it push him toward accepting the asymmetry, toward rejecting it, or toward recasting it in terms alien to the original debate?

Second, if Nietzsche would reject the asymmetry, specify precisely where he would break it. Would he deny that the absence of pain is good (perhaps because he holds that suffering is a precondition of greatness)? Would he deny that the absence of pleasure is not bad (perhaps because he thinks squandered creative potential is a loss even when no subject remains to feel it)? Or would he accept the value judgments but deny that they license the conclusion that existence is a net harm, on the grounds that the asymmetry's framing smuggles in a hedonistic calculus that his own evaluative framework does not share? Reconstruct the argument he would offer, staying faithful to the structure of his actual critiques of morality.

Third, identify the Nietzschean concepts or passages — such as the eternal recurrence, amor fati, or the critique of the "last man" — that, while not addressing the procreation asymmetry directly, would commit him to a determinate stance on it. Do not fabricate citations; rely only on well-known Nietzschean themes that a competent reader would recognize.

Finally, consider whether Nietzsche could have turned the asymmetry against its own pessimistic conclusion. Is there a reading of the asymmetry — perhaps one that treats the radical asymmetry between existence and non-existence as grounds for affirming the value of whatever life one has, precisely because there is no counterfactual claimant to a better alternative — that would make it serve an affirmative rather than an anti-natalist ethics? If such a reading is available, sketch it; if not, explain why the asymmetry is, by Nietzsche's lights, irredeemably life-denying regardless of how it is handled.
