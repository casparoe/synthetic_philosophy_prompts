I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar called "Philosophy of Biology" — about 16 students, mostly biology and philosophy majors, one three-hour session with a break. We've just finished a unit on the logic of natural selection, and next session we start Gould and Lewontin's "The Spandrels of San Marco and the Panglossian Paradigm." I want them to arrive at the paper having already felt the central problem in their own reasoning, not just read about it.

Design a classroom activity for this session that makes students experience the seductiveness of adaptationist explanations and the difficulty of the spandrels critique from the inside. The activity should work as follows: before they know the paper's thesis, they should be put in a position where they spontaneously generate adaptationist stories for organismal traits — and then discover that they were just as confident and inventive for traits that are not, in fact, adaptations. The penny should drop before I name it.

The activity needs: rules and group structure; a set of traits (real ones, with a mix of adaptations, byproducts, and traits whose status is genuinely disputed); a procedure for generating and presenting explanations; a mechanism for the reveal that makes the point land; and a structured debrief that maps what happened in the room onto the formal distinctions in the paper (adaptation vs. spandrel vs. exaptation, the Panglossian paradigm, the burden-of-proof problem, and the difference between "how come?" and "what for?"). The debrief should also flag: what the activity gets right about the critique, what it unavoidably distorts (e.g., the activity uses pre-classified cases, whereas real biology has to do the classifying), and what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — including which students will resist the point and on what grounds, and how to handle that without lecturing. No lecture up front; they should not hear the word "spandrel" until the debrief.

Please keep the response to at most 1,200 words, and structure it with clear sections for setup, rules, materials, timeline, the reveal, and the debrief.
