I work as a software engineer at a mid-sized technology company, and I serve on our internal Environmental and Social Impact Review Committee — mostly because I volunteered when they needed someone from engineering, not because I have any background in ecology or ethics. We are currently reviewing a proposal to build a new satellite office on a thirty-acre parcel of scrubland the company has acquired. The standard environmental assessment covers the usual items: stormwater runoff, carbon impact of construction, traffic, and whether any protected species are present on the site (none are). But at our last meeting, one committee member — an ecologist we contract with for these reviews — raised an argument I have not been able to stop thinking about. She pointed out that the parcel currently supports a dense population of small mammals, reptiles, and ground-nesting birds. These animals experience extremely high mortality rates: most die within their first year from predation, parasitism, disease, exposure, or starvation. She argued that if we are genuinely concerned with reducing suffering — and our corporate values statements do invoke that language — we should consider the possibility that displacing these animals through construction might actually reduce net suffering. The animals would either relocate to less crowded habitat or simply not be replaced, and the land would go from being a site of constant predation and deprivation to a parking lot and an office building where nothing suffers at all. She was not being flippant; she was entirely serious, and she wants the committee's final recommendation to acknowledge this line of reasoning rather than bracket it as beyond our scope.

The committee is split. Some members find the argument perverse — they say our mandate is to minimize environmental harm, not to act as utilitarian accountants weighing wild animal misery against office floorplans, and they worry about the precedent it would set for future projects. Others think we would be intellectually dishonest to ignore the argument now that it has been raised, since we routinely invoke welfare considerations when they cut in the opposite direction (for instance, when we highlight the wellbeing of local communities that would benefit from jobs). Our chair has asked me to draft a short written opinion — perhaps 800 to 1,000 words — that the committee can discuss and vote on at our next meeting. The opinion should recommend a concrete position: should our environmental impact statement for this project include a wild-animal-welfare analysis of the kind the ecologist is proposing, yes or no (or yes with specified constraints)? It should engage seriously with the ecologist's argument rather than dismissing it out of hand, and it should give an honest account of the strongest objection to whatever position I end up recommending. I am out of my depth on the underlying philosophical questions, but I am the one who has to produce the draft.
