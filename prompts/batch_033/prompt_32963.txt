I've been going down a bit of a rabbit hole with the wrong kind of reasons problem and I'd love to get it dramatized rather than just described in a paper. You know the setup: there's an evil demon who credibly threatens to punish you unless you admire him. So now you've got a reason to admire him, but it feels totally different from the reason you have to admire someone who's genuinely admirable — the demon didn't give you the *right* kind of reason, he just made the attitude pay off. The literature tries to draw this distinction rigorously: object-given reasons (bearing on whether the object is fitting) vs. state-given reasons (bearing on whether being in the state is good for you). But people keep arguing about whether the distinction can actually be drawn without begging the question.

I've got two colleagues — both sharp, both working in normativity — who are on opposite sides of this. One of them, let's call her the Fittingness Theorist, thinks the distinction is real and can be grounded in the constitutive aim or standard of correctness for each attitude: belief aims at truth, admiration aims at the admirable, fear aims at the dangerous, so a reason that doesn't engage with that standard is just the wrong kind of reason for *that* attitude. The other, the Skeptic, thinks the whole project is circular: to say what the "aim" of an attitude is, you're already making a normative claim about what counts as a good instance of that attitude, which is exactly the distinction you were trying to draw in the first place. And if you try to draw it naturalistically — say, by appeal to the functional role of the attitude — you lose the normativity you needed.

Could you write a dialogue between them? Maybe they're at a bar after a workshop, a couple drinks in, and they actually try to locate where their disagreement bottoms out. I want real engagement — each should respond to the other's actual moves, not just re-state their view. Both should be competent and charitable. And ideally the dialogue should surface the deepest point of divergence rather than just cycling through the familiar dialectical moves. If one of them ends up genuinely uncertain or the conversation shifts terrain, that's fine — I just want to see the reasoning in motion.
