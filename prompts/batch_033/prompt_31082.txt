I'm in this reading group that's been working through metaethics — we started with Mackie's error theory, then did some stuff on moral disagreement, and now we're circling around questions about how you tell a genuine moral dispute from a clash of mere preferences or biases. And there's this one guy, let's call him Dan, who has a habit of dropping "de gustibus non est disputandum" whenever someone brings up a moral disagreement he personally finds tiresome. Last week it was about eating octopus (he says it's just a preference, like preferring chocolate ice cream to vanilla), and the week before it was about polyamory. The slogan lands like a conversation-ender, and everyone just... moves on.

I can feel that the slogan is doing way too much work. The whole question we're supposed to be wresting with is exactly how to draw the line between preferences and moral judgments — and Dan is using a slogan that presupposes the line is already obvious and that the thing he doesn't care about falls on the "mere preference" side. But I'm not articulate enough in the moment to say why this bothers me.

So I want to come to the next session with a breakdown. What are the distinct claims that "de gustibus non est disputandum" could be making? I'm imagining there are at least five or six, ranging from something trivial (like "you can't argue someone into liking a flavor they don't like") to something substantive and controversial (like "there are no objective facts about value, period"). For each reading, I want to know: is it defensible? And — this is the key part — does it actually license what Dan is doing, i.e., dismissing a putatively moral disagreement as "just taste" without first establishing that it is one? My suspicion is that the readings that are defensible don't do the work he wants, and the readings that would do the work aren't defensible. But I want to see it laid out clearly so I can bring it to the group.
