I'm teaching an upper-division aesthetics seminar and want to give my students a decision tree that sorts them into a position on aesthetic testimony. The tree should start from a single entry question and branch through further distinctions until each student arrives at a named position, or at an unoccupied region of logical space.

The positions that must appear as leaves include: strong pessimism (aesthetic testimony can never transmit knowledge or justified belief), weak pessimism (it can transmit propositional knowledge that something is beautiful but not the grounds for that judgment), optimism (aesthetic testimony works like any other testimony), and the acquaintance view (aesthetic knowledge requires direct acquaintance with the object, so testimony is necessarily insufficient). If there are other defensible positions in the literature, include them.

Each branching node should be a question a student can answer without knowing which position it leads to. The questions should turn on concrete cases: e.g., whether reading a trusted critic's review can give you knowledge that a film is good before you see it, whether a wine novice can know that a wine has notes of blackberry solely from an expert's say-so, whether there is a relevant difference between moral testimony and aesthetic testimony, or whether someone blind from birth can know that a painting is beautiful from a detailed verbal description.

Flag any pairs of positions your tree cannot cleanly separate, and note any regions of logical space that are unoccupied — with a brief diagnosis of whether each empty region is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply underexplored in the literature.
