Take the plate tectonics revolution in geology as a case study for Kuhn's picture of normal science, anomaly, crisis, and paradigm shift. I know the broad outlines: Wegener proposed continental drift around the 1910s and mostly got dismissed, and then in the 50s and 60s paleomagnetism, seafloor spreading, and the magnetic stripe patterns flipped the field to mobilism and plate tectonics. What I want is the careful mapping, not the textbook version — dont just walk through the stages. Before the shift, who exactly was the relevant scientific community and what did normal science look like for them — what problems were they working on, and what background commitments made drift seem absurd? Which things should count as anomalies in Kuhn's sense — the jigsaw fit of the continents, the paleomagnetic pole wander, the mid-ocean ridges, the Vine-Matthews stripes — and why did some of them sit around for decades without producing a crisis? Then do the incommensurability part honestly: did the fixists and mobilists actually mean different things by terms like 'continent', 'crust', or 'mountain-building', or is that overblown and the disagreement was mostly about what evidence to trust? Then tell me where Kuhn's model strains. Was this one paradigm shift or a bunch of smaller ones? Did the old guard have to die off, or did the same people convert when the ocean-floor data came in? Does the speed of the 1960s shift support Kuhn, or does it look more like normal science paying off? And end with what the episode does to the theory: is plate tectonics a poster child for Kuhnian revolutions, or a case that shows the paradigm concept doing too much work?
