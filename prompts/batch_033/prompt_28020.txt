I'm working on a novel where one of the central characters is a dog — not a talking dog, not a magical dog, just a regular dog whose experience I'm trying to render from the inside. And I keep running up against this wall: how much interiority can I actually give this creature without cheating? I've been reading around in philosophy of mind and stumbled onto Descartes' view that animals are basically automata — that they have no minds, no consciousness, just mechanical responses to stimuli. It's a repellent idea in some ways (I've lived with dogs, I've seen them dream), but it's also weirdly generative for a fiction writer, because it forces you to ask what exactly you think you're doing when you write "the dog felt sad."

So here's what I'd love: could you read Jack London's The Call of the Wild through the lens of Descartes' views on animal minds? London is doing something fascinating — he gives Buck a rich inner life, motivations, memories, even something like moral reasoning, but the whole arc of the novel is Buck shedding domestication and returning to something more instinctual, more "mechanical" in a sense. Is London actually vindicating Descartes in some backhanded way, by showing that Buck's most authentic self is the one that operates without the trappings of something like human consciousness? Or does the novel make Descartes' position impossible to sustain, because we're inside Buck's head the whole time and it sure feels like a mind? I'm especially interested in the moments where London attributes complex emotions or quasi-rational deliberation to Buck — are those moments where the novel cheats, or are they precisely what makes the Cartesian view look thin? And if you were advising a novelist who wants to write animal consciousness without sentimentalizing it, what would you say The Call of the Wild gets right that the philosophical debates miss?
