I lead an undergraduate metaphysics reading group, and I want to open our unit on material constitution with a diagnostic quiz titled "Which theory of material constitution do you actually hold?" The quiz should sort people by their verdicts about concrete cases, not by which label they recognize or find attractive, so no question may ask anything like "are the statue and the piece of bronze identical?" or "do you believe in coincident objects?" Every question must present a case and ask the respondent to commit to a claim about what exists, what survives, or what goes out of existence in that case.

The leaves the quiz should sort into are these five:
- the one-object view: the statue is identical with the piece of bronze, and "statue" and "piece" are two descriptions of a single thing;
- the constitution view: the statue and the piece of bronze are two distinct material objects that coincide while the statue exists;
- the dominant-kind view: there is exactly one object, and while the bronze is statue-shaped that object is the statue, not the piece of bronze — the piece does not exist then;
- eliminativism about ordinary composites: strictly speaking there is no statue and no piece of bronze, only particles arranged statuewise;
- four-dimensionalism: the statue and the piece are distinct things that share a temporal part.

Please write eight to ten questions. Use at least these cases, adding one or two only if a distinction needs its own case: a bronze statue made from a pre-existing piece of bronze and later squashed (or the clay version of the same case); Tibbles the cat and the cat-minus-its-tail, before and after the tail is detached; a ship repaired plank by plank; and a toy tower built out of blocks and then knocked over. Phrase the answer options as concrete claims about the case.

Then supply a scoring key. Build it from the logical space of answers rather than from the list of labels: first lay out the combinations of answers your questions allow, then locate the five positions within that space. Flag any leaves your questions cannot fully separate, and say what the residual test is — I suspect the constitution view and four-dimensionalism will agree on most case verdicts and come apart only over whether an object is wholly present at each moment or is extended over time with temporal parts, so say explicitly whether that is the only separating item on your key. Also note any unoccupied or unstable combinations and diagnose each briefly as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. End with a one-sentence "most awkward commitment" for each of the five positions.
