I just had a long, frustrating argument with a friend who practices criminal law, and I can't get one of her challenges out of my head. We were discussing the extended mind thesis — the idea, associated with Clark and Chalmers, that cognitive processes don't stop at the skull, that a notebook or a smartphone can literally be part of your mind when it's reliably coupled to your cognitive system in the right way. She was skeptical of the metaphysics, but what really threw me was her practical question: if the thesis is true, why does the law treat your phone and your brain so differently?

Consider the asymmetry. If police want to search your phone, they need a warrant — but they can get one, and they do, routinely. Customs agents at many borders can search your phone without any warrant at all. If someone steals your phone and wipes it, they've committed theft and maybe criminal damage to property. If they delete the notes you rely on to remember your appointments, your deadlines, your loved ones' faces, it's data loss — not the kind of personal violation we'd recognize if someone used a drug to erase your biological memories. Meanwhile, the state cannot compel you to submit to a brain scan that reads your thoughts, and causing brain damage is aggravated assault or worse, not a property offense. The law draws a bright line at the skin, and everything outside it is just a thing you own.

I want to know what theory of the mind this practice presupposes. Reconstruct the implicit metaphysics: what view of where cognition lives makes the legal boundary between person and property coherent? Then tell me whether the extended mind theorist should see this as a genuine normative inconsistency — something the law gets wrong and ought to reform — or whether there are principled reasons, compatible with the extended mind thesis, for treating external cognitive resources differently from internal ones. And I want you to be explicit about what would change your mind: which argument or empirical consideration would most likely flip your verdict, or where in your reasoning you think the greatest uncertainty lies.
