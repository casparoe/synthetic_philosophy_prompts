I've been arguing with a friend about consequentialist cluelessness, and he ran an argument that I now suspect proves too much, but I can't find the exact step to reject. It goes like this.

(1) Consequentialism says an act is right just in case its consequences are no worse than those of any alternative.

(2) The consequences of an ordinary act include its remote effects. Buy the coffee or don't, take the job or don't — the difference ramifies through other people's lives, through who meets whom, and eventually through who gets born, and after a few links the chain is chaotic: tiny differences now can be the difference between a future person existing or not, or between a war and no war.

(3) So for any ordinary act, we can't know what its actual consequences are, and we can't assign well-grounded probabilities to the outcomes either, because the outcome space is unbounded and our information about the relevant mechanisms is basically nil.

(4) The usual consequentialist fallback is to maximize expected value. But expected value requires a probability distribution over outcomes, and (3) says we don't have one. So expected value is undefined for ordinary acts.

(5) If actual consequences are unknowable and expected value is undefined, consequentialism yields no verdict on ordinary acts — for all we can say, any two ordinary acts are on a par.

(6) A moral theory that yields no verdict on ordinary acts can't guide action. So consequentialism fails as a moral theory.

I was ready to accept this, until I noticed the same arguement seems to run for everyone. A deontologist also has to know what act she's performing — whether this is a lie, or a promise-breaking, or a killing — and that can depend on the very same remote chains (is the person I'm helping actually a murderer? will what I say now turn out to be false in a way I can't foresee?). If (5) and (6) are enough to sink consequentialism, then the same cluelessness means no moral theory can guide action beyond a short horizon, and no one can ever know wether an ordinary act is permissible. That seems absurd: it would make blame, moral confidence, and ordinary decision-making all illegitimate, and my friend doesn't accept that conclusion either.

So either the argument refutes every moral theory, which is too much, or one of the steps is fine against consequentialism but can be blocked for everyone else — or the argument is just valid and the price is that we really are that clueless. Which premise do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If I have to choose, I'd rather keep consequentialism than accept that no one ever knows what they're doing, but I want to know what I'm paying.
