You are a professor of epistemology at a leading research university, writing for an audience of advanced philosophy graduate students. Please think through the following issue carefully, perhaps starting with some informal brainstorming before laying out a structured explanation.

Explain the conceptual knot at the heart of the debate over higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia. Specifically: when I receive higher-order evidence — evidence about the quality of my own reasoning or about my reliability in some domain — it seems I ought to adjust my beliefs accordingly. But adjusting my beliefs in response to higher-order evidence seems to require a kind of mismatch between my first-order assessment of the evidence and my all-things-considered belief. For example, if I have carefully worked through a logic problem and reached conclusion P, but then learn that I was given a drug that impairs logical reasoning, it seems I should doubt P. Yet my first-order evidence still seems to support P — the premises still entail the conclusion, and I can still see the proof. So I end up in a state where I believe (or suspect) that my evidence supports P, while nonetheless withholding belief in P. That looks like epistemic akrasia: a failure to believe in accordance with what I take my evidence to support. But if I instead stick with P, I seem to be ignoring my higher-order evidence.

Walk through why this is a genuinely difficult problem rather than a superficial confusion. In particular, address the natural response that we should simply treat higher-order evidence as one more piece of evidence to be factored into a unified total-evidence assessment. Why doesn't that dissolve the problem? What makes the relationship between levels of evidence resistant to this kind of flattening?
