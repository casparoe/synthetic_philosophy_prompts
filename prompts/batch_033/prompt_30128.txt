I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar called "Ethics at the Limit," covering decision theory, population ethics, and the long-term future. We've just finished the unit on infinite ethics — Bostrom's paper and several responses. For the take-home final, I need an essay question that separates students who can reconstruct the dialectic from those who can actually diagnose what's at stake. Too many students come away thinking the takeaway is just "comparing infinities is hard" and then summarize SBI, hyperreals, and value-density as if they were competing iPhone models.

I want a question that forces them to ask: is the infinite ethics challenge a problem about *ethics* or a problem about *infinity*? That is, does it reveal that consequentialism's commitment to maximizing total value is structurally flawed in a way that no technical fix can patch, or is it merely an artifact of using tools — limits, cardinal arithmetic, the extended reals — that weren't designed for ethical reasoning, and which might dissolve once we have the right mathematics of infinite value? A student who has really understood the material should be able to argue both sides of this and then take a stand.

Please write the question, a model answer of about 800 words, and a marking scheme that allocates 25 marks across five criteria. For each criterion, include a brief note on what distinguishes an A-level response from a B-level response.

---

**The question:**

Some philosophers argue that the puzzles of infinite ethics are fundamentally puzzles about infinity — they arise because we are trying to extend finite aggregation techniques to infinite domains, and they will yield to the right mathematical tools, much as Zeno's paradoxes yielded to the calculus. Others argue that the puzzles are fundamentally ethical: they reveal that the very idea of "total value" lacks a well-defined meaning independently of substantive, contestable choices about how to compare infinite worlds, and that no mathematical formalism can make those choices for us.

Which side of this dispute is more plausible, and why? In answering, you must engage with at least two specific proposals for handling infinite ethics (e.g., the SBI approach, hyperreal-valued utilities, value-density, or any other proposal discussed in the course) and explain what they reveal — individually and collectively — about whether the problem is technical or ethical in nature.

---

**Model answer (approx. 800 words):**

The infinite ethics challenge begins from a simple observation: if consequentialism ranks actions by the total value of their consequences, and the world might contain infinitely many locations of value — an infinite future, an infinite universe, or a multiverse — then standard aggregation breaks down. Adding or subtracting any finite amount of value to an infinite total leaves the total unchanged; comparing two infinite totals requires a choice of ordering or measure that the mathematics alone does not supply. The question is whether this breakdown reveals a defect in our mathematical tools or a defect in the consequentialist project itself.

The case for treating the problem as fundamentally about infinity, and therefore as technical rather than ethical, runs as follows. Throughout the history of mathematics, infinities have generated apparent paradoxes that dissolved once the right formalism was developed. Zeno's paradox of the arrow seemed to show that motion was impossible until the limit concept made sense of summing infinitely many vanishingly small intervals. Early calculus was plagued by anxieties about infinitesimals until the epsilon-delta definition of limits, and later non-standard analysis, showed that infinitesimals could be handled rigorously. The infinite ethics problem, on this view, is at a similar stage: we are trying to do ethical arithmetic with tools — cardinal numbers, the extended reals, naïve limit-taking — that were never designed to represent ethical value. Once we develop a mathematics of value that is fit for purpose, the puzzles will dissolve.

This view draws support from the fact that several proposed solutions are mathematically well-defined. The hyperreal approach, for instance, assigns values from a non-Archimedean field, allowing us to say that one infinite world is strictly larger than another even when both are infinite in the standard reals. If this formalism yields consistent, principled rankings, then the problem was never that consequentialism is incoherent — only that we were using the wrong number system. Similarly, if a value-density approach (taking limits of finite truncations as the domain expands) converges to a well-defined ranking for the cases we care about, then the apparent underdetermination was an artifact of asking the question in the wrong order. On this picture, the ethical content of consequentialism — maximize total value — remains untouched; we are merely upgrading the arithmetic.

The case for treating the problem as fundamentally ethical begins by denying that the analogy with Zeno's paradoxes is apt. Zeno's puzzles concerned physical motion, and the calculus succeeded because there was an independent physical fact of the matter — the arrow does reach the target — that any adequate mathematics had to respect. In ethics, there is no independent fact of the matter about which infinite world is better. The mathematics does not discover a pre-existing ethical ordering; it constructs one, and different constructions encode different ethical commitments.

Consider the SBI (subsets of basis of infinity) approach. To apply it, one must specify a "basis" of locations — a privileged subset of the infinite domain relative to which comparisons are made. But which subset? If we privilege the present moment over the distant future, we get one ranking; if we privilege sentient beings over nonsentient systems, we get another. These choices are not mathematical; they are ethical decisions about what matters and what can be traded off against what. The mathematics of SBI does not tell us which basis to pick — it merely operationalizes a choice we have already made on other grounds.

The hyperreal approach faces a parallel difficulty. While hyperreal numbers allow us to represent infinite values and compare them, the assignment of specific hyperreal values to specific states of affairs is not determined by the mathematics. We must decide, for example, whether an infinite universe in which every third location contains a happy person is worth more, less, or the same as one in which every second location does — and the ratio we choose will reflect a substantive view about the diminishing marginal value of additional happy lives, or about whether spatial density of value matters at all. The formalism is ethically inert until we pour content into it.

This pattern — each formalism requires a choice that the formalism itself cannot justify — suggests that the problem is not that we lack the right mathematics, but that the consequentialist criterion of "total value" is indeterminate in infinite contexts. There is no such thing as the total value of an infinite world until we specify a rule for aggregating across infinity, and any such rule will be ethically controversial. The difficulty is not that we cannot do the sum; it is that there is no sum to be done until we make ethical choices that consequentialism was supposed to obviate.

On balance, the second view is more plausible. The Zeno analogy fails precisely because ethics lacks the independent constraints that physics provided. In physics, the fact that the arrow reaches the target ruled out formalisms that denied motion; in ethics, nothing rules out formalisms that give different answers to the same infinite comparison except our prior ethical commitments. The infinite ethics literature does not reveal that consequentialism is false, but it does reveal that consequentialism is less of a complete ethical theory than its proponents sometimes suppose. It is a framework that requires supplementation by a theory of how to weigh locations, times, and persons against one another — and in the infinite case, that supplementation cannot be supplied by formal convenience alone. The mathematics can ensure consistency, but it cannot supply the content.

**Marking scheme (25 marks total, 5 criteria × 5 marks each):**

**1. Accurate and precise statement of the infinite ethics problem (5 marks)**
- A-level (5 marks): Explains the structural issue with precision — e.g., that adding or subtracting finite value leaves an infinite total unchanged under standard arithmetic, or that comparing infinite sets requires a choice of measure that cardinality alone does not supply. Avoids hand-waving phrases like "you can't compare infinities" in favor of saying exactly why the comparison is underdetermined.
- B-level (3 marks): Gets the general idea right but is imprecise — e.g., says "infinity makes the math break down" without specifying how, or conflates the problem with Pascal's mugging.
- Common mistake: Students treat infinite ethics as identical to the St. Petersburg paradox or Pascal's wager, missing the specifically aggregative structure of the problem.

**2. Articulation of the "technical problem" position (5 marks)**
- A-level (5 marks): Makes the case that the problem is a mathematical artifact by drawing on specific analogies (Zeno, the history of infinitesimals, or the development of non-standard analysis) and explaining why they suggest the problem will dissolve. Engages with at least one specific proposal (hyperreals, value-density, etc.) as evidence that mathematically well-defined solutions exist.
- B-level (3 marks): Asserts that "better math might fix it" without developing the analogy or showing how a specific proposal actually resolves the difficulty.
- Common mistake: Students claim that hyperreal numbers "solve" infinite ethics without noting that hyperreal assignments themselves require ethical choices.

**3. Articulation of the "ethical problem" position (5 marks)**
- A-level (5 marks): Argues that the problem resists purely technical solution because any aggregation rule embeds substantive ethical commitments — e.g., about which locations matter, how to weigh spatial versus temporal density, or whether certain infinite orderings are commensurable. Shows that these commitments cannot be derived from the mathematics alone.
- B-level (3 marks): Asserts that "values aren't numbers" or "ethics can't be formalized" without showing exactly where the formalism underdetermines the ethical content.
- Common mistake: Students slide into general skepticism about formal ethics rather than identifying the specific locus of underdetermination.

**4. Engagement with at least two specific proposals (5 marks)**
- A-level (5 marks): Discusses at least two proposals (SBI, hyperreals, value-density, expansionism, etc.) with precision about how each works and what choices each requires. Uses the comparison between proposals to illuminate the technical-versus-ethical dispute — e.g., by showing that the proposals differ not just mathematically but in the ethical judgments they encode.
- B-level (3 marks): Mentions two proposals by name and gives a roughly accurate summary of each, but does not analyze their structure or use them to advance the argument.
- Common mistake: Students list proposals without comparing them, treating the essay as a taxonomy exercise rather than an argument.

**5. Quality and clarity of the student's own position (5 marks)**
- A-level (5 marks): Takes a clear, argued stand on whether the problem is primarily technical or ethical. Defends that stand against the strongest objection from the other side. Shows awareness of what would count as evidence against their view — i.e., acknowledges the conditions under which they would change their mind.
- B-level (3 marks): Takes a position but defends it weakly, ignoring the strongest counterargument, or hedges to the point of taking no position at all.
- Common mistake: Students conclude that "both sides have a point" without explaining which point is weightier or why the dispute matters for consequentialism as a going concern.
