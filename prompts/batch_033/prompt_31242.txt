I keep running into a line in arguments about utilitarianism and I honestly can't tell how much work it's doing. The line is "the separateness of persons." Someone will say that utilitarianism ignores the separateness of persons, and that's meant to be a deep problem with the whole approach — not with a particular calculation but with adding up well-being across different people at all. Most recently I was defending the idea that a policy that makes one person much worse off but makes thousands of people a little better off could still be the right call, and the reply was basically "you're treating the population as one big person, which is exactly the separateness of persons objection," and that was supposed to settle it.

The thing is, I can think of several different claims that phrase might be making, and they're not equivalent. It might mean there's no single subject who experiences both the loss and the gains, so the trade-off isn't a net benefit for anyone. It might mean trade-offs within one life are legitimate but trade-offs across lives aren't. It might mean a sum of utilities is just a number that doesn't correspond to anything real. It might mean each person is a seperate source of claims, so the distribution matters and not just the total. It might be something Rawls-flavored about justice, or something about the difference between a society and an individual agent. I don't know which of these, if any, is the actual objection.

I want a menu, not a single reading. For each precise thesis the slogan could be expressing, tell me: is it trivially true, substantive and plausible, or actually false? Then say which reading or readings would actually support the move my interlocutor was making — that is, which one, if true, makes it illegitimate to trade one person's large loss for thousands of people's small gains. And if the slogan is quietly sliding between two of these readings in ordinary use, I want to know where the slide happens.

Also, flag the verdict you're least confident about and tell me what consideration or counterexample would change your mind on it; I'd rather know where the reasoning is most likely to fail than get a false sense of closure. Write in American English.
