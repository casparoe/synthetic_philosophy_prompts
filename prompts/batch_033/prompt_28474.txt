Two friends of mine got into it pretty bad last weekend. My neighbor Sarah has a teenage daughter who ran away from her abusive dad a few months back, and Sarah's been hiding her. Last week the dad showed up at Sarah's door asking if she knew where his daughter was. Sarah looked him in the eye and said she hadnt seen her in weeks. Afterward my friends were debating this. Mark said Sarah did the right thing, obviously — telling the truth would've put the daughter in danger, end of story. Jen said no, lying is still wrong, and Kant was right that you can't just make exceptions whenever the consequences look bad, because then the whole practice of truth-telling collapses. Mark said that's insane, no reasonable moral system demands you hand someone over to an abuser. Jen said Kant's point isn't that you have to hand her over — you could refuse to answer, or slam the door, or call the police — the point is you can't *lie*. Mark said refusing to answer is functionally the same as telling the truth in that situation; the dad would know exactly what it means, so the distinction is pointless. Jen said that's the dad's inference, not Sarah's lie, and the distinction between what you say and what someone else concludes from your silence matters. Mark came back with: if the distinction matters that much, then Sarah could've just said something literally true but misleading — like "I havent seen her around the neighborhood" — and Jen would have to call that permissible, which is absurd because it's obviously just lying with extra steps. Jen said no, Kant explicitly rejects that too, because the maxim of deliberate deception still fails the universalization test. At that point they were going in circles and I couldnt tell who was landing better punches. Can you go through their exchange and say who's right about what — not just who wins overall, but which specific claims hold up and which don't?
