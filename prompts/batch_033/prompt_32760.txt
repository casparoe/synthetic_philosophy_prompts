I have been going back and forth on the conciliationism/steadfastness debate for months now, and I am tired of not having a settled view. I want to force myself to put a number on it, and I want you to help me build the decomposition.

Here is the proposition I want a credence for: *In cases of genuine peer disagreement — where two people share the same evidence, are roughly equally competent with respect to the domain in question, and have no independent reason to think the other is impaired or biased on this occasion — the rational response is to significantly revise one's belief in the direction of the peer's, rather than to remain substantially unmoved.* Call this Conciliationism (I am lumping equal-weight views and moderate conciliationism together, and setting aside the question of exactly how much revision is required). Steadfastness is the denial: you can permissibly stick roughly where you are, because you have access to your own first-order reasoning in a way you lack for theirs.

I want you to give me your own credence in Conciliationism, but more importantly I want the decomposition. What are the sub-questions that this credence rides on? For each, give me a rough probability and a one-sentence justification. Then tell me how they combine — are they multiplicative, do they stack in some other way, or is the structure more like a weighted average over competing pictures? Finally, tell me which single term your final number is most sensitive to: if you could learn the answer to one of the sub-questions with certainty, which would shift your credence the most, and why?

Some sub-questions you might consider, though I do not want you to feel constrained by this list: whether the Uniqueness Thesis is true (that a given body of evidence rationalizes at most one doxastic attitude); whether the notion of an epistemic peer is even coherent enough to do the work the debate assigns it; whether higher-order evidence about the unreliability of one's own reasoning necessarily defeats or screens off one's first-order evidence; whether there is a principled asymmetry between first-person and third-person access to reasoning that a steadfast view can legitimately appeal to; and whether the bootstrapping problem — roughly, that you cannot demote someone from peer status just because they disagree with you, since that would beg the question — is fatal to steadfastness.

I am not asking for a literature review. I am asking for your actual credence, laid out so that I can see exactly where the weight is. If you think the question is ill-posed in some way that prevents a probability, tell me what the nearest well-posed proposition is and give me a credence for that instead.
