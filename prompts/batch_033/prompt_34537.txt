I recently lost my father, and in the weeks afterward I found myself drawn almost compulsively to the same melancholy music he used to play — songs that reliably made me cry, and that I kept returning to not in spite of the tears but in some sense because of them. The experience was, in a way I struggle to articulate, satisfying. It wasn't masochism, and it wasn't that I was numbing myself; I genuinely wanted to feel that sadness, and I came away from it each time feeling something I can only call better. This led me into a rabbit hole of reading about the paradox of tragedy: the old puzzle about why we seek out, and apparently enjoy, representational art that elicits emotions — grief, horror, pity — that we ordinarily go to great lengths to avoid. The standard answers — Aristotelian catharsis, the aesthetic distance that makes fictional emotions safe, the meta-response of admiring the artist's skill, the conversion of painful affect into something pleasurable by the formal properties of the work — all struck me as capturing something real but not quite resolving the knot. I found myself wondering whether the "paradox" is even a genuine puzzle or whether it dissolves once we stop treating enjoyment as a single, unified kind of mental state.

Give me your credence that the paradox of tragedy is a genuine paradox — that is, that there is a real, nontrivial explanatory puzzle here rather than a confusion generated by sloppy concepts of "enjoyment," "pleasure," or "negative emotion." Decompose the number. I want sub-questions like: what is the probability that the emotions we experience when consuming sad art are genuinely negative in valence (as opposed to being phenomenologically similar to sadness but positively valenced in the aesthetic context)? What is the probability that resolving the paradox requires rejecting the simple hedonistic premise that we seek pleasure and avoid pain, as opposed to keeping that premise and explaining away the apparent counterexample?
