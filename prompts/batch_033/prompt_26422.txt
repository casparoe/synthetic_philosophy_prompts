I'm editing a pop-science book and there's a sidebar explaining Karl Popper's idea of what makes something scientific. The author's draft says:

"Popper solved the demarcation problem by arguing that for a theory to be scientific, it must be falsifiable — there has to be some possible observation that would prove it wrong. This is what seperates science from pseudoscience and metaphysics. For example, Freudian psychoanalysis isn't scientific because its proponents can explain away any counterevidence, while Einstein's general relativity was scientific because it made risky predictions like the bending of starlight. Popper also argued that science progresses through a cycle of conjecture and refutation: scientists propose bold theories, and when a theory is falsified by experiment, it's discarded and replaced."

This sounds mostly right to me based on what I half-remember from college, but I've also heard that Popper gets mangled a lot in popular accounts. Can you walk through this summary and separate what's accurate from what's oversimplified in a way that distorts the view, and flag anything a Popper scholar would just flatly reject?
