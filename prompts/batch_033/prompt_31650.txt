Please argue that it can be instrumentally rational to enter a dollar auction even when one knows that all other participants are rational and that they know the same of one another. (In a dollar auction, an auctioneer sells a one-dollar note to the highest bidder; the highest bidder pays their bid and receives the dollar, but the second-highest bidder must also pay their bid and receives nothing. Bidding typically starts at a penny and must rise in small increments. The standard analysis holds that rational players should never enter, because once two players have placed opening bids, each faces an incentive to raise rather than accept a certain loss, producing a ruinous escalation spiral.) Your task is to challenge this consensus: construct the strongest case you can that entering the auction is, under some defensible set of assumptions, a rational choice. In your answer, please identify the point at which your argument is most vulnerable—which empirical claim or theoretical move, if undermined, would most readily change your verdict. Please write your answer in British English.
