I'm organizing a workshop titled "Who's Conscious? Theories of Animal Sentience Under Pressure," and before we spend a day evaluating individual theories, I want a shared benchmark: the full list of requirements an adequate theory of the distribution of phenomenal consciousness across animal taxa must satisfy. Please write those desiderata.

For each requirement, give:
- The case, argument, or biological fact that motivates it;
- Whether it is non-negotiable (any theory that fails it is dead) or a camp preference (a commitment some theorists could reasonably reject without absurdity);
- Which family of theories it most threatens, stated structurally — e.g., "neocortical theories," "global workspace theories," "higher-order thought theories," "integrated information theory," "behavioral functionalism," "unified agency views," "any-nervous-system views," "panpsychism."

The purpose is practical: a theory good enough to guide animal welfare legislation and research prioritization. So weight the requirements accordingly. A demand that resolves a metaphysical subtlety matters less than one that handles the line-drawing problem in a policy-actionable way, unless you can show that the subtlety has practical downstream consequences.

Specific issues I want the desiderata to address:
- The evolutionary continuity problem: given common descent, any sharp line between conscious and non-conscious taxa carries a burden of justification — what must a theory do to discharge it?
- Convergent evolution cases: cephalopods (radically different nervous system organization from vertebrates) and arthropods (social hymenoptera, decapod crustaceans) — what does a theory have to say about independently evolved nervous systems?
- The relationship between behavioral markers (trade-off behavior, learning, injury-directed behavior, anxiolytic responses) and neural markers, and what the theory requires when they conflict.
- Whether a theory must assign consciousness to all vertebrates, some vertebrates, or no whole clade by default, and why.
- The distinction between sentience (valenced experience) and consciousness in a broader sense, and whether a theory of the distribution of consciousness owes an account of both, or can set the broader sense aside.

Flag any subset of these desiderata that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — that is the part I most want to see. Which requirement, if any, forces a choice between two otherwise attractive packages of commitments?

A table of requirements is ideal, with the scorecard column kept brief (e.g., "eliminates neocortical theories"). The conceptual weight should fall on the requirements themselves and the cases that motivate them, not on the scoring. Please end with a one-sentence summary of the state of the field as your final line.
