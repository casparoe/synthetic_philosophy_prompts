I am preparing a presentation for a philosophy of biology workshop, and I would like you to build me a Q&A prep sheet rather than a defense of my view. My thesis is that the familiar claim that DNA is a code is neither straightforwardly literal nor merely a metaphor, and that both the enthusiasts and the debunkers have overshot. I have gone back and forth on this in the way one does with disputes that look partly verbal but keep producing different downstream commitments, and I have settled on a middle position with three claims. First, the codon table really is a code in a strict and defensible sense: a systematic, largely arbitrary mapping between two alphabets, nucleotide triplets and amino acids. Second, a code in that sense does not by itself carry semantic information, so the existence of the genetic code does not license saying that DNA contains information about an organism's traits. Third, the only literal work coding vocabulary can do stops at the specification of a polypeptide chain; phrases like coding for a limb or for a behavior are metaphors doing causal-explanatory work under false pretenses. My audience is a philosophy of biology reading group whose regular members include two philosophers who defend a teleosemantic account of genetic information, a molecular biologist who thinks philosophers are making a fuss about a harmless and useful shorthand, a historian of molecular biology who studies how the information metaphor entered the field, and several graduate students without settled positions. The group cares mostly about whether information talk in biology can be made precise or should be eliminated, and about whether scientific metaphors can be shown to earn their keep.

I expect the teleosemanticists to be my main opponents, but I am also worried about a line of questioning I cannot yet answer cleanly: whether my distinction between specifying a protein's primary structure and coding for a trait is stable, given how much of the explanatory weight is carried by protein function. Please order the objections by a rough product of how likely they are and how much damage they would do, and phrase each one the way that particular audience member would actually put it, not as a generic undergraduate version. For each objection, give me a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, followed by an honest note on where that reply is soft. The objections should target my actual argumentative commitments, especially the move from the codon table being a code to its coding for nothing beyond a polypeptide, and the claim that semantic information requires something more than a systematic mapping. Finish with the question I should be hoping nobody asks, and say why it is the dangerous one. Do not suggest that I add citations or defer to the literature; I need substantive replies, not bibliography.
