I'm a high school student writing a paper for my government class on paternalism and nudging — we've been discussing cafeteria layouts, organ-donation defaults, and whether schools should automatically enroll students in certain programs unless they opt out. While trying to construct the strongest possible objection to nudging, I built an argument that I now find suspicious. It seems to prove that any presentation of options whatsoever is either manipulative or paternalistic, which would mean that a teacher listing assignment topics or a waiter reciting the specials is doing something objectionable. I don't believe that, but I can't locate the mistake. My teacher suggested I try to diagnose the argument step by step rather than just rejecting the conclusion.

Here is the argument:

(1) Whenever you present someone with a set of options, you must choose a particular way to frame them: an order, a default, a description, a level of detail, a visual layout. There is no such thing as a completely unframed presentation of options. Even alphabetical order is a frame.

(2) The framing you choose will, as a matter of psychological fact, influence which option the person selects. This holds even when you try to be neutral: the first item listed tends to be chosen more often, a "neutral" description still carries connotations, and so on.

(3) If you know that your framing will influence the person's choice, and you select that framing deliberately rather than by a randomizing procedure, then you bear responsibility for the influence your framing exerts. You cannot claim you were merely presenting options; you chose a presentation that you knew would push the person in a certain direction.

(4) If you are responsible for an influence on someone's choice, and that influence operates through psychological mechanisms rather than through reasons the person explicitly endorses, then you are engaging in a form of manipulation — you are bypassing their rational capacities, however mildly.

(5) If the influence is not manipulative in this sense — because you are giving the person explicit reasons — then the only remaining way to steer them is toward what you take to be in their interest. And that is paternalism.

(6) Therefore, every deliberate presentation of options is either manipulative or paternalistic.

(7) If (6) is true, then the concept of "respecting autonomy" in how we present choices is empty. There is no neutral way to present options; every presentation either manipulates or patronizes.

(8) But this conclusion is absurd. A doctor listing treatment options, a waiter reciting the specials, a teacher distributing a handout of paper topics — these are not acts of manipulation or paternalism. We do, in ordinary life, manage to present options without violating anyone's autonomy.

(9) So the argument must go wrong somewhere. But where?

I find myself wanting to reject (4) — maybe influence through framing is not manipulation if the influence is weak enough, or if the person could easily resist it. But then I worry: if I set the threshold for "manipulation" high enough to let a waiter off the hook, I might also let automatic enrollment in retirement plans off the hook, and I will have lost the objection to nudging that I originally wanted to make. If I reject (3), I seem to be saying that knowingly causing an outcome is not the same as intending it, which pulls me into the doing/allowing distinction and feels like a large commitment for a paper on cafeteria layouts. If I reject (1), I need to explain what a "neutral frame" would even look like — and I cannot think of one.

Which premise should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If there is a standard move in the literature that resolves this, I would like to know what it is and whether it brings problems of its own.
