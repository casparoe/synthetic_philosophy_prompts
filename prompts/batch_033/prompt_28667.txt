I've been trying to figure out what I actually think about hard choices — not just decisions that are difficult because the stakes are high or I don't have enough info, but the kind of hard that philosophers go on about, where the values themselves seem to resist comparison. I went through a bunch of cases and wrote down my gut reactions, then tried to extract a principle. It's not going great. Here are the cases:

1. Choosing between a career as a public defender and a career as a philosophy professor. Both feel deeply meaningful to me in totally different ways — one is about justice and helping specific people in awful situations, the other is about understanding and teaching. I don't think one is better than the other, and I don't even think they're equally good. The choice feels hard in a way that isn't about missing information. Verdict: genuinely hard, the incommensurability kind.

2. A rescue: I can save either my friend or two strangers, but not both. I feel terrible about it, but I think saving the two strangers is clearly the right thing to do. The choice is emotionally brutal but not philosophically hard — the values, awful as they are to weigh, do seem to trade off on a common scale. Verdict: not hard in the relevant sense.

3. Two identical cans of soup on the shelf. I have to grab one. Not hard at all, just arbitrary. Verdict: not hard.

4. Apartment A is nicer (more light, better layout, bigger kitchen) but a 35-minute commute. Apartment B is fine but nothing special, and an 8-minute commute. These trade off on dimensions I can roughly compare — I can ask myself how much the nicer apartment is worth in commute minutes, even if the answer isn't obvious. The choice is hard, but it feels like a different kind of hard from case 1. It's a close call on what's basically a single scale of overall desirability. Verdict: hard, but not because of incommensurability — more like a tough optimization problem where the inputs are fuzzy.

5. Whether to try to have a kid this year or wait two more years. The considerations are all over the place: career timing, relationship readiness, my parents' age and health, my own energy levels, what kind of parent I'd be at 32 vs. 34, finances, and then just a brute feeling about when it's "time." I can't get these onto one scale, and I don't think gathering more information would resolve it. Verdict: genuinely hard.

6. Donating $5,000 to the Against Malaria Foundation vs. GiveDirectly. I've looked at the cost-effectiveness estimates and they're close enough that I can't confidently say one does more good per dollar. But this feels like a near-tie on a single scale (expected good done), not like incommensurability. Verdict: not hard in the interesting sense — just a close call under uncertainty.

7. I promised my partner I'd move with them to Chicago for their new job, but then I get an unexpected offer in Seattle that would be amazing for my career. The values feel like they come from different normative sources — promissory obligation on one side, personal flourishing on the other — and I don't know how to weigh one source against the other. Verdict: genuinely hard.

So my tentative principle is: a choice is genuinely hard (in the philosophical sense) when the considerations on each side aren't measurable on a common scale of value, so it's not just that you don't know which is better but that "better than" doesn't even get a clean grip. But case 4 seems like a counterexample — it's hard and yet the considerations are commensurable. And case 6 is commensurable but not hard. So my principle is missing something, or I'm wrong about one of the verdicts.

What principle actually captures these? Do I need to distinguish two kinds of hardness and my principle only covers one? And which of my verdicts would the best principle force me to abandon? Also, what does the right principle say about a case I didn't include: choosing between two relationships that are good in radically different ways, where you can only pursue one?
