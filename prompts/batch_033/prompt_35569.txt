Aumann's agreement theorem shows that Bayesian agents with common priors cannot agree to disagree: if their posterior probabilities for a given event are common knowledge, they must be equal. The result has been cited for decades as a formal argument that persistent disagreement among rational agents requires either private evidence or divergent priors. Yet the debate about what the theorem actually tells us about real-world disagreement seems to have stalled. One side treats the theorem as a regulative ideal: the fact that we systematically fail to converge is a sign that someone is falling short of full rationality, or that we are failing to make our evidence and reasoning common knowledge, and the theorem gives us a reason to keep talking until we either converge or locate the hidden divergence. The other side insists that the theorem's conditions are so demanding — common knowledge of one another's posteriors, common knowledge of rationality, and common priors, none of which obtain among actual human beings — that the result is a formal curiosity carrying no normative force for epistemic practice. I have a hypothesis about what is going on: the two sides are answering different questions, one about the structure of ideal rationality and the other about the norms that should govern bounded agents, and they are talking past each other under a single heading. Diagnose the debate: is it a substantive disagreement about the same question, a verbal disagreement, or two groups answering different questions that only appear to be the same one? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a formal extension that relaxes some of the conditions, a conceptual distinction that clarifies what each side is asking, or an argument that the theorem was never well-suited to the question people tried to make it answer?
