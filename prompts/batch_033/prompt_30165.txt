Can you help me find the bad step in an argument my reading group got tangled up in while reading On Certainty? (1) I know ordinary things, like that I have hands. (2) If I know that I have hands, then my belief must rest on grounds that justify it. (3) Any grounds I could give for that belief eventually bottom out in hinge propositions: that there is an external world, that my senses are mostly reliable, that the world didn't come into existence five minutes ago with fake traces of a past. (4) Hinge propositions can't be supported by evidence, since any evidence I might offer already presupposes them. (5) So each hinge is either a belief held without grounds or not a belief at all. (6) If it's a groundless belief, it can't transmit justification to my ordinary beliefs; if it's not a belief, it's unclear how it can be part of my grounds either. (7) So my belief that I have hands is unjustified, and (1) is false. That's absurd, but I can't locate the slip. I'd rather keep (1) and ditch some other step, but I want to know what I'd be paying. Where does the argument go wrong, and what does rejecting that step cost?
