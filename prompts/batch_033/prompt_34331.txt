So I was reading this longform piece about evolutionary psychology and the author takes a detour into philosophy. They summarize the "evolutionary debunking argument" against moral realism like this, and I'm trying to figure out how much to trust it:

"Here's the idea. Our moral intuitions — things like 'hurting people is wrong' or 'fairness matters' — are products of natural selection. Evolution didn't give us these intuitions because they're true; it gave them to us because they helped our ancestors cooperate and reproduce. If moral realism were correct, there'd have to be objective moral facts out there in the world, and it would be an insane coincidence that our Stone Age brains happened to evolve to detect them. The much simpler explanation is that moral facts don't exist — we just feel like they do because evolution programmed us with useful fictions. Most philosophers now accept this and it's basically killed off moral realism as a serious position."

The author seems really confident about this, and honestly it sounds pretty convincing to me. But I've learned to be suspicious when pop-science writers declare a philosophical debate "settled." What parts of this summary would a moral realist actually accept, what parts are oversimplified or wrong, and does the conclusion follow from the accurate bits?
