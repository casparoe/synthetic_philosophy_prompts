I spent most of yesterday evening arguing with a friend about whether the unrealistic assumptions in economic models actually matter. He kept taking the line that a theory's assumptions need not be true so long as its predictions are accurate — the position I have seen called the F-twist — and I kept objecting that this makes it impossible to tell a genuinely explanatory model apart from a lucky curve-fit. The argument ended without either of us moving, and I would like to sharpen my side of it.

Please play a committed defender of the F-twist. I want a strong version of the position, not a caricature: you should have real answers for why false assumptions can be harmless or even necessary, and you may concede local points, but you should not give up the core claim that a model is to be judged primarily by its predictive success rather than by the realism of its assumptions. Stay in character until I say "stop," and keep your replies to about a paragraph so the exchange stays conversational.

Here is my opening move. You say the truth of a model's assumptions is beside the point, and only its predictions count. But then how do you distinguish a model whose false assumptions are harmless idealizations from a model whose assumptions are simply wrong and happen to fit the data by luck? For a long time the Ptolemaic and Copernican systems made predictions of comparable accuracy, yet few people would now regard them as equally good theories. Doesn't our preference for Copernicus show that we care whether the mechanisms a model postulates are right, and not just whether its outputs match?
