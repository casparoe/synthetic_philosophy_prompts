I cover health and biotech for a living, and I keep tripping over a contrast in US law that I can't quite get to the bottom of. You can walk into a plasma center, get hooked to a machine for an hour, and leave with fourty bucks on a prepaid card; the centers run new-donor specials like it's a gym membership. Egg donors can get paid several thousand dollars for a much more invasive process with real medical risk. But offer to sell one of your two healthy kidneys to a stranger who'll die without a transplant, and that's a federal crime — for you and for the surgeon too. So the same legal system treats payment for some body stuff as a normal market and payment for other body stuff as repugnant.

I can see the surface differences: plasma regenerates, a kidney doesn't; egg retrieval is less permanent than nephrectomy; kidney sales could exploit poor donors. But those don't line up neatly with what's legal. The risk of kidney surgery is identical whether the donor is paid or doing it out of love, so the risk alone can't be the whole story, and if the worry is exploitation of the poor, it's not obvious why paying egg donors several thousand dollars escapes that worry while paying a kidney donor does not.

What I want is a reconstruction of the implicit theory of commodification — or of why some markets are repugnant — that this pattern of law actually presupposes. What view of the body, of consent, of exploitation, of what can properly be bought, would make paid plasma and paid eggs fine but paid kidneys beyond the pale? Is that view coherent, or are these policies resting on different and maybe incompatible rationales? And what would have to change — in the kidney ban, in egg and plasma compensation, or both — if you swapped in the other side's rationale? If the same theory is also supposed to cover price gouging bans, where we make it illegal to charge the market price for something people urgently need, say whether it can, or whether that's a different kind of repugnance in a trench coat.
