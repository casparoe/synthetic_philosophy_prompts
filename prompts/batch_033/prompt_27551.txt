Here's the passage (paraphrased, since I don't have the book to hand):

Keynes says that in assessing an argument from evidence E to conclusion H, we normally focus on the probability — the degree of rational belief that E confers on H. This probability depends on a balance between the favourable and unfavourable evidence. But there is another dimension, he says, which is the "weight" of the argument. The weight turns on the total amount of relevant evidence, not the balance. Two arguments can have the same probability — the same balance of favourable to unfavourable evidence — and yet one has much greater weight because it rests on a larger body of evidence. He then says that in deciding how to act, the weight of the argument is just as important as the probability: a high probability based on very little evidence is a poor guide to action compared to the same probability based on a lot of evidence.

What I'm struggling with is this: If probability is a logical relation between propositions (as Keynes insists), then what does it mean to say the probability is the same in both cases but the weight differs? Isn't the probability supposed to be determined by the total evidence? If the total evidence is different, how can the probability be the same? Is he saying that in the low-weight case, we're simply ignoring some of the evidence when we state the probability? Or is weight meant to capture something about the reliability or stability of the probability judgment itself? And how does this connect to the distinction he later draws in the General Theory between situations where probabilities can be calculated and situations of genuine uncertainty where they can't — if at all? I keep going in circles on this and would really appreciate someone unpacking it step by step, especially the bit about whether weight is a seperate dimension of the evidential relation or something more like a second-order confidence judgement.
