I am a novelist. My current protagonist is a county judge in a small, religiously divided community, and a large part of the book turns on how she reasons about hard cases: a promise made to a dead friend, a lie that would spare a family, a sentence she is legally free to soften. I want her moral reasoning to be a genuine and stable intellectual position, not a mood that changes from scene to scene, and I want the reader to be able to reconstruct it from what she does and says.

Please build me a sorting instrument, a "which moral epistemology does this character actually hold?" quiz, that I can use to diagnose her and, later, to test whether any scene I draft is consistent with her.

Specifications:

- Eight to ten questions. Each question presents a concrete case from ordinary or professional life: a promise, a lie, a medical or judicial decision, a case involving a novel or unfamiliar consideration. Each question has two or three answer options. The options must be things the character would say, do, or feel, never statements of doctrine and never invitations to pick a label. The instrument must sort by how the character responds to cases, not by which school she admires.

- The scoring key should map patterns of answers onto at least these positions: (1) a generalism of pro tanto principles, of roughly Rossian shape; (2) a particularism of roughly Dancy's shape, on which what is a reason in one case may be no reason or an opposite reason in another and on which moral judgment is not codifiable; (3) a strict-rule generalism on which at least some moral rules hold without exception. Add any further positions that fall naturally out of the logical space you build, provided you characterize each one defensibly and give it a name or an explicit "unnamed" marking.

- Build from the distinctions first, not from the list of names. Lay out the axes you take to separate the positions, for example whether reasons must be general, whether there are codifiable principles, whether principles carry pro tanto weight or absolute force, whether a feature's moral valence can shift with context. Then locate each named position in the resulting space, and tell me which cells are empty. For each empty cell, say briefly whether it is empty because the combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored, to your knowledge.

- Include at least three cases on which a Rossian generalist and a particularist give different verdicts, not merely different post hoc rationalizations. These are the cases I most need, since only they will actually separate those two positions in a character's visible behavior. For each such case, tell me which feature of the case is doing the separating work.

- Flag explicitly any pair of positions the quiz cannot separate, and say what would need to be added, if anything would, to separate them.

- For each leaf, give me one short paragraph I can use while drafting: what this character is likely to notice first in a morally difficult scene, what she is likely to feel guilty about afterward, and what sort of thing she would say when asked to justify herself.

The point of the instrument is to keep a fictional character honest. A case that merely stipulates the verdict, or a question that rewards the character for mouthing the right slogan, is useless to me; the questions must generate the diagnosis from concrete responses.
