I've been working through the literature on how cooperation can arise in one-shot interactions where standard game theory would predict mutual defection, and I keep circling back to the idea of program equilibrium. The basic insight, as I understand it, is that if players in a game like the Prisoner's Dilemma submit programs rather than simply choosing actions—and if those programs can condition their behavior on the other player's program—then cooperative outcomes become attainable even without repetition, reputation, or external enforcement. But as I've dug deeper, it seems to me that there are really two quite different ways of fleshing out this core insight, and the literature doesn't always draw the distinction as sharply as I'd like.

The first approach, which I'll call the simulation-based or source-code reading approach, lets each program directly inspect the other's source code and simulate what the other program would do given its own code as input. This yields cooperative equilibria through essentially circular reasoning: I cooperate because I see that you would cooperate if and only if I cooperate, and vice versa. The second approach, which I'll call the proof-based or Löbian approach, instead has programs reason about each other through a formal system—they search for proofs about the other's behavior, using something like the modal logic of provability, and the possibility of cooperation turns on the quirky properties of self-referential reasoning captured by Löb's theorem. Both approaches promise to solve the same underlying problem, but they seem to rest on quite different pictures of what it means for one program to "model" another, and they make different demands on the logical omniscience or computational power of the agents involved.

And which approach, in your view, offers a more philosophically satisfying account of how rational cooperation is possible without the usual crutches of repetition or external enforcement?
