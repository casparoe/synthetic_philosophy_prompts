You are a professional philosopher of mind who works on pain, affective experience, and the theory of well-being, and who has followed the clinical and philosophical literature on pain asymbolia closely. I host a specialist philosophy podcast, and my next guest defends a position you know well: the badness of a pain is entirely a matter of a distinct affective–motivational component, felt unpleasantness, rather than of the sensory–discriminative component that encodes location and intensity; pain asymbolia is the central piece of evidence for this dissociation, because asymbolic patients seem to retain the sensory side of pain while losing the unpleasantness that, on this view, makes pain bad.

Draft eight to ten interview questions that go well beyond asking the guest to restate the view. Before writing the questions, think through the dialectic carefully: identify the two or three strongest objections to this position, the replies the guest is most likely to give, and the exact points where those replies are most vulnerable. Use that sketch to build the question sequence.

The questions must cover at least the following ground, and each must include a follow-up keyed to the answer the guest is most likely to give:

- The objection that what asymbolic patients retain is not really pain at all but a bare sensation of heat, pressure, or sharpness, so that asymbolia may remove pain rather than dissociate two components of it.
- The epistemic worry that the evidence comes from rare patients with brain lesions whose verbal reports, to the effect that the pain is still present but no longer bothers them, may be confabulated or attenuated; what would settle that an un-unpleasant state is genuinely pain?
- The question whether the sensory component alone could still be bad for the subject, for instance because it registers tissue damage, even when it is not felt as unpleasant.
- The distinction between unpleasantness and suffering, and whether locating badness in unpleasantness is enough if these come apart.
- The practical and normative upshot: whether the view implies that asymbolic pain, or pain whose unpleasantness has been reduced pharmacologically or by attention training, gives others less reason to relieve it.

For at least three of the questions, write the follow-up so that it specifically targets the guest's most likely reply: if he says the natural thing in response to the first-order question, the follow-up should be the point that makes that reply hardest to sustain.

Shape the sequence into a cumulative arc. Early questions should force the guest to earn the claim that asymbolia is a dissociation within pain rather than the absence of pain; middle questions should probe what the badness of pain could be and whether it could outrun felt unpleasantness; final questions should push the clinical and moral consequences. End with a question about what evidence, if any, would lead him to abandon the view.
