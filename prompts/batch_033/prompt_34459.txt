I've been going down a rabbit hole on this and I can't get it to settle in my head, so I want to see what principle actually fits my gut reactions.

A few months ago I read about pain asymbolia — it's this condition (usually from a brain lesion in the insula or something) where people still feel pain, they can tell you where it is and how intense it is, but they genuinely don't mind it. They don't pull away, they don't wince, they might even laugh while describing the sensation as sharp or burning. And somehow that broke my whole understanding of what pain is.

So here are my verdicts on a bunch of cases, and I want to know if there's a single principle about what makes pain bad (or not) that captures all of them. And if there isn't one, which verdicts do I have to give up?

Case 1: Ordinary pain. I stub my toe getting out of bed. It hurts. It's bad. No further analysis needed.

Case 2: Pain asymbolia. The patient feels the same sharp toe-stubbing sensation, describes it accurately, but shrugs and keeps walking. This feels to me like it's not bad — or at least, not bad in the way my pain is bad. It's more like a weird signal that happens to be on.

Case 3: The runner's high. I'm on mile four of a run I chose to go on, my legs are burning, and I'm kind of into it. The pain is there but it's part of something I value. I wouldn't say it's not bad at all, but it's not bad in the same register as the stubbed toe. It's bad-in-a-way-that's-part-of-the-good, if that makes sense.

Case 4: The masochist. Someone who experiences pain during sex and finds the whole thing pleasurable. My instinct is that the pain here isn't bad for them — not just that it's outweighed by pleasure, but that the pain itself takes on a different character when it's wanted in that way.

Case 5: Life-saving pain. You touch a hot stove, the pain makes you jerk your hand back before you get a serious burn. The pain itself is bad, but the capacity to feel it is good — you'd be worse off without it. This one's easy: the episode of pain is bad, the disposition is good. I don't think these two verdicts conflict.

Case 6: Morphine patient. Someone in hospice on morphine who still feels the pain but reports "it doesn't bother me anymore." Morphine is known to separate the sensory from the affective dimension of pain. Seems exactly like pain asymbolia but pharmacologically induced. I have the same verdict: not bad, or at least the badness is mostly gone.

Case 7: The hurt that doesn't warn. Someone has chronic neuropathic pain that serves no protective function — it's just there, for years, with no information about tissue damage. I feel like this is worse than ordinary pain, not better. It's bad in a pure, almost metaphysical way. If pain's badness is supposed to be tied to its function as a warning signal, this case should be less bad, but it's the opposite.

Case 8: The pill. Imagine a pill that doesn't change the sensation of pain at all but makes you completely indifferent to it — the sensory component is identical, the affective component is gone. I'd take it. I think the pain under this pill isn't bad. But this means the badness of my stubbed toe (Case 1) isn't in the sensation itself, it's in my reaction to the sensation. And that feels weird to say, because when I stub my toe it sure feels like the badness is right there in the toe.

Case 8 is the one that's really tripping me up. It pushes me toward saying the badness of pain is in the "ouch" reaction, not the sensation — but then Case 7 pulls me the other way, because with chronic pain there's no useful reaction to have, and yet it feels like the worst kind of bad.

So: what's the principle here? Is there a unified account of what makes pain bad that handles all of these? If I have to drop one or two verdicts to get a clean theory, which ones should I drop and why? And what does the principle say about a case I haven't thought of — maybe something involving non-human animals, or a newborn, or someone who's been in pain their whole life and can't imagine otherwise?
