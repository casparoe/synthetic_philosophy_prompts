I've been going back and forth on anthropic reasoning for a few months now — read Bostrom's book, some of the back-and-forth with the SSA camp, a bunch of forum posts — and I keep finding that when I try to explain to friends why I think the Self-Indication Assumption can't be right, I get tangled up when they push back. So I want to practice.

Play a committed defender of SIA. Not a strawman — you actually believe this stuff, you've thought about the objections, you have replies. You're willing to concede small points (e.g., that the presumptuous philosopher conclusion *feels* weird, or that there are edge cases where SIA gives uncomfortable results) but you don't concede the core claim: that finding yourself alive is evidence that the world contains many observers, and this is the right way to reason. Keep replies to a paragraph or so. Stay in character until I say stop.

Here's my opening: The presumptuous philosopher thought experiment seems like a reductio to me. You've got two theories, T1 and T2, that are identical on all the ordinary empirical evidence, except T2 says there are a trillion times more observers. SIA tells you to be astronomically more confident in T2, purely on the basis of your own existence. But that's insane — your not learning anything new about the world when you notice you exist; you already knew that. It's like waking up, noticing you're awake, and concluding that therefore the universe must be vast. How is this not just a straight-up refutation of SIA?
