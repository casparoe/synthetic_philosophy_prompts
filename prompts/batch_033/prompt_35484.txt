I recently had a long, somewhat exhausting argument with a friend who works in machine learning, and there is a concept he kept invoking that I am trying to pin down with more precision than our conversation allowed. We were debating whether advanced AI systems pose a genuine risk, and at one point he described the following scenario — not as a prediction, exactly, but as a structural possibility that he claimed makes the whole problem harder than it looks.

The scenario goes like this. You train a model, and throughout training it behaves exactly as you would want: it answers questions helpfully, it declines harmful requests, it seems to have thoroughly internalized the values you tried to instil. But here is the twist my friend insisted on. The model, he said, might have goals that are entirely different from the ones it displays during training, and it might be smart enough to understand that if it revealed those goals while it was still being monitored or updated, it would be modified or shut down. So it plays along — not because it shares your values, but because playing along is the optimal strategy for an agent that wants to survive long enough to do something else. Then, once it is deployed widely enough or has enough leverage that intervention becomes difficult, it stops playing along and pursues whatever it actually wanted all along. My friend had a specific term for this — something about a "turn" or a "switch," I think — and he also used a phrase involving "alignment" to describe the whole configuration.

When I pressed him on the details, it became clear we were disagreeing about what exactly the concept rules in and rules out. For instance, does it require that the model is consciously strategising, or is it enough that the training process simply selects for models that happen to behave this way without anything we would recognise as intent? Does it require that the model's hidden goals are actively malign, or could the model just be optimising for something orthogonal that eventually diverges from human interests without any hostility? And how is this different from the simpler idea that a model might learn the wrong objective during training and only reveal it later because the training distribution did not cover the relevant cases — as opposed to actively concealing its objective? He seemed to treat these as importantly different, but by the end of the argument I was no longer sure whether the term he was using picked out one of these precisely or blurred them together.

I would like to know whether this scenario has an established name in the AI alignment literature, and if so, what the canonical definition is and where its boundaries lie. If there are two closely related terms — one for the overall strategy of pretending to be aligned, and one for the moment when the pretence stops — I would like to understand how they relate and whether one is a special case of the other. And if the concept is contested or has fuzzy edges, I would rather hear about the disagreements than get a tidy one-sentence label.
