ok so i've been trying to get clear on what i actually think about blame and moral responsibility, going by gut reactions to cases rather than starting from a theory. strawson's "freedom and resentment" is in the background but i'm not wedded to his framework—i just want to know what principle, if any, captures my pattern of verdicts.

here are the cases:

1. a coworker takes credit for my idea in a meeting. i feel resentment, and it feels fitting—he knew what he was doing. later i find out he genuinely misremembered and thought the idea was his. my resentment evaporates completely, and i think it *should* evaporate. the blame was premised on something false about what happened.

2. same setup, but instead of misremembering, i learn he has a brain tumor that's been causing personality changes and erratic behavior. again my resentment goes away, but this time it's not because the blame was based on a false belief about what he did—it's because i now see him as not the kind of entity that resentment is *for*. i shift into something like concern or pity. and i think this shift was the correct response even before i knew about the tumor; i just didn't have the relevant information.

3. a friend consistently bails on plans. i'm annoyed, i hold it against her. then i learn she's been in a severe depressive episode. my resentment mostly dissolves but not entirely—there's a residue. i still think she should have sent a text, depression or not. the residue feels different from full blame but it's not nothing. is it still a reactive attitude? should it be there?

4. a four-year-old hits another kid at the playground. i feel a flash of indignation, but i don't think the kid is *blameworthy* in the way an adult would be. the feeling is real but it's not full-blooded moral resentment. i'm not even sure its the same kind of thing.

5. a company dumps toxic waste in a river near my hometown. i'm furious. but when i try to locate the target of my anger, it gets slippery—i'm not angry at any particular employee, most of whom had no idea. i'm angry at "the company." is this a reactive attitude in anything like strawson's sense? it feels moral, it feels like blame, but there's no agent on the other end who can register my blame or offer a response.

6. someone from a culture where direct eye contact is disrespectful avoids my eyes throughout a conversation. i feel slighted in the moment, then i learn the cultural context. the feeling of being slighted goes away and i think it *should* go away—but i also think i wasn't being irrational to feel it initially. can a reactive attitude be fitting-from-your-perspective but not fitting simpliciter?

7. a close friend betrays a secret, not maliciously but out of carelessness—they got drunk and it slipped. i'm hurt and i blame them. but here's the thing: i know myself well enough to know that if i'd been in exactly their situation—same level of exhaustion, same relationship to alcohol, same everything—i probably would have done the same thing. and yet i still blame them. this feels different from the brain tumor case, but i'm not sure i can articulate why. in both cases i'm acknowledging that their action was determined by factors outside their control. so what's the principle that lets my blame survive in case 7 but not in case 2?

so what i'm asking is: is there a coherent account of when reactive attitudes are fitting that gets most or all of these verdicts right? in particular i want to know what principle distinguishes the cases where new information dissolves blame (1, 2, and partly 3) from the case where it doesn't (7). and what does that principle say about case 5—is corporate anger a real reactive attitude or are we doing something else when we blame institutions?

i'd also like to know which of my verdicts the principle forces me to give up, if any. if the cleanest theory says case 3 and case 7 can't both come out the way i want, i'd rather know that than have a principle with six epicycles.
