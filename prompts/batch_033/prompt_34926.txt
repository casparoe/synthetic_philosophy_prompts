Write a dialogue between two metaphysicians, an endurantist and a perdurantist, who meet to work through their disagreement about how ordinary objects persist through time. The endurantist maintains that a persisting object is a three-dimensional thing wholly present at each moment at which it exists: change is one and the same object's having different properties at different times. The perdurantist maintains that a persisting object is a four-dimensional entity extended through time as well as space and composed of temporal parts: change is a difference in properties between earlier and later temporal parts of the whole.

The dialogue should be a genuine exchange rather than two alternating monologues. Have the two work through at least the following pressure points: (1) the problem of temporary intrinsics—how a single thing can be bent on Monday and straight on Tuesday without contradiction; (2) the endurantist's worry that the perdurantist has not explained change but analyzed it away, since a bent temporal part and a straight temporal part are no more the same thing than two distinct objects are; (3) the perdurantist's counter-worry that the endurantist's standard fix, relativizing properties to times, turns seemingly intrinsic properties like shape into relations to times; and (4) how each side handles coinciding objects, such as a statue and the lump of clay that constitutes it, including the question of how many objects are in the chair when both are there. Neither character should be a strawman: each should at least once concede that the other's view handles some case more neatly than their own, and then explain why they nonetheless find their own view preferable overall. End the dialogue without resolving the dispute, but with a sharper sense of what each side would have to give up in order to converge.
