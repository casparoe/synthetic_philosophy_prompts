I've been reading some Nyāya epistemology for a class, and I keep seeing people online talk past each other about whether you can "know" something by asking a large language model, so I want to mash the two together instead of rehashing the usual Anglophone lense. Here's the theory side: in Nyāya, knowledge (pramā) is the output of a knowledge source (pramāṇa), and the standard Nyāya list is perception, inference, comparison/analogy, and testimony (śabda), with testimony counting only when the speaker is reliable (āpta) and actually knows what they're saying. Here's the case: the last couple years of people treating LLM outputs as a source of knowledge — asking them factual questions, getting summaries and explanations, even using them to check their own reasoning — together with the discovery that these systems confidently hallucinate, have no direct perceptual contact with the world, and are trained to predict likely next tokens rather than to say what's true. Analyze the case through the pramāṇas. I want a disciplined mapping: which pramāṇa, if any, is an LLM answer supposed to be? Does it fit the definition of testimony, and if not, what exactly disqualifies it — the lack of an āpta speaker, the absence of any underlying pramā in the system, or something else? Don't just gesture at "it's not a real source"; give me the precise point where the mapping fails.

Then run it the other way. What does this case do to the theory? Does Nyāya handle it cleanly by just saying "not a pramāṇa, therefore no knowledge," or does the case expose a gap — for instance, that the theory never quite says what makes a string of words a piece of testimony rather than a text-like object, or that it quietly assumes a minded, truth-aiming source behind the words? And be honest about where the mapping strains: an LLM can produce true answers reliably fairly often, so if reliability is doing the work in the āpta condition, why exactly doesn't that count? I want the analysis to run in both directions — theory illuminating the case, case testing the theory — including the places the theory is silent.
