I'm a clinical ethicist at a teaching hospital. Six months ago my father, who has advanced Alzheimer's, developed pneumonia. He had written an advance directive years earlier refusing antibiotics in precisely this scenario — he had specified that if he were no longer able to recognize his family, he would want all life-sustaining treatment withheld. But when we visited him, he seemed to take pleasure in music, in holding my mother's hand, in chocolate pudding, and when the nurse approached with the IV he pulled away and said "no." The attending physician decided to treat, overriding the directive, and my father recovered. I still do not know whether that was the right decision, and I have come to realize that our ethics committee has no consistent framework for these cases. We have been improvising.

I want you to write a decision procedure — a step-by-step protocol — that our committee can use when a patient's current behavior or expressed wishes appear to conflict with their advance directive. The committee includes physicians, nurses, social workers, and a community member; most have no formal training in philosophy. The procedure should specify: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a recommendation, and where it must hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts.

The procedure needs to engage with the philosophical issue at the core of these cases: whether the earlier self who wrote the directive has the authority to bind the later self, and under what conditions that authority holds or lapses. I do not want a summary of the philosophical literature. I want the philosophy translated into operational steps that make the relevant distinctions visible to people who have never heard of personal identity theory.

Then apply the procedure to these two cases:

(a) A 72-year-old woman with moderate dementia wrote an advance directive five years ago refusing all life-sustaining treatment if she were to develop "severe cognitive impairment." She now recognizes her daughter, enjoys gardening and looking through photo albums, and when she developed a treatable infection she cooperated with treatment and said she wanted to "stay here." Her daughter argues the directive should not apply because her mother is not severely impaired and is clearly experiencing well-being. The treating team is divided.

(b) A 58-year-old man with early-onset Alzheimer's wrote an advance directive three years ago stating that if he no longer recognized his family, he would want all life-sustaining treatment withheld, including artificial nutrition and hydration. He now no longer recognizes his wife or children, is largely uncommunicative, but appears calm, eats when fed, and shows no signs of distress. He has developed a condition requiring minor surgery without which he will decline over weeks. His wife wants the surgery; his brother, who holds the healthcare power of attorney, wants the directive honored.

For at least one of these cases, the procedure should yield an answer that someone on the committee — perhaps even me — will find uncomfortable. Flag that explicitly. And tell me what the procedure cannot do: the cases it cannot settle, the distinctions it cannot operationalize, and where the philosophy itself runs out.
