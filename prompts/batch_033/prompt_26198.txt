I would like your credence that Quine's indeterminacy of translation thesis, taken in its strong form, is correct. By the strong form I mean the claim that for any given language, there exist at least two translation manuals into one's home language that are empirically equivalent — they account for all possible behavioral evidence, including all dispositions to verbal behavior — yet are mutually incompatible in their assignments of reference and meaning, and that there is no fact of the matter about which manual is the right one.

Please decompose your credence into at least the following components, giving a rough probability for each and a one- or two-sentence justification:

1. The probability that linguistic meaning is exhaustively determined by behavioral dispositions — that is, that there are no private semantic facts, no mental representations with determinate content that outrun behavioral criteria.
2. The probability that, conditional on (1), the totality of possible behavioral evidence genuinely underdetermines the choice between translation manuals — that is, that the underdetermination is not merely a practical limitation but holds even given all possible observations.
3. The probability that, conditional on (1) and (2), the underdetermination is in-principle rather than merely epistemic — meaning that no further facts, not even facts about neurophysiology or cognitive architecture, could settle which manual is correct.

Show how these components combine to yield your overall credence, and identify which component your final number is most sensitive to — that is, which sub-question, if you changed your mind about it, would shift your overall credence the most. Finally, tell me what evidence or argument would most shift your credence on that pivotal sub-question.
