I am a retired physicist who has recently taken up moral philosophy. I have been reading the debate about why promises bind, and I am struck by how often the two main camps talk past one another. On one side, the conventionalist: promising is a social practice whose norms are justified by the good the practice does — roughly, it is a coordination device we have reason to sustain. On the other, the assurance theorist: the obligation arises from the moral power to create expectations in another person by communicating an intention to act, and the wrong of breaking a promise is the wrong of disappointing an expectation one has freely invited.

I would like you to draft the document that a committed conventionalist and a committed assurance theorist would produce after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. The document should include:

(1) A numbered list of agreed claims — points about the phenomenology of promising, about what a successful account must explain, and about the empirical facts of human social life that are relevant.

(2) A disagreements table. For each point of genuine residual disagreement, give the conventionalist's formulation of their own position, the assurance theorist's formulation of their own position, and a judgement about whether the disagreement is substantive or partly verbal.

(3) A section on cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. For each crux, give a concrete case or thought-experiment both sides agree bears on it, together with each side's prediction.

I am particularly interested in whether the disagreement boils down to a difference about the moral significance of freely created expectations, or whether it runs deeper — into the nature of obligation itself. Please be precise: neither side should feel caricatured.
