At lunch last week, two friends of mine got into a heated argument about a third friend, Marcus, who had gotten himself into a bidding war at our school's charity auction. The item was a signed jersey from a local minor-league team — probably worth about $40. Marcus started bidding against another student, and by the time the argument broke out, Marcus had bid $65 and the other student was at $60. The auction rules said both the winner and the second-highest bidder had to pay their bids (the second-highest bidder gets nothing). Marcus was considering whether to bid $70.

Here is how the argument went, as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) David said Marcus should absolutely drop out. He'd already bid more than the jersey was worth, and every additional dollar he put in was just making the loss bigger. He called it a textbook sunk-cost fallacy.

(2) Elena replied that David was missing the structure of the situation. If Marcus drops out now, he pays $65 and gets nothing. If he bids $70 and wins, he pays $70 and gets the jersey — which is worth about $40, so he's effectively down $30 rather than $65. She said that's not sunk-cost reasoning; it's comparing future outcomes.

(3) David said that was still comparing the wrong things. The $65 is gone either way. The question is just: is the jersey worth $70 to Marcus? And the answer is no, because it's worth $40. So bidding $70 for a $40 item is a bad deal, full stop.

(4) Elena said David was ignoring the fact that the other bidder is in the same situation. If Marcus drops out, the other bidder pays $60 for a $40 jersey — also a loss. The bidding war is irrational at the collective level, but given that the other bidder might also drop out if Marcus bids one more time, bidding $70 could be Marcus's best move. She said this is why these situations are tricky: once you're in them, the rational thing might be to keep going, even though the rational thing was never to enter.

(5) David said that was exactly the logic that gets people to ruin in these situations — each person keeps bidding to avoid being the one stuck with the loss, and the bids keep climbing. If both bidders follow Elena's reasoning, they'll bid the jersey up to infinity. He said the only way out is for someone to refuse to play that game.

(6) Elena admitted that both people following that logic leads to disaster, but said that doesn't settle what a single person should do. If the other bidder is going to keep going, then Marcus dropping out is just handing the other bidder a smaller loss at Marcus's expense. The rational move depends on what you think the other bidder will do, and if you think they'll keep bidding, you should drop out — but if you think they're about to fold, you should bid one more time.

They went back and forth a few more times but never resolved it. I've been turning it over in my head and I can't sort out who's right about which points. I'd be grateful for a careful adjudication: please go through each of the six moves above and say whether it's correct, mistaken, or partially right, and why. Then say who you think is more right overall, and what (if anything) both of them are missing. Finally, I'd like to know what argument or evidence would change your mind about the overall verdict — where your reasoning is most likely to fail, and what would cause you to switch sides.
