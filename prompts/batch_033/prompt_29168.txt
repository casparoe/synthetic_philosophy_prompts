I've been going down a Sapir-Whorf rabbit hole lately, mostly through blog posts and comment sections rather than the actual linguistics, so I'm aware my picture of the literature is probably a bit carcitured. One thing I keep noticing is people reaching for Arrival as if it basically settles the strong version of linguistic relativity, or at least dramatizes it so vividly that the weak version feels undeniable. I rewatched it last week and started to think the film is doing something stranger than the standard language-rewires-your-brain take: Louise doesn't just pick up new labels, she acquires a writing system that seems to reorganize her experience of time, and the movie seems to put most of the weight on the nonlinear, simultaneous structure of Heptapod B rather than on vocabulary or grammar in the ordinary sense.

I'm leading a small discussion group (and maybe later a short course unit) that uses Arrival as the entry point to linguistic relativity, and I want to do the film justice without letting everyone walk away with the strong Whorfian version. So: read Arrival against the actual Sapir-Whorf debate. Where does it track the weak version—language shaping habitual attention, categorization, memory—and where does it smuggle in the strong version, where learning a language gives you genuinely new cognitive abilities or restructures perception wholesale? Is there a better reading where Heptapod B is more like acquiring a new notation or formalism, something like the difference between doing arithmetic in Roman numerals versus Arabic numerals, rather than an instance of Whorf at all? And does the film accidentally illustrate one of the standard objections to strong Whorf—namely that translation and language-learning seem to require enough shared conceptual structure to undercut the claim that different languages trap you in incommensurable worlds?

I'd rather get a close, scene-level reading than a survey. What does Arrival get right that the philosophy-of-language and psycholinguistics debates tend to miss, what does it get wrong in ways that would actively mislead my group, and which two or three scenes should I point people to as the load-bearing ones? If you think the movie is actually doing something closer to a philosophical thought experiment about time, memory, or free will than a linguistics claim, I'd want you to say so and defend it.
