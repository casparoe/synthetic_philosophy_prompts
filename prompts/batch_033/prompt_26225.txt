I'm a philosophy professor preparing a second-year metaphysics unit on infinity. Next week I have one fifty-minute class on Thomson's lamp. The students have already worked through Zeno's dichotomy and Achilles, and they understand that an infinite series can sum to a finite value. They have not yet seen a supertask whose outcome is underdetermined by the description. Don't explain Thomson's lamp to me; plan how I should explain it to them. I need the dependency structure: what idea must be in place before what, and why the pieces cannot be introduced in a different order. I need the one example that carries the whole session — I assume the lamp itself, but tell me if something else should do the carrying work and what the carrier must have and lack. I need a map of the misconceptions students predictably form: which ones they arrive with (e.g., conflating mathematical description with physical possibility), which ones the exposition itself creates, and exactly where in the fifty-minute sequence each should be headed off. I need one question to pose at the end that checks whether they have grasped the conceptual problem — that the difficulty is not about engineering or about our ignorance of the lamp's final state but about the description failing to determine any state at all — rather than merely being able to retell the story. Finally, tell me what I should openly refuse to settle in this session and why leaving it unsettled is pedagogically better than resolving it. Keep the plan to no more than 800 words.
