Okay here's a thing I keep going back and forth on. Consciousness researchers do something weird. In a typical experiment the whole point is to figure out when someone is conscious of something, and the way they decide is basically to ask them: the subject says "yep, I saw it" or presses a button to say the stimulus was visible. If they say they didn't see it, it gets filed under unconscious processing. So first-person reports end up as the gold standard for whether an experience happened at all. But a lot of the same researchers will turn around and say introspection is basically hopeless as a method: when people explain *why* they did something, or report on what was going on in their head, those reports get treated as confabulation, post-hoc storytelling, not real evidence. There are choice-blindness and split-brain type findings where people give confident explanations that are pretty clearly made up, and everyone nods along. A few labs have even built "no-report" paradigms to get around relying on the subject's say-so, which kind of proves they know the report is doing a lot of work.

So my question is: what theory of introspection does this practice presuppose? It seems to need some kind of priviledged access to the presence or content of experience, but not to the causes of experience. Is that a coherent line to draw, or is it just a pragmatic double standard? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology as best you can, and say whether the practice is strictly committed to it or could run just as well under several different theories. And if we decided introspection was unreliable across the board—or reliable across the board—what would actually have to change in how these experiments are done? Also, tell me how confident you are in the reconstruction and which single point you're least sure of.
