I'm giving a talk at the SETI Institute's weekly seminar next month, and I'm nervous. I'm a science journalist, not a working researcher, and I'm going to argue that the grabby aliens model is the most underappreciated resolution of the Fermi paradox — that it deserves to be taken seriously as a predictive framework, not dismissed as cute anthropic reasoning. The audience will be about forty people: mostly radio astronomers who've spent their careers on SETI searches, plus a few exoplanet atmosphere modelers and an origin-of-life chemist or two. They're not hostile to the Drake equation, but they're deeply allergic to anything that puts humans in a special temporal position. They'll hear "we're early" and think I'm just doing the same thing people did before Copernicus, but with equations.

I know the basics of the model (the hard-steps framework, the expansion-front argument, the "try-try" parts of the Hanson et al. paper), but I'm not a physicist and I'm worried about technical objections I can't field. Can you prep me a Q&A sheet? I want the objections I'll actually get from this specific crowd — radio astronomers, exoplanet people, origin-of-life chemists — in roughly the order they'll come, phrased the way they'd actually say them. For each objection, give me a two-minute reply and an honest note on where that reply is weak. Include the question I'm praying nobody asks. And please: separate what's actually settled from what's genuinely contested among people who know this literature. I don't want to present a contested view as the consensus, but I also don't want to cave on points that are actually well-supported.
