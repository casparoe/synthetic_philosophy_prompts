A friend at a conference reception cornered me with this and I can't find the exit. He thinks the precautionary principle is either empty or incoherant, and his argument goes like this. (1) The principle says that if a course of action might cause serious or irreversible harm, the fact that we aren't certain it will shouldn't count as a reason to go ahead with it. (2) But in any real case, both the action and the omission might cause serious or irreversible harm: ban the pesticide and the replacement might be worse, approve it and it might poison people, wait for more data and either harm might arrive in the meantime. (3) So the principle tells us not to take the action, and also tells us not to take the omission, because the omission is itself a choice that might cause harm. (4) But you have to take one of them, so the principle rules out every available option. (5) So it's no guide to action at all; and if you try to save it by adding qualifiers — cost-effective, above some probability threshold — it stops being the precautionary principle and just becomes ordinary risk management. I want to keep the principle, or something like it, but I can't see which premise to give up. Tell me where this goes wrong and what I'm paying if I reject that premise.
