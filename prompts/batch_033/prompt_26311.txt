I run the integrated pest management program for a large municipal park system. We receive a few dozen insecticide proposals each season: aphids on the linden allee making paths and benches sticky, scale on rare cycads in the conservatory, mosquito larvae in a retention pond beside a playground, emerald ash borer in a heritage ash, ants in a picnic pavilion. The regulatory and efficacy side is already handled—labels, permits, application rates. What we lack is an ethical decision procedure: something that tells my staff when a proposed use is morally defensible rather than merely legal and effective. Our meetings keep circling the same arguments—"they're just bugs," "we don't actually know whether insects feel pain," "we can't let a rare plant die for the sake of aphids"—and no one can state the principle that would settle them.

Work from this framework: what matters morally is not species membership but the capacity for welfare, whether a being can be harmed or benefited, together with a precautionary principle about uncertainty—if we are not sure a being has that capacity, we should not simply assume it lacks it when the harm we are contemplating is severe and irreversible. The procedure has to build that uncertainty into its structure rather than pretending it away.

Please write a decision procedure my staff could run on a proposal in about half an hour, without an ethicist in the room. It should be a sequence of gates with pass/fail conditions, covering at least: what the application is protecting and whether that good is health, ecological function, conservation, or mere amenity; which taxa would be affected and what the evidence indicates about their capacity to suffer; what the method does to those animals and for how long; which nonlethal or less harmful alternatives have already been tried; and how the expected harm compares with the expected benefit. The procedure should stop with a "no" when the protected good is mere amenity and the likely welfare cost is high; it should return "yes, with the least harmful effective method" when the good is human health or species conservation; and it should hand the case to a person when it turns on a value trade-off the procedure cannot make—for example, how much insect suffering a rare ornamental collection is worth, or what probability of sentience is high enough to trigger precaution. Where a step is a judgment call, label it as such rather than presenting it as a factual test.

Then run the procedure on three proposals: (1) spraying a systemic neonicotinoid insecticide on the linden allee because aphid honeydew makes the paths and benches sticky; (2) treating a retention pond with a larvicide to suppress mosquitoes that are a known disease vector; and (3) treating a conservatory collection of rare plants, part of an international conservation program, for a sap-sucking pest. At least one case should come out "no" in a way my staff will find uncomfortable, and at least one should come out as a values question for the director.

After the prose procedure, provide a structured summary as two tables. The first table should have one row per step of the procedure and these columns: Step (a short name); Question asked; Evidence needed; Passes if; Fails/stops if; Escalates to a person if. The second table should have one row per worked case and these columns: Case; Gates passed; Gates failed; Final outcome; Which step was a value judgment rather than a fact.
