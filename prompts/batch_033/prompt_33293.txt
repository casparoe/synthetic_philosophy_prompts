I've been working on a paper about the evidential problem of evil, and I've come to a position I want to stress-test before I commit to it in print. The position is this: skeptical theism — roughly, the view that because God's cognitive superiority is vast, we should not expect to be able to discern God's reasons for permitting particular instances of suffering, and therefore we are in no position to conclude that any given evil is gratuitous — is not merely unsuccessful as a response to the evidential argument, but actively self-undermining. The argument, in compressed form, is that skeptical theism cannot be cleanly contained. If our cognitive limitations prevent us from inferring, from the existence of apparently pointless suffering, that there probably is no God-justifying reason for it, then those same limitations should prevent us from inferring anything about God's intentions or actions from the world we observe — including from events that look like answered prayers, miracles, or providential order. The skeptical theist who wants to block the inference from evil to atheism, but who still wants to affirm the rationality of natural theology or of trusting divine revelation, seems to be making an ad hoc exception to a principle that, if taken seriously, would be far more corrosive. My position is that this is not just a dialectical problem for a particular formulation of skeptical theism; it is a structural flaw — the view purchases immunity to the evidential argument at the cost of general epistemic paralysis about God, and that cost is one most theists cannot afford to pay.

I want to know what would count against this position. I'm not asking for a survey of what skeptical theists have said in reply — I've read that literature. I want a systematic map of the kinds of defeaters that should genuinely move someone who holds the view I've described. In particular: are there principled ways to restrict the scope of skeptical theism so that the cognitive limitation applies specifically to the permission of evil but not to other kinds of divine action, and if so, what would have to be true about the nature of moral reasoning, about divine psychology, or about the structure of evidential inference for that restriction to be non-arbitrary? What would the world have to look like — what patterns of suffering, what facts about the limits of human cognition, what kinds of moral knowledge we do or don't possess — for the skeptical theist's position to be both coherent and non-trivial? Could theism itself make a difference here: that is, could a theist who already accepts God's existence on independent grounds legitimately deploy skeptical theism as a defensive move without falling into the trap I've described, because their prior commitment to God's goodness licenses them to restrict the skepticism in a way the atheist interlocutor would not grant? And if so, does that reduce the debate to a clash of priors with no neutral resolution?

I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to distinguish between those that a committed proponent of my position would concede as counting against them (that is, genuine vulnerabilities internal to the argument) and those that they would dismiss as question-begging or as merely shifting the burden. Finally, I want an honest assessment of whether the position I've staked out has its own insulation problem — whether it, too, has been qualified in ways that make it hard to falsify — and if so, whether there is anything about the structure of the debate that makes such insulation unavoidable, perhaps because the dialectic has reached a point where both sides are operating with epistemological principles that are underdetermined by the evidence.
