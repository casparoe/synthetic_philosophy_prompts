I'm a PhD student working on emotion, and last week I watched two people in my department go back and forth about whether emotions are judgments or perceptions. (The third option, that emotions are just feelings, got set aside early — both of them thought it couldn't explain why emotions are about things, and I'm inclined to agree, so I'll leave it out.) I reconstructed the exchange from memory as best I can.

Priya, defending a judgment theory: "The basic datum is that emotions are answerable to reasons. If I'm angry at you because I think you leaked my email, and then I learn you didn't, my anger should dissolve — and when it doesn't, we treat that as a failure of rationality. A mere feeling isn't the kind of thing that can be justified or unjustified by evidence. So the emotion has to be an evaluative judgment: anger is the judgment that I've been wronged, and it inherits its reason-responsiveness from that content."

Dev, defending a perceptual theory: "Perceptions are also answerable to how things are — seeing the stick as bent in water is an inaccurate perception, not an unjustified judgment. So reason-responsiveness doesn't force a doxastic attitude. And the recalcitrance cases cut the other way: I can fear flying while fully judging that flying is safe. If the fear just were the judgment that flying is dangerous, I'd hold contradictory judgments. The actual structure is like the Müller-Lyer illusion: I judge the lines equal while they still look unequal. The emotion is a perception of danger that persists against my better judgment."

Priya: "The analogy to illusions is weaker than it looks. Visual illusions are modular and informationally encapsulated — no amount of evidence or reflection makes them go away. Emotions are plastic in a way illusions aren't. Therapy, experience, and argument can actually change someone's fear over time. That revisability looks like the revisability of judgments, not the stubbornness of perception."

Dev: "But recalcitrant emotions persist even in people whose emotional lives are generally reason-responsive, and they don't vanish the moment the person changes their mind. You can't just decide to stop fearing flying the way you can revise a belief when you get new evidence. If you insist the emotion is a judgment, you have to say the person holds contradictory judgments — and that's a much stronger kind of irrationality than what's going on when someone with a flying phobia says 'I know it's safe, I just can't help it.'"

Priya: "I don't have to posit a contradiction. The fear and the standing belief can have different contents. The standing judgment is that flying is statistically safe; the emotional judgment is something like that this situation is dangerous for me, now, in a way that matters. Those are different propositions, so there's no contradiction — just two evaluative takes, one thin and one thick. And notice what your view costs: if emotions are perceptions of value, then there have to be value properties out there in the world to be perceived. My view needs no such ontology; evaluative contents are just contents of our appraisals."

Dev: "Two replies. First, the perception view doesn't obviously require mind-independent values, any more than color perception requires colors to be mind-independent — you can hold a projectivist or response-dependent account of the evaluative content. Second, if we're counting costs, your view over-intellectualizes emotion. Dogs and infants are afraid. If fear requires an evaluative judgment — conceptual, propositional content — then dogs and infants can't be afraid. That's a worse bullet to bite than value properties."

Priya: "The animal point doesn't force that. 'Judgment' doesn't have to mean 'linguistically articulated belief.' A dog can assess a threat without language; an infant can take a situation to be bad before it can say so. What makes something a judgment is that the organism assents to an evaluative content, not that it can put that content into words. And separate from that: we hold people answerable for their emotions — 'I can't help being angry' is not automatically an excuse. That's because emotions embody how a person has taken things to be. If emotions are just perceptions, they're more like headaches or afterimages: things that happen to you rather than things you're answerable for."

Dev: "But you're using 'judgment' thin enough to include a dog's threat-assessment and thick enough to do the responsibility work you just leaned on. You can't have it both ways. If the dog's state is a judgment in the full, responsibility-grounding sense, that's implausible; if it's a judgment only in a loose sense, you've conceded that the attitude isn't really doxastic. And the responsibility point fails anyway: we do hold people responsible for perception-like patterns too. We fault someone who habitually sees an ambiguous remark as a slight, when that's a pattern of their attention. Perceptions can be trained — radiologists and sommeliers learn to see things. So answerability doesn't require the attitude to be a judgment."
