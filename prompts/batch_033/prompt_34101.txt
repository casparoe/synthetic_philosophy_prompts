I've been working through the literature on indicative conditionals and came across a version of Lewis's triviality proof that seems airtight, but the conclusion is absurd — so something must be wrong. I can't figure out what though.

Here's the argument:

(1) Stalnaker's Thesis: the probability of an indicative conditional "If A, then B" is the conditional probability P(B | A), whenever P(A) > 0.

(2) By the law of total probability, P(If A then B) = P(If A then B | B) · P(B) + P(If A then B | ¬B) · P(¬B).

(3) Applying Stalnaker's Thesis to the conditional probabilities: P(If A then B | B) = P(B | A ∧ B) = 1.

(4) Similarly, P(If A then B | ¬B) = P(B | A ∧ ¬B) = 0.

(5) Substituting: P(If A then B) = 1 · P(B) + 0 · P(¬B) = P(B).

(6) But by (1), P(If A then B) = P(B | A).

(7) Therefore P(B | A) = P(B) for any A, B with P(A) > 0 — all propositions are probabilistically independent.

(8) That's false. So something is wrong with the premises or the reasoning.

Where exactly does this argument go wrong, and what does it cost to fix it? I'm especially interested in whether rejecting a step forces us to give up Stalnaker's Thesis entirely, or whether there's a way to restrict it that blocks the proof without making it ad hoc.
