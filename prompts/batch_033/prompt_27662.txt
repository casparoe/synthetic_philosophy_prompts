Last night a friend and I spent two hours arguing about the color phi phenomenon and wound up in a standoff over whether the Orwellian and Stalinesque interpretations of it mark a genuine distinction or a pseudo-distinction. I've been replaying the exchange and I'm no longer confident either of us handled every move correctly. I'd like you to go through our argument step by step and adjudicate it: who was right about which specific point, where did each of us go wrong, and what are we both missing?

For background: in the color phi phenomenon, two differently colored dots are flashed in quick succession at separate locations, and subjects report seeing a single dot that moves between the locations and changes color midway along the trajectory. The puzzle is that the reported color change seems to occur before the second dot appears, raising the question of how the brain could represent the second color prior to receiving it. The Orwellian hypothesis says the brain initially registers two distinct flashes and then retroactively revises the memory of the experience — the subject has a false memory of seeing a moving, color-changing dot. The Stalinesque hypothesis says the brain delays conscious experience until both dots have been processed, then presents a coherent percept — the subject genuinely has the experience of the moving dot, but only after a delay. Dennett's well-known claim is that these two hypotheses are empirically equivalent and that there is no fact of the matter as to which is correct, because the distinction presupposes a Cartesian theater — a privileged moment and place where processing "enters consciousness."

Here is our exchange, reconstructed as faithfully as I can manage.

(1) My friend began by defending the Orwellian story. He said the color phi phenomenon is a straightforward case of memory revision: you see two separate flashes, and then your brain retroactively edits the record to make it seem as though you saw a single moving dot that changed color. He argued this is the more parsimonious account because it doesn't require positing that consciousness is somehow held in abeyance, waiting for the second flash before showing you anything at all.

(2) I replied that the Orwellian story has its own hidden cost. It requires there to be a real initial experience — the experience of two separate flashes — that gets completely overwritten before you can report it, remember it, or access it in any way. What evidence is there that this initial experience ever occurred? The Stalinesque story, by contrast, says your brain simply waits until it has both flashes, constructs a coherent percept, and only then does anything enter consciousness. Given that conscious perception already lags behind stimulation by on the order of two hundred milliseconds, a small additional delay for the sake of editorial coherence is hardly mysterious. I said the Stalinesque story seemed more economical because it doesn't posit an inaccessible experiential event.

(3) My friend countered that I had walked straight into Dennett's trap. We now had two stories — Orwellian and Stalinesque — that make exactly the same predictions. In both, the subject says "I saw a dot move and change color." In both, the brain does the same processing in the same order. The only difference is whether we label a particular stage of that processing as "pre-conscious editing" or "post-conscious editing." He said this label has no empirical content, the distinction is idle, and there is simply no fact of the matter about which story is true. That was Dennett's whole point, he said.

(4) I pushed back that this argument generalizes in a way that should trouble him. For any unconscious process that contributes to a conscious report, you can always ask: did the editing happen before or after consciousness? If Dennett's reasoning is sound, the answer is always "there's no fact of the matter." But then there is no fact of the matter about the timing of any conscious experience whatsoever. That is not a modest conclusion about one perceptual illusion — it is eliminativism about the entire temporal structure of consciousness. I said I doubted he wanted to sign up for that.

(5) He replied that he did not see this as a reductio at all. He said Dennett's multiple drafts model explicitly denies that there is any Cartesian theater, any finish line where processing "becomes conscious." So of course there is no fact of the matter about exactly when something becomes conscious — asking for the precise millisecond is like asking for the precise hair whose loss makes a man bald. He said this is not eliminativism; it is just the recognition that consciousness is not the kind of thing that has sharp temporal boundaries. He thought my objection was question-begging because it assumed precisely the kind of theater that the model rejects.

(6) I answered that this still didn't sit right with me, and here is why. The original puzzle was a substantive one: how can a subject seem to experience a color change before the information about the second color is available? The Orwellian story gives one answer — you don't, you just misremember. The Stalinesque story gives a different answer — you do, but the experience is delayed. These are two different answers to a real question. If Dennett's move is to say the question itself is ill-posed and the distinction is a pseudo-distinction, then he hasn't answered the question; he has rejected its terms. And the mere fact that we cannot experimentally distinguish the two answers does not, by itself, show that the question is ill-posed. It might just show that we face an empirically underdetermined choice between two genuine possibilities. I said I suspected Dennett was conflating "we can't tell which is true" with "there is nothing to be true."

At this point we were going in circles and called it a night. Please go through the six moves above and assess each one: which points hold up, which don't, and where the argument took a wrong turn. I'd also like to know whether there is a way of framing the dispute that neither of us considered — something that would let us keep the intuition that the Orwellian/Stalinesque question is substantive without falling back into a Cartesian theater.
