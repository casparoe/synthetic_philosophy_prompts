I'm a PhD student in moral philosophy, and there's a style of objection I keep running into that I don't have a clean grip on. The target usually isn't the theory's verdict but the frame of mind it forces on the agent. Example one: a man can save either his wife or a stranger from drowning, not both. The theory says saving the wife is fine, maybe required. The objector says a man who, in that moment, even needs to run the justification—who treats it as a question of whether the neutral, everyone-counts-the-same standpoint would approve—is already going wrong; the extra thought is itself the failure. Example two: someone has built their whole life around a project, a decades-long book or a promise to a dead friend, and a theory says to drop it whenever the numbers show slightly more good elsewhere. The objector doesn't just say the numbers are wrong in this case; they say the theory asks the person to hold their deepest commitments at arm's length, as things to be weighed and traded, and that anyone who could do that would be cut off from their own life.

Are these one objection or two seperate ones? Does either have an established name? I know the words integrity and one thought too many get thrown around, but I'm after the carving: is the defect in the theory, the agent, or the deliberation? Does the second objection need the first, or do they come apart? And if what I'm describing only imperfectly matches the standard named view, where exactly are the edges? I'd rather have that than a bare label. Also, please say how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the point you're least sure of.
