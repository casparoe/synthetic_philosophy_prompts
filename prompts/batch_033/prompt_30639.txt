I spent most of last evening arguing with a friend about Quine's indeterminacy of translation, and I came away convinced that the sentence "translation is indeterminate" was doing several different jobs at once in his argument. We started from the gavagai example: he claimed that a field linguist who observes a native assenting to "Gavagai" in the presence of a rabbit cannot settle whether the native means rabbit, undetached rabbit part, rabbit stage, or something else. From there he moved to the conclusion that there is no fact of the matter about what the native's words mean, and then to the further claim that the same holds for any speaker — including a speaker of one's own language, and perhaps for one's own words as well. Each time I tried to separate "we cannot tell which translation is right from the behavioral evidence" from "there is nothing about the speaker that makes one translation right," he fell back on the slogan "translation is indeterminate," as though that single sentence settled which of us was right.

I suspect that sentence can express several different precise claims of very different strengths, and that only some of them license the moves my friend was making. I would like a response I can take to our next conversation, with the following sections in this order and roughly these lengths:

1. The readings (about 500 words). Lay out five or six distinct precise claims the sentence could be used to express, ordered from weakest to strongest. Give each a short label and state it in a sentence or two. Include at least: (a) a merely epistemic reading about our evidence; (b) an underdetermination-by-all-possible-behavioral-evidence reading; (c) inscrutability of reference; (d) indeterminacy proper, i.e., no fact of the matter between empirically equivalent translation manuals; (e) a broader meaning-nihilist reading; and (f) an anything-goes reading on which all manuals are equally good. Feel free to add or split readings where that is cleaner.

2. Verdicts (about 300 words). For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible thesis, or false, and note which readings are genuinely Quinean and which are caricatures.

3. What the argument actually needed (about 250 words). Identify which reading or readings my friend's move from the gavagai example to "no fact of the matter about anyone's words" actually requires, and say whether that reading is supported by the gavagai example itself, by the broader indeterminacy thesis, or by neither.

4. Bottom line (about 100 words). Give me the two or three sentences I should say when we resume the argument.
