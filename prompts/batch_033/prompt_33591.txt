I've been covering the algorithmic fairness beat for a few years now, and there's a claim that keeps surfacing in the science press that I can't quite get straight. The claim is that there are mathematical impossibility theorems showing that algorithmic fairness, in any meaningful sense, is unattainable — that the whole enterprise is conceptually bankrupt. The results usually cited are the ones from roughly the last decade showing that certain statistical fairness criteria can't be simultaneously satisfied: things like equalized odds, calibration by group, and predictive parity. The shorthand I keep seeing is that "the math says fairness is impossible."

But when I talk to researchers in the field, they don't seem to have packed up and gone home. They're still designing systems, still publishing, still using terms like "fairness" without scare quotes. So either they're in denial about a result that undermines their entire discipline, or the impossibility theorems don't actually say what the shorthand says they say.

What I'm trying to understand is: what exactly do these impossibility results rule out, and what do they leave open? If they show that certain statistical criteria conflict, why is that not the end of the story? And if the popular gloss — "fairness is mathematically impossible" — is a misreading, what is the right way to paraphrase what these theorems actually establish, in language a reader of a general-interest science magazine could follow?
