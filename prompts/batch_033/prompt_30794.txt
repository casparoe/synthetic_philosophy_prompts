You are a senior philosopher specializing in moral progress and the history of ethical change, with a particular interest in the structure of normative expansion. Please think carefully and reason step by step.

I am putting together a research initiative on moral progress, centered on the "expanding circle" hypothesis — the idea, associated most prominently with Peter Singer but with antecedents in the Stoics, Hume, and W. E. H. Lecky, that moral progress consists at least partly in expanding the set of beings to whom we extend moral concern: from kin to tribe to nation to all humans, and potentially to nonhuman animals, future generations, and beyond. The initiative can fund roughly five multi-year projects, and I need to decide which open questions about the expanding circle are most worth prioritizing.

I was discussing this with colleagues after a workshop on moral progress last month, and we kept coming back to the same frustration: there is a great deal of interesting philosophical work on the expanding circle, but no clear sense of which questions are load-bearing and which are decorative. Some people want to investigate whether the circle has actually expanded in the way the hypothesis claims — a descriptive question that quickly becomes historical and empirical. Others want to ask whether expansion is genuinely progress or merely change — a normative question that pulls in metaethics and raises the specter of moral realism versus antirealism. Still others want to know what drives expansion when it happens: is it reason, empathy, institutional design, material conditions, or something else? And a fourth group wants to ask about the endpoint: does the circle expand indefinitely, or is there a principled stopping point, and if so, what determines it? Meanwhile, barely anyone is asking whether the circle metaphor itself is misleading — whether moral progress might sometimes require contraction, or whether the spatial image of concentric circles obscures forms of moral concern that do not fit a center-periphery structure at all.

I am less interested in a literature review than in a strategic assessment. For each question you nominate for the top five, I want to know: (1) Why it is upstream of other work — what else depends on settling it, or what else would be substantially reframed by progress on it? (2) How tractable it is with the tools of contemporary philosophy, as opposed to requiring empirical work that philosophers are not best positioned to do. (3) What evidence, arguments, or conceptual shifts over the next three to five years would most change your confidence in the answer, or would most change how we think about the other questions on the list. (4) Which questions you would deprioritize despite their apparent importance, and why — because they are intractable, because their answer would not change much downstream, or because they are already well-covered by existing work.

Please begin by brainstorming a wider set of candidate questions before narrowing to your top five, and be explicit about the criteria you are using to weigh importance against tractability and neglectedness. I want to see your reasoning and your trade-offs, not just the final list.
