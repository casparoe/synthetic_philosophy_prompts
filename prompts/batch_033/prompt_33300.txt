I'm a PhD student sitting in on a formal epistemology seminar, and two of my classmates got into a sustained argument about Jeffrey conditionalization that I'm still trying to sort out. I'd like someone to go through it move by move and tell me who's right about what.

The dispute started with the textbook case: you examine a piece of cloth by candlelight, and your credence that it is green shifts from 0.5 to 0.8 without any proposition becoming certain. Jeffrey conditionalization says you update by holding your conditional probabilities fixed and reweighting: P_new(H) = P_old(H | green) × 0.8 + P_old(H | ¬green) × 0.2. All well and good.

Then David raised a problem. Suppose you have a visual experience as of a bird in a tree, and your credence that it's a robin goes from 0.1 to 0.7. But the experience doesn't merely shift your credence in "it's a robin" — it also gives you information about the lighting, about how far away the tree is, about whether your glasses are smudged, about the base rate of robins at this time of year given that you're now more confident the shape is bird-sized. These are all propositions whose probabilities shift, and they aren't conditionally independent of each other. So the rigidity condition — that P(H | robin) and P(H | ¬robin) stay the same — might simply fail. The experience changes the conditional probabilities themselves.

Sarah pushed back: that just means "robin" was the wrong partition. The experience changed your probabilities across a whole algebra of propositions — about the bird, the lighting, your perceptual state. If you pick the right partition — one that captures everything you learned — rigidity will hold and Jeffrey conditionalization goes through.

David replied that this is question-begging. The claim that such a partition exists, and that the experience changes only the probabilities of its members while leaving all conditional probabilities intact, is a substantive empirical claim about the structure of experience. Why should we believe it? Maybe uncertain experiences just don't work that way — maybe they fundamentally alter conditional probabilities, and no partition restores rigidity.

Sarah answered that any update can be represented as ordinary conditionalization on some proposition if you go back far enough in your prior probability space. The existence of a partition that makes Jeffrey conditionalization work is guaranteed by the mathematics, not a contingent psychological claim.

David's final move: "Then Jeffrey conditionalization is just a redescription, not a constraint. If for any update whatsoever I can find some partition that makes it look like a Jeffrey shift, the rule tells me nothing about which updates are rational and which aren't. It's a notation, not a norm."

At this point the professor moved us on, but I can't stop thinking about it. Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. Who has the better of each step? Is there a resolution neither of them saw? And does David's final objection stick, or is there a way to save Jeffrey conditionalization as a genuine normative constraint?
