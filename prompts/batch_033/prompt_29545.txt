You are a philosopher of physics who works on confirmation and scientific theory choice, especially in cases where direct empirical tests are absent. I have been trying to work out what I actually think about when a theory gets evidential support, and I want you to help me find the principle behind my case judgments. Please think through the cases one by one before committing to an account, and where two of my verdicts pull in different directions, say so explicitly.

Here are my current verdicts, stated as honestly as I can:

- The discovery of the Higgs boson strongly confirmed the Standard Model, even though the particle had been expected for decades and its absence would have been the real surprise.
- I feel some genuine pull toward string theory from its internal consistency, its unification of gravity with the other forces, and the web of dualities, even though it has produced no unique low-energy prediction that has been tested. I cannot tell whether this pull is evidential or merely aesthetic.
- The fact that our universe appears fine-tuned for life gives me almost no confidence in a multiverse, even though a multiverse with enough variation would make life-friendly constants less surprising. It feels like the multiverse is doing the explaining but not getting confirmed by what it explains.
- When a model predicts a new particle and the particle is found, I take that as strong confirmation. When a model predicts a new particle and the lower bound on its mass just keeps moving upward decade after decade, I take that as disconfirmation of the model, not a neutral no-result.
- That a theory is the only known consistent way to do something, say quantum gravity, feels like weak evidence at best; that it remains the only known way after a long and serious search feels like somewhat more, but I am unsure how much more or why.
- I no longer believe falsifiability is the right line. A theory can be so flexible that it cannot be falsified and yet be rationally rejected, and a theory can be untestable in practice and yet be rationally preferred.

Is there one coherent account of evidential support that delivers all or nearly all of these verdicts, or will I have to give one up? Please state the principle precisely, identify which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and apply it to two cases I keep going back and forth on: the many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics, which makes no new predictions but is simpler than collapse theories; and the claim that the failure to find supersymmetric particles at the LHC has disconfirmed string theory specifically, rather than only disconfirming particular supersymmetric extensions of the Standard Model. End by saying how confident you are in the account you propose and which part you are least sure of.
