My epistemology professor has given the following verdicts across a semester of cases about testimonial justification — that is, whether a belief formed by accepting what someone tells you counts as justified:

- A stranger tells you the time when you ask: justified.
- A known pathological liar tells you something: not justified.
- A stranger tells you they just witnessed a miracle: not justified.
- A young child tells you what happened at the playground: justified.
- A politician from a rival party tells you something damaging about their opponent: not justified.
- A close friend with a spotless track record tells you something mildly surprising: justified.

Next week's case is this: you are on a train and a stranger strikes up a conversation. They mention, in passing, that a certain neighborhood in your city has become unsafe after dark. The stranger seems sincere and otherwise unremarkable; you have never been to that neighborhood and have no independent evidence either way; the claim is neither mundane nor extraordinary.

Predict the professor's verdict on this new case. More importantly: identify at least two distinct rules or principles that fit all six of her earlier verdicts, say what each rule predicts about the train case, and give your best guess about which rule she is actually running — with your reasons for preferring it. Then tell me what further case would most cleanly tell the two rules apart.
