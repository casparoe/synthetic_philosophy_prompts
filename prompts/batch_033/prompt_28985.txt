I've been working through the anthropic reasoning literature for a seminar and I've constructed an argument that seems to lead somewhere unacceptable, but I can't locate the misstep. It goes like this:

(1) When you learn a new piece of evidence E, you should conditionalize on E in the standard Bayesian way: your posterior credence in a hypothesis H is proportional to your prior credence in H times the likelihood P(E | H).

(2) Your own existence is a piece of evidence you have learned — after all, you might not have existed, and you now know that you do.

(3) The likelihood P(I exist | H) is higher for hypotheses H that entail the existence of many observers than for hypotheses that entail the existence of few observers. There are simply more "slots" you could have turned out to occupy in a more populous world, so your existence is less surprising under such hypotheses.

(4) Therefore, by (1)–(3), the mere fact that you exist should shift your credence toward hypotheses that predict larger total numbers of observers in the universe.

(5) This shift can be enormous. Suppose two cosmological theories, T₁ and T₂, are equally simple, fit all the same astrophysical data, and differ only in that T₁ predicts roughly 10¹² observers will ever exist while T₂ predicts roughly 10²⁴. Before taking your own existence into account, you might reasonably assign them equal prior credence. After conditionalizing on your existence, you should be roughly 10¹² times more confident in T₂ than in T₁ — a staggering update driven by no new empirical observation at all.

(6) But this means you can draw strong, specific conclusions about the large-scale structure of the cosmos — how many galaxies harbor life, how many minds the universe contains — from the armchair, without ever looking through a telescope.

(7) That conclusion is absurd. You cannot settle empirical questions about the population of the universe by reflecting on the fact that you happen to be alive.

I find each of (1)–(5) individually plausible, and I don't see a way to reject (7) without giving up on the idea that cosmology is an empirical science. Yet (6) seems to follow from (1)–(5), and (7) flatly contradicts (6). So something has to go. I'm most reluctant to give up (1), since it's just Bayesianism, and (7), since it seems like basic scientific humility. But I also have a hard time seeing what's wrong with (2) or (3) — if existence is evidence, surely more observers make your existence more likely? Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost for the rest of one's epistemology?
