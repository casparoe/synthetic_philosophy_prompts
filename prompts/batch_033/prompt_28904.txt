A friend of mine has been reading about value theory and summarised the position like this: 'Some philosophers, notably Joseph Raz, argue that many of the values we care about most are incommensurable — they cannot be measured on a common scale the way we can rank objects by weight or length. This means that when you face a hard choice — say, between a career in law and a career in music — there is no fact of the matter about which option is better, and they are not equally good either. Reason cannot guide you here; it simply runs out. That is why hard choices feel so difficult: we keep looking for reasons where there are none. Any decision we eventually make is, at bottom, arbitrary — a matter of picking rather than choosing — and this is why no one can be criticised for choosing either way.'

I suspect this overstates things, but I am not sure exactly where. Could you go through this summary and tell me which parts a philosopher working on incommensurability and hard choices would accept, which parts they would push back on, and whether the conclusion that hard choices are arbitrary follows from the accurate parts? Please answer in British English.
