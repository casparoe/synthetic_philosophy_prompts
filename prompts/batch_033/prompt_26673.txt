I'm in a formal epistemology class and we spent two weeks on van Fraassen's Reflection principle, and I'm kind of frustrated because the debate in the readings seems like its been going in circles since the eighties and nobody has moved. As I understand it, the principle says your current credence in a proposition, conditional on your future self having credence r in that same proposition, should equal r — you're supposed to treat your future self as an expert. Van Fraassen's argument for it was a diachronic Dutch book: if you don't defer to your future self like that, a bookie who knows your credences can offer you a package of bets now and later, each of which you'll accept, that guarantees you a loss no matter what happens.

The problem is the obvious counterexamples: if I know I'm going to be drunk, or I've been told I'll be given a drug that makes me certain of something false, or I know I'm going to forget a key piece of evidence, it seems crazy to say I have to line my current credences up with that future self. The defenses I've read mostly seem to bite the bullet or restrict the principle to cases where the future self is rational, which feels like moving the goalposts. Then there's another camp that says the Dutch book argument can't establish a requirement of rationality, because the bookie is just exploiting an anticipated screw-up — the fact that someone can profit off your future irrationality doesn't show you're irrational now. And then there are people who say rationality is synchronic, a matter of how your credences hang together at a single time, so a principle that ties your current credences to your future credences is just aimed at the wrong object; from that angle Reflection is at best a theorem you get if you already assume conditionalization plus certainty that you'll keep conditionalizing, not a seperate norm.

So my working theory is that this debate isn't really about credences at all: it's a disagreement about what rationality attaches to — time-slices or temporally extended agents — and about what a Dutch book argument is even supposed to show. But I'm not confident, because it could instead be a substantive question about whether there are any diachronic norms at all, or just a verbal dispute where the two sides mean different things by "rational." I'm not asking you to pick the winner; I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is. And given that diagnosis, what would count as progress — a formal result, a distinction that dissolves the question, a clearer way of stating the principle — or is this just not the sort of question that can be settled?
