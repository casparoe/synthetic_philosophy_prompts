I'm currently enrolled in a philosophy of science module that devotes several weeks to the methodological status of evolutionary psychology. Our lecturer has presented us with what she calls the "just-so story" objection—the charge, associated most famously with Stephen Jay Gould and Richard Lewontin, that evolutionary psychological explanations are often reverse-engineered accounts that sound plausible but lack rigorous evidentiary support. The idea, as I understand it, is that for any given behavioural trait, one can concoct an adaptive story about how it might have been selected for in the ancestral environment, but the sheer flexibility of this explanatory framework means that many such hypotheses are untestable in practice, or at least insufficiently constrained by the available data. Gould and Lewontin's broader point, as our lecturer framed it, was not that adaptationist reasoning is always illegitimate, but that it becomes problematic when researchers treat the mere existence of a plausible adaptive narrative as sufficient evidence for that narrative, without subjecting it to the risk of falsification.

I am finding it difficult to settle on a view here. On the one hand, the objection seems to have genuine force: I have read papers that propose evolutionary explanations for everything from jealousy to music appreciation, and it is hard to see how one could decisively rule out competing adaptive hypotheses for most of these phenomena. The worry is not merely that individual hypotheses are speculative, but that the research programme as a whole lacks the kind of critical filters that would allow bad hypotheses to be reliably identified and discarded. On the other hand, defenders of evolutionary psychology—John Tooby, Leda Cosmides, Steven Pinker, and others—have argued that the criticism is overstated, that evolutionary hypotheses do generate testable predictions, and that the alternative is often an implicit blank-slate model that is itself empirically inadequate. They point to cases where evolutionary reasoning has led to novel discoveries rather than mere retrospective storytelling, and they contend that critics hold evolutionary psychology to an unreasonably high standard of proof that is not applied to other historical sciences, such as geology or cosmology.

I would like you to write an essay that weighs the arguments for and against the claim that evolutionary psychology is methodologically problematic in the way the "just-so story" objection alleges. Please reach a considered conclusion—do not simply list arguments on both sides and leave the matter unresolved. Defend a verdict, even if it is a qualified one (for instance, you might argue that some subfields of evolutionary psychology are more vulnerable to the objection than others, or that the objection applies with different force depending on the type of evidence marshalled). The essay should be written in American English.
