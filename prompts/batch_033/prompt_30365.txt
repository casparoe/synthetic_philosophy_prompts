I'm a journalist who covers philosophy and cognitive science for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a piece about the norms that govern everyday conversation. The philosophical literature on assertion has produced several competing accounts of what standard a speaker must meet to be properly positioned to assert something. The two views I want to juxtapose are the knowledge norm and the truth norm.

The knowledge norm, most prominently defended by Timothy Williamson, holds that one should assert that p only if one knows that p. The truth norm, defended by Matthew Weiner and others, holds that one should assert that p only if p is true — regardless of whether the speaker knows it, believes it, or has any justification for it. On its face, the truth norm is more permissive: it licenses assertions that happen to be true even when the speaker is guessing, and it withholds license from assertions that are false even when the speaker has excellent evidence. The knowledge norm, by contrast, rules out lucky guesses and permits some assertions that turn out to be false, provided the speaker knew at the time (since knowledge does not require infallibility).

I'd like you to walk me through the strongest cases that are meant to pull these two norms apart. In particular, please address:

- The lucky guess: someone asserts p on a mere hunch, p turns out to be true, but the speaker had no justification. Does our ordinary reaction — that the speaker spoke out of turn — favor the knowledge norm, or can the truth norm accommodate that reaction by distinguishing the norm of assertion from other conversational norms?
- Lottery propositions: someone asserts, purely on the basis of the odds, that their ticket will lose a fair lottery, and it does lose. The knowledge norm says this assertion was improper because the speaker did not know it would lose. The truth norm says it was proper because it was true. Which verdict better tracks how we actually respond to such assertions, and why?
- Gettier-style cases: a speaker has a justified true belief that falls short of knowledge because of some epistemic luck. The knowledge norm says the assertion is improper; the truth norm says it is proper. What does each view have to say about the intuition that there is something defective about such an assertion, even though what was said was true?
- The theoretical costs on each side: what broader commitments does each norm force us to take on? For instance, does the knowledge norm require a controversial account of knowledge itself? Does the truth norm struggle to explain why we ever criticize assertions that turn out to be true?

Finally, I'd like your assessment: which norm gives a better overall account of our practice of assertion, and what is the most serious unresolved problem for the view you favor? Please answer in American English.
