I'm a programme officer at a foundation that funds a mix of near-term global health and speculative 'high-upside' work. I triage about a dozen pitches a month before our investment committee sees them, and I keep hitting the same wall: the expected value of a proposal is often dominated by a tiny chance of an astronomically large payoff, like a 0.01% chance of preventing an existential catastrophe or a one-in-a-million shot at a technology that would help everyone. Straight maximisation says I should put all the money there, and it feels wrong, but I can't say why without throwing away expected value reasoning altogether. Could you write me a decision procedure I can actually run on one of these in about half an hour, before I write my recommendation: the questions to ask in order, when a probability is too small or too cooked-up to treat as a real probability, which objections are genuine criticisms of fanaticism and which are just my own risk-aversion, where the procedure says 'don't fund despite the expected value', and where it hands off to a person because it's a values call rather than a maths call. The only numbers I have are the applicants' own, and they always seem overconfident, so say how to handle that. Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) a 0.01% chance of preventing an existential catastrophe, (2) a one-in-a-million chance of curing a disease that affects a few hundred people a year, and (3) a 10% chance of doubling the income of a million people. Include at least one case where the procedure tells me to do something I won't like, and be honest about what the procedure cannot settle.
