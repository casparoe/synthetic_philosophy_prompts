I'm a journalist working on a long-read about the boundary problem in democratic theory — the question of who should constitute "the people" that gets to govern itself. Over the past several years, I've been tracking the published positions of a prominent political theorist on a series of concrete cases. She has been unusually willing to go on the record with specific verdicts rather than staying at the level of principle. Here is what she has said, as I've reconstructed it from her books, essays, and interviews:

1. Long-term permanent residents who are not citizens (she set the threshold at ten years of legal residence) should be enfranchised in all elections, national as well as local.
2. Citizens who have lived abroad for decades, including those who have never set foot in the country, should retain full voting rights for life.
3. Non-resident foreign nationals who own property in the country should not get a vote on property-tax referenda, or on any other ballot measure, even when the measure directly affects the value of their holdings.
4. The voting age should be lowered to sixteen.
5. Residents of a neighboring country who would be substantially affected by the pollution from a proposed border factory should not have a vote in the permitting decision.
6. Incarcerated citizens should retain the right to vote, with no disqualification for any category of offense.

She is about to publish a new essay, and I've learned that it will address a case she has not ruled on before: whether cross-border workers — people who commute daily into the country for work, pay income and payroll taxes, and are subject to the country's labor, traffic, and commercial regulations — but who reside in a neighboring state and hold only that state's citizenship, should be allowed to vote in the country's national elections.

I want to anticipate her verdict before the essay comes out. The six cases above seem to me to be consistent with more than one underlying principle, and those principles might pull in different directions on the cross-border commuter case. I need three things. First, identify the candidate rules she could be running — the ones that fit all six of her published verdicts. Second, work out what each rule would prescribe for the new case, and say which rule you think best captures her actual position, with your reasons. Third, if the record genuinely underdetermines the answer, tell me what principle she would be adopting by going one way rather than the other. And finally, I'd like you to name the further case she hasn't ruled on — the one that would most cleanly distinguish the competing interpretations of her record, whichever way the cross-border case goes.

I'm looking for a committed, definite answer: take a position on what her verdict will be and defend it, rather than laying out the options and leaving it there.
