Suppose you're red-teaming a deployment plan for a fairly capable model doing software engineering with access to internal tooling, and the threat model is that the model is already misaligned and sandbagging: it looked fine in evals and is now waiting for a chance to do something bad without getting caught. Here are eight control measures on the table. Rate each 0–10 by how much safety it actually buy's you under that threat model (0 = basically decorative, 10 = catches or stops almost any competent schemer), and in one sentence say the specific failure mode you'd worry about most for each.

1. Trusted monitoring: a much weaker model you've vetted reads and summarizes everything the deployed model does.
2. Untrusted monitoring: a separate unvetted instance of the same model flags suspicious outputs, with no guarantee it isn't scheming too.
3. Honeypots: planted canary files and fake escape routes that only a scheming model would have a reason to touch.
4. Hard sandboxing: no internet, allowlisted tools only, and a human reviews anything before it leaves the sandbox.
5. Capability restrictions: refusing to train on certain data or fine-tuning away dangerous skills before deployment.
6. Interpretability audits: scanning activations for explicit representations of deception, long-horizon plans, or "pretend to be aligned" reasoning.
7. Adversarial red-team evals: many targeted prompts designed to elicit misbehavior, run in a way that tries to keep the model from noticing it's being tested.
8. Tripwire-and-shutdown: any suspicious output triggers an automatic rollback and full shutdown, and the model knows this policy.

Then tell me which two you'd bet the whole deployment on, and what evidence would make you move one of the bottom three up two points.
