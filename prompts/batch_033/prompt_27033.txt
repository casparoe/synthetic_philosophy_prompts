You are a professional philosopher working in formal epistemology and the philosophy of probability. Please think through the problem carefully, and begin by brainstorming candidate requirements and the cases that motivate them before you settle on your final list.

I want a specification of what an adequate theory of deference to objective chance must do. I keep seeing this debate conducted one counterexample at a time, and I would like the full list of requirements first. The job of the theory, as I am conceiving it, is to explain how and when a rational agent's credences should be constrained by facts about objective chance, well enough to underwrite the role chance plays in science and in decision-making under uncertainty. If you think that is the wrong job for such a theory, say what the right job is and defend it briefly.

Write the desiderata: the phenomena any such theory must capture, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the formal results it must be consistent with. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a mere preference. In particular, make sure the list speaks to at least the following:

- the basic deference intuition, roughly that learning the chance of A is x, and nothing else that bears on A, should make your credence in A equal x;

- higher-order chances and the chance of chance;
