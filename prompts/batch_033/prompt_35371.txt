I keep encountering the dismissal "that's just a verbal dispute" across different philosophical debates — in metaontology (whether there are tables or only particles arranged table-wise), in philosophy of biology (whether viruses are alive), in social philosophy (whether certain classifications count as race), and in everyday arguments about whether a tomato is a fruit. The phrase is wielded as though it settles something: once you've classified a debate as merely verbal, you're apparently entitled to stop caring about it. But I suspect "verbal dispute" is doing several different jobs in these contexts. Sometimes it seems to mean the parties agree on all non-linguistic facts and differ only in which words they attach to them. Sometimes it seems to mean the question itself is ill-posed until we stipulate definitions. Sometimes it seems to mean the debate has no practical import — nothing hangs on the answer. Sometimes it seems to mean something stronger, bordering on anti-realism: there is no fact of the matter at all. Can you separate the distinct claims that "this is just a verbal dispute" could be making, from the least to the most metaphysically loaded? For each reading, tell me whether it's plausible, whether it's trivial or substantive, and — the part I most need — whether that reading actually licenses walking away from the debate, or whether the dismissal itself depends on smuggling in a stronger claim than the reading provides. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
