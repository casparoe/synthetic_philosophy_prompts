I'm designing a three-week module on moral luck for a seminar I teach called "Philosophy of Criminal Law." The students are law students with some philosophy background but no specialist knowledge of the moral luck literature. The module's animating question is: if a legislature actually took the problem of moral luck seriously, what would it change?

The standard taxonomy — resultant luck (how things turn out), circumstantial luck (what situations you face), constitutive luck (your character and dispositions), and causal luck (how prior causes determine your actions) — each seems to threaten the retributive idea that punishment should track desert. But they don't all threaten it in the same way, and they don't all point toward the same legal reforms. Some might call for narrow doctrinal fixes, like eliminating the sentencing differential between attempts and completed crimes. Others might call for sweeping institutional redesign, like replacing retributive sentencing with something more consequentialist. And some might, on closer inspection, have no practicable legal implications at all — the philosophical argument may be sound but the legal translation may simply not go through.

I'd like you to roleplay as a senior philosopher of law who has spent a career thinking about moral luck and criminal responsibility. Please think carefully and systematically. I want you to rank the four types of moral luck by how much practical weight their implications carry for criminal law reform — that is, if you could only reform the law in response to one type of moral luck, which would you pick, and why? For each type, identify the most concrete legal change it would motivate, and explain what makes that change more or less urgent than the alternatives. I also want you to flag any type of moral luck whose implications you think are overrated — where the philosophical argument is sound but the legal upshot is smaller than people assume, or where the proposed reform would create worse problems than it solves. The deliverable is a defended ranking, not a literature review. I care about the criteria you use to compare across types: importance of the injustice addressed, tractability of the legal reform, risk of unintended consequences, and how many other doctrines the change would destabilize.

At the very end of your response, provide a one-sentence summary of your overall position, formatted as \answer{...}.
