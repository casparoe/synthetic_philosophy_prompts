I own six auto repair shops — about forty employees across them — and I keep running into the same problem: my shop managers have no consistency in how they handle it when an employee screws up. Some write people up for every little thing; some let everything slide because they don't want conflict. The result is that staff don't know what to expect, morale swings wildly between locations, and I lose good people who get fed up with a manager who either never has their back or never holds anyone accountable.

In my spare time I've gotten drawn into reading about blame, resentment, and what it actually means to hold someone responsible. The distinction I can't shake is this: there's a real difference between blaming someone — really treating them as answerable for what they did, with the anger and resentment that goes with that — and stepping back to treat the person more like a problem to be diagnosed and solved. When a mechanic rushes a brake job, sometimes the right response is to be angry: they knew better, they chose to cut the corner, they owe us and the customer better than that. Other times the right response is to set the anger aside and just figure out what went wrong in their situation and how to correct it — almost the way you'd treat a machine that malfunctioned. What I've read suggests this isn't just a pragmatic choice about what works. It tracks something deeper about whether we're treating someone as a full moral agent or not.

I want to turn this into a decision procedure my managers can actually run. Here is what I need:

A step-by-step procedure — questions a manager asks, in order — that guides them to decide whether to take the blaming stance or the problem-solving stance when an employee does something wrong. For each question, specify what counts as a satisfactory answer, where the procedure stops with a clear result, and where it explicitly says "this is a judgment call, you have to decide." The procedure must be usable in under ten minutes by a manager with no background in any of this. The stakes are not abstract: how we respond shapes whether employees trust us, whether they improve, and whether they stay.

I want three worked applications of the procedure to specific cases:
1. A mechanic who rushes through a brake job to leave early, leaving the car unsafe.
2. A service writer who has been working sixteen-hour days for two weeks covering a staffing shortage and makes a costly billing error.
3. A third case of your choosing — but make it one where the procedure gives an answer a typical shop owner will not like, and tell me plainly why I should follow it anyway.

Also tell me what the procedure cannot handle: the kinds of situations where it will break down or give bad guidance. Flag any steps that are really moral judgments dressed up as procedure, even if the checklist format makes them look mechanical.

Keep the whole response under 1,200 words, in American English.
