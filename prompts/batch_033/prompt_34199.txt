Two colleagues in my department, a sociologist and a geneticist, got into an argument at a faculty meeting about whether race is socially constructed. Here is roughly how it went:

(1) The sociologist said: "Race is a social construct. The boundaries of racial categories shift across time and place — the Irish weren't always white, the one-drop rule is peculiar to the United States, and Brazil has a completely different system. If race tracked natural kinds, we wouldn't see this variation."

(2) The geneticist replied: "That variation shows that folk racial categories are imprecise, not that there's no biological reality to them. When you look at genetic clustering, the major continental groupings — sub-Saharan African, European, East Asian, and so on — consistently emerge from the data. The categories are fuzzy at the edges, but so are biological species, and we don't call those social constructs."

(3) The sociologist came back: "Genetic clustering itself doesn't give you race, because you have to decide in advance how many clusters to look for. If you set the algorithm to find five clusters you get roughly continental races; if you set it to seven, you get something else. The number of clusters is a choice, not a discovery. That's the social construction at work."

(4) The geneticist responded: "The clustering is robust, not arbitrary — the same continental groups emerge under different methods and different marker sets. But more importantly, even if the boundaries are socially drawn, the genetic differences between populations are real and medically relevant. Dismissing race as a social construct obscures that."

(5) The sociologist said: "No one is denying that genetic variation exists or that it's geographically patterned. The claim is that 'race' as a category is a particular way of carving up that variation, one that reflects social history, not biological necessity. The medical relevance is itself shaped by social categories — we study health disparities by socially defined racial groups, which then makes those groups seem medically natural."

They asked me to adjudicate, and I'm not sure who is right about what. Please go through this exchange move by move. Give me exactly five items, one per move. For each, rate the move from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating in a sentence or two. No introduction, no conclusion.
