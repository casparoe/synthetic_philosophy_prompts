I've been going back and forth on this argument for weeks and I feel like I'm losing my grip. It's about whether coming into existence can ever be a net benefit for the person who comes into existence. The core move is an asymmetry between pleasure and pain when we compare existing to never having existed. Here's how my friend laid it out for me:

(1) The presence of pain in a life is bad for the person who experiences it.
(2) The presence of pleasure in a life is good for the person who experiences it.
(3) When someone never exists, the pain they would have experienced is absent — and this absence of pain is good. (Not good *for* anyone in particular, but good in an impersonal sense.)
(4) When someone never exists, the pleasure they would have experienced is absent — but this absence of pleasure is *not* bad. Because there's no one around to be deprived of it; you need an existing subject for a deprivation to count as bad.
(5) So when we weigh existence against non-existence, the nonexistent person gets a "credit" for all the pain they avoided, but no "debit" for all the pleasure they missed. The ledger is rigged.
(6) Therefore, for any possible life — even one that's mostly happy — non-existence still comes out ahead on balance. The asymmetry guarantees it.
(7) So bringing someone into existence always harms them, and we have at least a pro tanto moral reason not to do it.

The conclusion is obviously nuts. If it's right, every parent who ever had a child did something wrong, and the whole human project is one long moral catastrophe. But I can't find the bad step. If I reject (3), I seem to be saying that the absence of pain doesn't matter — which would mean there's no reason to prevent future suffering, and that can't be right. If I reject (4), I seem to be saying we should grieve the nonexistence of every merely possible happy person, which is absurd in the other direction. If I try to deny the asymmetry itself, I need to explain why pain and pleasure are symmetric almost everywhere else in ethics but suddenly come apart exactly here, at the boundary of existence.

I've heard this is basically Benatar's argument from *Better Never to Have Been*, but I haven't read the book — my friend walked me through it and now neither of us can find the exit. Which premise do you reject, and what does rejecting it commit you to? And please say how confident you are in your diagnosis, and single out the point you're least sure about.
