Question: Explain the Epicurean argument that death is not bad for the person who dies, and evaluate one major objection to it.

Student's answer:

Epicurus argues that death cannot be bad for the person who dies because something can only be bad for you if you experience it. When you are dead, you do not experience anything, so death cannot be bad for you. The famous formulation of this is that when death is present, I am not, and when I am present, death is not — so the two never meet, and there is no subject to undergo the harm when death arrives. Lucretius later added a symmetry argument: we do not think our prenatal nonexistence was bad for us, so we should be consistent and not think our posthumous nonexistence is bad for us either.

The most influential objection to Epicurus is the deprivation account, developed by Thomas Nagel. Nagel argues that death is bad for you not because it involves any painful experience, but because it deprives you of the goods you would have enjoyed had you continued living. He compares this to being betrayed behind your back: the betrayal harms you even if you never find out about it, because your life goes worse than it would have otherwise. The deprivation account therefore shows that Epicurus's experience requirement is too demanding — we can be harmed by things we never consciously experience.

I think the deprivation account is largely successful. It shows that death can be bad for you at the moment it occurs, because that is the moment at which you lose all your future goods. The main weakness of the deprivation account is that it doesn't explain why we fear death but not prenatal nonexistence, since we were also deprived of goods before we were born. But Lucretius's symmetry argument already challenges our asymmetric attitudes, so this may not be a weakness so much as a feature.
