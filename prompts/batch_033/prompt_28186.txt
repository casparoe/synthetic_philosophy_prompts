In my early-modern political thought seminar someone shut down a debate by quoting Pascal: "Justice without might is helpless; might without justice is tyrannical," then the bit about how, being unable to make the just strong, we made the strong just. He concluded "so law is just force with a costume on." I've seen this line used to support three different conclusions: that all legal authority is ultimately coercion, that we should stop moralizing about politics, and that the only real question is how to make justice strong. I want something precise to bring to office hours. Lay out the distinct claims that one-liner (plus the "made the strong just" punchline) could be making, from banal to radical. For each reading say whether it's a claim about what is, what can be, or what should be; whether it's trivial, substantive and defensible, or just false; and which readings, if any, actually liscence the "force with a costume" conclusion and which ones need an extra premise to get there. End with a short note saying what would change your mind about which reading Pascal most likely meant, or where your interpretation is most likely to fail. Then, after the prose, give me a table with columns: Reading (one-line label); Precise claim (the exact thesis in a sentence); Is / Can / Ought (descriptive, feasibility, or normative); Verdict (trivial / substantive-defensible / false); Supports "force with a costume"? (yes / no / only with an extra premise); Extra premise needed (what it is, if any). Keep the whole thing under about 1,200 words.
