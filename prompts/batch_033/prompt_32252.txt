I've been teaching public reason liberalism in my political philosophy course for a few years now, and I've gotten pretty convinced by the core idea: when we're debating constitutional essentials and matters of basic justice, we should only appeal to reasons that are in principle shareable by all reasonable citizens. That means religious arguments that depend on revealed truths or the authority of a particular scripture are out — not because they're false, but because they're not the kind of thing you can reasonably expect your fellow citizens to accept as a reason. I know Rawls says this better than I just did, but that's the gist.

Anyway, I want to know what would actually count against this view. I don't mean the standard objections I've already heard a hundred times — that it's unfair to religious citizens, that it asks them to "privatize" their deepest commitments, that there's no neutral way to draw the line between public and nonpublic reasons. I know those. What I want is a map of where the view is genuinely exposed: what empirical findings, what conceptual arguments, what developments in neighboring debates would have to go a certain way for the view to be in trouble. Which of these would a committed public reason liberal concede as counting against them, and which would they dismiss as missing the point? Are there things that have already happened in the literature that the view hasn't adequately dealt with?

I also want to know whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability. I can feel myself doing this sometimes — when a counterexample comes up, I find myself saying "well, that's not really a constitutional essential," or "that's not really a reason, it's a background belief." Is that a problem? Or is this the kind of view where some insulation is fine because it's not making empirical predictions?

And here's the thing: what would actually change your mind about this? If you're the one answering, I want to know which argument or consideration would lead you to a different verdict, and where you think your own reasoning is most likely to fail. None of this "here are the arguments on both sides" fence-sitting — I want to know what you'd need to see to switch.
