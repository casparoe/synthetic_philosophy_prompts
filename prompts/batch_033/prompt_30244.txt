Animal welfare law in most jurisdictions draws its line at vertebrates: a dog may not be subjected to gratuitous pain, but an insect may be killed, farmed, or used in ways that would be prosecutable if inflicted on a vertebrate, and the law does not so much as ask whether it suffers. A few jurisdictions have recently extended recognition to cephalopods and decapod crustaceans, but insects remain wholly outside the law's concern. Taken at face value, the practice seems to presuppose a view about where sentience or moral considerability begins — perhaps that insects are not sentient, or that their sentience is too improbable to matter. Now consider the scale argument: even if the probability that any individual insect is sentient is low, the number of insects alive at any one time is so vast — many orders of magnitude greater than the human population — that the expected number of sentient individuals among them may dwarf the expected number of sentient humans and farmed vertebrates combined. Reconstruct the implicit theory of moral status that this exclusionary practice presupposes, distinguishing what it strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say which rival views of moral status it quietly rules out. In particular, assess whether the practice is best read as resting on a factual claim that insects are not sentient, a normative claim that their suffering does not count, or an epistemic claim that a low probability of sentience entitles us to ignore them. Then say whether the scale argument, taken seriously, undermines the practice, or whether the practice can absorb the argument by rejecting expected-value reasoning about moral status in favour of a threshold or precautionary view. Finally, set out what an animal welfare regime built on expected sentience rather than a probability threshold would have to change in practice, and indicate where such a regime would be philosophically underdetermined.
