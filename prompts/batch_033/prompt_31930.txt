I'm a PhD student in political theory and I've been going down a formal social choice rabbit hole. Lately I've found myself defending the epistemic case for majority rule on the back of the Condorcet jury theorem: roughly, if each voter is more likely than not to get the right answer on a binary common-interest question, and their votes are independent conditional on the right answer, then a majority of a sufficiently large group is almost certain to get it right, and that's supposed to underwrite democracy against epistocratic alternatives. I keep using this as my go-to move, but I've got a nagging sense that the assumptions are doing more work than I'd like to admit, and I dont have a good sense of which failures actually damage the argument.

So take the position that the Condorcet jury theorem gives a real, non-question-begging epistemic justification for majority rule. What would count against it? I know the standard list — independence, competence above .5, common interest, sincere voting, two alternatives — but I want more than just the observation that the assumptions are unrealistic. I want a map of the view's exposure: what would have to be true, conceptually or empirically, for the argument to fail as a justification of majority rule. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and for each say whether a committed defender would concede it as counting against the view or dismiss it as changing the subject. I also want a sense of which escape routes are live rather than merely logically possible, and whether the position has been insulated into unfalsifiability by treating the theorem as a pure idealization — and if it has, whether that's a problem for the kind of justificatory work its being asked to do. And if you can point to the kind of observations that would actually move the dial — about correlations among voters, about the distribution of competence, about strategic incentives — that would be the most useful thing for me.
