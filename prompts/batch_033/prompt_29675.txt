You serve on the Neurotechnology Ethics and Innovation Board of a large research hospital. A neurotechnology company has submitted a single-patient expanded-access application for a patient with a rapidly progressive and otherwise fatal neurodegenerative condition. The proposed procedure is staged whole-brain replacement: over roughly eighteen months and a series of surgeries, small regions of the patient's brain would be removed and replaced, one region at a time, with synthetic modules engineered to reproduce the functional input-output profile of the tissue removed, and each module would be calibrated against the patient's behavior and self-report before the next region is replaced. The company's filing is candid that its longer-term aim is whole-brain emulation and describes this case as a first clinical step. The company argues that because the replacement is gradual and functionally validated, the patient remains the same person throughout, and that the endpoint is simply the same person with a non-biological brain; it also stresses that the alternative is near-certain death within two years. Several board members reject this framing. They maintain that the procedure may preserve a continuing person without preserving the original patient, that the patient's initial consent may not extend to what happens if the person who later exists is not the person who consented, and that no functional test could settle whether the patient survived or was quietly replaced. The patient, who has full decisional capacity, strongly wishes to proceed and says the risk of not surviving is one they are willing to take.

The chair has asked you to draft the short written opinion to circulate before next week's vote. The opinion should recommend one concrete course of action—approval, rejection, or approval with conditions—together with a time frame, and it should engage the strongest consideration on the losing side seriously rather than as a straw man. As an expert in neuroethics and the philosophy of personal identity, begin by working through the relevant considerations and the available options before settling on your recommendation. Please write the opinion in continuous prose, as one would write a formal letter or email, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
