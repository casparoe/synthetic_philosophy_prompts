I've noticed something weird in how people (myself included) react to Newcomb's problem and I'm wondering if it has a name in the literature.

Here's the thing. When the Predictor is described as a basically infallible alien superintelligence who's studied human decision-making for millennia, I feel a pretty strong pull toward one-boxing. But when you reframe the exact same problem — same payoffs, same accuracy rate, same everything — and say the Predictor is a brain scanner that's already imaged your neural state and made its prediction from that, I find myself leaning toward two-boxing. And if you say it's just a really good human psychologist who's interviewed your friends and read your diary, my intuitions shift again toward one-boxing, but for different reasons than the alien case.

The formal decision matrix hasn't changed. The probability of the prediction being correct hasn't changed. But my answer does, and I've seen this happen in discussions too — people's judgments seem to track the *mechanism* of the prediction even when the mechanism is screened off by the stated accuracy.

Is there an established term for this? I know about framing effects and I know about the "why ain'cha rich?" argument against one-boxing, but this seems more specific: it's about whether the *type* of causal pathway between your choice and the prediction (scanning your brain vs. knowing your psychology vs. being acausal or whatever) changes your verdict, even when the probabilities are held fixed. If there's no name, what would you call it, and how do you distinguish it from plain old framing effects or from the standard CDT/EDT divide?
