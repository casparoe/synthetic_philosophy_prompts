I am the student representative on my high school's Honor Council, and I need to circulate a short written opinion before our meeting this Thursday. Please act as a philosopher of law who is experienced at turning disputes in legal theory into documents a non-specialist committee can actually act on. Before drafting, think carefully and brainstorm the strongest arguments on each side, locating exactly where the procedural issue and the fairness issue each do their work.

The case. Over the summer the administration inserted into the student handbook a new academic-integrity rule: any use of an AI tool (such as ChatGPT) to "generate, outline, or substantially revise" any part of a graded assignment is a first-offense violation carrying a semester-long suspension and a permanent notation on the transcript. The handbook requires changes to academic-integrity policy to pass a Faculty Senate vote and a two-week public comment period with the Student Government. Neither step occurred; the administration says the rule was posted in the handbook before the first day of school and has been in force since. A junior, M., used an AI tool to outline an essay but wrote the essay herself. She was reported, and the charge is now before us. M. argues (1) that the rule is not a real rule because it was never properly enacted, and (2) that the penalty is so disproportionate to what she did that no fair body should apply it. Some Council members add that "substantially revise" is too vague to give fair notice of what is prohibited.

The Council is split. Some say our only job is to apply the rules as written and leave questions of validity or fairness to the administration. Others say an improperly enacted or grossly unfair rule is no rule at all, and that enforcing it would make us complicit. Still others accept that the rule is binding but want us to recommend a reduced penalty on fairness grounds. I suspect the underlying issue is the one we touched on in Philosophy Club as the Hart–Fuller debate, or legal positivism versus natural law: whether a rule's validity is one question and its fairness another, or whether a sufficiently defective or unjust rule stops being a rule at all. The memo itself, though, needs to be usable by students who have never heard those terms.

Please write the memo with these sections, in this order and roughly these lengths:

- Recommendation (80–120 words): the concrete course of action and time frame the Council should vote on.
- Background and the question before us (100–150 words).
- The case for treating the rule as binding and enforcing it as written (150–200 words).
- The case against treating the rule as binding, or at least against applying its penalty (150–200 words).
- Analysis: separating the procedural-validity question from the proportionality question (200–250 words).
- Settled and contested ground (100–150 words): among people who work on this, what is not seriously disputed and what remains genuinely contested; please do not present one side of the positivism/natural-law debate as the consensus.
- Conditions and next steps (100–150 words): what the Council should do if the administration rejects our reasoning, and what follow-up, if any, should accompany the decision.

Keep the total around 900–1,100 words and the tone formal but concrete, appropriate for a school document.
