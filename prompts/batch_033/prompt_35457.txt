I am a PhD student in philosophy of science, and my current work concerns the metaphysics and epistemology of causal explanation. Over the past year I have moved away from more traditional counterfactual theories of causation and have come to defend an interventionist account in the spirit of Woodward's work. The view, as I hold it, is that causal relations are relations that are invariant under an appropriate range of interventions, where an intervention is an exogenous causal process that overrides the other causes of a variable and sets that variable independently of its normal causal history. I take it that a claim of the form 'X causes Y' is not made true simply by what actually happened, nor by bare counterfactual dependence of one event on another, but by the existence of a generalisation linking X and Y that is stable across some relevant range of possible manipulations of X. The account earns its keep, I think, by making sense of common-cause cases, by explaining why controlled experiments are informative, and by giving causal talk in the special sciences a principled basis.

I would like you to tell me what would count against this position. I want a map of its exposure rather than a recital of the standard objections: for each would-be defeater, I want to know whether it is fatal, serious, or merely awkward, and whether a committed interventionist would themselves concede that it counts against the view or would dismiss it as an artefact of a particular formalisation. The sorts of things I have in mind include, but need not be limited to: pre-emption and overdetermination; omissions and double prevention; the charge that 'intervention' is itself a causal notion and therefore circular; the problem of selecting variables, contrast classes, and ranges of invariance; the question whether the view can say anything determinate about singular causation; and the question whether there are domains — fundamental physics, biology, or mental causation, for instance — in which the interventionist framework fits poorly. I would also like an honest assessment of whether the view, as it has been refined, risks anything at all, or whether it has been qualified to the point of unfalsifiability — and, if it has, whether that should worry someone who holds a theory of causation of this kind. The considerations I would personally find most persuasive are those showing that the view cannot do the work it was introduced to do in a domain where it ought to be at home, rather than purely logical counterexamples. Please write your answer in British English.
