I'm preparing a paper for a philosophy of mind seminar on mental causation, and I've been trying to get my commitments straight before I start writing. The seminar has been working through Hofstadter's careenium thought experiment — the frictionless arena of bouncing balls where certain stable configurations function as simmballs (symbol-bearing clusters that refer to things, combine into larger structures, and exhibit a kind of intentionality), and the central question is whether these higher-level symbolic patterns genuinely push the physical substrate around or merely ride on top of it as epiphenomenal shadows. I find myself pulled in several directions at once, and I'd like an audit of whether my views form a coherent package. Here is where I've landed:

(1) I'm a physicalist. There are no non-physical substances, properties, or forces — everything that exists is either physical or constituted by the physical.

(2) The physical domain is causally closed: every physical event has a sufficient physical cause at the microphysical level. If you had a complete microphysical description of a system, you would not need to appeal to anything outside that description to account for its physical evolution over time.

(3) Mental causation is real and not merely apparent. When I decide to raise my arm because I want to hail a taxi, the belief–desire pair genuinely brings about the arm movement. This is not just a useful fiction, a stance, or a façon de parler.

(4) The careenium gets something importantly right about how mental causation works. The simmballs are not epiphenomenal — the high-level symbolic patterns exert a genuine downward influence on the trajectories of the individual balls, and this serves as a good toy model for how thoughts influence the neural substrate in real brains.

(5) Mental states are multiply realizable. The same belief type — say, the belief that it is raining — can be realized by radically different physical state types across species, across individuals, or even within the same individual over time. Type identity between mental kinds and physical kinds is too strong.

(6) The special sciences — psychology, economics, biology — track real, objective patterns that are explanatorily autonomous from physics. You cannot replace a psychological explanation with a neural one, even in principle, without losing something essential to the explanation.

I can feel that there are tensions in this set, but I am not confident I have located all of them, and I suspect that at least one pair that looks problematic on a first pass turns out to be fine on closer inspection. What I would like is a systematic audit. Please go through these six commitments and identify every genuine tension, rank them by severity, and for each say what would have to give to resolve it. Also flag any pair that might seem to conflict but does not, and explain why the apparent tension dissolves. Finally, tell me which single revision to my set would do the most to restore overall coherence, and what that revision costs me philosophically — that is, what attractive thesis I would have to abandon or weaken.
