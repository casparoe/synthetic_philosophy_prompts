I spent forty years in a lab where you change one variable at a time and see what happens, so I'm finding this maddening. I've been reading around in behavioural economics and political philosophy, and I keep coming back to the same problem: I can't figure out what principle actually seperates the nudges I'm fine with from the ones that bug me. I thought listing my gut reactions to a bunch of cases might help, and I'd like someone who knows the literature to tell me what principle (if any) captures them, and which of my verdicts I'd have to give up if I wanted a clean rule.

Here are the cases and my verdicts:

1. The government requires chain restaurants to print calorie counts next to each menu item. Fine.
2. My employer auto-enrols me in the pension scheme at 5% of salary; I can opt out anytime with a one-page form. Fine.
3. The works canteen puts the salad bar by the entrance and puddings round the corner past the till. Fine.
4. Cigarette packs carry photographs of tumours and diseased lungs. Fine.
5. A public health campaign runs posters saying '8 in 10 people in your postcode have quit smoking'—but the real number is just under 6 in 10. Not fine.
6. New York tried to ban large sugary drinks. Not fine.
7. My phone switches to greyscale at 10pm; I can override it but it takes four taps. Fine.
8. The council puts a painted housefly on the porcelain of urinals to reduce, well, splash. Fine.
9. A supermarket places fruit at eye level and moves the chocolate biscuits to the bottom shelf where you have to bend down. Fine.
10. My energy company sends me a letter saying 'your neighbours use 15% less gas than you do,' with a smiley face next to my name because they're trying to nudge me to turn the thermostat down. Fine, I suppose, though I find it a bit creepy.
11. A social media platform makes the 'delete account' button three menus deep, greyed out in tiny text, while the 'deactivate for now' button is bright and prominent. Not fine.
12. My GP practice auto-enrols me in a 'text reminders for appointments' service; I can opt out but only by phoning a number that plays hold music. Fine, but barely.

Now, cases 3 and 11 both involve fiddling with the choice architecture to make one option harder to reach. Why do I give them opposite verdicts? And case 5 and case 10 both involve invoking social norms—one I reject, one I accept grudgingly. Is there a principle that gets all twelve right? If not, which verdicts should I overturn, and what would the resulting principle say about a case I haven't listed: a government that makes organ donation opt-out rather than opt-in?
