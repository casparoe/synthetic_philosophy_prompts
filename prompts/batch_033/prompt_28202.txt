I'm part of a reading group that's been working through Turing's 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence." We've been struck by how much of his vision for machine intelligence centers on what he called the child machine — a system with minimal initial structure that acquires competence through a process he explicitly analogized to human education, complete with rewards, punishments, and a curriculum administered by a teacher.

Last week someone raised the obvious question: what would Turing have made of large language models? On the one hand, LLMs seem to realize something like his program: they aren't hand-coded with adult-level competence but acquire it through a kind of learning. On the other hand, the details diverge sharply. Turing emphasized the importance of the machine having something like a sensory-motor interface with the world — he talked about giving it sensory organs and letting it roam and interact with its environment — whereas LLMs are trained purely on text. He stressed the role of a teacher who structures the machine's experience and administers something like rewards and punishments; LLM pretraining is self-supervised with no teacher in his sense, though RLHF reintroduces something like human feedback. And he was deeply interested in the machine's capacity for initiative, randomness, and something like curiosity — he wanted it to sometimes depart from its instructions to explore — which raises questions about how he'd view the essentially imitative character of next-token prediction.

I'm trying to write a short essay that reconstructs, as carefully as possible, what Turing would likely have thought about modern LLMs if he could see them. I don't want a surface-level "he'd be amazed" take. I want to go through the specific commitments in his writings on learning machines and work out, point by point, where LLMs align with his vision, where they depart from it, and what he'd likely identify as the most important gaps. In particular, I'm interested in whether he'd see RLHF as a genuine implementation of his teacher-and-curriculum idea, whether he'd consider the lack of embodiment a temporary shortcut or a fundamental mistake, and what he'd make of the fact that LLMs can pass his own imitation game but arguably through a route he didn't anticipate.
