I've been trying to get clear on when I think an omission is a cause of something, as opposed to just a failure to prevent it, and I'm realizing my gut verdicts don't line up neatly. Here they are.

A lifeguard on duty, up in the chair, watching a swimmer struggle and doing nothing: I say his not jumping in caused the death. A tourist standing right next to him who also does nothing: I don't say the tourist caused the death, even though if either had acted the swimmer would have lived. A parent who doesn't feed their infant: caused the death. A doctor who doesn't give a clearly indicated antibiotic to a septic patient: caused the death. A mechanic who doesn't tighten a brake bolt he was paid to tighten: caused the crash. A drug company that learns its pill increases stroke risk but doesn't update the label: I say the company caused the strokes, or at least the excess strokes.

So far it looks like the principle is "you caused it if you had a duty to act." But then: I dig a hole in the sidewalk in front of my house, don't cover it or put up a light, and someone falls in at night and breaks a leg. I don't have any official duty, but I'd still say I caused the injury by not covering the hole. And a friend who promises to water my plants while I'm away and forgets: I say their not watering caused the plants to die, though that feels less like causing a death and more like breaking a promise — still, I do say caused. But a neighbor who never promised and just didn't bother: I don't say they caused the plants to die.

The pair I can't stop going back and forth on: an off-duty lifeguard, fully trained, sits on the same beach and watches someone drown. No job, no special relationship. I want to say she didn't cause the death, same as the tourist. But she could of saved the person just as easily as the on-duty guard, and she knows she could. It feels wrong that the only difference is a paycheck and a red chair.

Is there a principle that captures my verdicts, or most of them? And what does it say about some cases I haven't listed — like someone who forgets to replace a dead smoke alarm battery in their own home and a guest dies in a fire, or a stranger who sees a child drowning in a shallow pond and does nothing? Which of my verdicts does your principle force me to give up?

When you answer, tell me where your reasoning is most likely to fail and what argument or case would change your mind about the principle. American English please.
