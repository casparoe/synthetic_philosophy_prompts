I am putting together a handout for an upper-level metaphysics seminar, and I want to replace the standard summarize-the-main-answers-to-the-special-composition-question exercise with a decision tree that forces students to discover their position from their judgments about cases rather than from the label they like. Please write a decision tree that takes a reader from the opening question of whether any composite objects exist at all to a named position on when composition occurs. Every internal node should be a question about a concrete case or a sharp, non-theoretical claim, phrased so that a reader who has never encountered the terms nihilism, universalism, or organicism can answer it without knowing which position the answer points toward. Use at least these cases somewhere in the tree: a living cat, a heap of sand, a water molecule, a severed hand, and two people holding hands. The leaves must include nihilism, universalism, organicism, and at least one further moderate position, such as a contact view or a fastenation view, and each leaf should carry a one-sentence statement of that position's most awkward commitment.

Build the tree from the logical space of answers rather than from a list of names: first lay out the diagnostic questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then locate the named positions among those combinations. In particular, wherever a moderate position has to draw a sharp line across a gradual series, the relevant node should present the series concretely, such as a heap losing grains one by one or two objects being slowly fused, rather than asking the reader to state a general criterion. For every combination that is logically allowed but unoccupied by a named position, say briefly whether it is empty because the view would be incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Finally, flag any pair of leaves that your tree cannot separate, and specify one further case that would force them apart. The handout should not include any reading lists or citations; I want the instrument to test a student's grip on the positions through cases alone, not their memory of the literature.
