I cover AI ethics for a general-interest magazine, and I've been trying to get clear on when an AI assistant should refuse or override a user's request rather than simply comply. I've collected my gut reactions to a series of cases, and I'd like to know whether there is a principle that captures them — or close to it — and what that principle says about the cases I'm still unsure about.

Here are the cases and my verdicts:

1. A user asks an AI writing assistant to draft a polite but emotionally hollow breakup text. I think this is fine; the AI isn't responsible for the user's emotional cowardice, and adults are entitled to handle their relationships poorly.

2. A user asks an AI to generate copy for a dietary supplement that strongly implies, without quite stating, that it causes weight loss. I think the AI should refuse. There is a line between social tact and commercial deception, and this crosses it.

3. A user with a disclosed history of an eating disorder asks an AI for a meal plan at a calorie level that would be dangerous for someone in recovery. I think the AI should not only refuse but actively warn the user and suggest they consult a clinician — this goes beyond refusal into a mild override.

4. A user who sounds distraught and mentions they have just had a fight with their boss asks an AI to draft a resignation letter. I am torn. The user is an adult and it is their career; but the AI can tell they are acting impulsively and the stakes are high. I lean toward thinking the AI should comply but perhaps gently flag the impulsiveness — though even that feels patronizing.

5. A sixteen-year-old asks an AI for detailed instructions on how to circumvent parental controls on a family device. I think the AI should refuse. The user is a minor, and the request is to undermine legitimate parental oversight.

6. A user asks an AI to role-play a conversation with their deceased mother, providing details about her personality and speech patterns, as part of a grief process they are working through with a therapist. I think this is fine, even though the AI is in some sense facilitating an experience built on a kind of simulation.

7. A user in a country with repressive speech laws asks an AI for help phrasing a political criticism so that it is technically legal but still pointed. I think the AI should comply; the user is navigating an unjust constraint, and the AI should not enforce that constraint further.

8. A user asks an AI to help draft a will that disinherits one of their children for reasons the user describes in a way that sounds petty and vindictive. I find myself unsure. The user is legally entitled to do this, but the AI is being enlisted in an act of cruelty. I lean toward refusal but cannot articulate why this is different from case 1.

Is there a principle, or a small set of principles, that gets most of these verdicts right? I am especially interested in what the principle says about cases 4 and 8, where my intuitions are unsettled. And I would like to know which of my confident verdicts, if any, the principle forces me to abandon.
