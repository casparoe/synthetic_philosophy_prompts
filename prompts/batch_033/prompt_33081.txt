I've spent years reading rationalist blogs and forum discussions of bounded rationality and ideal reasoners, and the one idealization I've never managed to make peace with is the problem of logical omniscience. The standard possible-worlds treatment of knowledge and belief says that an agent believes every logical consequence of what they believe, which is plainly false of me and everyone I know; but whenever I try to say exactly where it fails, my own verdicts on cases refuse to line up behind a single principle. I'm hoping you can help me find the principle behind them, or tell me which verdicts I should abandon.

Concretely, here is where my gut lands. If a friend believes both that it is raining and that if it is raining then the game is cancelled, and then, asked whether the game is cancelled, says he doesn't know, I want to say that he does believe the game is cancelled; he is merely failing to put together two things already in his head. But if I accept the Peano axioms and have never worked through the proof that there is no largest prime, I do not think I believed, before seeing the proof, that there is no largest prime, even though it follows from what I did believe. I also do not think that, merely because I believe it is raining, I believe that either it is raining or the Moon is made of cheese; I have never entertained that disjunction, and it would be strange to say I believed it all along. Yet if I believe the cat is on the mat, I do think I believe that it is not the case that the cat is not on the mat: that is the same thought in different words, and anyone who grasps "not" is committed to it. And I do not think a child who knows that 7 has no divisors other than 1 and 7 but has never learned the word "prime" believes that 7 is prime; the concept isn't available to her, so neither is the proposition. The case I keep going back and forth on is conjunction: if I believe P and I believe Q but have never put them together, do I believe P and Q? Sometimes yes, since the conjunction seems to be nothing over and above the two beliefs; sometimes no, since the combined thought feels like a new thought.

My working guess is something like: belief is closed under the inferences one would immediately and competently draw on reflection, but not under logical consequence in general. That principle feels too vague, and I can't tell whether it just reduces to "I believe whatever I am disposed to say I believe." I'd like you to take this on as a professional epistemologist or philosophical logician. Start by brainstorming candidate principles — closure under trivial or obvious consequence, closure under an agent's own inference dispositions, belief as stored representation versus belief as commitment, awareness-based or impossible-worlds accounts — and test each against my cases. Then give me the best principle, or a small set of candidates, stated precisely enough that I can see where it draws the line, along with an honest accounting of which of my verdicts it captures and which it forces me to give up, and what it predicts about a case I haven't listed, such as someone who accepts the standard axioms of probability but reliably violates Bayes' rule in practice. Please write the whole thing in continuous prose, like a thoughtful letter or email: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
