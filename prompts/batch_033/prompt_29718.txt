I'm a retired chemist who's only recently gotten into philosophy, so I may be missing some of the technical vocabulary, but here's something that's been bugging me. In the AI alignment world the arguement about process-based versus outcome-based supervision of reasoning models has been running for years now, and from where I sit nobody has moved an inch. The process camp says you should reward each step of the model's chain of thought, because a final answer can be right for the wrong reasons and a good intermediate step can sit inside an ultimately wrong answer; if you only ever look at the final output, you're blind to most of what matters about reasoning. The outcome camp says that's hopeless: step-level labels are themselves produced by another model or by human judges who are unreliable, so you're just pushing the supervision problem one level down, while the final answer is the only thing you can actually check. Both sides have empirical wins and failures, and each seems to think the other is solving a different problem. My working theory is that this isn't really an empirical disagreement at all, but a disagreement about what supervision is for — shaping the model's internal reasoning versus producing outputs that can be checked. Is that diagnosis right, or is there a hidden factual question here (say, about which kind of reward signal generalizes better to genuinely novel problems) that could settle it? And if my diagnosis is on the right track, what would actually count as progress — a distinction, an experiment, a change of question — rather than just another round of benchmark comparisons?
