I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of biology — 16 students, one 75-minute session — and I want an in-class game that makes students feel, rather than hear from me, why honest signaling in nature tends to require costs, and why mere costliness is not enough: the cost has to fall more heavily on low-quality signalers for a signal to carry information. The distinction I most want them to leave with is between a signal that is expensive for everyone and a signal that is a genuine handicap because its cost is conditional on the signaler's quality.

Design the game from the ground up. It should run in the time available with only cards or paper slips, use a points payoff scheme rather than real money, and be playable by 16 students split into signalers and receivers. I want at least three rounds so the contrast emerges from their own behavior: a cheap-talk round in which signals cost nothing, a round in which signals carry the same cost for every signaler, and a round in which signal costs depend on the signaler's type in the right way. Set the payoffs so that, if students play self-interestedly, the first two rounds collapse toward dishonest signaling and distrust while the third can sustain separation; keep the payoff units comparable across rounds so the class can compare how much honesty each condition supported. Do not tell them in advance where it is heading; where possible, let them notice the collapse themselves before you name it.

Then give me a debrief that maps what happened in each round onto the concepts: the divergence of interests between signaler and receiver; pooling versus separating equilibria; why a uniformly costly signal merely taxes everyone and can still be sent by low-quality signalers; and why honesty requires differential cost, which is the handicap principle. The debrief should also mark the distinction between index signals, which are honest because the signal's intensity is physically tied to quality and cannot be faked, and handicap signals, which are honest because they are differentially costly to produce, and should say which of these the game does and does not capture.

Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure — collusion, receivers who keep trusting against the incentives, students concluding that any costly display is honest, or the differential-cost rule feeling rigged — and be frank about what the game cannot reproduce, such as the fact that real signaling equilibria are shaped by selection over many generations with receivers coevolving rather than by one-off rational choice.
