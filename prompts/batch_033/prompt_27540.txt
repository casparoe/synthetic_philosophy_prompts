Write a villanelle about the Sphex wasp and the idea of sphexishness. As background: the female wasp paralyzes a cricket, drags it to the entrance of her burrow, leaves it on the threshold, goes inside to check that the burrow is in order, comes back out, and only then drags the cricket in. If someone moves the cricket a few inches away while the wasp is inside, the wasp brings it back to the threshold but not inside, then goes in to check again, and this can be repeated dozens of times; she never simply pulls the cricket straight in. What looks like intelligent, purposeful behavior turns out to be a fixed routine that cannot adapt to a small change in the situation, and the term sphexishness names precisely that gap between apparent flexibility and actual mechanical rigidity.

The form should carry the philosophy. Let the two villanelle refrains be the two fixed steps of the wasp's loop, perhaps one line about leaving the cricket at the threshold and another about going inside to check once more, so that the mechanical return of the refrains enacts the mechanical return of the behavior. In the final couplet, where the refrains at last sit side by side, turn the poem toward the question the example is usually made to raise about us: how much of what we call our own flexibility is only a more elaborate version of the same loop. The poem should be accurate enough that someone who knows the Sphex example and the debates about free will it feeds would recognize it rather than find it a caricature, and good enough that someone who has never heard of the wasp would still find it a genuine poem.
