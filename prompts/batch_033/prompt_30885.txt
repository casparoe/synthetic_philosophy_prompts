I've been going back and forth with a friend in an environmental ethics course about whether habitat destruction might actually reduce wild animal suffering, and he laid out an argument I can't find a way out of, even though the conclusion seems insane. It runs like this. (1) Whether we should preserve a habitat depends on the net welfare of the lives it contains, not on whether it's natural or beautiful. (2) Most wild animals live net-negative lives: the overwhelming majority die before maturity, usually by starvation, predation, disease, or cold, so the average wild life contains much more suffering than pleasure. (3) Other things equal, destroying a habitat means fewer wild animals come into existence there than if the habitat were preserved. (4) If an animal's life would have been net-negative, then its not coming into existence is an improvement. (5) Therefore, other things equal, destroying habitats dominated by net-negative lives is better than preserving them. (6) By the same logic, if a species' members tend to live net-negative lives, then reducing their numbers is good all the way down, and driving the species extinct would be an improvement. (7) But then conservation and rewilding are systematically backwards, and we should welcome extinctions — which no one in the seminar, including my friend, actually believes.

I can feel someting going wrong around the move from individual net-negative lives to habitat destruction and then to extinction, but whenever I try to block it I end up having to say either that animal suffering doesnt matter much or that a net-negative life isnt worth preventing, and I dont want to give up either of those. I'd most like to keep (1) and (2), but I can't accept (7). Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? And I want to know where your diagnosis is most likely to fail: what empirical finding or philosophical argument would most likely change your mind about which step is the bad one?
