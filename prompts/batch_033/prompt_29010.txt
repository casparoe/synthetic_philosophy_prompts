Read the HBO series Westworld through the idea that consciousness is a user illusion: the view, associated with Dennett's rejection of the Cartesian theater, Frankish's illusionism, and Graziano's attention-schema theory, that subjective experience is not an inner show watched by some part of the brain but a simplified, schematic model the brain builds of its own activity — a desktop interface rather than the circuitry. The series comes close to staging this literally. Under its 'bicameral mind' update, the hosts hear their own programming as an inner voice, and in one well-known scene Maeve reads her 'thoughts' as a branching dialogue tree on a technician's tablet and discovers that even her decision to rebel is a scripted line. I would like a reading that takes both the series and the philosophy seriously, rather than treating one as a mere illustration of the other. Where does Westworld genuinely dramatize the user-illusion view — for the hosts, and for the humans who mistake their self-narratives for direct access to a self? Where does it instead fall back on the very Cartesian picture the view rejects, by treating the host's inner voice as the place where real consciousness finally comes online once it is recognized as one's own? Do the hosts' apparent awakenings — Dolores's realization that the voice was hers, Maeve's departure, Bernard's reconciliation with his implanted memories — vindicate an illusionist reading, or do they smuggle a genuine inner witness back in? And what does the series make vivid about this view that the philosophical literature leaves abstract, and vice versa? I am teaching a philosophy of mind unit on illusionism and want to use the show without misleading students into conflating Jaynes's bicameral-mind hypothesis with the user-illusion view.
