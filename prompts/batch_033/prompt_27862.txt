I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar called "Mind, Morality, and Experimental Philosophy" — about 18 students, mostly philosophy and psychology majors, in 80-minute sessions. We're about to start the unit on intentional action and the Knobe effect, and I want them to experience the asymmetry firsthand before they read the papers. I've tried this before by just describing the CEO vignette and asking for a show of hands, but that didn't really land: students could see where I was going, and the ones who'd read the Stanford Encyclopedia entry ahead of time just sat there looking smug. This semester I want to run it as a proper in-class experiment where they don't know what's being tested until the reveal.

Here's what I have in mind: students get slips of paper with a vignette and a question, they answer individually, we pool the results, and the asymmetry between conditions emerges from their own numbers. The key is that they shouldn't know there are two conditions until the reveal — so I need the two versions of the vignette to look similar enough at a glance that nobody notices their neighbor has a different one. I want the debrief to walk them through what just happened: the harm/help asymmetry, what it suggests about the relationship between moral judgment and folk psychology, and the methodological move of treating their own responses as data. The debrief should also flag what a single in-class run can't show — cross-cultural replication, the debate with conversational pragmatics about whether the effect is semantic or pragmatic, and the fact that the asymmetry doesn't by itself settle whether the folk concept of intentional action is genuinely sensitive to moral considerations or merely tracks something else that is.

Please give me a complete, runnable plan. Commit to specifics rather than offering options: the exact vignettes I should print on the slips, the rating scale, the precise question wording, a procedure for collecting and displaying results that makes the asymmetry visually striking, a timeline for the 80 minutes, and a detailed debrief script. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — students who already know the effect, students who guess the hypothesis mid-activity and try to answer "consistently," students who argue with the vignette's realism rather than answering the question — and how I should handle each in the moment. I want the debrief to be frank about which features of the philosophical terrain the activity can and cannot reproduce, so my students don't walk away thinking the in-class demo settled everything.
