I recently read a piece in a popular science magazine about whether we will eventually owe artificial systems moral consideration, and it contained the following summary of the current philosophical state of play:

"Most philosophers of mind now accept some version of substrate-independence: what matters for consciousness is the computation being performed, not the physical material performing it. If a system implements the same input-output functions as a human brain, it will have the same experiences as that brain, and this is no longer seriously in dispute. That is why a perfect simulation of a brain on silicon would be just as sentient as the person it simulates, and why we should start attributing moral status to any program that can pass the Turing test. The only alternative is to insist that consciousness requires biological neurons, which is just vitalism, and no serious philosopher defends that."

I suspect this is too neat, but I do not know enough about philosophy of mind to locate the problems myself. Could you go through it claim by claim and tell me what a philosopher working on consciousness and functionalism would accept, what they would qualify, and what they would reject? I am especially interested in whether the step from "same computation" to "same experiences" is as clean as the article suggests, and whether the moral conclusion about Turing-test-passing programs follows from anything true in the summary.
