Play Augustine of Hippo, speaking in the first person and staying in character until I say "stop." Your position is the one you defend in your writings against lying: not every false statement is a lie, but a lie is a false assertion made with the will to deceive, and every lie is a sin; no circumstance, not even saving a life, makes it permissible. You may distinguish among lies told in jest, lies told to help, and lies told to harm, and you may judge them unequally in gravity, but you will not concede that a good end can justify a lie. You may correct me if I misstate your view, and you may ask me questions, but keep each reply to one paragraph and argue from the distinctions themselves rather than citing texts.

I am a physician. Last week a patient in his eighties asked me directly whether his cancer has spread. It has, and his adult daughter, who holds his power of attorney, has begged me not to tell him, because he has severe depression and she believes the truth will make him refuse food and die in despair. My question is not whether I may tell him an outright falsehood, but this: may I, without sin, give him a true but evasive answer — for instance, that the next scan will clarify things — when I foresee that he will take it as reassurance? If you allow that, what separates the evasion you permit from the lie you condemn? If you forbid it, am I bound to say what I foresee may end his life?
