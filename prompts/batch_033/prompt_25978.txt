"Gettier's 1963 paper 'Is Justified True Belief Knowledge?' is standardly taken to refute the tripartite analysis of knowledge. First, state the tripartite analysis clearly. Then explain the structure of a Gettier-style counterexample, using one of Gettier's own cases for illustration. Here is the student's answer:

"The traditional analysis of knowledge, which traces back at least to Plato's Theaetetus, holds that knowledge is justified true belief. On this JTB analysis, S knows that P if and only if (i) P is true, (ii) S believes that P, and (iii) S is justified in believing that P. Each of the three conditions is individually necessary, and together they are supposed to be jointly sufficient for knowledge.

Gettier's 1963 paper challenges the sufficiency claim. He presents two cases that share a common structure: a subject has a justified belief in a proposition that is in fact false; from this false but justified belief, the subject validly deduces a further proposition; this deduced proposition turns out to be true, but its truth is entirely disconnected from the subject's justification — it is true for reasons the subject knows nothing about. In Gettier's first case, Smith has strong evidence that Jones will get a job (the company president told him) and that Jones has ten coins in his pocket (Smith counted them). Smith deduces that the man who will get the job has ten coins in his pocket. As it happens, Smith himself gets the job, and Smith also has ten coins in his pocket, though he doesn't realize it. So Smith has a justified true belief that the man who will get the job has ten coins in his pocket, but intuitively he does not know this — his belief is true only by luck.

What Gettier's argument shows is that the three JTB conditions, even when jointly satisfied, are not sufficient for knowledge. There remains a gap between justified true belief and knowledge; something further must be added.

Gettier's own diagnosis of the problem is that in both of his cases, the subject's belief rests on a false premise. Smith's belief that the man who will get the job has ten coins in his pocket depends on the false premise that Jones will get the job. This suggests a natural fix: knowledge requires that one's belief not be inferred from any false lemma. The 'no false lemmas' condition adds a fourth requirement: S knows that P only if S's justification for P does not depend on any false proposition.

This response handles Gettier's original cases neatly — in both, the subject's belief does depend on a false lemma. However, one limitation is that the condition may be too strong. It seems possible to have knowledge even when one's chain of reasoning includes a false step, provided that step is inessential. For example, if a detective correctly identifies a culprit using ten pieces of evidence, nine of which are sound and one of which is a minor false assumption that plays no real role in the inference, we might still want to say the detective knows who did it. The no-false-lemmas condition would rule this out, which suggests it overcorrects. Still, it represents a natural and compelling response to the problem Gettier identified."
