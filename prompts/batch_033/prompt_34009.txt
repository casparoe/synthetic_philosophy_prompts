I'm on my school's honor council, the student/faculty board that decides what happens to kids who get cought cheating. We meet like twice a month, we get a short report on what happened plus a written statement from the student, and then we vote on a punishment from a fixed menu: warning, zero on the assignment, grade cap for the course, suspension, expulsion. None of us have any training, we get maybe 20 minutes a case, and the faculty advisor keeps saying we need to be "consistent" but never says consistent with what.

I know there's this whole philosophy debate between retributivism and consequentialism about punishment — retributivism says punish people because they deserve it and the punishment should fit the crime, consequentialism says punish only if it makes things better overall, by deterring people, protecting the school, rehabilitating the cheater, whatever. What I want is for you to turn that into a decision procedure we could actually run at a meeting. Give me the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure says stop, and where it has to hand the decision to a person because it's a values call and not a facts call. Then run the procedure on two cases: (1) a junior copied three sentences from SparkNotes into a short homework essay and got flagged by a plagiarism checker, and (2) a senior paid somebody to take their AP exam for them. Be honest about where retributivism and consequentialism point in different directions, and which one the procedure actually commits us to. Also tell me what the procedure can't do, like what it will systematically get wrong.
