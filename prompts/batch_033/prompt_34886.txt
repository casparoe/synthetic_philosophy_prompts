I've been going in circles on split-brain cases and the unity of consciousness, and I've landed on an argument that seems to force me into a conclusion I can't accept. Maybe you can spot where it goes wrong.

(1) In commissurotomy patients, the disconnected right hemisphere can guide the left hand to pick out an object that the speaking left hemisphere sincerely reports not seeing. This sure looks like two streams of conscious awareness running in parallel inside one skull.

(2) If cutting the corpus callosum splits one subject into two, then what keeps my hemispheres unified into a single subject right now is just the fact that they're chatting across the callosum.

(3) But that cross-talk isn't binary — it varies continuously with arousal, task demands, and even across individuals. And split-brain patients still have subcortical connections; they're not fully disconnected.

(4) If the unity-making factor is continuous, then the number of subjects in a skull isn't an all-or-nothing matter either. It would be vague whether someone is one subject or two.

(5) But it can't be vague whether I'm one subject. I either am or I'm not, and I'm pretty sure I am.

(6) So something in (1)–(4) has to give. But (1) is the empirical consensus, (2) seems like the obvious inference from (1), (3) is just how brains work, and (4) follows from (2) and (3). So I'm stuck.

Where's the mistake? And whatever I give up, what else does that cost me?
