I direct the grants program at a private foundation that funds work aimed at reducing large-scale catastrophic risks and improving the long-term future. Every quarter we receive a handful of proposals whose case is an expected-value argument of a distinctive shape: the proponents assign a very small probability to an enormous benefit — a one-in-a-million chance of averting a global catastrophe, a one-in-a-billion chance of unlocking a technology that would transform the economy — and then argue that the product of probability and benefit is so large that the expected value of funding dwarfs that of our ordinary grants. My program officers have to triage these proposals before they reach the board. They are not decision theorists; each initial screen gets about two hours; we cannot settle the underlying empirical claims at our level; and the board must sign off on every seven-figure grant. I want a decision procedure, grounded in the decision-theoretic problem known as Pascal's mugging and in what is known about the limits of expected-value reasoning under tiny probabilities of unbounded payoffs — including uncertainty about the probability estimate itself, the role of bounded utility, and the difference between a well-defined expected value and one dominated by an unverifiable tail — that a nonspecialist program officer can actually run. The procedure should say which questions to ask and in what order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values or of unresolved empirical judgment rather than procedure. It should distinguish, in operational terms, between an expected-value argument that deserves to move us and one that is a Pascal's mugging, and it should be honest about where that distinction cannot be drawn cleanly. Please write it as a checklist or a flow of questions that a person can execute in two hours, flag the steps where judgment cannot be eliminated, and say how the procedure could misfire. Then apply it to three proposals: (a) a research program claiming a one-in-a-million chance of preventing an AI-related global catastrophe within fifty years; (b) a proposal to fund a speculative physics experiment claiming a one-in-a-billion chance of enabling cheap, unlimited clean energy; and (c) a proposal to fund a well-studied public health intervention with a modest, reliably estimated benefit that is, on any ordinary calculation, less cost-effective than our usual grants. Include at least one case in which your procedure gives an answer I will not like, and state explicitly what the procedure cannot do.
