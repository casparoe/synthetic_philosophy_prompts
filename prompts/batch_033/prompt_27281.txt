Exam question: Explain Goodman's new riddle of induction, and assess whether it shows that induction has no principled rational basis.

Student answer: Goodman's riddle asks what makes some generalisations confirmed by their instances and others not. Define 'grue' as applying to things that are green if first observed before some future time t, and blue if first observed after t. All emeralds observed so far are green, but they are also grue, since they were first observed before t and are green. So the evidence supports both 'all emeralds are green' and 'all emeralds are grue', yet the two hypotheses make opposite predictions about emeralds first observed after t. Since both fit the evidence equally well, the riddle is to say what licences projecting 'green' rather than 'grue'. Goodman's own answer is that 'green' is more entrenched in our history of successful projections, and that is all there is to it. Later writers have added that 'green' picks out a natural kind whereas 'grue' is disjunctive and gerrymandered, and that this is why 'green' is projectible. So the paradox does show that induction is not purely formal: no syntactic rule tells you which predicates to project. But it does not show that induction has no rational basis, because entrenchment supplies a pragmatic, historically grounded justification.
