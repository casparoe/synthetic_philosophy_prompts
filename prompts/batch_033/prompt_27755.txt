I organize a reading group on the foundations of quantum mechanics, and two of its members have been deadlocked for months over the status of probability in Everettian many-worlds theory. One holds that the Born rule is not an independent postulate but can be derived—or at least justified—from the unitarity of the quantum state together with decision-theoretic or rational-choice constraints on how an agent should distribute her concern across the branches she expects to split into; on this view, once the commitments of a rational agent in a branching universe are made explicit, squared-amplitude weights emerge as the unique rationally permissible way to weight outcomes, and branch-counting can be ruled out as incoherent rather than merely unappealing. The other holds that this program is at best a consistency result and at worst question-begging: the decision-theoretic premises, the notion of a measure of concern over one's future successors, or the symmetry assumptions quietly import the very probabilistic content that was supposed to be derived, while the hard questions—why squared amplitudes rather than branch counts, and what probability could even mean when every outcome occurs—have been relocated rather than answered. They have agreed to produce the document that an adversarial collaboration between their two positions would yield.

Please write that document. It should include: (1) a numbered list of claims both sides can sign, covering what the unitary formalism does and does not by itself entail, what any adequate account of probability in many-worlds must explain, and what the standard objections and derivation attempts do and do not establish; (2) a disagreements table in which the residual differences are stated precisely, with each side's own view phrased in wording that side would accept, followed by the crux sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side; and (3) for each crux, a concrete case, argument, or constraint that both parties agree bears on it, with each side's prediction or verdict about it recorded in advance. If part of the dispute turns out to be verbal—for instance, over what counts as deriving probability or over what probability is being asked to mean in a theory where every branch is real—say which part is verbal and state the substantive disagreement that remains.
