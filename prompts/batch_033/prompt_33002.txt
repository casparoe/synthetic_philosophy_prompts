I've been going down the agent-foundations rabbit hole, specifically the stuff about why a formal reasoner can't just "trust itself," and I keep tripping over the fact that people use "self-trust" to mean two different things. Sometimes they mean the reflection principle: for any sentence φ, if the system proves φ then φ is true, which in provability-logic notation is □φ → φ. Other times they mean the consistency statement: the system doesn't prove a contradiction, i.e. ¬□⊥. These feel like they should be expressions of the same basic stance — the system vouching for its own reasoning — but the obstacles look different in the two cases. Löb's theorem seems to say that a consistent system rich enough to do arithmetic can't adopt reflection across the board, while Gödel's second incompleteness theorem says it can't prove its own consistency. I'd like a real comparison of these two candidate formalizations of self-trust, not just a gloss.

Specifically: (a) spell out what each one actually asserts in provability-logic terms, including the scope of the φ in the reflection schema; (b) say why a designer of a self-modifying or self-regulating agent might want each one as an axiom or rule; (c) walk through exactly how Löb's theorem blocks wholesale reflection — maybe touch on why reflection over restricted classes like Σ₁ sentences is fine — and contrast that with how Gödel II blocks the consistency statement; and (d) say what the Löbian principle □(□φ → φ) → □φ, wich is derivable in PA under the standard derivability conditions, actually gives the agent, and whether you think that deserves to be called a form of self-trust at all.
