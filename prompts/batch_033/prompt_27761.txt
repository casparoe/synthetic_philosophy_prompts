The problem of interpersonal utility comparisons goes back to Lionel Robbins, who argued in the 1930s that we cannot compare the satisfactions of different people because utility is a subjective mental state. Since we can't get inside other people's heads, any claim that one person gains more utility than another loses is scientifically meaningless. This is a serious challenge for utilitarianism because utilitarianism requires us to sum utilities across persons to determine which action maximizes total welfare.

One response to this problem comes from John Harsanyi. Harsanyi argued that if we imagine ourselves behind a veil of ignorance — not knowing which person in society we will be — and we assume each position is equally likely, then rational choice under uncertainty leads us to maximize average utility. This is essentially the same move Rawls later makes in A Theory of Justice, except Harsanyi gets a utilitarian result rather than a maximin one. By using expected utility theory, Harsanyi shows that interpersonal comparisons are possible because we can imagine what it would be like to be someone else and compare their preferences to our own.

A second response comes from Amartya Sen, who argues that we should abandon utility altogether and focus on capabilities — what people are actually able to do and be. Since capabilities like being well-nourished or being able to read are objectively observable, we can make interpersonal comparisons without needing to compare subjective mental states. This completely sidesteps the problem.

In my view, both responses are successful, and the interpersonal comparisons objection is not fatal to utilitarianism. Harsanyi shows that we can make comparisons through an impartial spectator framework, and Sen shows that we can avoid the problem entirely by switching to a different metric of well-being. The real challenge for utilitarianism is not interpersonal comparisons but rather issues like demandingness and the separateness of persons.
