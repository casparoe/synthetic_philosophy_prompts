I've been reading about the end of the Bretton Woods system, and one episode keeps bothering me in a way that I think has philosophical stakes. On August 15, 1971, Nixon announced that the US would no longer convert dollars into gold at a fixed rate. Before that date, a dollar was, at least in principle, a claim on a specific quantity of gold held by the Treasury. After that date, a dollar was backed by nothing except the US government's say-so and the fact that everyone else kept treating it as money. And yet the system didn't collapse — it transitioned to a pure fiat regime that, by most measures, functioned. What I find puzzling is the ontology of this: the physical objects (paper bills, ledger entries) didn't change, their legal tender status didn't change, and many ordinary people probably didn't register what had happened. But the thing that was supposed to ground the dollar's value simply disappeared overnight, and the dollar survived.

I'm trying to analyze this episode through the lens of the idea that money is socially constructed — that what makes something money is not its physical composition or its backing by some more fundamental asset, but rather a network of collective attitudes, practices, and institutions. The social-construction view seems to predict that the end of gold convertibility should be less like a bridge losing its foundations and more like a community changing the rules of a game. But that framing raises questions I'm not sure how to answer. Who exactly had to change their collective intentions for the dollar to survive? Did the construction happen at the level of central banks and finance ministries, or did it require the broader public to go along? And if many people kept accepting dollars without understanding that the gold window had closed — if the collective intentionality was in some sense uninformed — does that cause trouble for the social-construction account, which seems to require some kind of shared awareness or agreement?

I'd also like the analysis to run in the other direction: what does the Nixon shock reveal about the strengths and weaknesses of the social-construction view of money? Are there features of what followed — the inflationary 1970s, the role of oil being priced in dollars, the way the dollar became the world's reserve currency precisely after it was unmoored from gold — that the theory handles elegantly, and others that it struggles with or is simply silent about? I'm less interested in the economic history as such than in what the episode can teach us about what money is, and whether "socially constructed" is a precise enough concept to capture what changed and what didn't when the gold window closed.
