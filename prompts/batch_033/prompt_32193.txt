I teach an upper-level seminar on blame and moral criticism, and next week we read Arthur Miller's The Crucible alongside the philosophical literature on standing to blame. I would like a reading of the play that takes the debate about hypocrisy seriously rather than using the play as a mere illustration of it. On the standard view, a hypocrite lacks standing to blame because he does not take the reasons he invokes seriously in his own conduct; John Proctor, guilty of adultery with Abigail Williams, looks like a textbook case of such a compromised blamer, and yet the play plainly wants us to treat his denunciation of the court and his final refusal to sign a false confession — "I have given you my soul; leave me my name!" — as moral clarity, not self-righteousness. So: does Proctor have standing to blame the court, and on what account of standing? Does the play treat standing as something that can be forfeited and then recovered, and if so, by what — public confession, the refusal to profit from the lie, the acceptance of death, or something else? And where does the play put pressure back on the theory: does Proctor's case show that a wrongdoer can retain or regain standing to condemn a wrong of an entirely different order, and that the hypocrisy condition needs refining? I want a reading that is faithful in both directions — one that honors the details of the play and does not smooth over the philosophy.
