I was reading this pop-philosophy book and it had a section on Al-Ghazālī that went something like:

"Al-Ghazālī, the 11th-century Islamic theologian, argued that there is no such thing as causation. His famous example was fire and cotton: when fire touches cotton and the cotton burns, we assume the fire caused the burning, but Al-Ghazālī pointed out that all we actually observe is one event followed by another. We never observe the causal connection itself. He concluded that God is the only true cause of everything, directly creating each event moment by moment. This means that for Al-Ghazālī, science is impossible in principle—if fire doesn't really cause burning, then we can't predict anything, and the whole project of discovering natural laws is undermined. He basically invented Hume's problem of induction 700 years early, but unlike Hume he used it to defend religous faith against the rationalist philosophers of his day."

I've read a bit about occasionalism but I'm not sure how much of this is fair. It feels like it's glossing over some distinctions but I can't put my finger on which ones. Can you go through this and tell me what's accurate, what's subtly wrong, and whether the conclusion about science being impossible follows from Al-Ghazālī's actual view?
