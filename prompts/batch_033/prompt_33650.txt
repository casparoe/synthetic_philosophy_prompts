I'm grading for a second-year ethics course and I've got a student answer I'm not sure how to mark — the kind where it's mostly right but something feels wierd about it and I can't quite put my finger on it. The exam question was:

"Explain Williams's one thought too many objection. What does the example of the man and his wife show, and what does it not show? In your answer, distinguish the integrity objection from the demandingness objection, and say whether the one thought too many point applies to Kantian ethics as well as to utilitarianism."

The student wrote:

"Williams asks us to imagine a man who can save only one of two people drowning, his wife or a stranger. The man saves his wife, and that seems fine. But Williams says that if the man's fully spelled-out thought is 'I may save my wife because in situations like this it is permissible to save one's wife,' then he has had one thought too many. The thought that should move him is simply 'it's my wife.' The objection, then, is that impartial moral theories force the agent to step back from his own life and justify his actions from a detached, impersonal standpoint, and this alienates him from his ground projects and close relationships. This is different from the demandingness objection, which says that consequentialism asks us to sacrifice too much of our own welfare. Williams's point is subtler: even when the theory gives the right answer, the way it requires the agent to get there is destructive. The integrity objection is aimed at act-utilitarianism in particular, because act-utilitarianism tells the man to save whichever person would maximise overall utility, so if the stranger would contribute more to the general happiness, the man is morally required to let his wife drown. That is the one thought too many problem: the theory might demand that the man abandon the person he loves for the sake of an impersonal calculation. Kantianism, by contrast, can accommodate special relationships through imperfect duties and the idea that we may give priority to those near to us, so Williams's objection doesn't really get a grip on it."

Can you give this a grade (letter or percentage, your call) and walk me through what's right and wrong in it, point by point? I want the kind of comments I'd actually write in the margin. And please just write in normal paragraphs — no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables.
