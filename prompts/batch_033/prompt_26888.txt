Okay so I'm a novelist and lets say I've let myself get talked into giving a paper at a workshop on perception and fiction. The thesis I'm stuck defending is that cognitive penetration of perception is real and stronger than most vision scientists will admit — what you expect, want, and know can literally change how things look, not just how you judge or remember them afterwards — and that literary style is best understood as a toolkit for doing this to a reader on purpose. My evidence is mostly the usual stuff: Balcetis and Dunning's wishful-seeing studies, the weapon-bias literature, and expert perception, like radiologists seeing a lesion in a blur that a novice literally doesn't see. The audience is about twenty people, mostly philosophers of mind and vision scientists, including two or three hardline modularists who think Pylyshyn was basically right and early vision is sealed off from cognition, and one person who did a postdoc with Firestone and will spot every methodological confound before I finish my second slide. These are people who care about experimental design and about keeping the perception/cognition boundary clean; they will not be moved by a nice sentence. I'm a novelist, not a trained philosopher, so please make this a prep sheet and not a pep talk. Give me the objections I'll actually get from this crowd, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way they would put them, each with a two-minute reply, a note on where the reply is soft, and what I should just concede. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks. And tell me the weakest point in my whole argument so I can decide whether to defend it or quietly cut it.
