I'm a PhD student in political theory, and I'm working through Dewey's *Democracy and Education* (1916) for a chapter of my dissertation on pragmatist conceptions of democratic deliberation. I keep getting stuck on a passage in Chapter 11, "Experience and Thinking," where Dewey lays out his account of what experience actually is. Here's the passage:

"The nature of experience can be understood only by noting that it includes an active and a passive element peculiarly combined. On the active hand, experience is trying—a meaning which is made explicit in the connected term experiment. On the passive, it is undergoing. When we experience something we act upon it, we do something with it; then we suffer or undergo the consequences. We do something to the thing and then it does something to us in return: such is the peculiar combination. The connection of these two phases of experience measures the fruitfulness or value of the experience. Mere activity does not constitute experience. It is dispersive, centrifugal, dissipating. Experience as trying involves change, but change is meaningless transition unless it is consciously connected with the return wave of consequences which flow from it. When an activity is continued into the undergoing of consequences, when the change made by action is reflected back into a change made in us, the mere flux is loaded with significance. We learn something. It is not experience when a child merely sticks his finger into a flame; it is experience when the movement is connected with the pain which he undergoes in consequence. Henceforth the sticking of the finger into flame means a burn."

I get the basic picture—experience is a transaction where you do something and then register the consequences, and that's what makes it learning rather than just random activity. What I'm struggling with is how this maps onto his democratic theory. Dewey seems to be describing experience in a way that's fundamentally first-personal: *I* act, *I* undergo the consequences, *I* make the connection. But when he talks about democratic inquiry later in the book, he's clearly talking about something collective—communities solving problems together, public deliberation, shared intelligence. My adivsor says Dewey's experimentalism is supposed to unify the two, but I don't see how. Is the idea that democratic publics are somehow a collective subject that "undergoes" consequences? That sounds kind of metaphorical and hand-wavy. Or am I reading too much into the individualism of the flame example and missing something about how the active/passive structure is supposed to scale up? Would appreciate any help untangling this—I feel like there's a move here that Dewey's making that I'm just not seeing.
