An introductory textbook on the philosophy of social science summarizes Lewis's account of convention this way: "For Lewis, a convention is a regularity in the behavior of a population such that it is common knowledge that everyone conforms to it, that everyone expects everyone else to conform, that each person prefers to conform on condition that the others do, and that some alternative regularity could have served in its place. Because a convention is an equilibrium of a coordination game, each person is at least as well off conforming as not, given what the others do; it follows that a convention is always collectively beneficial, and that a convention cannot persist if it systematically favors some members of the population over others. What distinguishes a convention from a mere behavioral regularity, on Lewis's view, is that it rests on tacit agreement: the parties must at some level have accepted the rule together, even if no explicit bargain ever took place." I find the first sentence plausible, but I suspect the second and third sentences slide between claims Lewis would have wanted to keep apart. Could you audit the paragraph sentence by sentence? Which parts would Lewis accept, which conflate distinct claims or overstate a modest thesis, and does the conclusion about collective benefit follow from the accurate parts?
