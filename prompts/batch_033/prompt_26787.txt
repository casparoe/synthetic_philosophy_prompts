In the epistemology of testimony, the debate between reductionism and anti-reductionism has been active for several decades now. Reductionists hold that testimonial justification reduces to other epistemic sources: the hearer needs positive reasons — inductive evidence of the speaker's reliability, or at least case-specific grounds — to justifiably accept what they are told. Anti-reductionists hold that testimony is a basic source of justification: we have a default entitlement to believe what others tell us, absent defeaters, in something like the way we have a default entitlement to trust our perceptual experience. Hybrid views have appeared — Jennifer Lackey's dualism, Paul Faulkner's trust theory, and others — but the core camps have not moved.

What I find puzzling is that both sides seem to agree on a great deal: everyone grants that testimony is generally reliable, that we often lack explicit inductive checks on individual speakers, and that gullibility is epistemically irresponsible. And yet the debate persists. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not really about testimony at all — it is a proxy for a deeper dispute about the structure of epistemic justification, specifically about whether justification always requires the subject to possess reasons or can be grounded in reliable processes the subject need not access. If that is right, the testimony debate is stalled because the real action is elsewhere.

I want a diagnosis, not a survey. Is my hypothesis correct? If not, what is the real structure of the disagreement? Is it a clash of bedrock intuitions about what counts as a basic source of knowledge? A verbal dispute about the word "justification"? A hidden empirical question about developmental psychology? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a new distinction, an empirical result, a formal result — or should we expect no progress and simply accept that the two camps are engaged in different projects?
