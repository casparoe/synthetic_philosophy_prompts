I'm a retired biologist — spent my career in developmental biology, not philosophy — and I've been reading the split-brain literature for the past year or so. The classic commissurotomy studies from the sixties, the philosophical commentary, the more recent stuff. Here's what's driiving me nuts.

The debate about whether split-brain patients have one consciousness or two has been going for more than fifty years, and the basic observations havent changed. Show something to the left visual field, the patient says they saw nothing, but their left hand picks out the matching object. The speaking hemisphere confabulates reasons for actions the right hemisphere initiated. Sometimes the hands fight over a shirt. Nobody disputes the facts.

But the interpretations are locked in a loop. One camp says: you've got two systems that perceive, decide, and act, one of them just can't talk — that's two conscious minds. The other camp says: all you've shown is that some subverbal processing goes on, and calling it a second consciousness is a huge philosophical leap. Same arguments, same rebuttals, since the early seventies. Nobody moves.

So here's my question. What kind of disagreement is this? Is it a factual dispute where some new experiment could settle it, or is it a verbal dispute about how to use the word "consciousness," or is it a clash of bedrock intuitions that no amount of data can move? My working hypothesis — and I'm a biologist, not a philosopher, so tell me if I'm off — is that the two sides are using different criteria for what counts as a conscious mind. One side treats flexible behavioral responsiveness as sufficient; the other wants reportability or some kind of higher-order integration. As long as neither side admits that's what's doing the work, the debate just cycles. But maybe that's too simple. Diagnose this, and tell me honestly whether anything could break the impasse.
