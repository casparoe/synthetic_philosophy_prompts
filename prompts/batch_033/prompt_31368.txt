I'm a philosophy PhD student giving a paper next month at a Kant workshop, and I'm defending a reading of the murderer-at-the-door essay that I think gets Kant out of the he'd-rat-out-your-friend caricature without going full revisionist. My thesis, roughly: the essay is arguing about whether there's a right to lie grounded in benevolence, which is a juridical question, and not directly about whether a maxim of lying-to-protect passes the Formula of Universal Law, which is the ethical question. Kant's rigorism is real but it lives at the level of the right to truth as a condition of rightful relations, and once you keep the two levels apart you can see both why he sounds so extreme and why the standard counterexample mostly misses. The audience is a mixed group: two Kantians who know the Groundwork and the Doctrine of Right cold and will not let me get away with a soft reading, a legal philosopher who works on deception and thinks the whole right-to-the-truth framing is confused, a virtue ethicist who thinks this whole debate is a reductio of rule-based ethics, and a couple of applied ethicists who work on medical and refugee cases and will want the bottom line for when lying protects someone. My argument sketch: (1) separate the juridical question from the ethical question; (2) show that the essay's actual claim is narrower then people think, that there's no right to lie, and that such a right would indeed be self-defeating because it would undermine declaration as such; (3) run the universalization test on a carefully specified maxim like lie to a would-be murderer in order to protect an innocent person, and argue it's not obviously forbidden, which shows the ethical question is doing surprisingly little work in the essay; (4) concede that Kant still seems to want the unconditional conclusion, so my reading might end up a friendly amendment rather than a faithful interpretation. Write me the Q&A prep sheet: the objections in roughly the order they'll actually come, phrased the way these specific people would phrase them, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is weakest. Include which ones I should concede up front, which I should hold, and the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
