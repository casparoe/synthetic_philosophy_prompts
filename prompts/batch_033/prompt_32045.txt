A charge one hears constantly against evolutionary psychology is that its explanations are just-so stories: for any trait, so the objection runs, one can invent a plausible adaptive history after the fact, and the hypothesis is therefore a narrative post hoc rationalisation rather than a testable scientific claim. The comparison is not merely rhetorical; it is used to dismiss whole research programmes, not just particular bad hypotheses. I would like an audit of this analogy rather than a verdict on the field. Spell out the intended correspondences: what is supposed to play the role of the storyteller, the audience, the tale, and the moral? Where does the mapping genuinely hold, and where does it break down? Which disanalogies matter for the dismissive use of the analogy, and which are incidental? In particular, does the force of the charge depend on features that a just-so story has but a scientific hypothesis need not have — for instance, being constructed to fit one already known case and being protected from refutation rather than put at risk — and can adaptationist hypotheses be reformulated so as to shed those features? Conclude by saying whether the dismissive conclusion survives the audit, and what a better analogy would be, if there is one, for the activity of proposing and testing adaptationist explanations.
