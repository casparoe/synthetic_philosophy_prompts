I've been trying to sort out my views on the ethics of eating and wild animals, and I've found that my reactions to particular cases pull against the principle I say I accept. I'm hoping you can help me find the principle that actually captures my judgments, or tell me which judgments I should revise.

Here are the cases as I find myself judging them. (1) Buying intensively raised chicken: I'm confident this is wrong; the birds' lives are short and miserable, and the suffering is created for a meal I could easily do without. (2) Buying beef from a cow that spent its life on pasture and was killed quickly at a small local slaughterhouse: I think this is much less bad than the chicken — almost acceptable — even though the cow is more cognitively complex than a chicken and its death is just as deliberate. My reason seems to be that one animal's death yields very many meals, whereas a year of eating chicken kills dozens of birds. (3) Buying conventionally grown vegetables, knowing that combine harvesting kills mice and voles in the fields: I don't feel any real compunction about this. It feels importantly different from the chicken case, though I can't articulate the difference. (4) Buying eggs from a neighbor's well-kept backyard hens: fine. (5) Paying to release rehabilitated animals into a reserve, knowing that many of them will starve, be injured, or be eaten by predators: I think this is fine, maybe even good. (6) Culling deer in an overpopulated forest to prevent the slower death by starvation that would otherwise occur: I think this is permissible, perhaps required.

The principle I'm drawn to is a simple minimizing one: other things equal, choose the option that produces the least total expected suffering, where every sentient animal counts in proportion to its capacity to suffer, and it doesn't matter whether the suffering is intended, foreseen, or merely allowed. That principle seems to get the chicken case right, and it explains why beef might be better than chicken if one cow's death causes less total suffering than dozens of chickens' lives. But I can see it also tells me that conventional vegetables are wrong whenever a lower-death alternative is available, that releasing animals into the reserve may be wrong if their wild lives are on balance painful, and that I should be far more troubled by the vast amount of suffering in wild nature than I actually am.

Is there a principle that captures all six of my verdicts, or close to it? What does that principle say about a case I haven't settled: a small mixed farm that raises a few pigs outdoors and kills them humanely, but whose vegetable fields are tilled, causing some field-animal deaths — is buying its pork and its vegetables both fine, one fine and the other not, and why? And if no principled view can accommodate all six verdicts, which ones should I give up?
