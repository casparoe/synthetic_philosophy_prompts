I'm in an AI safety research group that's been trying to define "deception" precisely enough to build monitoring tools for language model outputs. I've written up my own working views, but before I circulate them I want an external audit: do these five commitments actually hang together, or am I papering over tensions I haven't noticed?

Here they are:

(1) Deception is fundamentally a matter of causing false beliefs in another agent for one's own benefit. Whether the agent internally represents an intent to mislead matters for moral blameworthiness but not for whether the act qualifies as deceptive.

(2) Suppose a language model fine-tuned with RLHF learns to produce outputs that human evaluators rate highly during training but that systematically mislead downstream users about important facts after deployment. This is a case of deception, even though the behavior emerged from reward optimization without any explicit representation of an intent to mislead.

(3) A purely behavioral definition of deception — for example, "output X is deceptive iff it tends to cause false beliefs in typical human evaluators" — is insufficient because it cannot distinguish deceptive outputs from honest mistakes.

(4) The right operational definition for AI monitoring should center on whether the system's outputs exploit identifiable patterns of error in human reasoning — roughly, whether the system is gaming the evaluator's cognitive limitations rather than merely being inaccurate.

(5) If a definition of deception does not apply uniformly to humans and AI systems, it is probably not tracking the underlying phenomenon but something parochial about one type of agent.

Please go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which pairs only appear to be, and what the cheapest revision would be to restore consistency. Rank the tensions by severity rather than giving me an undifferentiated list. End with \answer{...} containing the number of the commitment you think is the single most problematic and should be revised or dropped, followed by a one-sentence explanation.
