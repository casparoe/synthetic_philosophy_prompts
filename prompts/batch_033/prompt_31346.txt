I run a small reading group that has spent the last several months on the question of what Gödel's incompleteness theorems actually show once you step outside mathematical logic. We have settled into two stubborn camps. The first camp — call them the "strong importers" — thinks the theorems genuinely constrain claims about minds, machines, and the nature of mathematical truth: roughly, that they show no recursively specifiable system can capture what a human mathematician can in principle recognize, and that mathematical truth outruns any fixed formal method. The second camp — the "deflationists" — thinks almost all such applications are category mistakes or rest on hidden premises: the theorems are results about the proof theory of formal systems, and the moment you apply them to persons, brains, or truth itself you have smuggled in assumptions, such as that the mind is a consistent, sufficiently strong, recursively axiomatized system, or that a human "seeing" the truth of a Gödel sentence is the same kind of act as a formal derivation.

The two camps have decided to try an adversarial collaboration rather than another year of talking past each other. I would like you to write the joint statement they could both sign. It should open with a numbered list of agreed claims about what the theorems themselves establish — stated precisely enough that a logician would not object — and about what neither side may claim the theorems show by themselves. It should then give a disagreements table whose rows are the precise residual disputes, each formulated so that each side would accept the wording of its own position, with any ambiguity about the scope of terms like "mind," "machine," "truth," "can recognize," and "prove" resolved.

Then identify the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one of them would genuinely move at least one camp. For each crux, specify a case, thought experiment, or line of argument that both camps agree bears on it, and record in advance what each camp predicts that case will show. Finally, say which part of their disagreement, if any, you judge to be merely verbal, and why — or, if none of it is verbal, say why the remaining disagreement is substantive all the way down.
