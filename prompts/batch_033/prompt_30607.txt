The debate over Frankfurt-style cases and the Principle of Alternative Possibilities has now run for more than fifty years. On one side, those persuaded by Frankfurt argue that an agent can be morally responsible for an action even when they could not have done otherwise, because the counterfactual intervener — who would have forced the action had the agent shown any sign of doing otherwise — plays no actual causal role in what the agent does. On the other side, defenders of PAP have developed increasingly sophisticated responses: the flicker-of-freedom strategy, which insists that some alternative possibility remains even in Frankfurt cases; the dilemma defense, which argues that Frankfurt cases either presuppose determinism (and thereby beg the question) or leave the agent with some alternative after all; and various attempts to show that the kind of alternative possibility Frankfurt eliminates is not the kind that responsibility requires.

What puzzles me is not that the debate continues but that its dialectical structure has remained essentially unchanged for decades. Each new Frankfurt-style case is met with a new flicker or a new horn of the dilemma; each new defense of PAP is met with a more elaborately engineered case designed to close off the remaining alternatives. The moves on both sides are now highly refined, and I suspect most participants in the debate could predict with considerable accuracy what the other side will say about any given move. Yet almost no one changes their mind.

I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is. My working hypothesis — and I offer it only as a hypothesis — is that the two sides are operating with different conceptions of what it is to be the source of one's own action, that these conceptions are not themselves being tested by the cases but are instead being projected onto them, and that the cases therefore function not as counterexamples in the usual sense but as vehicles for making each side's prior commitments vivid. But I could be persuaded that something else is going on: perhaps the disagreement bottoms out in irreconcilable modal intuitions about the cases themselves; perhaps it is partly verbal, turning on different senses of "could have done otherwise"; perhaps there is a hidden empirical question about how ordinary people actually judge these cases and whether their judgments should constrain philosophical analysis; or perhaps the entire framing in terms of a single, shared concept of moral responsibility is the problem.

Given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress? And if your diagnosis implies that progress in the usual sense is not to be expected — that the two sides will continue refining cases and responses indefinitely — what would have to change for the debate to move forward rather than simply continue? I am less interested in a verdict on who is right than in understanding why the impasse has the particular shape it does, and whether the shape itself tells us something worth knowing.
