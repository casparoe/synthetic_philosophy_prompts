Two friends of mine got into it at game night and I'm hoping you can sort out who's right. We'd been playing Hex, and someone mentioned it's a proven fact that the first player has a winning strategy. My friend Dan said yeah, that's the strategy-stealing argument — you assume the second player has a winning strategy, then the first player makes an arbitrary opening move and thereafter "steals" the second player's strategy, which yields a contradiction. My other friend Maya said she's always found that argument weirdly hollow. Dan asked what she meant, and she said: the proof doesn't give you the strategy. It just shows one exists by contradiction. So as a player, you're exactly where you were before — you still don't know what to do. Dan said that's true of loads of mathematical proofs; non-constructive existence proofs are standard and nobody calls them hollow. Maya said but in a game, "having a strategy" means something different than in pure math. A strategy is supposed to be an algorithm you can follow. If I tell you "there exists a winning strategy but I can't tell you a single move," have I actually told you anything about how to play Hex? Dan said you've told me the game isn't a second-player win, which is real information — it rules something out. Maya said it rules something out in a way that's completely inert. You can't act on it, you can't teach it, you can't even check it except by re-deriving the contradiction. Dan said so what, lots of knowledge is inert — I know there's a largest prime less than a googol but I can't tell you what it is. Maya said that's different because you could in principle compute it; the strategy-stealing argument gives you no computational path at all. Dan said that's just finitist scruples and nobody actually holds math to that standard. They went in circles after that.
