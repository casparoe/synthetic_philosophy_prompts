I've been struggling with something for years now and I'm hoping you can help me think through it philosophically rather than just giving me productivity advice.

Every evening, I make a genuine plan to wake up at six, go for a run, and start work by eight. I mean it when I make the plan — I'm not lying to myself or half-heartedly intending it. I can vividly imagine the morning, I want the outcome, and I believe the plan is feasible. Then the alarm goes off, and I turn it off and sleep another two hours. The person who made the plan and the person who broke it both feel like me. There's no sense of an alien force taking over; it's just that at 6 a.m., going back to sleep seems, in that moment, obviously the right thing to do.

I've read enough pop psychology to know about things like hyperbolic discounting and present bias, and I've tried the usual remedies. I put my phone across the room so I have to get up to turn off the alarm. It worked. For about a week. Then I started getting up, turning it off, and going back to bed — fully aware, even as I did it, that I was defeating a system I had designed specifically to outsmart myself. I've tried apps that lock me out of social media after a certain hour; I've ended up using my partner's phone instead. I've made bets with friends where I owe them money if I miss a deadline; I've paid up and then missed the next deadline anyway.

What bothers me isn't simply that I fail to do what I planned. It's that I seem to be both the planner and the saboteur, and I can't tell whether this means I'm irrational in a philosophically interesting way, or whether it just means my preferences are more complex than I thought. When I circumvent my own commitment device, am I revealing that my real preference is to sleep in, and the evening planning was merely aspirational — so that there's no deep puzzle, just a forecasting error about my own future tastes? Or is there a genuine conflict within a single agent, and if so, what does that say about the unity of the self? I suppose what I'm asking is: does philosophy have a way of describing what's going on here that goes deeper than 'your limbic system overrides your prefrontal cortex', and does it have anything to say about what I should actually do about it — or even about whether 'I' am the planner, the saboteur, or something else entirely?
