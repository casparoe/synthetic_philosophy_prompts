I am a novelist at work on a book whose central character, a woman in her late fifties, learns that she has an inoperable condition and roughly a year to live. I want the narrative to engage seriously — but entirely through scene, image, and character — with the Epicurean claim that death is not bad for the person who dies, because when death is present the person is not. I find myself particularly drawn to the asymmetry problem that Lucretius pressed: if we are not troubled by the eternity of nonexistence before our birth, why should we be troubled by the eternity after? And I am equally drawn to the deprivationist reply — that what makes death bad is not any positive experience but the sheer loss of the life she would have had. I would like you to write a short story, perhaps 1,500 to 2,000 words, that dramatizes this tension. The protagonist need not settle the philosophical question; I want the reader to feel its pressure. The story should avoid philosophical jargon and explicit argumentation. Instead, let the ideas surface through the particulars of her situation: the way she looks at her garden, the things she says and does not say to her daughter, the strange new weight of ordinary mornings. I would be especially interested if the story finds a way to make the pre-birth and post-death asymmetry felt — perhaps through a dream, a memory, or some small narrative device that gives the comparison a visceral shape.
