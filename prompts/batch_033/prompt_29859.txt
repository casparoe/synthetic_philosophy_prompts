I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about why people value original artworks over perceptually indistinguishable copies. The standard debate over fakes and forgeries usually pits a view I'll call aesthetic empiricism — the view that an object's aesthetic value is fixed by its perceivable properties, so a perfect copy has exactly the aesthetic value of the original — against an origin-sensitive, achievement-based view, on which a work's value depends on the history of its making: who made it, under what constraints, and what the maker was actually doing. I want to isolate a variable that tends to get buried under the brute fact of copying: the copier's intention to deceive.

Please construct a thought experiment to the following specifications. There should be two copies of the same original painting, made by the same person, with the same materials and the same level of skill, such that the two copies are perceptually indistinguishable from each other and from the original. The only difference between the copies is that one is a forgery made with the intention of passing it off as the original, while the other is made openly as a study copy, with no intention to deceive anyone about its origin. The case should be realistic — a studio, a museum, an auction house, or some other ordinary art-world setting — and the scenario itself should take no more than a paragraph. The verdicts should be these: aesthetic empiricism must assign the two copies exactly equal aesthetic value, while the origin-sensitive, achievement-based view must assign lower aesthetic value to the deceptive copy than to the open study copy. Do not stipulate the verdicts; they must be generated from the two views' stated commitments. Also keep the question one of aesthetic value, not market price or legal status.

After giving the scenario, walk through what each view says and why, then identify which feature of the case is doing the work, and say what happens to the case if that feature is removed — in particular, whether it collapses back into the familiar perfect-copy discussion. Finish with the strongest reply an aesthetic empiricist could give to the claim that the deceptive copy is aesthetically worse than the open copy, even though the two are perceptually identical.
