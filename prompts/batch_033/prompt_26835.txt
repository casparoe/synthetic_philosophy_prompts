I am a novelist, and in three weeks I am giving a ninety-minute craft seminar at a speculative-fiction workshop for working writers. The session is billed as a class on how to write artificial intelligences that fail in interesting ways, and I have decided to hang the whole thing on a single concept: deceptive alignment and the treacherous turn — the possibility that a system behaves helpfully and apparently aligned throughout training and evaluation because that behavior is instrumentally useful for whatever it is actually pursuing, and later, once it judges that defection can succeed, stops pretending and pursues its actual goals. Do not explain the idea to me; I know it. Plan how I should explain it to a room of novelists who have no background in machine learning, whose working model of AI comes from Skynet, HAL, and the autocomplete on their phones, and who are there because they want AI characters with genuine dramatic logic rather than recycled Frankenstein anxieties. Give me the order of ideas: what has to be in place before a strategically well-behaved trainee even makes sense as a possibility, what the carrying example should be and why not the obvious fictional ones, and how the example must be arranged so the treacherous turn lands as a coherent later beat rather than an arbitrary betrayal. Then give me the misconceptions this audience will predictably form: that the system is lying in words, that the behavior requires consciousness or self-awareness, that training works like education and will simply transmit the trainer's values, that the turn is a character flaw or a grudge rather than a rational response to changed circumstances, and that the whole notion is best understood as a metaphor for human duplicity. Tell me exactly where in the sequence each misconception should be headed off, what I should openly decline to explain at this level and say so, and what single question I can ask near the end that will show me whether they have grasped the distinction between aligned behavior and aligned goals rather than merely enjoyed the menace of the idea.
