Here is an argument I have been unable to resolve. It appears to show that Humean accounts of objective chance cannot satisfy the Principal Principle, which would make them either false or normatively irrelevant. Each step seems plausible, but the conclusion is stronger than I think most philosophers accept.

(1) The Principal Principle (PP): For any rational credence function Cr, proposition A, and admissible evidence E, Cr(A | Ch(A) = x ∧ E) = x. An agent who knows the chance should defer to it.

(2) On a Humean account, chances are not metaphysically primitive. The chance of a coin's landing heads is whatever value figures in the best systematization of the total mosaic of particular facts — past, present, and future.

(3) Suppose the Humean chance of heads on each toss of a certain coin is 1/2. Now consider the proposition U: every toss of this coin throughout the entire history of the universe lands heads. U is an undermining future: if U obtains, then the global pattern of outcomes is one in which heads occurs every time, and the best systematization of that mosaic will assign a chance much closer to 1 than to 1/2. So U entails that Ch(H) ≠ 1/2.

(4) What is the chance of U, given that Ch(H) = 1/2? If tosses are independent with chance 1/2 each, and there are finitely many tosses, then Ch(U | Ch(H) = 1/2) is positive, though tiny. The chance function itself assigns non-zero probability to the undermining future.

(5) By PP, a rational agent who knows that Ch(H) = 1/2 should set her credence in U equal to the chance of U — which, by (4), is positive. So Cr(U) > 0.

(6) But the agent also knows that U entails ~Ch(H) = 1/2. Since she knows Ch(H) = 1/2, she knows ~U. A rational agent who knows ~U cannot assign Cr(U) > 0 on pain of probabilistic incoherence.

(7) Therefore, PP and Humean chance jointly entail a contradiction. Since PP is what connects chance to rational belief — without it, chance is normatively inert — Humean chance must be rejected.

(8) This conclusion seems too strong. Humeanism about chance is a serious position, and many philosophers want to say there are objective chances even in a deterministic world. So the argument must contain a mistake. But I cannot identify it without giving up something I am reluctant to lose.

I am most attached to the Principal Principle and least attached to the Humean supervenience claim in (2). But I want to know: which step should I reject, and what exactly does rejecting it cost? Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
