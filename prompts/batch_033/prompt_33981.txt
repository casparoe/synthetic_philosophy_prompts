Just got back from a long argument with a friend about GEB — specifically whether the move from Gödel sentences to self-aware minds actually explains anything, or its just a fancy metaphor. I came away thinking the analogy earns its keep: that the kind of self-reference Gödel exposed, a system talking about itself, strange loops and all, is the right skeleton for self-awareness, and that this dissolves the mystery rather than just restating it. To be clear, I don't mean the Lucas/Penrose line that Gödel shows minds outstrip machines; I mean the GEB line that self-reference is how awareness could arise in a mechanical substrate. My friend kept saying it's an unfalsifiable metaphor dressed up as an argument. So what would count against my view? I don't want the one-liner that a Gödel sentence isn't conscious. I want the defeaters sorted: which considerations would show the analogy is merely decorative, which would show it's actively misleading, and which would show the whole thing has been patched into unfalsifiability. The worries I can half-see: the analogy never gets cashed out precisely, it explains self-reference but not the felt what-it's-likeness of awareness, lots of self-referential systems aren't self-aware so loops can't be sufficient, and maybe it gets the explanatory direction backwards by using already-interpreted symbols to explain the interpreter. Which of these should actually move me, which would a defender of the GEB line concede, and which can I safely ignore? Rank them from fatal to merely awkward, and be straight about whether the view risks anything at all — and if it doesn't, whether that's a problem for this kind of claim.
