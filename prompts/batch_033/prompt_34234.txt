I have been trying to get clear on what revealed preference theory actually commits one to, and the Exxon Valdez litigation strikes me as the ideal test case. In 1989 the tanker ran aground in Prince William Sound and released roughly eleven million gallons of crude oil, and the ensuing legal battle forced the question of how to put a dollar figure on injuries to things that are not bought or sold on any market: beaches, seabird colonies, subsistence fisheries, the continued existence of an intact ecosystem. The issue sharpened when the D.C. Circuit rejected the Interior Department's rule that would have treated survey-based contingent valuation as a last resort behind market prices, and the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration convened a panel of economists, chaired by Kenneth Arrow and Robert Solow, to assess whether survey responses could reliably measure the value of environmental damage, including non-use values that leave no trace in any observable choice. I know the broad outline of the episode; what I want is the conceptual anatomy.

Analyze this episode through the lens of revealed preference theory and the choice-theoretic interpretation of utility. Be precise about the mapping: on the revealed-preference view, what is a preference, what is utility, and what kind of evidence is admissible as showing that someone holds a preference or that a thing has value? On that basis, what does the theory have to say about existence values, hypothetical willingness-to-pay questions, and the gap between willingness to pay and willingness to accept that the surveys kept turning up, and where should it refuse to speak rather than force an answer? Then run the analysis in the other direction: what does the theory illuminate about the episode, for instance the initial privileging of market prices and the insistence that actual choices are better evidence than answers to survey questions, and what does it misdescribe or leave unexplained? Finally, give an honest verdict on what the episode shows about the theory: is this a limitation that revealed preference can acknowledge without damage, or does it reveal that a purely choice-based notion of utility cannot carry the normative weight this legal and policy context asked it to carry?
