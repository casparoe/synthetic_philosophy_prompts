I teach a graduate seminar on anthropic reasoning, and I would like to see how a considered respondent weighs the main considerations in the dispute between the Self-Sampling Assumption and the Self-Indication Assumption.

Consider the following claim:

(SIA) The Self-Indication Assumption is the correct principle for updating on one's own existence. It holds that one should reason as if one were randomly selected from the class of all possible observers, so that, other things being equal, a hypothesis is favoured in proportion to the number of observers it predicts. Its main rival, the Self-Sampling Assumption, holds instead that one should reason as if one were randomly selected from the class of all actual observers in one's reference class. If you think the claim needs a precisification of observer or reference class before it has a determinate truth value, state the precisification you are using and assign your credence to that.

I am not asking for a survey of the debate, nor for a verdict of the form that the answer depends on the problem. I would like your actual credence in (SIA), stated as a single number between 0 and 1, together with the reasoning that produces it.

Please decompose that reasoning into at least five sub-questions. For each, give a rough probability and a one-sentence justification. Include at least the following:

(i) The intrinsic prior plausibility of the SIA rule, on which hypotheses predicting more observers are favoured, as against the SSA rule, on which one samples from actual observers.

(ii) The Presumptuous Philosopher thought experiment. Two cosmological theories are equally well supported by all ordinary evidence, but one predicts vastly more observers than the other, and SIA appears to license near-certainty in the more populous theory without any distinguishing observation. What is the probability that this is a genuine reductio of SIA?

(iii) The Doomsday argument contrast. SSA appears to support a Doom Soon conclusion from one's observed birth rank, whereas SIA cancels that inference. What is the probability that this contrast is a genuine point in favour of SIA over SSA?

(iv) The reference-class problem for SSA. How likely is it that the difficulty of specifying, non-arbitrarily, which actual observers one should regard oneself as sampled from is serious enough to discredit SSA?

(v) The residual possibility that neither SSA nor SIA is correct — for instance, that there is no single general principle of anthropic reasoning, or that the correct principle is something else entirely.

Then show how these terms combine to yield your overall credence in (SIA). Identify which term your final number is most sensitive to, and which term you are least confident in. State what single argument or piece of evidence would move your credence the most.

Finally, two colleagues of mine hold credences of roughly 0.75 and 0.2 in (SIA). On your decomposition, which term most plausibly accounts for the gap between them?

Please write your answer in British English.
