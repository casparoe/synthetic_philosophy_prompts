I am a professor of philosophy preparing a graduate seminar on formal epistemology, and our current unit concerns anthropic reasoning. Over the past several weeks, my students have worked through the canonical cases — the Sleeping Beauty problem, the Doomsday argument, the Presumptuous Philosopher, and Bostrom's incubator gedankenexperiment. They can now state the formal difference between the Self-Sampling Assumption (SSA) and the Self-Indication Assumption (SIA) and can reproduce the standard analyses of those cases. Yet I have noticed that whenever I press them to explain the practical import of the distinction in a new context, they default to paraphrasing one of the familiar examples, which suggests they may be pattern-matching rather than genuinely internalizing the reasoning dynamics that separate the two principles.

For our next meeting, I intend to present the class with a novel scenario — one that, to the best of my knowledge, does not appear in the published literature — in which SSA and SIA recommend sharply different credences or courses of action. The scenario should be specified in enough detail that one can actually compute, or at least rigorously compare, the posterior probabilities that each principle would assign, and the divergence should carry genuine practical stakes: it should matter not merely for what an agent abstractly believes, but for what a rational agent ought to do. I would like you to construct such a scenario, walk through the SSA-based and SIA-based reasoning step by step, and explain why the disagreement between the two principles has nontrivial consequences for action or decision. Please ensure that your answer is written in American English.
