I cover cognitive science and artificial intelligence for a general-interest magazine, and I have become preoccupied by a particular kind of puzzle that seems to isolate something important about thinking. The puzzle presents you with two panels of simple, abstract drawings — six on the left, six on the right. The left-hand drawings all obey some hidden rule; the right-hand ones all break it. You are not told what sort of rule to look for. You just have to look at the twelve drawings and state the rule.

The drawings themselves are deliberately bare — circles, triangles, squares, lines, dots, arranged in various configurations — so that no outside knowledge can help. For example, you might see on the left: a circle inside a square, a triangle inside a circle, a small square inside a larger one — six cases where one closed shape encloses another. On the right: a circle next to a square, a triangle with a line through it, a square with a dot outside it — all cases where no shape encloses another. The rule is "every figure on the left contains one shape wholly inside another." In another puzzle, the left-hand figures might all be convex and the right-hand ones concave. In a harder one, the left-hand figures might all be ones where the number of enclosed regions equals the number of shapes that touch the outer boundary — a rule that takes even adults a minute or two.

What strikes me is that people can often solve these after seeing just twelve examples — six positive, six negative — even though the space of logically possible rules is vast. A child of six or seven can manage the simpler ones. The solution often arrives not by gradual elimination but in a sudden reorganisation: you stare at the panels, and then you see it. Yet this seems to be a different kind of thing from the pattern recognition that machine-learning systems do when trained on thousands of labelled examples, and different from the kind of reasoning tested by standard intelligence-test items where you complete a sequence. It is more like: from a handful of positive and negative cases of an unknown category, you extract the category itself, in a form you can put into words.

I am trying to find out whether this specific cognitive capacity — the ability to land on the right concept from a few positive and negative exemplars, without being told what kind of concept to look for, and to produce an explicit rule — has an established name in the cognitive science or AI literature. I am not merely after a label; I want to understand what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its boundaries lie. I know the general area is called concept learning or rule induction, but those terms are too broad. I also know that machine-learning researchers speak of "few-shot learning," but that term describes a system's ability to classify new instances after seeing a few, not the ability to articulate the concept that governs the instances. Is there a more precise term for this capacity, and if not, what would you call it, and how would you distinguish it from neighbouring notions?
