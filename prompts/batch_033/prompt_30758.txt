Debates over moral particularism have now been running for decades, and from the outside the dialectic looks oddly stationary. The generalist says an action's moral status is fixed by general principles: features such as causing pain or breaking a promise carry a stable default valence, and a particular fact matters only as an instance of a general consideration. The particularist replies with cases in which the valence of a feature appears to flip with context — pleasure that is bad because it is pleasure taken in another's suffering, or a fact that counts in favour here and against there — and concludes that no finite codification of moral reasons is available. The generalist then absorbs the cases by complicating the principles: the valence is stable once the feature is fully specified, or the apparent flip is handled by enablers, defeaters, and intensifiers. The particularist replies that the enriched specification is either gerrymandered or smuggles the context-sensitivity back in under another name. And so on.

I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is, not another contribution to the debate. Is it a substantive disagreement about the metaphysics of reasons — about whether there are context-invariant facts about what contributes to rightness — or an epistemological disagreement about whether moral competence is rule-governed or a form of skilled perception? Is it a semantic disagreement about what "a reason" means, or a dispute about what a moral principle is supposed to do: decide, explain, justify, or codify? Or are the two sides largely answering different questions under one heading, so that the appearance of a stalled dispute is an artefact of a shared vocabulary? My working hypothesis is that much of the stalemate comes from an unstated difference about the function of a principle — guidance versus justification — but I want that hypothesis tested rather than assumed.

In answering, engage the actual moves each side makes: the particularist's stock examples of valence-switching and the generalist's strategy of absorbing them; the Rossian appeal to prima facie duties and the particularist's claim that such duties are placeholders for the very sensitivity they deny; and the recurring argument over whether holism in the theory of reasons refutes moral principles or is compatible with generalism once contributory and overall reasons are distinguished. Then tell me what, if anything, would count as progress: a shared criterion for when a ceteris paribus clause is doing real work, an agreed taxonomy of reasons versus enabling and disabling conditions, a candidate codification both sides accept as non-gerrymandered, a formal argument that no finite codification can capture the relevant patterns, or an honest conclusion that the question is ill-posed and should be replaced. If your diagnosis is that progress requires the two sides to stop using "reason" and "principle" in different senses while appearing to contradict one another, say what the replacement questions should be.
