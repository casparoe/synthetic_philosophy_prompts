I've been thinking about preregistration in psychology — you know, where journals now make you lock in your hypotheses and analysis plan before collecting data, and anything you change later gets labeled exploratory. The rationale is that it stops p-hacking and HARKing and brings down the false-positive rate. But here's what bothers me: preregistration seems to assume that the evidential force of a dataset depends on what the researcher *intended* to do before seeing it. If you run twenty tests and cherry-pick the significant one, that's bad science. If you preregister that exact same test and it comes out significant, suddenly it's good science — even though the data could be identical. What philosophy of evidence does this whole practice presuppose? Does it commit the field to a frequentist (or error-statistical) view where stopping rules and multiplicity of tests matter for inference, and does it implicitly reject the Likelihood Principle? And if it does, is that commitment actually consistent with other things the same journals do — like encouraging Bayesian analyses, or treating replication failures as evidence against the original finding? I'm trying to figure out whether preregistration is a philosophically neutral reform or whether it quietly picks a side in the frequentist-vs-Bayesian debate. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning — no preamble, no restating the question.
