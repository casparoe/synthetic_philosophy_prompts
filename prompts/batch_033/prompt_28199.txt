In a seminar on mental causation, we have been working through the causal exclusion argument, and I keep finding myself returning to Benjamin Libet's timing experiments as a kind of natural test case for it. I know the standard philosophical story about Libet: a readiness potential begins to build in the brain a few hundred milliseconds before subjects report the conscious intention to flex their wrist, and this temporal gap is often taken to show that conscious intention comes too late to do the causal work. What I want is a disciplined treatment of the Libet results specifically through the lens of the exclusion argument, not the free-will debate in general. Map the argument onto the experiment precisely: what plays the role of the mental cause, what plays the role of the physical cause, and how should the effect be specified — the muscle movement, the action, the subject's report — if the exclusion argument is to get a grip at all? Then say where the mapping is forced or breaks down. Does the readiness potential preempt the conscious intention in the way a physical realizer is supposed to preempt its mental counterpart, or is the relation between them better described in some other way?

Finish by telling me what the episode shows about the exclusion argument itself. Is Libet's setup a genuine empirical illustration of causal exclusion, a case the argument was never designed to handle, or a case that exposes an ambiguity in the argument's key terms? I am less interested in whether Libet threatens free will than in whether taking the experiment seriously forces a defender of the exclusion argument to say something precise about what counts as a cause, what counts as the same effect, and what counts as a genuine rival to a neural event.
