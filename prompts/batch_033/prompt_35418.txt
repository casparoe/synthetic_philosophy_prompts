I'm trying to work out what principle governs the rationality of choosing to undergo a transformative experience — roughly, an experience that changes you in ways you cannot fully anticipate from the outside, because it alters your preferences, your values, or the cognitive and perceptual capacities you would use to evaluate it. I've gone through a series of cases and recorded my intuitive verdicts about whether it is rationally permissible for the agent to choose the experience. I suspect at least two of my verdicts are hard to reconcile, and I want to know what principle best captures the pattern — or what I have to give up.

Here are the cases:

1. **Parenthood.** A person who has never had children and has no direct experience of parenting decides to have a biological child. They have read extensively about parenthood, spoken to many parents, and understand that the experience will change their daily life, priorities, and emotional landscape in ways they cannot fully grasp in advance. Verdict: rationally permissible.

2. **New sensory modality.** A safe, one-time pill permanently gives a person a new sensory capacity — something like echolocation or magnetoreception — that no amount of description can convey. Everyone who has taken the pill reports that it enriches their life and they would never go back, but they also say they cannot describe what it is like. Verdict: rationally permissible.

3. **Religious conversion retreat.** A secular person is invited to a weekend retreat that, by all reliable accounts, has a very high probability of inducing a sincere religious conversion. The convert's fundamental values and sources of meaning would shift dramatically; they would, post-conversion, be glad they converted, but their current self does not endorse the religious framework. Verdict: not rationally permissible.

4. **Emigration.** A person decides to move permanently to a country whose language they have learned to fluency and whose culture they have studied extensively through books, films, and conversations, but they have never visited. They know the move will reshape their habits, social norms, and possibly their values. Verdict: rationally permissible.

5. **Career change.** A mid-career accountant quits to become a high school teacher, a profession they have trained for and observed closely — student teaching, shadowing — but never practiced full-time. They know the day-to-day reality will differ from their current understanding. Verdict: rationally permissible.

6. **Marriage to a long-distance partner.** Two people who have only interacted through video calls, texts, and letters for two years decide to marry without having spent more than two consecutive weeks in person. They know cohabitation will reveal aspects of each other and the relationship that they cannot access now. Verdict: not rationally permissible.

7. **Personality-altering treatment.** A safe, voluntary neurological treatment permanently alters a person's Big Five personality profile — say, substantially raising conscientiousness and lowering openness — in ways that recipients uniformly describe as positive and life-improving, but that the person considering it cannot subjectively preview. Verdict: not rationally permissible.

8. **Cochlear implant.** A congenitally deaf adult who has never heard sound is offered a cochlear implant with a high probability of restoring functional hearing. They have learned about the experience from others who received implants, but they cannot auditorily imagine what hearing is like. Verdict: rationally permissible.

I can feel that cases 2 and 7 are in tension — both involve a safe, irreversible change that others uniformly praise and that the agent cannot preview, yet I judge one permissible and the other not. Cases 1 and 3 may also be in tension: both involve a change to core values and sources of meaning, yet I permit parenthood and forbid conversion. And I am not sure I can distinguish emigration (case 4) from the conversion retreat (case 3) in a principled way, since both can reshape values through immersion in a new framework.

What I want from you: the best principle — or a small set of candidate principles — that captures as many of these verdicts as possible. State it precisely, show how it handles each case, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it forces me to overturn. Then apply it to a few cases I haven't listed, especially ones that stress-test the boundary between permissible and impermissible transformative choices.

After the prose, please provide a structured summary as a JSON object with the following fields:

- `principle`: the precise statement of the principle you recommend.
- `principle_type`: a short label for the kind of principle it is (e.g., "value-endorsement constraint," "expected utility with transformative discounting," "two-tier deliberative framework").
- `verdicts_on_my_cases`: an array of objects, each with `case` (the case number and short label), `verdict` (one of "permissible" or "not permissible"), and `rationale` (one to two sentences explaining how the principle reaches that verdict).
- `overturned_verdicts`: an array of case labels for any of my original verdicts that the principle rejects.
- `verdicts_on_new_cases`: an array of objects, each with `case` (a short description of a new case I did not list), `verdict`, and `rationale`.
- `alternative_candidates`: an array of objects, each with `principle` (a brief statement of an alternative principle you considered), `why_rejected` (why you think it is worse than your recommended principle), and `which_verdict_it_saves` (which of my verdicts it handles better, if any).
- `hardest_case_for_principle`: which of my cases is hardest for the recommended principle, and why.
- `further_case_to_test`: a description of one additional case that would most help discriminate between your recommended principle and the best alternative candidate, with your prediction for each.
