ok, here's the thing. i keep seeing people argue about whether an LLM or a crow or a savant is really intelligent by pointing at exactly one case and then building the whole theory downstream of that. i want the spec first. You're a philosopher of cognitive science who has actually worked through this mess; take your time, brainstorm a bit before you commit, and then give me the desiderata.

The purpose that matters to me: I want a theory of intelligence that can do three jobs at once — (1) compare a human, an octopus, and a neural network without just changing the subject, (2) say whether intelligence is one thing or a family of loosely related capacities, and (3) keep us honest about the difference between being intelligent and merely doing intelligent-looking things. Adequacy is relative to those jobs, so tell me when a requirement is only needed for one of them.

Give me the list with the reasoning, not just labels:

- The cases any adequate theory has to get right, each with one sentence on why it's a requirement. At minimum use: a savant who can do calendar calculation but can't manage ordinary life; an LLM that passes a professional licensing exam and then fails a simple novel task a young child gets; a crow or octopus solving a multi-step problem it has never seen before; the Flynn effect, where scores keep rising across cohorts; and a person with high test scores and persistently terrible real-world judgment. Add any others only if they're genuinely load-bearing.
- The distinctions it can't collapse, with what goes wrong if you collapse each one. I suspect at least: performance vs. competence; fluid vs. crystallized; domain-general vs. domain-specific; intelligence vs. knowledge; and intelligence vs. rationality. Push on the ones you think are overrated.
- The empirical patterns it has to be consistent with or explain away: the positive manifold (people who do well on one cognitive test tend to do well on others), the way brain damage can knock out one ability and leave others intact, and whatever else you'd treat as non-negotiable.
- Mark wich requirements are genuine desiderata and which are just the tastes of particular camps (the g-factor people, the multiple-intelligences people, the embodied-cognition people, the AI-capabilities people). I want to know where the list would be controversial.
- The part I most want: are any two or three of these jointly unsatisfiable? Is a single scalar g compatible with the savant and dissociation cases? Is a fully domain-general account compatible with abilities breaking apart under brain damage? Tell me where the list itself is a result rather than a shopping list, and bold any pair that can't both be met.
- Then, briefly — one short paragraph each, keep it tight — how the main families fare against the list: psychometric g, multiple intelligences, computational/rational-analysis, and embodied/enactivist. The criteria are the point, so don't let the scorecard take over.
- End with the single requirement you'd bet knocks out the most candidates, and why.

A table is fine for the requirements if it helps, but I want the reasoning in prose around it.
