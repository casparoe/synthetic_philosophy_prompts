I've been teaching an undergraduate seminar on markets and morals, and I've realized that my own views on when markets are appropriate are a bit of a mess. I'd like you to help me work out what principle, if any, I'm actually operating with. Here are my gut verdicts on a range of cases — I'm not claiming these are correct, just that they're what I find myself thinking when I try to be honest.

1. Selling a kidney for transplant, even in a well-regulated system with protections against exploitation: not fine.
2. Selling blood plasma for medical use: fine. (I've donated plasma myself and was paid for it, and it didn't bother me.)
3. A hardware store tripling the price of bottled water and generators in the two days before a hurricane makes landfall: not fine.
4. Uber's surge pricing kicking in during a snowstorm, doubling or tripling the fare: fine, or at least much less bad than case 3, though I struggle to say why.
5. Selling your vote in a national election: not fine.
6. Paying someone to take a job as a deep-sea welder, where the fatality rate is significantly higher than average: fine.
7. Paying for a FastPass to skip the line at an amusement park: fine.
8. Paying someone to hold your spot in a DMV queue so you can sleep in and then take their place: not fine.
9. A woman being paid to carry a pregnancy for an infertile couple, with proper medical care and legal contracts: fine, though I feel a little unease I can't quite place.
10. Paying healthy volunteers to participate in a Phase I drug trial where there's a non-trivial risk of serious side effects: fine, as long as the pay is commensurate and consent is informed.

What's bothering me is that cases 3 and 4 seem structurally similar — both involve raising prices sharply when demand spikes due to an emergency — but I judge them differently. The same goes for 1 and 2: both involve selling something from your body, but one feels unacceptable and the other doesn't. And 7 and 8 both involve paying to avoid waiting in line, yet my verdicts diverge. I'm not sure whether these inconsistencies mean I'm missing a subtler principle that distinguishes the cases, or whether I need to revise some of my verdicts.

I'd like you to take on the role of a moral philosopher who works on commodification and the limits of markets. Please think carefully and try to identify a principle, or a small set of principles, that captures as many of my verdicts as possible. For each candidate principle, walk me through which cases it gets right and which it gets wrong, and explain why it succeeds or fails on the cases that seem to pair up problematically. If no single principle captures all ten, tell me which verdicts I'd have to revise to achieve consistency, and make the case for why those are the ones to give up rather than others. Finally, apply the best candidate principle to a case I haven't listed: the sale of admission slots at elite universities. What does the principle say about it, and does that verdict fit with the overall pattern of my judgments, or would it force me to reconsider something?
