I'm a PhD student in political philosophy, and I'm drafting a dissertation chapter on constitutional precommitment. Before I write the chapter, I would like an audit of the set of views I have been developing — I want to know whether they hang together, and if not, what the most economical fix would be.

Here are the commitments I am working with:

(1) Constitutional precommitment — entrenching rights, supermajority amendment thresholds, and similar mechanisms — is justified on essentially the same normative grounds as individual Ulysses contracts. In both cases, the agent (individual or collective) recognizes a susceptibility to short-sighted or passion-driven decision-making and binds itself to a more reflective course, thereby acting more autonomously over time rather than less.

(2) The binding is legitimate only if the precommitment mechanism was itself adopted through a sufficiently democratic process. A constitution imposed by a narrow elite or a foreign power cannot generate the same normative force, whatever the merits of its content.

(3) The familiar dead-hand objection — that constitutional provisions bind people who never consented to them — is overrated. So long as an amendment procedure remains available, the living generation is not trapped; it is merely required to act through a more deliberative channel. The fact that amendment requires supermajorities does not by itself make the arrangement illegitimate.

(4) Judicial review is a legitimate mechanism for enforcing constitutional precommitments, even when it results in striking down legislation supported by a current legislative majority. The court, in such cases, is not substituting its own policy preferences but is enforcing a prior democratic choice to remove certain matters from ordinary majoritarian decision-making.

(5) The counter-majoritarian difficulty is real but not fatal. Its severity depends on whether the constitutional provision being enforced is a genuine precommitment — a structural constraint on the decision-making process — rather than a substantive policy choice dressed up as one. The harder cases are those where a provision does both, and here the difficulty genuinely bites.

(6) There is a structural disanalogy between individual and collective precommitment. An individual who has bound herself can always unbind herself — Ulysses can ask his crew to untie him — because the decision to reverse the precommitment is hers alone. Constitutional amendment, by contrast, is a collective act; no single individual, nor even a simple majority where supermajority thresholds apply, can initiate reversal. The individual citizen is thus bound by a precommitment she cannot unilaterally undo.

I would like you to go through these six commitments and identify which pairs are in genuine tension, which are only apparently in tension, and which are fully compatible. For each genuine tension, please rank it by severity, explain exactly what the conflict consists in, and identify the least costly revision that would resolve it — which commitment would have to be modified or dropped, and what the philosophical cost of doing so would be. I am not confident about where the problems lie and would prefer not to tip my hand; I want the analysis to identify the trouble spots from scratch. Please write the response in American English.
