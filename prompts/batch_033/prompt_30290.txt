I'm working through Langdon Winner's essay "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" (1980) for a course on the philosophy of technology, and I've been using a study guide to check my understanding. The guide summarizes the essay this way:

"Winner argues that technologies are never neutral tools: every artifact has a politics built into it. His most famous example is Robert Moses, the New York planner who had the overpasses on Long Island's parkways built deliberately low so that buses couldn't pass underneath; the result was that only people with cars — mostly white and well-off — could reach Jones Beach. Winner's point is that the discrimination is in the hardware itself: the bridge does its political work automatically, just by existing, regardless of anyone's intentions or the surrounding society. He then makes a stronger claim that some technologies are inherently political — nuclear power, because a reactor accident is so catastrophic, can only be managed by a centralized, hierarchical authority, so choosing nuclear power means choosing an authoritarian political structure. His other example is the mechanical tomato harvester, developed by the University of California, which worked well for large farms and badly for small ones, so adopting it settled a political fight in favor of agribusiness. The lesson Winner draws is that we should stop treating technology as a neutral tool and instead make the political choices embodied in our machines explicit."

Something about this summary feels off to me. In particular, I doubt Winner actually claims that every artifact has a politics, and the phrase "the discrimination is in the hardware itself" strikes me as stronger than what he can establish — but I'm not confident, and I don't want to misjudge either the guide or the essay. Could you go through the summary claim by claim and tell me which parts a careful reader of Winner would accept, which are subtle misreadings or overstatements, and what Winner himself would likely object to? I'd also like to know whether the closing lesson — that we should make the political choices embodied in our machines explicit — actually follows from Winner's argument once it is stated accurately, and whether the summary blurs a distinction Winner is trying to draw between the Robert Moses example and the nuclear power example.
