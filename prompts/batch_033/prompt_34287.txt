In arguments about whether ordinary citizens should have an equal say in government, I keep hearing the old ship-of-state analogy, and I want to know how much weight it can carry. The argument goes like this: a ship should be steered by a trained navigator, not by a vote of the passengers on every turn of the helm, because the passengers lack the skill and would wreck the vessel. Likewise, a state faces economic and security crises, and ordinary voters lack the relevant expertise, so political decisions should be left to those who know how to steer rather than to the ballot box. I would like an audit of this analogy. What maps onto what — the navigator, the passengers, the crew, the destination, the storms, the skill of navigation — when we carry the image over to a political community? Where does the mapping break, and which breakdowns matter for the intended conclusion that the franchise should be restricted to the knowledgeable? What does the analogy quietly import about shared ends, the objectivity of the relevant expertise, and accountability that may not hold for a state? Once the disanalogies are on the table, does any version of the conclusion survive, and is there a better analogy for what should and should not be delegated to experts? Finally, please say how confident you are in your assessment and which point you are least sure of.
