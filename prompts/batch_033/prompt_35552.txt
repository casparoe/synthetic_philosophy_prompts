So I'm a therapist, been practicing about 9 years, and I do a lot of dream work — the kind where you treat the dream like something the client actually went through and you unpack it together. But here's the thing that's been messing with me. About four months ago I started having these super vivid lucid dreams, the kind where you know you're dreaming and you can actually make decisions. And when I wake up I genuinely cannot tell whether I was *having an experience* or whether my brain just assembled the whole thing at the moment of waking and handed it to me as a memory. It feels like a memory of something that happened. But I can't tell if anything actually happened.

This has kind of thrown my clinical work into question, because I've been treating dream reports as if they're reports of experiences — the client experienced the dream, we work with it like we'd work with a memory. But now I'm not sure that's always right. I fell down a rabbit hole reading about the Malcolm vs. Dennett debate on whether dreams are experiences (Malcolm says no, you're asleep, you can't make judgments, the whole thing is a story you tell when you wake up; Dennett says yes, they're experiences, you just can't report them while they're happening). And I realized I need an actual framework for this in session.

Can you write me a decision procedure I can use? Something I can run in my head while the client is talking. The procedure should help me decide, for a given dream report, whether to treat it as a report of an experience (and do the usual dream-work) or whether to treat it as a waking narrative the client is constructing right now (and shift to a different approach — like, what does this story you're telling me right now say about what's on your mind).

Constraints: I have 50-minute sessions, I'm not a philosopher, and the steps need to be clear enough that I can actually use them. The stakes aren't life-or-death but I could waste a lot of sessions going down the wrong path. Give me the steps in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure stops with "treat as narrative" and where it stops with "treat as experience," and flag the points where the procedure has to hand off to clinical judgment because the philosophy genuinely can't decide.

Then run it on three dream reports:

1. Client says they dreamed they were back in their childhood home, searching every room for their mother and not finding her. They felt panicked, and they remember thinking "she must be in the basement, check the basement." They woke up before finding her.

2. Client says they dreamed they were a tree. Not a person turning into a tree, just a tree, standing there. They felt peaceful. They don't remember making any decisions or having any thoughts.

3. Client says they dreamed they were at their wedding but their spouse's face kept changing into different people's faces — their ex, their boss, a stranger. They felt confused but also kind of detached, like "oh, this again."

And be straight with me: give me a case where the procedure tells me to do something I'm not gonna like, and tell me what the procedure just can't handle.
