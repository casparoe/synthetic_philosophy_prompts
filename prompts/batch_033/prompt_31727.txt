I've been thinking for a while now about a puzzle that comes up when you try to say what it is for something to be good or valuable. The view I've settled on goes like this: to call something good is, at bottom, to say that certain ways of responding to it — admiring it, wanting it, respecting it, being pleased by it — are the responses that fit it. But I've noticed that not every reason to have a response is a reason that makes the response fit. Here's the kind of case I have in mind. Suppose someone offers you a fortune to be delighted by a painting you find clumsy and ugly. You now have a reason to be delighted by it — a strong one, even. But the painting hasn't become any better. The reason you have is about the benefits of being delighted, not about the painting's merits. Or suppose that believing your friend is trustworthy would make your life easier, and you know it. That's a reason to believe it, but it's not the kind of reason that bears on whether he actually is trustworthy. I've been calling these two kinds of reasons object-reasons and state-reasons: object-reasons are features of the thing itself that make a response appropriate to it, and state-reasons are features of what you'd get out of having the response. I hold that this distinction is real, that it's inescapable, and that any attempt to understand value without it will end up saying that a painting really is better just because someone paid you to like it.

What I'd like to know is: what would count against this position? I want the arguments, examples, or theoretical pressures that would show the distinction is illusory, or that it can't be drawn in a principled way, or that it's not doing the work I think it is. I'd like the defeaters sorted by how serious they are — from fatal to merely awkward — and I want to know which of them a defender of this kind of view would concede as genuine problems and which they'd dismiss. I'd also like an honest assessment of whether the position has been insulated against counterexample by adding qualifications, and if so, whether that's a problem for a view of this kind.
