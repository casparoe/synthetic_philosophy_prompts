Write a sorting quiz for a philosophy-of-probability blog whose readers are comfortable with basic probability theory and computability but are not specialists in the logical induction literature. The quiz is titled "Which position on logical uncertainty do you actually hold?" and should contain eight to ten questions, each presenting a concrete case or claim with three or four answer options. No question should ask respondents to identify a label, and no option should be recognizable as "the logical induction answer" or "the algorithmic probability answer" in so many words. The quiz must sort readers by what they say about cases, not by which named view they prefer.

The scoring key must map answer patterns onto the following seven positions, each of which should be attainable as a result:

1. The logical-omniscience idealization: degrees of belief properly attach to empirical possibilities; a fully rational agent already knows all logical consequences, so ideal theory has no distinct phenomenon of logical uncertainty to model.
2. Subjective Bayesianism with computational constraints: any probabilistically coherent assignment over logical sentences is permissible; logical uncertainty is managed by ordinary Bayesian conditioning on observed computations, with no uniquely rational prior.
3. Objective Bayesianism / logical probability: there is a unique or near-unique rational prior over logical sentences, fixed by symmetry or invariance considerations rather than by program length or subjective coherence.
4. Algorithmic probability: the rational prior over mathematical statements is the Solomonoff-style algorithmic prior, on which probability is tied to program length — shorter programs receive more weight — rather than to symmetry or coherence.
5. Logical induction: the rational standard is the logical induction criterion — a computable learner whose prices cannot be exploited by any efficiently computable trading strategy — with no stronger requirement of a "correct" prior.
6. No-probabilities-over-logic: a mathematical statement is true or false, not probable; assigning a degree of belief to an open conjecture is a category mistake, not a hard problem.
7. Logical uncertainty as ordinary empirical or computational uncertainty: what looks like uncertainty about a mathematical claim is really uncertainty about a physical or computational process, one's own or a machine's, and is no different in kind from other empirical uncertainty.

At least one question must be built around each of the following cases: (a) a bet offered before a long but purely deductive derivation has been carried out; (b) Goldbach's conjecture or another open number-theoretic statement; (c) P versus NP; (d) the halting status of a specific long-running program; and (e) a deterministic but computationally intractable physical prediction, such as a chaotic system's trajectory. Include one question designed to separate objective Bayesianism from algorithmic probability — for example, a case in which symmetry and program-length considerations pull in opposite directions — and one designed to separate logical induction from subjective Bayesianism with computational constraints — for example, a sequence of bets whose exploitability depends on a trader's ability to predict the respondent's future updates. Include one question that separates the logical-omniscience idealization from no-probabilities-over-logic, for example by asking whether a bounded human's 0.7 credence in Goldbach's conjecture is a non-ideal approximation or a category mistake. Choose the remaining questions so that every pair of positions that is in fact separable is pulled apart by at least one question.

Construct the scoring key from the space of answer combinations first: lay out the combinations the questions allow, locate the seven positions within that space, and only then attach names to clusters. After the scoring key, add a brief diagnostic note that (i) identifies any pair of positions the quiz cannot cleanly separate and explains why; (ii) notes any answer combination that is internally coherent but has no named defender, and says whether that position is empty because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored; and (iii) states whether logical induction and algorithmic probability are better treated as rivals or as answers to different questions. Write the quiz so that a reader can take it end to end without having heard of any of the seven positions beforehand.
