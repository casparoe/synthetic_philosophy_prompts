I am a novelist, and I am currently researching a book set in a near-future Britain in which the Treasury's method for pricing the future becomes a matter of public controversy. I keep running into a practice that puzzles me, and I would like a philosopher's reconstruction of what it assumes rather than a summary of the economics. When governments and international bodies weigh the costs of climate policy, they convert harms that will fall on people a century from now into present values by applying a fixed annual discount rate, so that a death or a lost livelihood in 2125 counts in the official arithmetic as a small fraction of an equivalent death or lost livelihood today. The rate is presented as a technical parameter with, as I understand it, two main ingredients: one reflects the expectation that future people will be richer than we are, and the other is pure time preference — the sheer fact that a harm lies in the future being treated as a reason to count it for less. I can see how the first ingredient might be defended by appealing to diminishing marginal utility, or to a reluctance to transfer resources from the poorer present to the richer future. But the second ingredient seems to be doing a great deal of moral work while wearing the clothes of bookkeeping. I have been trying to write a scene in which a Treasury official explains to a young activist why her grandchildren's suffering is worth less than her own, and I have realised I cannot tell whether the official is expressing a philosophical position or merely applying a neutral technical convention.

What I would like to know is this: what theory of intergenerational justice does the practice of using a positive pure rate of time preference presuppose? Does it presuppose that later people matter less than earlier people simply because of when they live, or can it be fully justified by uncertainty about whether future people will exist, by the opportunity cost of capital, or by the thought that our successors will be better able to absorb losses? Is the practice consistent with the way the same governments speak of the rights of future generations in their preambles, treaties and constitutions? And if the presupposed view were replaced with a strict temporal egalitarianism — the view that a harm is a harm whenever it occurs — what would have to change in the cost-benefit arithmetic, and would the practice collapse or merely change its parameters? I am less interested in the name of any position than in the carving: what the practice is genuinely committed to, where it is merely compatible with several rival views, and where the moral content has been hidden inside a rate. I would be grateful if you would write your answer in British English.
