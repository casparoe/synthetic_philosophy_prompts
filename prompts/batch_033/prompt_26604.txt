So I'm a novelist, not a philosopher — my day job is making up people and putting them in rooms together — but I've been going down a rabbit hole on consciousness because my next book has a character who's a neuroscientist and I want to get the texture right. I've read a bunch of Dennett, Metzinger, Graziano, some Clark, and I've ended up with a set of views that feel right to me but I'm not sure they all fit in the same drawer. Here's roughly where I am:

(1) Consciousness is a user illusion — the brain generates a simplified, schematic model of its own activity, and what we experience as "consciousness" is that model, not the underlying processing. The desktop metaphor, basically.

(2) The self is a narrative construct, a kind of center of gravity the brain posits to make sense of its own outputs. There's no Cartesian theater, no place where it all comes together.

(3) First-person phenomenology — what it's actually like from the inside — is still our primary and most reliable source of data about consciousness. If your theory says something that flatly contradicts what it's like to be me, your theory has a problem.

(4) The conscious/unconscious distinction is real and matters. Some mental states are conscious, some aren't, and this isn't just a difference in degree of reportability or whatever — there's something going on there that a theory owes us an account of.

(5) Calling it an "illusion" doesn't mean it doesn't exist. Illusions are real phenomena — a mirage is a real optical phenomenon, it's just not what it naively seems to be. Consciousness is real, it's just not what it naively seems to be.

(6) Free will is in the same boat: a user illusion, the brain's simplified representation of its own decision-making, and we're not getting rid of it any more than we're getting rid of the visual experience of a stable world by knowing about saccades.

I'm worried that (3) and (4) are doing a lot of work that (1) and (2) make hard to cash out. If consciousness is a user illusion, and the self is a narrative construct, then what exactly is first-person phenomenology data *about*, and what makes the conscious/unconscious distinction more than just another part of the illusion? But I also don't want to give up (3) because honestly what else do we have — and I don't want to give up (4) because it seems like the whole conversation falls apart if we can't mark that distinction. Plus (6) feels like it should just fall out of the rest but I'm not actually sure it does.

So: go through these and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension and how severe the tension is. I want to know which ones only *look* like they conflict, which ones actually do, and what the cheapest revision is. I have a feeling (1) and (3) are the real problem but I keep going back and forth on whether they actually conflict or whether I'm just thinking about (3) wrong. Lead with your bottom line — which commitment has to go, or which needs the most rethinking — and then walk me through it, no preamble, no restating the question.
