My mother was recently diagnosed with early-stage Alzheimer's, and I have become her healthcare proxy. I am trying to decide how much weight to give the advance directive she wrote years ago, refusing life-sustaining treatment in late-stage dementia, when she now sometimes says she wants to go on living. A colleague has pressed the following argument on me; I cannot find the exit, and its conclusion is one I cannot accept.

(1) Once a patient can no longer decide for herself, her medical care should be governed by her present interests and preferences.
(2) An earlier self's directive can override those present interests only if the earlier self and the present patient are the same person.
(3) What makes someone the same person over time is psychological continuity — the persistence of enough memories, values, projects, and character traits to constitute the particular person she is.
(4) In advanced dementia, that continuity is largely destroyed.
(5) So the earlier self and the present patient are not the same person in the sense that matters.
(6) Therefore the advance directive has no authority, and the patient's present interests should decide.
(7) The same reasoning applies to any commitment whose authority depends on personal identity: a promise, a contract, a debt, or responsibility for an earlier act would lose its grip after any sufficiently large psychological change.
(8) That is unacceptable; we do, and should, hold people to their promises and hold them responsible for their earlier acts despite psychological change.

Which premise or step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would most like to keep (1) and (8), but I suspect the argument makes them hard to hold together.
