So I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind and decision theory, and two people in my cohort got into it at the pub last week — I keep turning it over and I honestly can't sort out who's right. It was about whether transformative experiences (having a kid, going deaf, converting to a religion, etc.) pose a genuine problem for rational choice, or whether the whole thing is overblown.

The back-and-forth went roughly like this:

(1) Mara started by saying she thinks L.A. Paul is basically right: for genuinely transformative decisions, you can't assign subjective probabilities to what the outcome will be like, because the experience changes your core preferences in ways you can't model from the outside. So standard expected utility theory just doesn't apply, and calling the decision "rational" or "irrational" is a category mistake — the framework lacks the resources to even formulate the question.

(2) Dev pushed back that this is way too strong. He said: look, people make transformative decisions all the time and they're not paralysed. They have kids, switch careers, emigrate. They use testimony from others who've done it, they use their current values as a rough guide, and they turn out fine. If Paul's argument entails that all those decisions are irrational, that's a reductio of the argument, not a deep fact about decision-making.

(3) Mara replied that Dev was missing the point about testimony. Testimony doesn't help, she said, because you can't know whether post-transformation-you will share the values that make the testimony positive or negative. A parent tells you "it's the most meaningful thing I've ever done" — but that judgment is made from inside the transformation, using values you don't currently have. You can't import it into your current decision framework without begging the question.

(4) Dev said: okay but that's true of literally any experience you haven't had. I don't know exactly what it's like to eat durian, but I can still decide whether to try it. You're drawing a bright line where there's actually a continuum. Some experiences change you a little, some a lot — but decision theory handles uncertainty about your own future preferences just fine; that's what models of changing tastes are for.

(5) Mara came back with: no, the difference is that for transformative experiences, the change isn't just in your preferences — it's in the very standards by which you'd evaluate whether the decision was good. After becoming a parent, you might think the sleep deprivation and loss of freedom were completely worth it. Before, you'd have thought that package deal sounded terrible. There's no Archimedean point from which to compare the two evaluations. That's not on a continuum with trying durian.

(6) Dev paused and then said: fine, but then Paul's argument proves too much in a different way — it applies to any decision where your future self might have different values, which includes basically every major life choice. If that means expected utility theory is inapplicable to all of them, then expected utility theory was never meant to handle those cases and we should just use a different normative framework, not conclude the decisions are arational.

At that point they ordered another round and I couldn't tell who'd won. Can you go through this move by move and say who's right about what? I'm especially interested in whether Mara's point (5) about there being no Archimedean point actually distinguishes transformative experiences from ordinary preference change, and whether Dev's reductio in (6) lands — or whether they're just talking past each other somehow.
