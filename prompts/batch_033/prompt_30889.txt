You are a professional philosopher specializing in formal ethics and population ethics, and you are fluent in the working vocabulary of a research mathematician: measure theory, functional analysis, summability methods, order theory, and set-theoretic constructions such as ultrafilters and ultrapowers. I am a research mathematician with exactly that background — I work comfortably with σ-algebras, measures and integrals, conditionally convergent series, Banach limits, and cardinal arithmetic — but I have no background in the philosophical literature. I want you to restate the central problems of infinite population ethics in my vocabulary as faithfully as you can, and then tell me what did not survive the translation.

Take your time and think through each mapping carefully before writing. A good answer resists loose analogy: it should track, item by item, where a piece of mathematics carries the ethical content, where it distorts it, and where the ethical claim has no mathematical counterpart at all. Do not quietly import assumptions of my framework and present them as translations; flag every point at which a piece of standard mathematics is doing normative work.

Translate the following five positions and arguments:

1. Total utilitarianism applied to infinite populations: the value of a world is the sum of the welfare of all its inhabitants. When infinitely many lives have comparable nonzero welfare this sum diverges, and when positive and negative welfare are both infinitely present the sum may be only conditionally convergent, so that its value depends on the order in which the lives are summed.

2. Average utilitarianism and its limit-based relatives: the value of a finite world is average welfare, and the value of an infinite world is obtained by some limiting procedure over an expanding sequence of finite regions, with the result that different choices of the expanding sequence can yield different limits.

3. The Pareto principle together with the intuition that adding one more happy life to an infinite world leaves the world no better: a change that helps someone and harms no one is an improvement, yet a single added happy life against an infinite background seems to make no difference.

4. The paralysis worry: if an action changes only finitely many lives and leaves an infinite backdrop untouched, then under aggregative rules that let the infinite backdrop swamp finite changes, no finite action can be better or worse than any other.

5. Proposals to enlarge the value domain so that infinite totalities can still be compared — for example, ranking worlds with hyperreal numbers or transfinite ordinals rather than real numbers.

For each of the five, give me: (a) the parts that translate cleanly into my vocabulary; (b) the parts that translate only with distortion, and where exactly the distortion lies; (c) the parts that have no expression in my vocabulary at all.

Then write a residue report. I want to know whether the failures of translation show that my mathematical vocabulary is missing something ethically relevant, or that the philosophical claims were less precise than they looked. In particular, tell me where my standard tools commit me to a substantive ethical assumption — the choice of a limit procedure or an ultrafilter, translation invariance, σ-additivity, or the demand that value be a real number — and whether those commitments are doing hidden normative work in the original debate.
