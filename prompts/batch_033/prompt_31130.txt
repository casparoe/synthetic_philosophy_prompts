Consider a standard feature of regulatory practice in many countries. When a government agency must decide whether a proposed safety or environmental rule is worth its costs, it monetizes the benefits, including the value of deaths prevented, and then discounts benefits that arrive in the future by an annual rate, commonly between 3 and 7 percent. At a 7 percent rate, a death prevented one hundred years from now counts for less than one percent of a death prevented next year; even at 3 percent it counts for only about a nickel on the dollar. The same governments, in other contexts, require nuclear waste repositories to isolate material for tens of thousands of years, adopt climate targets for the end of the century, and maintain sovereign wealth funds intended to benefit citizens not yet born. Some philosophers hold that what matters most, morally, is how our choices shape the far future, since the far future may contain enormously many lives; the discounting practice seems to be in direct tension with that thought. What theory of intergenerational value, and of the moral status of future people, does the discounting practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory carefully, distinguishing what the practice strictly presupposes from what merely fits well with it: in particular, does the discount rate embody the view that the welfare of future people matters less simply because they live later, or can it be fully explained as an account of the opportunity cost of capital, which would presuppose no such view? Assess whether the practice is consistent with the long-horizon policies mentioned above, and spell out what would have to change in an agency's calculation if it adopted the principle that the mere timing of a life, considered in itself, makes no difference to its value.
