I've been putting together my views on precommitment, autonomy, and personal identity for a discussion group, and I want a consistency check before I defend the package. I hold the following:

1. A currently competent adult's informed and settled refusal of life-sustaining treatment should be respected, even when others think the decision is mistaken.

2. An advance directive in which a competent person refuses life-sustaining treatment in the event of later severe dementia should generally be honored, even when the later self is content and actively resists the treatment.

3. Ulysses contracts are legitimate: a person may bind their future self to an earlier considered decision when they foresee their future self's resolve weakening.

4. The later self's current experience is morally weighty. If a demented person is content and becomes distressed when treatment is administered, that distress matters, and it should not be dismissed as noise from a self that no longer counts.

5. Personal identity is largely a matter of psychological continuity: a later self that shares few memories, values, and plans with the earlier self is only weakly the same person, if at all.

Go through these and tell me which pairs genuinely pull against each other, which only appear to, and how severe each real tension is. For each genuine tension, say what would have to give and what the cheapest revision would be. I want a ranked audit rather than a list that flags every pair.
