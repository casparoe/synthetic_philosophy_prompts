Act as a philosopher of biology known for clear reconstructions of evolutionary theory. I am preparing a two-page handout for a graduate seminar on the units of selection debate, and I want a decision tree that sorts a reader by what they accept about the causal structure of evolution, not by which label they prefer. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names: first work out the branching questions and which combinations of answers are coherent, then locate the named positions in that space. The leaves must include at least the gene's-eye view, organism-level selection, multilevel selection that treats group selection as a real level rather than a shorthand, and species selection. Each branch question should use a concrete case or claim a student can answer without already knowing the terminology, such as a segregation-distorter allele that spreads while lowering organismic fitness, a trait that is costly within groups but raises group persistence, or a species-level trait such as geographic range affecting extinction rate; no branch question should name any of the positions. Pay particular attention to whether the tree can separate multilevel selection from species selection, since both place selection above the organism but for different reasons, and flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot cleanly distinguish. Briefly diagnose any unoccupied combination: is it empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored? Keep the final tree to at most eight leaves and six branch questions. Start by brainstorming candidate diagnostic questions and the dimensions they track before writing the final tree.
