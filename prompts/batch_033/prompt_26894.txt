So I got pulled into an argument about the color phi thing and I cant find the exit. The setup: you flash a red dot on the left, then a green dot on the right, with the timing done right, and people report seeing one dot glide across the screen and turn from red to green about halfway along. The weird part is that the dot seems to turn green before the green flash has actually happened. So the brain has to be using information from later to shape what you seem to have experienced earlier.

My friend lays it out like this. (1) There are only two ways the brain could do this. Either it edits the signal before you ever become conscious of it, so you never really consciously saw the red dot sitting there; what consciousness got shown was already the cleaned-up moving-and-changing-color version. He calls this the Stalinesque option, like a show trial where the evidence is doctored before the audience sees it. (2) Or you did consciously see the red dot, and then the green dot, but the brain rewrites the memory afterwards, so you only ever remember the tidy moving dot. That's the Orwellian option, like the records being altered after the fact. (3) No possible experiment could tell these two apart, because any test you run just produces another stream of data that the brain could either edit before consciousness or after, and each story can always be adjusted to fit whatever the test shows. (4) If no possible evidence can distinguish two hypotheses, then there is no fact of the matter which one is true. (5) So for the color phi case, there is no fact of the matter about whether you really had the moving-dot experience or just have a doctored memory of one.

Then he pushes it further. (6) The same reasoning applies to any situation where we talk about someone's experience versus their later memory of it — a patient who says they felt no pain during surgery, a witness who swears they saw the attacker's face. All we ever get is the later report, so by (3) and (4) there is no fact of the matter about whether they actually experienced it or are confabulating after the fact. And that's where I get off the train, because it seems like he has just proven that the experience/memory distinction is empty everywhere, not just in a lab.

I tried saying the flaw is in (6), since ordinary cases usually have other evidence — surgical records, other witnesses, whatever. But he says (3) and (4) already handle that: any "other evidence" is itself just more data the brain could have edited before or after consciousness. So I'm stuck. Which step should I actually reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd like to keep the experience/memory distinction for ordinary cases, and I'd also like to keep that the brain does sometimes edit after the fact — but I cant see how to stop the argument between (5) and (6) without giving up one of those.
