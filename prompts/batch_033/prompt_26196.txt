I'm a clinical ethicist at a teaching hospital, I work maybe four or five consults a week — end-of-life stuff, treatment refusals, the occasional resource fight. And I keep running into the same basic problem: when am I supposed to treat someone else's moral judgment as something I should just defer to, the way I'd defer to a cardiologist about an ECG? 

Sometimes its obvious. A patients family understands what the patient would have wanted better than I do — that seems like a straightforward case for deference. Other times it feels like moral deference is just a category mistake, like I'm outsourcing the very thing I was consulted to do. And then there's the murky middle: the attending who's been doing this for thirty years and has deeply settled views about, say, truth-telling. He's thought about it more than I have. Does that make him a moral expert I should defer to? Or is moral expertise not like that?

I want you to write me a decision procedure I can actually use — a sequence of questions I can run at 2 AM, that ends in one of three outputs: defer, don't defer, or escalate to the full committee. I need it to distinguish at least three kinds of thing people might be offering me:
- knowledge of values (the family knows the patient)
- moral reasoning or argument (someone's thought harder or longer)
- moral perception (the nurse who's been bedside sees something I'm missing)

Then apply it to two cases. First: a family demands we keep going with aggressive treatment that the whole team agrees is futile; the family says they know the patient would want everything tried. Second: an attending with a thirty-year career tells me it is categorically wrong to conceal a terminal diagnosis even when the family, for cultural reasons, is begging us not to disclose it, and he expects me to back him.

Flag where the procedure is most likely to give the wrong answer, and tell me what kind of case would make you substantially revise it.
