Since retiring from a career in experimental physics, I have been reading my way through the philosophy of mind, and I keep running into a move that strikes me as too quick. In my reading group, and in at least one popular book on the shelf, someone will cite von Neumann's short posthumous book The Computer and the Brain as authority for the claim that the brain simply is a computer, that the remaining differences are mere implementation details, and that questions about machine intelligence or whole-brain emulation can therefore be treated as engineering problems. My own memory of that book, assembled after his death from the Silliman lectures he never delivered, is that von Neumann was at least as interested in the respects in which the nervous system is unlike the stored-program digital machines of his day: he seems to have emphasized that the brain operates both digitally and in an analog or chemical mode, that it is massively parallel where the machines of his time were serial, and that it achieves reliable behavior from unreliable components. I would like a careful audit of this analogy as it is actually deployed, not a generic discussion of brain-as-computer. Please (1) reconstruct, as accurately as possible, what von Neumann actually said about the brain–computer comparison and where his emphasis fell; (2) lay out an explicit correspondence table between a stored-program digital computer and the nervous system as he understood them, noting which features of each side have no clean counterpart on the other and which properties of the machine the analogy quietly imports into the brain; (3) separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from those that are merely accidents of 1950s hardware; and (4) say whether the conclusion being drawn, that the analogy licenses treating the mind as computational and whole-brain emulation as an engineering problem, survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If it does not, state what von Neumann's comparison can legitimately support, and whether some other analogy would serve the argument better.
