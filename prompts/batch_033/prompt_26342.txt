I spent forty years as a geophysicist, and throughout that career I held what I took to be a simple, almost definitional view of explanation: to explain a phenomenon is to show that it follows from general laws plus particular boundary conditions. When a glacier retreats, you explain it by appealing to the laws of heat transfer, the albedo of the surface, the ambient temperature record, and so on — plug in the initial conditions, crank the machinery, and the outcome falls out. When I retired, I turned to philosophy of history, expecting to find historians doing something analogous: explaining why the Roman Republic fell, why the Industrial Revolution began in Britain, why Napoleon lost at Waterloo, each by reference to general regularities about institutions, economies, or military strategy, perhaps less precise than those of physics but recognizably the same kind of thing. I quickly discovered this is a live and unsettled debate, and that my instinctive position has a name: the covering-law model, canonically articulated by Hempel. The rival view, which seems to hold the field among many philosophers of history and working historians, is that historical explanation is irreducibly narrative — that it proceeds not by subsuming events under laws but by constructing a coherent story, tracing contingent sequences, and rendering actions intelligible in ways that resist nomological capture because what matters is not lawlike regularity but the particular unfolding of events in context.

I find myself genuinely uncertain. The covering-law model feels right to me — it is how I was trained to think — but I am not so attached to it that I cannot imagine being shown it is wrong. So I want to ask the question squarely. Please take the role of a professional philosopher of history or philosopher of science who is sympathetic to the narrative-explanation camp. Think carefully and methodically. Start by briefly sketching what you take to be the strongest version of the covering-law position (since I want to make sure we are assessing the best case, not a straw man). Then tell me: what would count against it? I want a systematic map of its exposure. What observations about actual historical practice, what conceptual arguments, what thought experiments or counterexamples would a covering-law theorist have to reckon with? Sort these from the merely awkward to the genuinely decisive. Which ones, if any, would force a reasonable covering-law theorist to abandon the view? Are there escape routes — qualifications that preserve the letter of the position while draining it of substance — and if so, has the covering-law model already retreated into such qualifications? I would particularly value your treatment of whether the debate turns on a disagreement about what explanation is (so that the two sides end up talking past each other) or on a substantive dispute about what the world is like (so that evidence could in principle decide between them). If you think the covering-law model ultimately fails, I want to know exactly what fact about historical reality or about our cognitive relation to the past makes it fail, and whether that fact is itself contestable. I am less interested in who said what when than in what the arguments actually are, so feel free to engage the ideas directly rather than rehearsing the literature.
