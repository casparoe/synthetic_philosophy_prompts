I'm giving a department colloquium next month defending the following thesis: when we say a hypocrite "lacks standing" to blame, we are not identifying a defect in the blame's content or truth — the blame may be perfectly accurate and proportionate — nor are we merely registering that the hypocrite is in a poor epistemic position to judge. Rather, standing is a distinct normative relation between blamer and blamee, and hypocrisy undermines it because blame functions partly as a demand for moral repair, and a demand issued by someone who commits the same wrong themselves fails to constitute a genuine demand. The hypocrite's speech act misfires, in something like the Austinian sense: the conventional conditions for issuing that kind of demand are not met.

The department is roughly fifteen people. There is a cluster of Kantians who think standing is fundamentally about whether the blamer has the authority to hold others to norms they themselves flout — they will press me on whether my view collapses into theirs. There are several relational theorists working in the second-personal tradition who think the real issue is what the blamee is owed, and they will want to know why the blamee's complaint against the hypocrite is not simply that the blame is unfair. There is one philosopher of language who will want to know exactly what kind of speech act blame is and whether my appeal to Austinian misfire does any work that a simpler account of hypocrisy as a conversational defeater would not. There is a feminist philosopher who has written on how accusations of hypocrisy are weaponized to deflect legitimate moral criticism, and she will ask whether my account inadvertently gives cover to that move. There is a senior consequentialist who thinks the whole debate is confused because blame is just a tool for shaping future behavior and "standing" is a philosophers' fiction. There are several agnostics, and one Nietzschean who will want to know whether I have simply reinvented ressentiment under a more respectable name.
