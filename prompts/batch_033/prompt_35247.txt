I'm a science journalist who covers bioethics and philosophy of mind, and I sit on my hospital's ethics commitee. We've got a case that's splitting us right down the middle. Patient is a 74-year-old woman, call her M. Ten years ago, cognitively intact, she signed an advance directive refusing antibiotics, feeding tubes, the works, if she ever developed advanced dementia — she'd watched her mother go through it and was adamant she never wanted to live "like that." Fast forward: M now has moderate-to-severe Alzheimers. She doesn't recognize her daughter consistently, but she's not distressed — she enjoys sitting in the garden, listening to music, holding her granddaughter's hand. She smiles a lot. Now she's got pneumonia. With IV antibiotics she'll almost certainly recover and go back to her baseline. Without them, she'll die within days. Here's the twist: when her doctor asks gently whether she wants treatment, she says yes, she wants to get better. The advance directive says no. Her daughter, the healthcare proxy, is torn and wants the committee's gudiance. Half the committee says the advance directive is sacrosanct — the earlier competent self rules. The other half says the person in front of us right now wants to live, she's not suffering, and we should treat.

Please draft the short written opinion I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting. Recommend a concrete decision — treat or don't treat — with the reasoning laid out. Engage the strongest argument on the losing side honestly; don't just dismiss it. Structure the opinion as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion — just the five points. Think of yourself as a clinical ethicist with a philosophy background who's done this for twenty years. Before you write, take a moment to think through the hardest objection to your own recommendation and make sure your points actually answer it.
