I'm leading a two-hour reading group for political science grad students next month on the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem, and I don't want to just walk them through the proof until everyone's eyes glaze over. Most of them have seen Arrow's theorem but have never thought about strategy-proofness as a property of a voting rule. Don't explain the theorem to me — plan how I should teach it. What has to go on the board first, and what depends on what: do I need them to have a clean notion of a social choice function before I can even say what misreporting is, or does the manipulation idea come first and motivate the formalism? I'd like the one carrying example for the whole session — I was thinking three candidates with a spoiler, but tell me what the example has to have and what it has to lack, and if plurality-with-a-spoiler isn't the right vehicle, say why and give me a better one.

Then the misconceptions I should expect: people thinking strategy-proofness means nobody has an incentive to lie after the outcome is known, or that the theorem says all voting is a sham, or that single-peakedness is just a technical patch, or that a dictatorial rule is only bad because the dictator is selfish. Where in the sequence should I head each of these off? And what should I openly refuse to cover at this level — the full proof? ties, weak preferences, incomplete information? Finally, give me one question I can ask in the last ten minutes that will tell me whether they've actually got the theorem's content, as opposed to just being able to restate it.
