In my economics class we learned that when government agencies perform cost-benefit analyses of climate policy, they convert future benefits and costs into present value using a social discount rate: each year of delay shrinks a cost or benefit by a fixed percentage, on top of inflation, so that preventing a ton of emissions in 2125 counts for only a small fraction of preventing that ton today. I understand why comparing costs and benefits over time requires some way of making them commensurable, but I am confused about what the practice is quietly claiming about the people who will actually live in 2125. Does discounting future climate damages presuppose that a person's suffering in 2125 matters less than the same suffering today simply because it is further away in time? Or can the practice be read as saying something only about money, risk, or the likelihood that future people will be richer than us, without claiming that future people themselves count for less? I am also struck by an apparent tension: we would not say a child's broken arm matters less if the child is born in a hundred years, yet the arithmetic of discounting seems to treat distant harms as less important. What theory of fairness between generations does this practice presuppose, and how much of it is genuinely forced by that theory rather than merely compatible with it? Please separate what is settled among people who study this from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
