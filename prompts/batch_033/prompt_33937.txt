Consider the following examination question and a student's answer.

**Question:** In Adam Smith's *The Theory of Moral Sentiments*, what is the impartial spectator, how does it differ from the actual spectators we encounter in social life, and what role does the operation of sympathy play in the formation and functioning of the impartial spectator?

**Student's Answer:**

The impartial spectator is Smith's term for the internalized moral perspective that each person develops over the course of their moral education. It is an idealized, disinterested observer, free from the partiality that inevitably affects real spectators. Unlike actual spectators, who may be uninformed, biased, or self-interested, the impartial spectator is perfectly informed about an agent's circumstances and motives and has no personal stake in the outcome. It serves as a normative criterion for moral judgment: we evaluate our own conduct and that of others by asking whether the impartial spectator would approve.

The key difference between the impartial spectator and actual spectators is that the impartial spectator is a purely hypothetical construct. Actual spectators supply the raw data — we observe how others react to our conduct, and we adjust our behavior to secure their approval — but the impartial spectator itself is a standard we arrive at through private reflection, by abstracting away from the inconsistencies and biases of the social feedback we receive. Smith therefore offers a rationalist moral epistemology: the impartial spectator is a device of reason that corrects for the distortions introduced by the messy realities of social life.

Sympathy is the mechanism through which the impartial spectator operates. For Smith, sympathy is our capacity to share in the feelings of others by imaginatively placing ourselves in their situation. The impartial spectator judges an agent's conduct by sympathizing with the agent directly: it enters into the agent's motives and feelings, and if it can share in those sentiments, the conduct is approved. If the spectator cannot sympathize — if the agent's sentiments remain inaccessible or alien — the conduct is disapproved. Sympathy is therefore both the test of propriety and the foundation of the impartial spectator's authority.
