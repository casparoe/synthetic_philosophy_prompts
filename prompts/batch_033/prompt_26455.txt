I've been going down a rabbit hole about how the Unicode Consortium decides what gets its own code point and what doesn't, specifically for letters. Here's what's puzzling me. They treat the roman 'a' and the italic 'a' as the same character — just font variations, one code point. But they treat Latin 'a' and Cyrillic 'а' as different characters with different code points, even though they're visually identical in most fonts. And they've got Han characters where what looks like basically the same thing to a non-expert gets unified across Chinese, Japanese, and Korean, while a community proposing a slightly different shape of an existing Latin letter for their minority language sometimes gets told no because it's "just a glyph variant." What theory of letter identity does this practice presuppose? Is it shape, function, historical origin, user intention, or some mix? And is the practice actually consistent — does it apply one theory across the board, or does it kinda switch between them depending on the script and the politics of who's asking? Reconstruct the implicit metaphysics of the letterform that you'd need to believe for most of these decisions to make sense. And then tell me what would break if they adopted a pure shape-based criterion instead.
