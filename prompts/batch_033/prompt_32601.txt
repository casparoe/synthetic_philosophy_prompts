So I'm organizing this panel at a philosophy department event on whether death is bad for you, and I've somehow convinced two people who've been going back and forth on this for years to do an adverserial collaboration. One's an Epicurean — she holds that death is never bad for the person who dies, because there's no subject around to experience the harm. The other's a deprivation theorist — he holds that death is bad precisely because it deprives you of the goods you'd otherwise have lived to enjoy, and that this doesn't require experiencing the deprivation.

They've both admitted they're tired of talking past each other and are willing to try to produce a joint statement. I've been tasked with drafting it, and I'd love help thinking through what such a document should look like.

What I'm imagining:

Section 1: Numbered agreed claims. These should be substantive — claims that actually constrain the debate and that both sides can genuinely sign. Not just "death is the end of life" or filler like that. I want claims where someone reading them would think, "huh, I'm surprised they both agree on that."

Section 2: A disagreements table with these columns: (a) the precise issue, phrased neutrally; (b) the Epicurean's formulation of her position, in wording she'd accept; (c) the deprivation theorist's formulation of his position, in wording he'd accept; (d) your judgment on whether this is a genuine crux, a verbal dispute, or a downstream consequence of a deeper crux.

Then for each genuine crux: what kind of case, argument, or evidence would both sides agree bears on it? And what would each side predict that evidence would show?

I'm particularly worried that the whole thing might boil down to just one crux: whether something can be bad for you without you experiencing it (or being around to be the subject of the badness). If that's the crux, I want it stated as precisely as possible and I want to know whether there's anything both sides would accept as a test. I'm also curious whether the Lucretian symmetry point — that we don't regret our prenatal nonexistence, so why regret our postmortem nonexistence? — is a separate crux or just a rhetorical move that depends on the first one.

And honestly, if there's a part of their disagreement that you think is just verbal, I want that called out. They'll be annoyed but it's better to know before the panel.
