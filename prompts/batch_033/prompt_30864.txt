As a novelist, I keep returning to George Eliot's attempt to construct a religion of humanity: she discards Christianity's supernatural claims yet wants to preserve its moral seriousness — the conviction that we are saved, if at all, through sympathetic imagination, renunciation, and fidelity to unheroic duties. I would like a reading of Middlemarch through the lens of her religious views. Does the novel actually deliver a coherent ethic once the theology is removed, or does it quietly lean on Christian categories such as vocation, grace, and atonement that it has not earned? Trace this through two or three of the novel's strands — Dorothea, Lydgate, Bulstrode, and perhaps Farebrother or Casaubon — and be honest about where the text resists the humanist reading as well as where it supports it.
