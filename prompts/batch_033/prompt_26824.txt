Most written constitutions are harder to amend than ordinary statutes: they typically require legislative supermajorities, approval by multiple successive sessions, ratification by subnational units, or some combination of these. The standard justification is that entrenchment prevents temporary majorities from dismantling the basic framework of government in a moment of panic or partisan fervour. But this justification seems to presuppose a particular view of collective agency over time — and perhaps to rule out others. What view of the relationship between a polity's present and future decisions does constitutional entrenchment presuppose? Does it model the polity as a single agent that can bind itself, in the manner of an individual Ulysses binding himself to the mast, so that the later self is continuous with the earlier one and the precommitment is genuinely self-imposed? Or does it presuppose something else — that later majorities are epistemically or morally inferior to the founding generation, or that the demos at the moment of ratification enjoys a special normative authority that subsequent generations lack? Reconstruct the implicit theory of collective agency and intertemporal rationality that the practice requires. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it is merely compatible with, and identify the points at which the practice is philosophically underdetermined — where several rival theories of collective selfhood or democratic legitimacy would licence the same institutional design. Then tell me what would have to change in the practice if one of those rivals were substituted for the presupposed view.
