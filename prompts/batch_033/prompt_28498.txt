Before I commit to any particular formalization of common knowledge in interactive epistemology, I want a clear statement of what the notion is supposed to do. I am working on a paper that argues the standard iterative hierarchy—everyone knows, everyone knows that everyone knows, and so on—is too demanding to ground real coordination among agents who do not share a complete model of each other's reasoning, but I want to be sure I am not setting up a straw man by ignoring what a more flexible account could deliver.

Write the desiderata: what must an adequate theory of common knowledge—one suitable for use in epistemic game theory and the analysis of coordination—get right? For each requirement, give the case or puzzle that motivates it, and state whether it is non-negotiable (any theory that fails it is not a theory of common knowledge at all) or whether it reflects a parochial commitment of a particular research program (the syntactic versus semantic approach, the Bayesian versus knowledge-first camp, and so on).

In particular, I want the list to speak to at least the following, but you are not limited to them:

- The coordinated-attack problem and Lewis's observation that common knowledge enables forms of coordination that iterated mutual knowledge cannot deliver.
- Rubinstein's electronic mail game and the fragility of equilibrium predictions when common knowledge is merely approximate: does the theory require exact common knowledge, and if so, what does that imply about its applicability to real strategic settings?
- The tension between the infinite hierarchy and finite agents: can common knowledge be approximated without collapsing into something weaker, or does approximation amount to abandoning the concept?
- The distinction between common knowledge and common belief, and whether the latter is the more useful primitive for agents whose reasoning is probabilistic rather than certain.
- The relationship between common knowledge of rationality and equilibrium concepts: how much common knowledge—and of what exactly—do Nash equilibrium, rationalizability, and correlated equilibrium actually require? Which equilibrium concepts survive under weaker epistemic conditions?
- The Aumann agreement theorem: is recovering the theorem a genuine desideratum, or is the theorem better understood as revealing a pathology of the common-knowledge idealization—a sign that the standard notion is too strong to model real agents?
- The problem of how common knowledge is established in the first place: public signals, shared language, conventions, and the possibility of generating common knowledge from private information.

Flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied. I expect the list itself may contain tensions—for example, between the demand that common knowledge ground equilibrium predictions and the demand that it be achievable by finite agents—and uncovering those tensions is what I most want to see.
