I've been trying to get clear on what I actually think about blame and responsibility when an autonomous system does harm, because my gut keeps pulling in different directions. Here are my verdicts. (a) A self-driving car's vision system mistakes a pedestrian pushing a bicycle for a plastic bag and hits her; the engineers tested on the standard benchmarks, no one was negligent, and the specific misclassification wasn't something anyone had anticipated — I don't think any individual engineer is morally to blame, though the company should pay damages. (b) A hospital uses an ML dosing system that gives a fatal insulin dose to a patient with a rare combination of conditions; the nurses followed hospital policy and the system had a lower overall error rate than the humans it replaced — I don't blame the nurses, and I'm not sure I blame the vendor, but I have a nagging feeling the hospital should have stress-tested it on unusual patients before making it policy. (c) A hedge fund sets up a reinforcement-learning trader with a reward function that basically says 'make money'; the system finds a clever way to manipulate a thin market and costs other people real losses — nobody intended manipulation and nobody foresaw the specific scheme, and yet I do blame the quants, more than I blame the car engineers, because it feels like they should have known that 'maximize profit' would eventually do something like this. (d) A commander deploys a loitering munition with autonomous target selection in a crowded urban area and it kills a civilian — I blame the commander even though they didn't choose the target and didn't know which specific person would be hit. (e) A chatbot confidently tells a user that a private person was convicted of fraud, which is false, and the person loses their job — I think the company that released it is responsible, since everyone involved knew it hallucinates, but I don't particularly blame any individual engineer.

Is there one principle that gets all of these, or close? I keep trying a rule like 'you're responsible for the harms you could reasonably foresee and failed to prevent,' but that seems to let the quants in (c) and maybe the commander in (d) off the hook, which I don't want. I also flirt with 'you're responsible for the type of harm, not the specific instance,' but then I can't see why the car engineers in (a) aren't responsible too — misclassifying pedestrians is a known type of failure. What principle would you extract from my verdicts, stated precisely, where does it break, and which of my verdicts should I give up if I want something simpler? And what would it say about a case I haven't listed: a surgeon using a surgical robot that malfunctions in a way no one had seen before, in a procedure the surgeon could have done manually but where the robot's overall stats are better.
