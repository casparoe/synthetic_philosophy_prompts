I am a PhD student in moral philosophy, working on the ethics of deception, and I would like you to produce a careful philosophical dialogue that I can use as a teaching and discussion text.

Please act as a professional philosopher specialising in the ethics of language and testimony, with an even-handed command of the debate about whether lying is morally worse than merely misleading. Begin by brainstorming the strongest arguments and hardest cases on each side, and only then write. Think carefully about each character's reasoning, and ensure that neither is a strawman: each should be able to restate the other's position in a form the other would accept, and each should respond to the other's actual points rather than talking past them.

Write a dialogue between two characters, 'A' and 'B', about whether there is a morally important difference between lying and misleading.

Character A defends the view that lying is, in general, a distinct and more serious wrong. A's core idea should be roughly this: when one asserts something, one gives one's word — one offers a kind of guarantee or warranty of truth — so a lie is a betrayal of the hearer's trust in the speaker's testimony. A merely misleading utterance says something true and leaves the hearer to draw the false conclusion; it manipulates, but it does not break that same assurance.

Character B defends the view that there is no deep moral difference. B's core idea should be roughly this: what matters is the intention to induce a false belief and the harm done to the hearer's agency, not the linguistic means by which it is done. Misleading can therefore be exactly as wrong as lying, and sometimes worse, because the misleading speaker can disclaim responsibility ('I never said that') and in effect makes the hearer complicit in their own deception.

The dialogue should test these positions against concrete cases, including at least the following three:

(1) A witness, asked whether they saw the defendant enter the building on the night of the fire, truthfully replies, 'I saw him leave the building before the fire started', knowing the hearer will infer that the defendant could not have started it.

(2) A politician, asked whether they have ever accepted illicit campaign donations, answers with a carefully worded true statement designed to leave the opposite impression.

(3) A doctor, asked directly about a patient's prognosis, tells the patient a true fact about survival rates, selected and framed so that the patient forms a falsely reassuring view.

As the dialogue develops, the characters should engage with at least the following questions, without merely name-dropping the concepts:

- whether the wrong of lying is best understood as a betrayal of an assurance given in assertion, while the wrong of misleading is best understood as manipulation;
- whether misleading also exploits the hearer's reliance on conversational implicatures, and whether that is as much a breach of trust as asserting a falsehood;
- whether the hearer's having drawn the false inference themselves reduces the speaker's responsibility, or instead makes the deception more insidious;
- whether the misleading speaker's ability to deny having lied is a mitigating feature, an aggravating feature, or morally irrelevant;
- whether any asymmetry between lying and misleading is explained by the norms governing assertion, or is merely a contingent social convention;
- whether there are cases in which misleading is clearly worse than lying and cases in which lying is clearly worse than misleading, and what the existence of both kinds of case shows.

The characters should keep in view that the question is about comparative wrongness, not about whether either form of deception is ever permissible. They should press each other on the hardest cases, concede where the other side has exposed a weakness, and try to isolate exactly where their disagreement lies by the end. It is acceptable for them to converge on a narrower claim, or to conclude that the dispute partly rests on different pictures of what assertion is; but the closing exchange should state precisely what each would need the other to concede.

The finished dialogue should be roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words, written in British English throughout (for example, 'colour' rather than 'color', 'defence' rather than 'defense'). Do not include citations, footnotes, or references to named authors; the arguments should stand on their own.
