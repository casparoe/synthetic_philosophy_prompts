I'm a retired geologist who has recently taken up the study of philosophy, and I'd like you to think through a problem I've encountered. Please approach this as a scholar of ancient philosophy with a careful command of Plato's dialogues — take your time, consider the relevant passages, and then give me your best judgment.

I've joined a monthly philosophy discussion group in my town. The group's organizer, David, has a firm view about how our meetings should be run. He insists we all drink wine during the discussions — not heavily, a glass or two — and he defends this on philosophical grounds. He says the practice is authentically Socratic, that Plato himself endorsed drinking wine as an aid to philosophical conversation, and that the Symposium is the model: philosophy done properly, among friends, with wine. When I once expressed hesitation, he added that the Laws develops a detailed argument that wine, rightly used, loosens the soul and opens people to truth.

I've now read the Republic, the Apology, and most of the Symposium, and something about David's claim strikes me as off. In the Symposium, as I recall, the participants quite deliberately agree to drink lightly — precisely because they want a serious discussion rather than a rowdy drinking party. And in the Republic, Socrates seems to forbid drinking outright for the guardians. But I'm new to this, and I don't yet trust my own reading. David's confidence makes me wonder whether I'm missing a deeper thread across the dialogues that he's drawing on.

So here's my question: is David's appeal to Plato defensible? If you were to lay out what Plato actually says — across the dialogues that bear on the matter — about the role of wine in the pursuit of philosophical understanding, what would the picture look like? And in the end, should I drop my skepticism and pour myself a glass, or is David distorting the texts?
