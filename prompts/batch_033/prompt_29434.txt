I spent my career in experimental physics, and since retiring I've been working through the philosophy of perception. I keep returning to an argument in the cognitive penetration literature that I cannot decide whether to accept or reject. It runs roughly as follows:

(1) If cognitive penetration occurs, then a subject's antecedent beliefs, expectations, or desires can causally influence the content or phenomenal character of her perceptual experience.
(2) If perceptual experience is causally influenced by antecedent beliefs, then perception cannot provide evidence that is independent of those beliefs — the experience is, at least in part, a reflection of what the subject already thinks.
(3) If perception cannot provide belief-independent evidence, then it cannot serve its characteristic epistemic role of testing, correcting, or grounding our beliefs.
(4) Perception does serve this role: we routinely gain knowledge through observation, and empirical inquiry would be impossible without it.
(5) Therefore, cognitive penetration must either not occur or be epistemically irrelevant wherever it does occur.

The conclusion strikes me as too strong. Perceptual learning seems to be a counterexample: the trained radiologist sees a tumor that the novice literally does not see, and her prior knowledge is clearly shaping her perceptual experience, yet we take her to be better justified, not worse. But if I reject the conclusion, I owe an account of which premise fails. Each one, read in isolation, seems hard to deny. Where does the argument go wrong, and what does the correction cost?
