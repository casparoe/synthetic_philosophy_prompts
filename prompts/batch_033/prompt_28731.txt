The debate over whether historical explanation requires covering laws has been running in something like its present form since the middle of the twentieth century, and it seems to have reached a standstill. On one side, the covering-law tradition, associated most strongly with Hempel's mid-century work, holds that to explain an event is to show that it was to be expected given antecedent conditions and general regularities, and that what historians actually produce are at best explanation sketches: incomplete gestures toward regularities they cannot state. On the other side, the narrative tradition holds that historians make events intelligible by situating them within a story, tracing agents' reasons, the contingencies of sequence, and the way later events reconfigure the significance of earlier ones, in a form whose explanatory force does not reduce to subsumption under laws. Some versions emphasize rational explanation, the reconstruction of an agent's deliberation; others emphasize colligation, binding an event to a larger process; still others emphasize the configurational or emplotment work of narrative form itself. The same stock examples circulate — revolutions, wars, the fall of a government — and each side can redescribe the other's favored case in its own idiom without yielding an inch. The two camps agree on the historical facts, agree that historians write narratives, and agree that historians rarely state laws; the dispute concerns what follows from that.

My working hypothesis is that this is not primarily a disagreement about history but about what an explanation has to do: the covering-law side treats explanation in the natural sciences as the standard to which any genuine explanation must answer, while the narrative side treats explanation as whatever removes a particular puzzlement for a particular inquirer, so that a narrative can explain even when it cites no law. But I can also see it as a hidden empirical question about whether narrative explanations tacitly depend on regularities, or a merely verbal dispute about the word explanation, or a difference about whether the aim of history is general knowledge or the understanding of particulars.

I want a diagnosis rather than a survey or a verdict on who is right. What kind of disagreement is this, exactly — substantive, verbal, normative, two questions under one heading, or something else? Anchor the diagnosis in what each side actually says, and then say what would count as progress given that diagnosis: a distinction, an empirical finding, a formal result, a reformulated question — or an argument that no progress should be expected, and why.
