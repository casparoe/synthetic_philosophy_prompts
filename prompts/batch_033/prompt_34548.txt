I'm in a philosophy of probability reading group, and last week two members got into it over Bertrand's paradox and whether it basically kills the principle of indifference. I've been chewing on it since and I honestly can't tell who has the better of the argument. Here's roughly how it went down:

Marco started by saying Bertrand's paradox is a stake through the heart of the principle of indifference. You've got a circle, you want the probability a random chord is longer than the side of an inscribed equilateral triangle. Three ways of applying indifference — pick two random endpoints on the circumference, pick a random point along a random radius, pick a random midpoint inside the circle — give you 1/3, 1/2, and 1/4. All three seem like totally natural ways to be "equally likely," and they give different answers. So the principle isn't just in need of fine-tuning; it's incoherent.

Lena pushed back: the principle isn't the problem, the problem is people applying it naively to whatever parameterization they happen to think of first. The principle says assign equal probabilities when you have no reason to discriminate, sure, but you first need to identify the right set of alternatives to be indifferent over. If you're actually drawing chords by some physical process — say, dropping a straw onto a circle — only one of those parameterizations matches the physics. The paradox is a warning about sloppy application, not a refutation of the principle itself.

Marco said that's exactly his point. The principle of indifference was supposed to be an a priori principle — you don't need to know the physics, you just apply it to the space of possibilities from your armchair. If you now need to know the actual generating process, you've abandoned the principle and replaced it with empirical frequency. The whole selling point of indifference was that it lets you assign probabilities without having to go out and measure anything.

Lena said that's too quick. Maybe the principle was never meant to be applied without any constraints on the description at all. She brought up Jaynes's attempt to solve the paradox using invariance under rotation, translation, and scaling — you pick the parameterization that leaves the problem unchanged under the symmetries that shouldn't matter. That singles out the 1/2 answer, and you didn't need to drop a single straw. So you don't need empirical input; you need a better theory of which partitions are admissible.

Marco came back: Jaynes's symmetry argument is clever, but it's not the principle of indifference anymore — it's a seperate principle about invariance. And it doesn't obviously generalize. What about the water-and-wine paradox, or van Fraassen's cube factory? You can't always invoke geometric symmetry to save you. If the principle of indifference needs a patchwork of auxiliary principles to work, and those auxiliaries differ from case to case, then indifference itself isn't doing the heavy lifting. It's just a label for "be uniform after you've already solved the hard problem."

Lena admitted it's not a complete theory, but said that's setting the bar too high. Lots of philosophical principles — charity in interpretation, simplicity in theory choice — are incomplete and need case-by-case supplementation. That doesn't make them worthless. The principle of indifference still tells you something real: once you've settled on the right partition, equality is the default. That's not nothing. And the fact that the paradox forces us to think harder about parameterization is a feature, not a bug — it's the principle doing its job by revealing hidden assumptions.

At that point they'd been going for twenty minutes and the group moved on, but I've been stuck on it. Can you go through their exchange and adjudicate move by move? Who's right about what, and where does each of them overreach or miss something? I'm less interested in who "wins" overall and more in which specific claims hold up and which don't.
