I'm a philosophy grad student and I've spent the last year reading the naturalism vs. normativism debate about health and disease, and I still can't tell weather it's a real argument or two people describing different things with the same word. The naturalist side (the classic biostatistical theory, Boorse's version) says health is just normal biological functioning: a condition is a disease when it's an internal state that puts you statistically below typical functional efficiency for your reference class (age, sex, species), and that's a value-free, empirical question about biology. The normativist side says that can't be right, because you can't pick out the reference class or decide which functions count as 'typical' without already making value judgments, and because the same biological state (infertility, deafness, being very fat) gets treated as a disease in some contexts and a difference in others depending on what people value. They also point to how homosexuality stayed in the DSM until social norms shifted even though the biology didn't change. Both sides agree on the easy cases; they disagree about what makes the easy cases easy.

The frustrating thing is that neither side moves. The naturalist keeps saying the normativist has confused the concept of disease with the separate question of what we should treat or accommodate, and the normativist keeps saying the naturalist is smuggling in values while claiming to be purely descriptive. The hybrid accounts (like the harmful dysfunction view) are maybe the most telling: naturalists reject them because the 'harm' component is unscientific, normativists reject them because the 'dysfunction' component still has a hidden norm buried in it. My working theory is that this is two projects wearing one label — one is trying to reconstruct 'disease' as a scientific/explanatory category for pathology, the other is trying to account for the practical, value-laden boundary between what medicine treats and what it leaves alone. But maybe it's a genuine bedrock disagreement about whether facts and values can be seperated at all, or maybe it's verbal. I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is — where the impasse lives — and then, given the diagnosis, what would count as progress: a distinction that would reframe it, a kind of case that would decide something, or an argument that we should stop expecting it to resolve. Don't survey the positions; I've read them. Diagnose the disagreement itself.
