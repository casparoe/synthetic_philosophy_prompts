I run an upper-level philosophy of physics seminar, about 16 students, 75 minutes. Design a betting game that makes them feel why the thermodynamic arrow of time can't be read off the microscopic laws, using only a particle-in-a-box simulation I can run forward or reversed. I want two phases: first they bet points (no real money) on whether each clip is playing forward or backward, across conditions that vary the number of particles and whether the initial state is a low-entropy "special" one or an equilibrium one; then teams try to produce a clip of their own that could pass as running backward, and we bet on those too. The debrief should map their actual hit rates in each condition onto coarse-graining and the Past Hypothesis, not onto a time-asymmetric law. Tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it — especially students concluding the simulation itself is rigged because the code isn't time-symmetric — and which feature of the full view the activity can't reproduce and I'll have to supply in the debrief myself.
