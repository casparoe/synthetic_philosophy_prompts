Question:

Karl Popper argues that unlimited tolerance is self-defeating. (a) State the paradox of tolerance in your own words. (b) Explain how Popper thinks a tolerant society should respond to intolerant movements, being precise about what he does and does not recommend. (c) Present what you take to be the strongest objection to Popper's position and assess whether it succeeds.

Student's answer:

(a) The paradox is that tolerance, if made an unlimited principle, undermines itself. A society that extends full tolerance to everyone, including the intolerant, gives the intolerant the room to use its freedoms to destroy the institutions and norms that make tolerance possible in the first place. The consistent tolerant society thus prepares its own disappearance: the tolerance it practices hands its enemies the means to abolish tolerance. Tolerance is therefore not self-sustaining unless it is limited in some way.

(b) Popper's resolution is to deny that tolerance must be extended to the intolerant. He argues that a tolerant society should claim the right not to tolerate the intolerant, and should be prepared to back that right with force if it becomes necessary. In practice, this means the state should legally prohibit the expression of intolerant views, because such expression is already an attack on the tolerant order rather than a contribution to it. On Popper's view, the intolerant have forfeited their claim to be tolerated, since they would not extend tolerance to others if they gained power; the principle underlying the resolution is one of reciprocity, on which a person is owed toleration only on condition of being prepared to be tolerant. Popper accordingly holds that incitement to intolerance should be treated as criminal, much as incitement to murder is.

(c) The strongest objection is that this gives whoever holds power a standing excuse to suppress whatever views it finds threatening, since what counts as intolerance will be settled by the state itself. I think the objection fails. Popper's target is not ordinary political disagreement but movements that reject rational argument and answer it with force, and that is a fact about a movement's own stated commitments rather than a matter of the state's discretion. So the worry that Popper licenses the censorship of any unpopular view rests on a misreading of his criterion.
