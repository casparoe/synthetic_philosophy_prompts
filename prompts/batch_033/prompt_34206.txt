I'm a science journalist and I've been covering a long-running taxonomic dispute — whether some particular population of lizards is its own species or just an isolated variant of a widespread one. Over months of reporting, I've noticed a pattern I can't unsee, and I'm wondering if philosophers of biology have a name for it.

Here's the thing. When I ask Expert A why they consider this population a distinct species, they point to genetic divergence: the sequences are different enough, so it's a species under the phylogenetic species concept. When I then ask Expert B why they disagree, they say: well, they can still interbreed with the main population and produce fertile offspring, so under the biological species concept they're the same species. Fair enough. But then I talk to Expert C, who works on a totally different group — say, some bacteria — and they tell me the biological species concept doesn't even apply to their organisms, so they use something else. And Expert D, who studies fossil taxa, says of course they use morphological species because that's all the fossil record gives you.

Here's the pattern that's bothering me. It's not just that different subfields use different species concepts. It's that when there's a dispute *within* a subfield — two researchers disagreeing about the same group of organisms — they each seem to reach for whichever species concept gives them the answer they already wanted, and they defend the choice as if it's the obviously correct one for that group. But when you press them, they can't give a principled reason why *this* group of lizards calls for the phylogenetic concept while *that* closely related group calls for the biological one, except that it yields the taxonomy they prefer. The concept choice seems to follow the conclusion, not the other way around.

And here's the kicker: I've started noticing the same thing in informal arguments too. Someone will say "well, species are just groups that actually interbreed in nature" when that makes the lumping case they want to make, but switch to "species are lineages with their own evolutionary trajectory" when that supports splitting. The move is fluid, and it's very hard to catch in real time because each definition is individually defensible.

I'm sure I'm not the first person to notice this. Is there an established term for this specific thing — strategically switching between competing definitions of a contested concept to get your preferred result, where the concept itself is genuinely unsettled so you can't just say they're "misapplying" it? It feels adjacent to cherry-picking, but it's not quite that because each species concept is a legitimate contender in the literature — you're not picking bad data, you're picking among equally respectable frameworks. It also feels adjacent to "moving the goalposts," but the goalposts aren't moving mid-argument; they're just different goalposts for different arguments. And it's not quite ad hoc reasoning, because the framework you're invoking is pre-existing and independently motivated. It might be somewhere near "equivocation," but there's no single term being used in two senses — it's closer to having multiple terms (or multiple operationalizations of one term) and switching which one you treat as authoritative.

You're an expert in philosophy of biology and conceptual analysis. Please think through this carefully — start by brainstorming the different phenomena this could be confused with, then narrow down to what's distinctive about the pattern I'm describing. If there's no established name, I'd love for you to coin one and define it carefully, with attention to how it's different from the neighboring concepts I mentioned and what its boundary cases are. Either way, I want to understand what this pattern tells us about the species problem itself — is it a sign that "species" is just a conventional label and we should stop worrying about it, or is there a way to keep the concept while ruling out this kind of strategic switching? And please write in American English.
