I'm taking a philosophy of mind course and we just started the unit on Dennett. The textbook we're using has this paragraph explaining heterophenomenology, and something about it is bugging me but I can't quite articulate what. Here's what it says:

"Heterophenomenology is Dennett's method for turning consciousness into a proper object of scientific study. The starting point is that we can't directly observe anyone's conscious experiences, so the primary data have to be subjects' reports about what they're experiencing, collected under controlled experimental conditions. Dennett proposes we treat these reports the way an anthropologist treats informants' testimony about their religious beliefs: you record it carefully, take it seriously as data, but you don't assume it's all true. From the reports, you construct what Dennett calls the subject's 'heterophenomenological world' — the world as it seems to the subject, which has the same ontological status as the world of a novel. The method itself is meant to be theoretically neutral: you can be a dualist, a materialist, or a mysterian and still use heterophenomenology to gather data. But Dennett argues that once you adopt the method consistently, you'll find that all the data are exhausted by the reports and the physical processes that produce them. There's no need to posit any additional 'phenomenal' properties — the so-called qualia. Consciousness isn't eliminated, exactly, but it's explained as nothing over and above the subject's dispositions to make certain reports under certain conditions. This is why many critics say Dennett is really an eliminativist who changes the subject rather than explaining consciousness."
