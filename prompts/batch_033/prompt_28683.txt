I'm a novelist, about a third of the way into drafting something set in a near-future synthetic biology lab — the central character is trying to build a minimal cell from scratch, and the book keeps circling the question of when, if ever, the thing in the dish stops being a chemical system and starts being alive. I've been re-reading *Frankenstein* for the first time since university, and I'm struck by how much the novel resists the obvious reading. Everyone remembers it as a cautionary tale about playing God, but the creature itself — once it's animated — seems so unambigously alive: it learns language, it feels loneliness and rage, it makes demands, it wants a companion. Victor never seriously entertains the idea that he *hasn't* created life; his horror is that he has. And yet the novel also treats the whole enterprise as an abomination.

What I'd like is a reading of *Frankenstein* through the lens of the philosophical debate about definitions of life and its borderline cases. In particular: does the novel dramatise a tension between operational or behavioural criteria for life (the creature walks, talks, suffers, desires) and something like a normative or origin-based criterion (it *shouldn't* be alive because of how it came to be)? The philosophy of biology has all these edge cases — viruses, prions, synthetic protocells, maybe future AI — where the standard definitions (metabolic, evolutionary, homeostatic) either include too much or too little, and some philosophers have argued that 'life' isn't a natural kind at all but a concept we police for extra-scientific reasons. It seems to me that *Frankenstein* is already staging exactly that argument, two centuries early. I want to know whether the novel actually takes a side, or whether it just lets the tension sit there unresolved. Which scenes would you point to? And where does the novel get something right that the philosophical literature misses, or vice versa?

I'm planning to use this to think through my own novel's structure, so I'd especially appreciate attention to moments where the text underdetermines the answer — where a reader could reasonably come out either way. And if you could write in British English, that'd be grand.
