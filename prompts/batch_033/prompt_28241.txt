Alright, here's what I need. I'm a product lead at a company that makes a fairly complex B2B SaaS tool — think something like a data visualization platform. We have a ton of behavioral telemetry: clickstreams, feature adoption curves, workflow paths, drop-off points. And we have a standing argument in every roadmap meeting about what the data is actually telling us about what users want.

The problem, as I understand it from reading some summaries, is basically the inverse reinforcement learning problem: you observe an agent's behavior and try to infer the reward function they're maximizing. But the mapping from behavior to reward is radically underdetermined — a million different preference structures could produce the same observed actions, especially once you factor in that users are bounded, distracted, habit-driven, and often acting under constraints they didn't choose (our UI defaults, their boss's requirements, whatever).

I want you — act as someone who deeply understands both the formal IRL literature and the philosophical problems around preference inference and revealed preference theory — to write me a practical diagnostic procedure my team can use. Not math. A sequence of questions we ask when we see a behavioral pattern and are tempted to conclude "users prefer X."

The procedure should:
- Be a step-by-step set of questions in order, with guidance on what a satisfactory answer looks like at each step
- Say clearly where the procedure bottoms out with "we can't infer a preference from this data"
- Mark the points where judgment can't be eliminated and a human has to make the call
- Be honest about the assumptions you're importing from IRL (like that behavior is approximately optimal given preferences + constraints) and where those assumptions break in the wild

Then apply it to three cases:
1. Users overwhelmingly take a three-click workaround path instead of the one-click button we built for that exact task. (Do they prefer the workaround, or is our button somehow invisible to them?)
2. A feature has high daily active usage but Net Promoter Score tanked after we shipped it. (Are they getting value from it but hate something else, or are they compelled to use it while resenting it?)
3. Enterprise admin users configure a security setting one way, but when we interview the end-users in those same orgs, they report wanting the opposite setting. (Whose preference counts, and can behavior even reveal the answer?)

Finally, tell me what this procedure fundamentally cannot handle — the cases where behavioral data, no matter how cleverly interrogated, won't tell you what users want, and you need a completely different method.

Think through this carefully — I don't want a shallow "talk to users!" checklist. I want the actual conceptual structure of the inference problem made usable.
