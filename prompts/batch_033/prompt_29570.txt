Last night a friend and I argued for hours about Aumann's agreement theorem — he insists the common-prior assumption does all the work, while I think the real force is in what gets ruled out once two people's posterior probabilities become common knowledge. I want to end the argument with something that captures the shape of it rather than another proof. Please write a villanelle in which the two refrains are the two reasoners' incompatible posterior probabilities for the same event — one posted at three-to-one and the other at even odds — so that the alternation of the refrains mimics the common-knowledge regress of I-know-that-he-knows-that-I-know. The final two lines, where the form places both refrains side by side, should do the work of the theorem's conclusion: two rational Bayesian agents with a common prior cannot make their disagreement common knowledge unless their posteriors are equal, so one of these refrains must give way. The tercets should carry the private evidence each agent is conditioning on, and the quatrain should turn on the moment each begins to treat the other's announcement as information rather than opinion. Make it precise enough that a specialist would recognize the common-prior and common-knowledge conditions, and good enough as a poem that a reader who has never seen the theorem can follow two minds circling the same fact. Please write in American English.
