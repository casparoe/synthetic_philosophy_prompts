I keep circling back to an argument that seems to show Dennett's three-stances picture can't stop itself from attributing beliefs and desires to markets and natural selection, and I can't tell whether the right move is to bite that bullet or to reject one of the premises. I've tried steelmanning both horns and keep flip-flopping; I'd rather get a diagnosis than a survey.

The argument runs like this. (1) On the three-stances view, you're entitled to adopt the intentional stance toward a system just when treating it as a rational agent with beliefs and desires yields predictions you can't get more cheaply from the physical or design stance. (2) If the intentional stance is the right stance to take toward a system, then the beliefs and desires posited by your best intentional-stance model of it are real in the only sense that matters — the whole move is to deny that there's any further fact about "really" having mental states over and above the success of the stance. (3) There are systems where an intentional-stance model clearly pays it's rent and posits substantial beliefs and desires, but where most people (me included, on most days) would balk at saying the system has a mind: natural selection treated as an optimizing agent with preferences over variants, a market treated as an agent that expects rates to fall, a corporation treated as an agent that wants to avoid liability. (4) From (2) and (3), the view is committed to saying those systems literally have beliefs and desires — not as loose metaphor, but as real patterns of the same kind our own beliefs are. (5) But that can't be right: it makes mindedness far too cheap and erases the difference between a mind and a merely goal-directed or optimizing process. (6) So either the stance-based criterion is wrong, or the anti-essentialism in (2) has to go, or we swallow (4) and stop treating (5) as obvious.

I've gone back and forth on this: I'd most like to keep (2)'s refusal to posit a hidden Cartesian fact, and I'm genuinely unsure whether (5) survives scrutiny. Tell me where the argument goes wrong, if it does — and if it doesn't, which premise is least costly to give up and what the resulting view looks like.
