Question (10 points): What is a strictly proper scoring rule, and what role does it play in the accuracy-first argument that rational credences must obey the probability axioms? In particular, does strict propriety by itself establish that non-probabilistic credences are irrational?

Student answer: Accuracy-first epistemology says the fundamental epistemic good is having accurate credences. To make that precise they use scoring rules, which assign a score to a credence in a proposition given whether the proposition is true or false. The Brier score, for example, is (1 - c)^2 when the proposition is true and c^2 when it's false, with lower scores being better. A scoring rule is proper when a probabilistic credence function's expected score, calculated by its own lights, is at least as good as the expected score it assigns to any other credence; it's strictly proper when its own credence is the unique best. The Brier score is strictly proper. Strict propriety means a credence function always thinks it's doing better than any alternative it could adopt. So yes, strict propriety by itself gives us probabilism: since the Brier score is strictly proper, any non-probabilistic credence function will expect some probabilistic one to do better, so it fails by its own lights. Therefore rational credences must be probabilities.
