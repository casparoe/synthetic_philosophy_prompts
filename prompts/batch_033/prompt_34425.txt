I'm currently taking a course on rational choice and transformative experience, and I've been struggling with a tension in L.A. Paul's argument that I'm hoping to get clearer on.

As I understand her position, Paul argues that some life decisions — having a child, changing careers, undergoing a religious conversion — are "transformative" in the sense that they change your preferences, values, and very sense of self in ways you cannot anticipate from your current perspective. Because you cannot assign subjective probabilities to what it will be like to be the person you become, standard expected utility theory provides no guidance for these decisions. So far, this seems compelling.

But Paul also insists — repeatedly — that her argument is not meant to show that transformative choices are irrational, nor that we should avoid them. And this is where I lose the thread. If we accept that the standard model of rational choice is the right way to think about practical rationality, and if transformative choices cannot be evaluated within that model, then it seems to follow directly that transformative choices are not rational. And if a choice is not rational, what else could it be but irrational? The category of "neither rational nor irrational" strikes me as unstable: a decision either tracks reasons or it doesn't.

It feels like Paul is trying to have it both ways — to show that our most important life decisions lie outside the scope of rational evaluation, while also insisting that this doesn't impugn those decisions. But if rationality is our guide to how we should act, and rationality goes silent, aren't we left with the conclusion that we shouldn't act on those decisions at all? Or is there a way of understanding rationality that makes room for a genuine third category here, and if so, what does it look like?
