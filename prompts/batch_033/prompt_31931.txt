I've been trying to sort out what I actually think about eating animals, and my gut keeps handing me verdicts that don't obviously fit together, so I'd like help finding the principle behind them, if there is one. Here's where I've landed. If I compare a beef burger to a chicken sandwich, I lean toward thinking the chicken is worse, because a single chicken is so small that a given meal kills many more individual animals than a beef meal does. But if I imagine one cow and one chicken both about to be killed and I can only save one, I'd save the cow without hesitating, and I'd feel worse about the cow's death. I also think a year of eating eggs from caged hens is probably worse then a year of eating chicken meat, because the hens are kept alive and miserable for much longer, and the male chicks get killed either way. I think farmed salmon is worse than wild-caught salmon, even though the wild fish's death can be slow and painfull, because the farmed fish were suffering their whole lives beforehand. I think tofu and lentils are better than all of these, but I don't have a good story about why the field mice and voles killed during harvesting count for so much less in my thinking, since those deaths are entirely foreseeable rather than accidental in any interesting sense. I also think cricket flour is better than chicken but worse than lentils, though I can't tell whether that's a real view or just squeamishness about insects.

Is there one principle, maybe something built out of expected suffering, number of individuals, length and quality of life, and the intended-versus-foreseen distinction, that gets all or most of these verdicts? Which of my verdicts does it force me to give up, and what does it say about a case I genuinely haven't settled, like eating mussels or oysters when I'm not sure they're sentient at all?
