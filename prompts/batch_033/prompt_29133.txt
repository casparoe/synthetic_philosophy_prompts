I'm a backend engineer — been writing Go and Rust for about a decade, some distributed systems, some compilers work. A philosopher friend has been trying to get me to read about "embedded agency" (apparently this is from the MIRI / agent foundations world, people like Scott Garrabrant and Abram Demski). The rough idea as I understand it: standard decision theory and epistemology model agents as standing outside the world, with a perfect god's-eye model of the environment, unlimited compute, and the ability to just "choose" actions without those choices being physical events inside the world. But real agents are *in* the world — they're made of the same atoms, their reasoning *is* a physical process that takes time and resources, and their model of the world has to include themselves, which creates self-referential tangles. Some of the specific problems: (1) the agent's decision procedure is part of the environment other agents are modeling, so you can't just treat the environment as fixed; (2) the agent doesn't know its own boundary — it doesn't know which parts of the world are "me" and which are "not me" (the ontological crisis / Cartesian boundary problem); (3) the agent can't step outside its own reasoning to evaluate whether its reasoning is correct; (4) the agent's act of reasoning changes the world, because reasoning is a physical process.

My friend keeps insisting this is deep and novel. I keep saying it sounds like a bunch of things we already have names for: processes that are part of the system they monitor, the observer effect in distributed tracing, the halting problem, quines, self-hosting compilers, reflection in programming languages, the fact that a debugger changes the timing of the program it's debugging, the fact that you can't fully model a system from inside the system without being slower than the system. I'm not tryna be dismissive — I genuinely want to know if I'm missing something.
