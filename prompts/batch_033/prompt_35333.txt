Here is the student's answer:

"The identity of indiscernibles (PII) is the principle that if two objects share all of the same properties, they must be identical — that is, they are not two objects at all but one and the same object. It is important not to confuse this with the indiscernibility of identicals, which is the converse claim: if an object is identical to itself (trivially), then it must have all the same properties as itself. The indiscernibility of identicals is uncontroversial and follows directly from Leibniz's law; PII, by contrast, is a substantive metaphysical thesis that many philosophers have challenged.

Max Black's famous thought experiment asks us to imagine a universe containing nothing but two iron spheres, each two miles in diameter, separated by a distance of two miles. The spheres are qualitatively identical in every respect: same size, shape, material, temperature, color, and so on. Because the universe contains nothing else, there are no other objects relative to which the spheres could differ. Black argues that the two spheres are distinct objects — after all, there are two of them — yet they are indiscernible, sharing all their monadic properties and all their relational properties (since the only relation available is the symmetric spatial relation between them, which each bears in exactly the same way). Therefore, Black concludes, PII is false.

One attempt to defend PII against Black's example is the appeal to haecceities, or 'thisnesses.' The idea, originating with Duns Scotus, is that each object has a primitive non-qualitative property — the property of being this very object — which no other object can share. On this view, the two spheres are not genuinely indiscernible, because sphere A has the haecceity of being sphere A, and sphere B has the haecceity of being sphere B. Since these are different properties, PII is saved. However, this defense has been widely criticized for being ad hoc. If haecceities are introduced solely to preserve PII against counterexamples, they seem to do no other explanatory work, and many philosophers find them metaphysically extravagant. A further problem is that haecceities appear to make PII trivially true: if every object automatically has a unique haecceity, then PII holds but only at the cost of stripping the principle of any interest, since it no longer tells us anything substantive about how the world is structured. For these reasons, most contemporary metaphysicians regard the haecceity defense as a failure, and Black's argument is generally taken to have refuted the identity of indiscernibles."
