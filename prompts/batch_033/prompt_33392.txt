You are a philosopher specialising in personal identity and the metaphysics of mind, consulting for a clinical ethics body. Please think through the issues carefully before writing, and where you encounter a genuine dilemma in the design of the procedure, flag it rather than papering it over.

The setting: A national regulatory body — call it the Neural Interventions Authority — must decide whether to classify a proposed gradual neural replacement therapy as (a) a therapeutic intervention that preserves the patient's personal identity and thus requires only standard informed consent, or (b) a procedure that terminates one person and brings about another, which would trigger the far more stringent legal framework governing life-ending procedures. The therapy in question replaces biological neurons one at a time or in small batches with functionally equivalent synthetic units, maintaining continuous psychological function throughout. The Authority's staff are not philosophers; they include clinicians, lawyers, and biomedical engineers. They will use this procedure repeatedly, under time pressure, for therapies that vary in replacement rate, total percentage of tissue replaced, and the nature of the synthetic units. They need a procedure they can run without a philosopher in the room.

Write a decision procedure the Authority can follow. It should take the form of a sequence of questions or tests, ordered so that earlier questions rule out the clearest cases first. For each step, specify what counts as a sufficient answer and what the next step is. Mark the points where the procedure hands the decision to a human judgement rather than a rule. The procedure must handle the hard cases that the gradual-replacement literature makes salient: for instance, what if the synthetic units are not exact functional equivalents but are superior? What if the replacement is not total — say, 70% of the cortex? What if the replacement is done in a single session rather than gradually? What if the patient is already unconscious or has advanced dementia? What if the synthetic units are not installed in the same skull but in a remote substrate, with the biological neurons being silenced one by one as their remote counterparts come online?

Then run the procedure on at least two worked examples:

- One where the therapy clearly preserves identity (for instance, a cochlear nucleus replacement for hearing restoration).
- One where the procedure gives an answer the Authority will find uncomfortable — for instance, classifying a widely discussed hypothetical therapy as identity-terminating, or refusing to classify a procedure as identity-preserving even though the patient walks out of the clinic and insists they are the same person.

Finally, say what the procedure cannot do: which cases it will systematically misclassify, which philosophical assumptions it bakes in that a reasonable dissenter could reject, and what the Authority would need to add if it wanted to handle those cases.

Write the answer in British English.
