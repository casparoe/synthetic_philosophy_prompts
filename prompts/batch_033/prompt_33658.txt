The Kohnstamm Fund for Conceptual Biology has committed three years of postdoctoral support to work on the units of selection problem. The fund's directors have grown tired of the familiar gene-versus-group framing and believe the most interesting work now lies in narrower, more tractable sub-questions whose answers would rearrange how the broader debate is conducted. They have asked you to produce a ranked list of the five or six open sub-questions you believe most deserve that three-year investment.

The directors want the ranking defended, not merely stated. For each question, lay out: (i) why it is upstream of other questions in the debate — what else depends on it, and what would become easier to settle once it is settled; (ii) how tractable it is for a philosopher of biology using the conceptual, formal, and empirical resources now available; and (iii) what you would need to see over the next three years — an argument, a model, a case study — to move the question up or down your list by two or more places.

They are also interested in fragility. Identify one or two claims that are widely accepted in the debate — perhaps about the equivalence of kin and multi-level selection, about the causal status of group selection, or about the primacy of the gene's-eye view for certain classes of problem — that you suspect might not survive the next decade of work. Explain whether and how those fragile points should affect the prioritisation: should a question be funded partly because it threatens a consensus, even if it would not otherwise rank highly?

Finally, the fund asks you to be self-critical: which argument or empirical finding would most undermine your ranking? Where in your reasoning are you least confident, and what would have to be true for a substantially different ordering to be correct?

Please answer in American English.
