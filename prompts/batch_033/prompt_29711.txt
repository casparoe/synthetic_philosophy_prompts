I'm writing a dissertation chapter on the debate about whether genuine moral dilemmas are possible, and I've realised that much of the literature talks past itself because different authors are answering subtly different questions under the same label. Before I evaluate the main positions — the Williams-inspired argument from moral remainders, the Brink-style sceptical dissolution, the Marcus and deontic-logic line, the various appeals to 'ought implies can' — I want to nail down the prior question: what would an adequate theory in this area actually need to get right? What are the desiderata?

Act as a senior moral philosopher with expertise in metaethics and the dilemmas literature. I'd like you to produce the full specification of requirements that any satisfactory theory of the possibility (or impossibility) of genuine moral dilemmas must meet. For each desideratum, give me the motivating case or consideration — why it earns its place on the list, what sort of example or argument forces it, and what we lose if we abandon it. Then mark which requirements are non-negotiable (any view that fails them isn't really engaging with the same phenomenon) and which are contestable commitments of specific camps. I am especially interested in whether any subset of these desiderata turns out to be jointly unsatisfiable — if, for instance, adequately capturing the phenomenology of tragic choice pushes you towards commitments that conflict with the logical constraints on consistent normative systems. That kind of tension in the desiderata themselves would be a substantive result about the debate, not merely a preliminary to it. Please separate what is genuinely settled in the literature from what remains contested among those who know the topic, and do not present a live dispute as consensus. I'd welcome a tabular or structured format for the requirements, but think it through carefully first — I want a map of the conceptual terrain, not a hasty inventory.
