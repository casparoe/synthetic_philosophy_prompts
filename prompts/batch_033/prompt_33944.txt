I teach a graduate seminar on experimental philosophy and moral psychology, and I am preparing a case study for a session on the Knobe effect. I would like you to work through this as if you were an expert in experimental philosophy presenting at a workshop — think carefully, consider objections, and do not rush to a conclusion.

The case I want analyzed is the Volkswagen emissions scandal (Dieselgate, 2015). Here are the salient facts as I understand them, so we share a common starting point: Volkswagen installed "defeat device" software in roughly 11 million diesel vehicles worldwide. The software detected when the car was undergoing emissions testing and activated full pollution controls; during normal driving, it reduced those controls, resulting in nitrogen oxide emissions up to 40 times the US legal limit. VW's public position was that this was the work of a small group of engineers acting without executive knowledge. The company eventually pleaded guilty to criminal charges and paid over $30 billion in fines, settlements, and buybacks.

The Knobe effect, as you know, is the finding that people's moral judgments affect their attributions of intentional action. In the classic CEO vignette, subjects are told that a chairman adopts a profit-maximizing program that, as a side effect, will either harm or help the environment. When the side effect is harmful, most subjects say the chairman intentionally brought it about; when it is helpful, most deny it. The asymmetry is robust: across many replications, the morally bad side effect is judged intentional, the morally good one is not, even though the chairman's attitude toward the side effect is identical in both conditions — he does not care either way.

Here is what I want you to do. Analyze the Volkswagen case through the lens of the Knobe effect, running the analysis in both directions: use the theory to illuminate the case, and use the case to test the theory. Be specific and disciplined throughout.

Start by mapping the Volkswagen case onto the structure of a Knobe-style scenario. Identify the agent (or agents), the primary goal, the side effect, and the agent's attitude toward the side effect. Note any structural disanalogies — for instance, the Knobe vignettes involve individual agents, whereas here we have a corporation, a chain of command, and diffuse responsibility. Do these disanalogies matter for the applicability of the effect, or can they be accommodated?

Next, spell out the predictions the Knobe effect makes about ordinary attributions of intentionality in this case. Would we expect the public to judge that Volkswagen intentionally polluted? Would we expect different judgments for the engineers, the middle managers, and the C-suite? What does the experimental literature on the Knobe effect and collective agents suggest about these questions?

Then turn the analysis around. What does the Volkswagen case reveal about the Knobe effect that the standard vignette studies do not? Consider features such as: the role of public outrage and media framing in amplifying or dampening the effect; the fact that the harm is distributed across millions of anonymous victims rather than a single concrete one; the fact that the agents have strong practical and legal incentives to present themselves as non-intentional; and the fact that the case played out over years, not in a one-shot survey response. Does the case expose important limits of the Knobe effect, or does it extend its reach in ways that the lab cannot capture?

Finally, offer an overall assessment: on balance, is the Volkswagen case a confirmation of the Knobe effect's real-world significance, or a reason to doubt that it travels outside the laboratory? Be honest about where the theory is silent — what aspects of the scandal does the Knobe effect simply have nothing to say about?

I am not looking for a summary of the scandal or a recitation of the Knobe effect literature. I want a careful mapping and a genuine two-way analysis.
