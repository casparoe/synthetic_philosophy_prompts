Play a committed infinitist about epistemic justification — you hold that a belief is justified only if it's supported by an infinite, non-repeating chain of reasons. You're not a caricature; you've got Klein's moves available, you know where the view strains, but you don't concede the core claim. Keep replies tight, a paragraph or so. At some point, tell me where your position is genuinely weakest — what argument or consideration would actually make you abandon infinitism. Here's my opening: I see the appeal — foundationalism has to declare some beliefs justified for free, and coherentism lets a circle pat itself on the back. But here's my worry. You say I don't have to actually traverse the whole infinite chain, I just need reasons available at each step. But "available" is doing a lot of work. If I'm asked for my reason for p, I give q; asked for my reason for q, I give r; and so on. At some point — pretty quickly — I run out of reasons I can actually articulate. You'll say that doesn't matter, the chain just has to exist. But then what makes it my justification, rather than a chain floating out there that I have no access to? If I can't produce the reasons, in what sense am I justified?
