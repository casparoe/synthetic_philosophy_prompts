So I was up till like 2am arguing with my friend about teleosemantics — you know, the whole Millikan/Dretske project of explaining mental content in terms of biological proper function. And we got stuck on this one point that I can't stop thinking about. The basic move in teleosemantics is that a mental state's content is whatever it's the state's proper function to indicate, where "proper function" cashes out in terms of evolutionary history. Like, my frog-brain states mean "fly" because that's what the mechanism was selected to detect.

But here's where we kept going in circles: what about beliefs whose content essentially involves counterfactuals — like "if I had taken the other job, I'd be happier now" or "this glass would break if dropped"? These seem to have perfectly determinate content. I know what I mean when I think them. But from a teleosemantic point of view, what could possibly fix that content? The selection history of my cognitive mechanisms only ever involved actual states of affairs — actual flies, actual predators, actual mates. Evolution doesn't get to run the tape again and select for mechanisms that track what *would have* happened. So it seems like either (a) teleosemantics has to say counterfactual beliefs don't really have determinate content, which is wild, or (b) there's some story about how counterfactual content reduces to or supervenes on the kind of actual-world-tracking content that selection can explain. But my friend couldn't give me a story for (b) that didn't feel like hand-waving, and honestly I can't think of one either.

So here's what I want: make the strongest argument you can that teleosemantics *can* handle counterfactual content — that it's not a fatal problem for the view. Even if you don't buy the view yourself, give it the best run for its money. I'm especially interested in whether the consumer-side version (Millikan) has resources here that the indicator-side version (Dretske) lacks, or vice versa.
