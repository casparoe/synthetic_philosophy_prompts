I'm a PhD student in political philosophy trying to sort out whether compatriots have special claims on one another — whether the bare fact of shared national membership generates moral reasons that don't extend to outsiders. I've been collecting my intuitive reactions to cases, and I'd like you to help me find the principle behind them, if there is one.

Here are my verdicts:

1. When I give to charity, I feel an emotional tug toward domestic causes, but on reflection I don't think that tug tracks a genuine moral reason. I think I should give to the charity that does the most good per dollar, regardless of where the beneficiaries live.

2. When I vote, I feel entirely comfortable voting for policies that benefit my fellow citizens even when they impose modest costs on foreigners — for instance, agricultural subsidies that help domestic farmers but hurt farmers in developing countries. I don't think this is mere self-interest; I think it's morally permissible to weigh compatriots' interests more heavily at the ballot box.

3. If I see two children drowning and can save only one, and one is my neighbor's kid while the other is a foreign tourist, I think I'm permitted to save the neighbor's kid. But I think the permission comes from the thicker relationship — I know the family, I'll face them afterward — not from shared nationality. If I learned both children were complete strangers to me, differing only in nationality, I'd think I should flip a coin.

4. A doctor in a public hospital has one dose of a scarce drug and two patients identical in all relevant medical respects except that one is a citizen and the other is a foreign national. I think it would be wrong for the doctor to use citizenship as a tiebreaker.

5. I think a state is permitted to restrict immigration in ways that predictably prevent many foreigners from dramatically improving their life prospects, in order to protect domestic wages or preserve cultural continuity. I'm uncomfortable with this verdict, but I can't bring myself to reject it.

6. When a natural disaster strikes abroad, I think my government should send aid, but I'm not convinced it's required to spend as much as it would to address a comparable disaster at home. I think this asymmetry is morally permissible.

The tension I can't resolve is between (2) and (4). Voting and triage both involve life-and-death stakes, both occur within institutional roles, and both require choosing between compatriots and foreigners. Yet I want to say the voter may favor compatriots but the doctor may not. Is there a principle that captures all six verdicts, or at least most of them? If some have to go, which ones, and why? And what does the resulting principle say about a case I haven't considered: a university admissions committee giving a slight preference to domestic applicants over equally qualified international ones?
