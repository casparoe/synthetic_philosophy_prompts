A friend recommended a philosophy podcast to me, and in one episode the host gave the following summary of the Knobe effect:

"Experimental philosophers have discovered something pretty devastating for how we think about intentional action. In the classic study, you give people a scenario where a CEO makes a business decision, knowing it will have a certain side-effect. When the side-effect is harmful—say, damaging the environment—people overwhelmingly say the CEO brought it about intentionally. But when the side-effect is helpful—say, benefiting the environment—people say he didn't bring it about intentionally, even though the CEO's state of mind and the structure of the decision are identical across the two cases. So what's driving the judgment isn't anything about the agent's beliefs, desires, or plans—it's purely whether the outcome is morally bad. This shows that our concept of intentional action isn't really a factual concept at all; it's just a disguised moral evaluation. And if that's right, then a lot of legal reasoning—which treats whether someone acted intentionally as an objective factual question, separate from whether what they did was wrong—is built on a confusion."

I'm skeptical that the host is being entirely fair here, but I don't know the literature well enough to pinpoint the problems. Could you go through this summary and tell me which parts are accurate, which are overstatements or misrepresentations, and whether the leap to the claim about legal reasoning actually follows from what the Knobe effect shows?
