Here's the exam question: State the Condorcet jury theorem as precisely as you can, including its assumptions. Then evaluate the following claim: because the theorem shows that large groups are more likely than small groups to reach the correct answer, it provides a strong epistemic argument for using majority rule in democratic elections. Discuss at least one assumption of the theorem that is likely to fail in real electorates, and explain what that failure does to the argument.

Here's the student's answer: Condorcet's theorem says the following. Take a binary choice with one correct option and an odd number of voters, each of whom votes independently and has probability p > 1/2 of voting for the correct option. Then the probability that a simple majority picks the correct option is greater than p, and this probability approaches 1 as the number of voters goes to infinity. So the theorem gives a straightforward epistemic case for majority rule: bigger electorates are more reliable than smaller ones. The only condition that really matters is that voters are more likely to be right than wrong, and this is really a condition on the average voter; as long as the electorate on average is more competent than a coin flip, the argument goes through. Independence is the least important assumption. In real elections voters don't copy each other's ballots, so independence holds approximately, and anyway dependence just means some voters share information, which should make them more reliable, not less. So the claim in the question is basically correct: the theorem gives a strong epistemic argument for majority rule in democratic elections, and voter ignorance doesn't undermine that as long as the average voter is more likely right than wrong.
