**Question:** In 'Brain Bisection and the Unity of Consciousness', Thomas Nagel examines split-brain data to argue for a sceptical conclusion about the unity of consciousness. Explain the experimental data Nagel draws on, outline the four interpretations of those data that he considers, and set out his final position on what the data show about the concept of a mind. Does Nagel take his conclusion to have consequences for the concept of a person, and if so, why?

**Student's answer:**
Nagel begins with the well-known split-brain experiments, in which epileptic patients have had their corpus callosum severed, preventing direct communication between the left and right cerebral hemispheres. In normal life these patients appear largely unimpaired, but controlled experiments reveal striking dissociations. For instance, an object presented briefly in the left visual field (which projects only to the right hemisphere) cannot be named by the patient, because speech is lateralised to the left hemisphere. However, the patient can retrieve the object with the left hand, which is controlled by the right hemisphere. Similarly, if different words are flashed to the two visual fields, each hand may independently retrieve the object named on the contralateral side. Emotional responses to stimuli presented only to the right hemisphere can occur without the patient being able to report what caused them, and the speaking left hemisphere will sometimes confabulate an explanation. Nagel takes these data to show that the two hemispheres can operate as independent centres of consciousness, each with its own perceptual field, volition, and even emotions.

Nagel considers four possible interpretations of the data. The first is that the patient has a single mind, associated entirely with the left hemisphere, and that the activities of the right hemisphere are unconscious, automatic processes. The second is that the patient has two minds, one per hemisphere, with the right hemisphere's mind being largely non-verbal. The third is that the patient has a single mind that spans both hemispheres but is unusually fragmented, so that its contents are not fully integrated. The fourth is that the patient normally has one mind, but that the experimental setup temporarily causes it to split into two.

Nagel argues that the first interpretation fails because the right hemisphere's activities are too sophisticated to be dismissed as unconscious — they involve perception, memory, and goal-directed behaviour. The third interpretation fails because it cannot make sense of the simultaneous, independent mental activity observed in the experiments: if there is only one mind, what could it be like for that mind to experience two separate streams of consciousness at once? The fourth interpretation is implausible because the experiments do not alter the patient's physiology; they only reveal pre-existing capacities. Having eliminated these three, Nagel concludes that the second interpretation — two minds — is the only one that can accommodate the data, and he therefore endorses it.

Nagel then draws out the implications of this conclusion for the concept of a person. He reasons that if split-brain patients literally have two minds, then our ordinary concept of a person — which assumes a one-to-one correspondence between a human being and a single mind — is in trouble. The data show that a single human organism can house two distinct subjects of experience. Since the psychological theory of personal identity ties personhood to the continuity of mental states, the existence of two minds in one body means we cannot coherently say how many persons are present. Nagel's final position is that we should abandon the concept of a person as a useful theoretical notion, because it cannot handle the split-brain challenge.
