I am a novelist, and over years of constructing characters and watching how they justify themselves to one another, I have noticed a pattern I cannot name. It has two movements, and they seem to be mirror images.

The first movement: when a character possesses power — social standing, wealth, physical strength, or simply the momentum of the plot — their actions attract a distinctive vocabulary. What they do is bold, decisive, noble, frank. Their pleasures are refined; their anger is righteous. Even their cruelties are described, by themselves and by those who admire them, as natural or magnificent. The same actions, performed by a character without power, are brutal, crass, scheming, vulgar. I am not describing authorial judgment here. I am describing the way the characters within the world assign these terms, and the way readers, at least initially, tend to follow them.

The second movement: at some point the powerless characters develop an entire counter-vocabulary. Weakness becomes humility; inability to retaliate becomes forgiveness; fear becomes prudence; subservience becomes loyalty. And the very qualities they lack — strength, confidence, appetite — are recast as coarseness, arrogance, animality. This is not simply sour grapes, because the new vocabulary is genuinely felt and often elaborated into a whole moral system, one that the powerful now find themselves on the wrong side of. The reversal can be so complete that a reader who began the novel admiring the strong characters ends it finding them contemptible, not because they changed, but because the language for describing them did.

What interests me is that these two movements appear to be the same operation running in opposite directions: moral language bending to serve the interests of the speaker's position, whether that position is high or low. The powerful call their own traits good and the weak call the powerful traits bad — but the weak also call their own traits good, using a different set of virtues entirely. The result is not a single scale with the powerful at the top and the weak at the bottom; it is two scales that do not quite share terms, and a given action can be "noble" on one and "brutal" on the other without either speaker contradicting herself by her own lights.

I have used this pattern in fiction, but I suspect philosophers have mapped it. Does it have an established name, or a family of names? I am not looking for a one-word label so much as a precise carving: what exactly this two-directional reframing is, whether the two movements are genuinely the same mechanism or only superficially similar, and where the boundary lies between this phenomenon and simpler things like hypocrisy, self-deception, or ordinary partisanship. If there are rival accounts of how the two movements relate — for instance, whether the second is a reaction to the first or an independent dynamic — I would want to know that too.
