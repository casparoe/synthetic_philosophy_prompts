A freind walked me through this and I can't find the exit. It seems to show that if every truth is knowable, then every truth is actually known. Since that conclusion is obviously false, something in the argument has to go, but each step looks airtight to me.

(1) The knowability principle: for any proposition P, if P is true, then it's possible for someone to know P.

(2) Pick any proposition Q you like — say, that the number of hairs on a particular dog at a particular moment is even. Now let P be the proposition: Q is true, and nobody ever knows that Q.

(3) If P is true, then by (1), it's possible for someone to know P.

(4) Suppose someone knows P. Then they know the conjunction "Q is true, and nobody ever knows that Q." You can't know a conjunction without knowing each part, so this person knows Q, and they also know that nobody ever knows Q.

(5) Knowledge is factive: if they know that nobody ever knows Q, then it's true that nobody ever knows Q. But they themselves know Q, so somebody knows Q. Contradiction.

(6) So P can't be known in any possible situation. Given (3), that means P is never true.

(7) But Q was arbitrary. So for every proposition Q, "Q is true, and nobody ever knows Q" is never true. In other words, whenever Q is true, somebody knows Q. So every true proposition is actually known by someone — not necessarily by one mind, but spread across all minds. That's nuts.

(8) For instance, the exact number of grains of sand on a particular beach at noon on July 3, 1857 is either even or odd, and presumably nobody ever knew or will know which. So one of the steps above has to go.

I'd most like to keep (1), because giving it up feels like admitting that some truths are in principle closed off to inquiry, and I hate that. But I can't see any other step to blame. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If the right move is to keep something in the neighborhood of (1) by restricting it, what's the most defensible restricted version, and does it still capture the thought that motivated the principle in the first place?
