I'm a novelist working on a book where my protagonist is a theologian who genuinely holds something like Leibniz's view — that this is the best of all possible worlds, that God had to create this world because it's the one that maximizes goodness while minimizing evil, the whole theodicy package. The arc of the novel involves him gradually losing this conviction, and I need to write that unraveling in a way that feels intellectually honest, not just "bad things happen so he stops believing."

So here's what I'm after: take Leibniz's position — the real, serious version, not the Voltaire caricature — and tell me what would count against it. I mean, what are the arguments, observations, thought experiments, or logical pressures that should genuinely move someone who holds this view? I want to know which defeaters are fatal, which are merely awkward, and which ones a sophisticated Leibnizian would have already absorbed and defanged.

Also, I want to know whether the position, in its strongest form, has been qualified so much that it's basically unfalsifiable — and if it has, whether that's actually a problem for a view of this kind, or whether theodicy just isn't the sort of thing that admits of empirical defeat. If there are ways the view has been insulated that you think are illegitimate, flag those. If there are ways it's been insulated that are perfectly fine given what the view is trying to do, flag those too.

I'm especially interested in defeaters that would hit someone emotionally and intellectually at the same time — the kind of thing that makes a worldview crack, not just a premise you adjust in a seminar room. But don't give me cheap shots. Give me the stuff that would trouble someone who actually knows the view inside and out, and who's already done the work of distinguishing "this looks bad from my limited perspective" from "this genuinely shouldn't be true if the view were correct."
