I'm teaching an undergraduate philosophy of language course, and I want to use Denis Villeneuve's film Arrival (2016), based on Ted Chiang's story "Story of Your Life," as a way into Donald Davidson's theory of radical interpretation. The film follows a linguist, Louise Banks, tasked with deciphering the language of alien visitors from scratch, with no shared language, culture, or conceptual framework to start from. That is, in its essentials, Davidson's thought experiment of the radical interpreter made cinematic.

I would like a reading of the film through Davidson's lens that takes both seriously. Specifically: Davidson argues that the radical interpreter can only get started by applying the principle of charity — assuming that the speakers she is interpreting are largely rational and hold mostly true beliefs about a world the interpreter shares with them. Does Louise Banks employ anything like this principle, and does the film show it working as Davidson claims it must? The film also introduces a strong Sapir-Whorf element: mastering the heptapods' written language, which is non-linear and atemporal in structure, literally rewires Louise's cognition so that she experiences time non-sequentially. Davidson's account of the relationship between language, thought, and the world is quite different from this; on his view, the very idea of a conceptual scheme radically different from our own is deeply problematic. Does the film's Whorfian metaphysics undermine a Davidsonian reading, or can the two be held together? If the heptapods genuinely experience time in a way that makes their beliefs about past and future events radically unlike ours, does the principle of charity still get traction, or does it break down?

I want the reading to be faithful in both directions: not reducing the film to a mere illustration of Davidson, and not distorting Davidson to fit the film. Where the film resists the Davidsonian reading, I want that acknowledged honestly. And I would like you to end by saying what the film might add to the philosophical debate that Davidson's abstract argument alone does not capture — whether it dramatizes something about the actual experience of radical interpretation that the thought experiment misses, or whether the film's narrative structure itself makes a philosophical point that argument cannot.
