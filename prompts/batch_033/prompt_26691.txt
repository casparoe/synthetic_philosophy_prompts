I spend a lot of time in online spaces where people argue about whether art should be political, and someone inevitably drops the line that W. E. B. Du Bois said all art is propaganda. The exact words, as I've seen them passed around, are something like: "Thus all Art is propaganda and ever must be, despite the wailing of the purists." The person quoting it usually means to settle the argument right there — if even the stuff that calls itself apolitical is really propaganda, then the only question is whose side your art is on, and pretending otherwise is either naïve or dishonest. I've seen it used to dismiss the idea that an artist could aim at beauty for its own sake, to argue that refusing to take a political stand is itself a political stand, and sometimes to claim that the aesthetic value of a work just is its effectiveness as propaganda.

I find the line arresting, and I don't think the people quoting it are simply confused, but I also suspect it's doing too much work in these conversations. What I'd like is a careful unpacking of what "all art is propaganda" could actually mean — the distinct precise claims that sentence might be expressing, from the least to the most demanding. For each reading, I want to know: is it trivially true, interesting but defensible, or probably false? And for each, does it actually support the move people make when they use the slogan to shut down the idea of apolitical art?

I can already feel a few different claims tangled up in there. One might be about the artist's intentions: every artist intends to change how their audience thinks or feels about something, so all art is persuasive in aim. Another might be about effects rather than intentions: regardless of what the artist meant to do, all art has political consequences in the world, so it functions as propaganda whether anyone admits it or not. Another might be about the impossibility of neutrality: every work takes some features of the world for granted and makes others salient, and that selective framing is itself a kind of advocacy. Another might be about the social determination of taste: what we call "purely aesthetic" standards are themselves shaped by power, so art that presents itself as above politics is actually reinforcing the status quo. And another might be a normative claim: art ought to serve the liberation of oppressed people, and calling something propaganda is not an insult but an honest description of what worthwhile art does.

I want these separated out and examined one by one. Which of them would Du Bois himself have endorsed, based on the argument he was actually making in that essay? Which of them are people in my online arguments likely reaching for? And are there any readings of the slogan that are so weak that quoting it as a conversation-ender is basically a bluff — something that sounds deep but collapses as soon as you ask what it's actually asserting?
