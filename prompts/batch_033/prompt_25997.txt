We'd been discussing higher-order evidence — you know, evidence about the quality or reliability of your own first-order evidence — and the question of whether you can ever rationally "stick to your guns" when your higher-order evidence tells you you've probably messed up. The argument was between Jake and Lena (not their real names) and I scribbled down notes right after because I wanna figure out who's right. I'll reconstruct it as best I can.

Jake started with a case: suppose you've just worked through a long division problem and you're confident the answer is 347. Then a reliable friend tells you that you've been awake for 30 hours, that sleep-deprived people make arithmetic errors at something like triple the normal rate, and that she'd bet against your answer. Jake said it's obviously irrational to just shrug and say "well, I still think it's 347" — you have to downgrade your confidence, and refusing to do so is a kind of epistemic stubborness that's no different from ignoring ordinary counterevidence.

Lena pushed back and said he was conflating two different things. The evidence about the math problem itself — the numbers you manipulated, the steps you took — hasn't changed at all. What's changed is evidence *about you*. She said you can perfectly coherently think "the math says 347, but I'm in a state where I'm likely to have botched the math." That's not a contradiction, she said; that's just acknowledging you're a fallible creature. You're not believing P while believing your evidence supports not-P; you're believing P while believing you might have misread your evidence.

Jake said that's exactly the thing the literature calls epistemic akrasia and it's supposed to be irrational for the same reason practical akrasia is: you're in a conflicted state where your own lights tell you you shouldn't be in the state you're in. If you genuinely think you're unreliable right now, then from your own perspective your conclusion is untrustworthy, and you can't simultaneously treat it as trustworthy.

Lena came back with a distinction she said Jake was missing. There's a difference, she said, between "my total evidence supports not-P" and "my total evidence supports P, but I have reason to think I'm bad at assessing my total evidence right now." The first would be genuinely incoherent to combine with believing P. The second is just ordinary fallibility plus self-knowledge. She said if Jake's view were right, you could never rationally hold any belief while knowing you're tired, or distracted, or biased — which is basically all the time — and that's an absurd result.

Jake said that's not absurd at all: you *should* be less confident when you know you're tired, that's exactly the point. The disagreement, he said, is just about whether this confidence reduction is mandatory or optional, and he couldn't see how it could be optional if you genuinely take the higher-order evidence seriously.
