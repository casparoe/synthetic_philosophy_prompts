Question:

Higher-order theories of consciousness hold that a mental state is conscious in virtue of being the object of a higher-order representation. Distinguish between higher-order thought (HOT) theory and higher-order perception (HOP) theory. Then assess which of the two is better positioned to answer the objection that higher-order theories make consciousness an extrinsic property of the state that is intuitively conscious.

Student Answer:

Higher-order thought theory, defended by David Rosenthal, holds that a mental state becomes conscious when it is the target of a higher-order thought — a cognitive state that represents the first-order state as being present. The HOT is assertoric in character; it registers that one is in the relevant state. Importantly, the HOT must itself be a conscious thought; otherwise, it is unclear how an unconscious representation could make another state conscious. If the HOT were non-conscious, we would have no more reason to think the target state is conscious than we did before the HOT was tokened.

Higher-order perception theory, defended by William Lycan and influenced by David Armstrong, holds that the higher-order state is not a thought but a perception-like state generated by an inner-sense faculty that scans first-order mental states in roughly the way outer sense scans the environment. On this view, we are conscious of our mental states through a quasi-perceptual monitoring mechanism. Unlike HOT theory, HOP theory does not require the higher-order state to be conscious; the inner sense operates automatically, and its outputs confer consciousness on their targets without themselves being conscious states.

Both theories face the objection that they mislocate consciousness. If a state is conscious only because a distinct higher-order state is directed at it, then consciousness is an extrinsic, relational property rather than an intrinsic feature of the conscious state itself. This seems to conflict with the compelling intuition that the painfulness of a pain is a property of the pain, not a property of some relation between the pain and something else.

HOP theory is better positioned to answer this objection for two reasons. First, because perception is inherently relational — perceiving just is standing in a relation to something perceived — HOP theory can claim that the relational character of consciousness is exactly what we should expect, rather than an embarrassment. The pain and the inner perception of the pain form a unified conscious episode; the relational structure does not make the pain any less genuinely conscious. Second, because HOP theory does not require the higher-order state to be conscious, it avoids a further problem that plagues HOT theory: if the HOT must be conscious, then by the theory's own lights it must be the target of a third-order thought, which must be the target of a fourth-order thought, and so on. HOP theory blocks this regress at the first step, since the inner perception is not itself conscious and therefore needs no further higher-order state to make it so.
