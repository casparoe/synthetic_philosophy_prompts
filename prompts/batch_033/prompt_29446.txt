I'm writing a paper on the opening moves of Descartes' Meditations, and I've gotten stuck on a question that seems straightforward but keeps unraveling the more I think about it. Descartes arrives at the cogito — "I think, therefore I am" — and treats it as the Archimedean point from which he can rebuild knowledge. But what exactly is the content of this discovery?

On one reading, Descartes has established the existence of a thinking substance — a res cogitans, a thing that thinks. But that seems to smuggle in a lot of metaphysics that the cogito itself hasn't earned: why should the fact that thinking is occurring entail that there is a substance doing the thinking, rather than just that thinking is occurring? Lichtenberg's objection — that Descartes was entitled only to "it thinks," not "I think" — presses on exactly this point. On another reading, what Descartes has really shown is just the indubitability of the proposition "I exist" when I entertain it, but this is a much thinner result: it doesn't get you a self that persists through time, or even a self that is the subject of multiple thoughts. And then there's the question of whether the cogito is an inference at all — Descartes sometimes seems to treat it as a direct intuition, which would affect both what it can prove and how it fits into the larger argumentative structure.

I'd like a careful unpacking of these interpretive options: what are the main candidate answers to the question "what does the cogito prove?", what metaphysical commitments does each answer carry, and which readings are ruled out by the actual text of the Meditations (as opposed to the Discourse on Method, where the presentation is significantly different)? I'm less interested in the standard "Descartes refutes skepticism" summary and more in the internal structure of the insight itself — what it delivers and what it leaves for later.
