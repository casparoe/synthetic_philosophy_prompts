Some background: I've been arguing with a friend about a stripped-down rescue case, and I want to find out where the disagreement actually lives rather than just trading intuitions. The case is this. You are in a burning building and can rescue either one stranger to your left or five different strangers to your right. You cannot rescue both groups or do anything that saves some but not all of a group; there are no special obligations to anyone involved; the cost and risk to you are identical either way; and there is no uncertainty about how things will go. I put my credence at roughly 0.75 that you are morally required to rescue the five—that rescuing the one instead would be wrong. My friend puts his at roughly 0.25, on the grounds that the only way to respect the single person's claim is to give each side an equal chance, e.g., by flipping a coin, and that treating "the five" as a unit illegitimately adds up separate lives.

I'd like your own number, but the number is only useful if I can see its derivation. Please give me your credence that, in the case as described, you are morally required to rescue the five rather than merely permitted to do so. Decompose that credence into the sub-questions it depends on, and assign each a rough probability with a one-sentence justification. I'd especially want to see: whether aggregative reasoning across lives is applicable here in the first place; whether equal respect for the single person requires an equal chance or only requires that their life be weighted equally in a calculation; whether the anti-aggregation position can be extended to cases of unequal harms without collapsing or becoming absurd; and whether "morally required" is determinate enough in this case to support a sharp credence at all. Then show how the terms combine, identify which term your final number is most sensitive to, and say what case, argument, or evidence would move that term the most. Finally, say which sub-question most likely explains the gap between my 0.75 and my friend's 0.25.
