I've been reading about the distinction between lying and misleading for a few years now — not in any formal setting, just following arguments online and thinking through cases on my own. The basic question is whether there is a moral difference between lying (saying something you believe to be false) and misleading (saying something true but in a way you know will cause someone to draw a false conclusion). The debate seems to have been running for decades, and from what I can tell, the two sides have settled into positions that haven't moved in a long time.

On one side, you have people who think lying is worse — that when you assert something false, you take on a special kind of responsibility for the truth of what you say, and breaking that is a distinct wrong that misleading doesn't commit. On the other side, you have people who think the moral difference is mostly illusory — that what matters is the intent to deceive and the harm caused, and the choice between a false statement and a true-but-misleading one is just a difference in tactic, not in moral weight.

What I find frustrating is that both sides seem to agree on nearly all the cases. Give them a particular example — a misleading answer to a question, a carefully worded true statement that deceives — and they'll both agree that something wrong has been done. They'll both agree the speaker intended to deceive. They'll both agree the listener ended up with a false belief. The disagreement is purely about whether the wrong is the same kind of wrong as lying, or a lesser one, or a different one entirely. And yet neither side seems able to produce an argument that moves the other.

I'm trying to understand what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it a verbal dispute about how we use the word "lying" — and if we just agreed on definitions, it would dissolve? Is it a substantive moral disagreement where the two sides have different foundational intuitions about what makes deception wrong, and neither can argue for those intuitions without begging the question? Or is there some hidden empirical or conceptual question that, if answered, would settle it — something about how people actually process misleading statements versus lies, or about the social function of assertion, or about the structure of moral responsibility?

I'd like a diagnosis of the impasse, supported by what each side actually says, and an honest answer about what would count as progress — a distinction, a set of cases, a result from psychology or linguistics — or whether the debate is likely to remain stalled because it rests on bedrock commitments that neither side can argue for.
