I keep noticing a pattern in how people assign causal responsibility, and I'm wondering if philosophers have carved this out as a distinct topic. The pattern is: we treat omissions as causes only when the person who omitted had some kind of duty or was expected to act. A gardener who doesn't water your plants "caused" them to die; a random passerby who also didn't water them didn't cause anything. A lifeguard who doesn't jump in "caused" the drowning; a sunbather who also didn't jump in didn't. But here's what bugs me: in the physical world, both omissions are identical — no water was applied, no rescue was attempted. So it looks like whether an omission counts as a cause depends on social norms or role expectations, which seems like it makes causation weirdly subjective. I've also noticed that people switch between treating omissions as causes and not depending on what they're trying to argue — like in policy debates, someone will say budget cuts "caused" deaths (treating an omission as a cause) and then five minutes later say you can't blame them for something they merely failed to prevent. Is there a name for this whole cluster of issues, and what are the main philosophical positions on whether omissions can be genuine causes? I'm not just after a label — I want to know if there's a live debate here and what the fault lines are.
