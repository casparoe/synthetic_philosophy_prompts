I've been teaching a graduate seminar on the moral psychology of blame, and we've spent the last three weeks on the standing to blame. The literature is subtle — G.A. Cohen's piece on castigation, the exchange between Macalester Bell and her critics, the recent work on whether standing is about the blamer's moral position or the relationship between blamer and blamed — but I've found myself wanting to step back from the theoretical apparatus and check whether my own case-by-case judgments actually hang together. I've written down my verdicts on a dozen scenarios, and I'd be grateful for help extracting the principle (or small set of principles) that might underlie them — or, failing that, an honest diagnosis of which verdicts I should reconsider and why.

Here are the cases:

1. A serial adulterer who is currently cheating on his wife delivers a blistering moral lecture to a colleague who has just been caught having an affair. I say he lacks the standing to blame, even though everything he says about the colleague's conduct is true.

2. Someone who drove drunk exactly once, twenty years ago, felt genuine remorse, and has never done it again blames a friend for driving drunk. I say she has the standing to blame.

3. A person who harbours deeply racist beliefs but has never voiced or acted on them blames someone else for making a racist remark in public. I find myself genuinely uncertain — I don't think the blamer's standing is clean, but I'm not convinced it's entirely absent either.

4. A citizen of Belgium blames the Belgian government for atrocities committed in the Congo under Leopold II. I say this person has standing to blame, even though they are not personally responsible for those acts and are a beneficiary of the colonial past.

5. Someone who has never shoplifted, never committed any theft, and lives an entirely law-abiding life blames a stranger they witness shoplifting. I say they have standing — no problem at all.

6. A person who routinely parks in disabled spaces without a permit blames a neighbour for tax evasion. The wrongs are different in kind, and I find myself thinking the blamer does have standing, even though their own conduct is hardly exemplary.

7. A person who committed a violent assault a decade ago, served their sentence, and now expresses genuine remorse blames a friend for a smaller act of violence — a drunken shove at a party. I say they lack standing, or at least their standing is compromised, and this verdict troubles me because it seems to conflict with case 2.

8. A parent who smokes two packs a day blames their teenage child for smoking. I say the parent lacks standing.

9. The same parent, having quit smoking five years ago, blames the child for smoking. I now say the parent has standing.

10. A person who was cheated by a business partner years ago blames a friend for cheating on their spouse. I say the blamer has standing, even though the wrongs are different in kind — the blamer seems to care about betrayal as such, not merely about the particular species of betrayal they suffered.

11. Someone who kept slaves in the antebellum American South and never repented would, if they were alive today, lack standing to blame anyone for any moral wrong. I am confident of this. But I am less confident about what to say of a contemporary person who benefits from the legacy of slavery and blames a present-day employer for discriminatory hiring: I think they have standing, but the intuition is not as firm as I would like.

12. A person who regularly tells small social lies — flattery, polite excuses — blames a politician for a massive campaign of systematic deception. I say the blamer has standing; the difference in scale and context matters.

I want you to do two things. First, try to formulate a principle — or a small set of candidate principles — that captures as many of these verdicts as possible. State each principle precisely, and for each clause, identify which case or cases motivate it. Second, tell me which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and whether you think I should overturn the verdict or the principle. If there are borderline cases the principle leaves unsettled, flag those too. Please write in British English, and begin with the proposed principle itself, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
