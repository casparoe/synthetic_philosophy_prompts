I serve on the risk assessment committee of a private foundation that funds research on global catastrophic and existential risks. We are considering a proposal requesting $2.3 million over three years to investigate whether a specific class of high-energy physics experiments could trigger a vacuum decay event — a phase transition of the quantum vacuum that would propagate at light speed and render the observable universe uninhabitable. No laboratory currently plans such experiments, but the proponents argue that since the stakes are effectively infinite (the loss of all future value in our light cone), any nonzero probability of preventing this outcome justifies the expenditure, and they place the annual probability on the order of 10⁻¹⁵. Two committee members have already objected that this reasoning would obligate us to fund any proposal that claims to address an extinction-level risk at any nonzero probability, since the expected-value math always favors infinite stakes. Another member counters that refusing on those grounds amounts to abandoning expected-value reasoning exactly where the stakes are highest. Draft the committee's decision memo: it should recommend approval, rejection, or approval with conditions, and it should address whether the foundation needs a standing policy for proposals that invoke this form of reasoning — and if so, what that policy should be. The memo should engage seriously with the strongest arguments on both sides.
