I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on rational choice and decision theory — 12 students, one 80-minute session — and most of them can compute an expected value but have never met the Pasadena paradox or the idea of an undefined expectation. Design an in-class activity in which they feel, before anyone names it, the force of this fact: a gamble with infinitely many outcomes can fail to have a well-defined expected value, because the sum of probability-weighted payoffs depends on the order in which the outcomes are listed. They should arrive at that realization through their own arithmetic and their own bids, not through anything I say up front. The terms "Pasadena paradox," "conditional convergence," and "Riemann rearrangement" should not be spoken until the debrief.

The mathematical engine is the following gamble. A fair coin is flipped until it first lands tails. If the first tails occurs on the nth flip, the payoff is

P(n) = (-1)^(n-1) · (2^n / n) tokens,

where a negative payoff means the player pays that many tokens to the bank. The probability of first tails on flip n is 2^(-n), so the probability-weighted payoff for outcome n is (-1)^(n-1) / n. In the natural order n = 1, 2, 3, ..., the running total converges to ln 2 ≈ 0.693 tokens. Use three tickets that describe exactly the same gamble but list the outcomes in three different orders:

- Ticket A, natural order: n = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, ...
- Ticket B, two positive terms of the alternating series followed by one negative term: outcomes in the order 1, 3, 2, 5, 7, 4, 9, 11, 6, 13, 15, 8, ...; the running total converges to (3/2) ln 2 ≈ 1.040 tokens.
- Ticket C, one positive term followed by eight negative terms: outcomes in the order 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, 16, 3, 18, 20, 22, 24, 26, 28, ...; the running total converges to -(1/2) ln 2 ≈ -0.347 tokens.

Each ticket should come with a pre-printed worksheet whose columns are: outcome number n, probability, token payoff, probability-weighted payoff (pre-printed, to keep the arithmetic light), and a blank running-total column. Groups compute running totals for the first 16 listed outcomes, then write down the most they would pay to play the gamble once, allowing negative amounts for groups that conclude they would have to be paid to take it. Follow this with a sealed-bid token auction: each group starts with, say, 10 tokens, bids may be negative, and the winning group pays its bid and then plays a scaled, capped trial run of the gamble for token stakes (payoffs divided by 100, flips capped at 10, no payoff if no tails by flip 10), with the scaling and cap stated openly as a practical stand-in for the infinite gamble. Then — and only then — reveal that all three tickets describe the same gamble, and ask the room which fair price was right.

The debrief should map what happened onto the mathematics: why the sum is only conditionally convergent; why rearranging its terms can make it converge to any real number, with the three tickets as three instances; why expected utility theory therefore needs absolute convergence or some other restriction before an expectation is well-defined; and how this differs from the St. Petersburg paradox, where the expectation is infinite rather than undefined. Also say what the activity cannot reproduce — in particular, that the trial run is finite and capped, so it has a well-defined expectation, while the genuinely infinite object is only being priced, not played.

- The debrief (500 words): the mapping from the running totals and bids to conditional convergence, rearrangement, and undefined expectations.
