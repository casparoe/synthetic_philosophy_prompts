I'm teaching a unit on distributive ethics and want a handout that works as a decision tree for where students actually stand on the leveling-down objection. Start everyone from the same concrete case: a policy lowers the welfare of the best-off group while helping no one, not even indirectly, and thereby makes the distribution more equal. The first branch shouldn't be 'are you an egalitarian?'—it should be 'is there anything at all good about that outcome, even if you wouldn't call it better all things considered?' Then keep branching on claims, not labels. I want these distinctions in there somewhere: whether the good of equality lives in the outcome itself or in something relational like fairness or what people are owed; whether benefits to the worse off matter more than equal benefits to the better off even when inequality itself doesn't; and whether there's a threshold below which losses matter especially or lexically. Build the leaves from the answer combinations first, then attach names. The leaves should include telic egalitarianism (split pluralist vs monist if the answers force it), deontic or relational egalitarianism, prioritarianism, sufficientarianism, and plain utilitarianism. For each leaf add one sentence on its most awkward commitment—for instance, telic egalitarianism has to say leveling down is good in at least one respect. Also flag unoccupied combinations: is there anyone who says inequality is intrinsically bad but denies that leveling down is even pro tanto good? Or someone who accepts priority for the worse off and also thinks inequality itself is bad? Say briefly whether each empty cell is incoherent, unattractive, or just underexplored. And flag any pair of leaves the tree can't really seperate—I suspect deontic egalitarianism and prioritarianism can answer the leveling-down case identically and only come apart on a question about what grounds the complaint. Keep it to six or seven branch nodes, and phrase every question so a student can answer it without already knowing the terms.
