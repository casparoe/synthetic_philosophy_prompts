I'm a journalist covering the rationalist/EA scene and I'm doing a piece on decision theory — specifically this thing called XOR blackmail. The basic idea (if you haven't come across it) is that a blackmailer can write you a letter saying "I have dirt on you and I'll send it to your spouse unless you pay up," but the blackmailer wrote the letter *before* checking whether they actually have dirt. The weird thing is, even if you read the letter and know the blackmailer might be bluffing because they literally don't know if they have anything on you, it can still be rational to pay. The logic has this acausal flavor that messes with people's heads.

I'm running a workshop at a small ideas festival — about 25 people, mixed backgrounds but intellectually curious, 75 minutes. I want to design an in-person game or role-play where participants end up *in* the structure of an XOR blackmail without me having to explain it first. By the end of the game, before I even name the concept, people should feel the pull — like "wait, I think I have to pay this person even though they might be totally bluffing." Then a debrief that connects what their group just lived through to the formal puzzle, and explains where the paradox bites and where it doesn't.

I need: rules, materials, a payoff scheme that doesn't use real money, the faciliator script or timeline, and an honest postmortem. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and which part of the formal XOR blackmail setup the activity can't reproduce — the thing I'll have to just explain in the debrief.
