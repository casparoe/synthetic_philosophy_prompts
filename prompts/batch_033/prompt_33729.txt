I've been reading about the problem of building AI systems that can reason about their own reasoning in a trustworthy way, and I keep running into references to something called the Löbian obstacle. From what I gather, the idea is roughly this: an agent that wants to endorse its own deductive processes — to trust the principle "if I prove that φ, then φ" — runs into trouble because of Löb's theorem, which is a somewhat arcane result in modal logic related to Gödel's second incompleteness theorem. But I'm having trouble understanding the precise shape of the problem, and the secondary literature I've found tends either to presuppose too much or to gesture at the idea without really unpacking it.

Specifically, I'd like a clear explanation of what the Löbian obstacle is and why it's philosophically significant. What does it mean for an agent to "trust" its own reasoning in the relevant sense, and why does Löb's theorem get in the way? I've seen the theorem stated as □(□φ → φ) → □φ, but I don't see how to map that formalism onto an agent trying to reason about its own future deductions. Is the obstacle simply that the agent can't prove its own consistency, or is it something more subtle — something about the structure of self-referential trust itself? And if the obstacle is genuine, what are the candidate ways around it? I've heard that probabilistic reflection principles, or the idea of a sequence of ever-stronger reasoners that each trust only the ones earlier in the sequence (a "reflectively stable" hierarchy), might escape the problem, but I'd like to understand whether those approaches genuinely circumvent Löb's theorem or merely relocate the difficulty to a higher level. I'm not a logician by training, so I'd appreciate an explanation that doesn't presuppose fluency with provability logic, though I'm comfortable with the broad strokes of Gödelian incompleteness and the idea that formal systems can encode claims about their own proofs.
