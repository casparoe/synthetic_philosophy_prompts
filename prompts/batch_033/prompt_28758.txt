I've been trying to work out what I actually think about how much we owe future generations, so I wrote down my gut reactions to a bunch of scenarios. I'd like someone to tell me whether there's a principle that captures all of these, or close to it, and which verdicts I might have to give up if not.

Here goes:

1. A government has the option to build sea walls that will cost £2 billion now and will prevent catastrophic flooding in 150 years that would otherwise displace millions. No one alive today benefits directly. I say: build them. Easy.

2. Same as above, but the cost is £200 billion — a sum that would meaningfully reduce public spending on health and education for a generation. I say: no, that's too much to ask of people alive now for people that distant. I'm less comfortable with this verdict than I'd like to be but it's where I land.

3. A city is deciding whether to invest in a tree-planting programme that will cost £5 million now and will start providing significant cooling and air-quality benefits in about 30 years, continuing for decades after. Many people alive today will see the benefits, including children and young adults. I say: obviously do it.

4. Suppose we discover a technology that, if deployed at enormous cost over the next century — say, diverting 5% of global GDP for 100 years — would guarantee a stable climate and abundant resources for the next 50,000 years, benefiting trillions of future people. I feel like we should do it, but I find myself hesitating in a way I don't with case 1, and I can't articulate why. The numbers clearly favour it. Something about asking for sacrifices on that scale, for people that unimaginably far in the future, makes me baulk. Is that just irrational?

5. A policy would impose moderate costs on the current generation — higher energy prices, say — to avoid severe harms to people living 200 years from now. But we have good reason to think those future people will be much richer than us, because of continued economic growth. I say: they can look after themselves. We shouldn't make ourselves poorer for the sake of people who'll be richer than we are.

6. Same as case 5, except the future people won't be richer — we're imagining economic stagnation or collapse, so they'll be about as well-off as we are or worse. I say: then we should make the sacrifice.

7. A policy would impose moderate costs now to prevent a catastrophe that has a 2% chance of wiping out humanity entirely in the next 300 years. I say: spend the money without hesitation, even if it's expensive. This feels like it matters more than any of the other cases.

8. Last one. A policy to phase out a polluting industry would impose severe costs on a specific poor community today — job losses, economic decline — but would prevent significant harms to a much larger number of future people, most of whom will be better off than that community is now. I say: you can't do it, or at least not without compensating that community so fully that the policy might not be worth it. Sacrificing the vulnerable now for the comfortable later is obscene.

So that's where I am. Cases 1, 3, and 7 feel solid. Cases 5 and 6 suggest I care about relative wealth, not just about whether future people exist. Case 4 bothers me because I can't square my hesitation with my answer to case 1 — the scale is different but the logic feels like it should be the same. And case 8 complicates things because it makes me think distribution within generations matters as much as distribution across them, which doesn't sit neatly with how I treated cases 5 and 6.

My question: is there a principle — or a small family of principles — that gets all or most of these verdicts? If not, which ones should I reconsider? And if you think I'm wrong to hesitate on case 4, tell me why and what that implies for the rest. Also, if the whole exercise is misguided somehow — like I'm smuggling in assumptions that don't survive scrutiny, or the cases are set up in a way that makes the question impossible to answer cleanly — I'd rather hear that than have you bend a principle into a pretzel to fit my intuitions.
