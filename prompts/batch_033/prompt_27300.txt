I've been going down a rabbit hole on no-trade theorems — Aumann's agreement theorem, Milgrom-Stokey, all that — and I've ended up somewhere like this: if you take the theorems at face value, they show rational agents with common priors can't agree to disagree, and can't profitably trade on private information once that info is common knowledge. The implication for how we think about markets is that most of what looks like genuine disagreement or speculative trading is either irrationality or unjustified heterogeneous priors. The theorems basically put the burden of proof on anyone who thinks markets are full of rational people genuinely disagreeing about fundamentals.

What I want is a map of what would count against this position. I know the obvious moves — people contest the common prior assumption, they bring up computational limits — but I want something more systematic. What observations, formal results, or kinds of market behavior would a defender of this view have to concede as counting against it? Which defeaters are fatal, which are just awkward, and which would defenders dismiss as missing the point?

I also want to know whether the position has been insulated into unfalsifiability — like, "rational agents with common priors won't disagree, but real agents don't have common priors and aren't fully rational, so the theorem doesn't actually predict anything we observe." If that's where things end up, is that a problem? Or is the theorem doing different work — setting a normative benchmark rather than making empirical claims — and that's fine?

And please seperate what's settled among people who work on this from what's genuinely contested. Don't give me a contested reading dressed up as the consensus.
