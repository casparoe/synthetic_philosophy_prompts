Before I retired I spent my career as a physical chemist, and I have only recently begun working through the philosophical literature on conditionals. In the laboratory I was constantly reasoning about what would have happened if some variable had been different, and for years I assumed that the difference between "if A, B" and "if A had been the case, B would have been the case" was a matter of grammar rather than logic. I no longer believe that, and I have been collecting my own reactions to pairs of conditionals in the hope of finding the principle behind them.

Here are the cases. (1) Kennedy: I accept "If Oswald didn't shoot Kennedy, someone else did" almost without hesitation, but I reject, or at least greatly distrust, "If Oswald hadn't shot Kennedy, someone else would have." The first seems to record something about my information, while the second seems to make a claim about how history would have unfolded, and I see no reason to think some other assassin was waiting. (2) The shattered flask: a flask in my old laboratory broke during the night; the only two ways this could have happened were the cat knocking it off the bench or the wind blowing it off, and I do not know which occurred. I accept both "If the cat didn't knock it over, the wind did" and "If the cat hadn't knocked it over, the wind would have," with roughly equal confidence. (3) Barometer: I know the barometer fell sharply before a storm, and I also know the barometer merely registers atmospheric pressure. I accept "If the barometer fell sharply, a storm followed," but I reject "If the barometer hadn't fallen sharply, the storm wouldn't have followed." (4) Coin toss: I have no idea whether a certain fair coin was ever flipped, and I reject both "If the coin was flipped, it landed heads" and "If the coin had been flipped, it would have landed heads."

My working hypothesis is that indicatives are epistemic—they record what follows from my information—while counterfactuals are causal or metaphysical—they record how things would have gone in an alternative course of events. That would handle (1), (3), and (4), but it seems to break on (2), where I accepted a counterfactual on what look like purely epistemic grounds; and I cannot see a principled difference between (2) and (1), which troubles me. Please work through the cases one by one, try out candidate principles against them, and tell me (a) what principle, if any, captures all four verdicts, (b) whether my hypothesis can be repaired or whether one of my verdicts should be abandoned, and (c) what the principle predicts for two further pairs I am still undecided about: the manager-and-guard case ("If the manager didn't open the safe, the guard did" / "If the manager hadn't opened the safe, the guard would have," when I know exactly one of them did it), and the diagnostic-test case ("If the test came back positive, the patient had the disease" / "If the test had come back positive, the patient would have had the disease," for a reliable test that does not cause the disease). Please answer as a professional philosopher of language working on conditionals, and take your time rather than pattern-matching to familiar positions. I am not asking for a survey of the literature or for citations; I want a precise principle and an honest statement of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate.
