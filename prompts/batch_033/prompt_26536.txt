Take the October 2024 longshore strike on the US East and Gulf coasts and analyze it through Marx's views on technology and automation. I know the broad facts: the ILA walked out for three days, shut down a big chunk of container port capacity, and got a tentative deal with a wage increase around 62% over six years, while the actual fight over semi-automated cranes and other automation got defered to later talks. The union leadership talked about it as a fight against machines taking jobs; the employers talked about it as productivity and staying competitive.

What I want is a careful mapping, not the standard automation-kills-jobs summary. In Marx's terms, who are the actors and what are their structural positions? What options and payoffs does the framework say each side faced, and what should each have done by it's lights? Then tell me which features of the strike the machinery analysis explains well -- why automation is the real sticking point, how the wage deal relates to productivity, the shape of the conflict -- and which features it gets wrong or has nothing to say about, like a union that isn't actually anti-technology, that won big raises by choking a bottleneck, and a deal where automation is postponed rather than defeated. If the fragment on machines material is relevant, bring it in, but don't just name-drop it. Finally, run it the other direction: is this episode a confirmation of the theory, an anomaly for it, or a case it never anticipated? Be honest about whether Marx's claim that machinery under capitalism immiserates labor fits a case where dockworkers seem to have bought themselves a bigger share of the productivity gains, and say whether that means the theory is wrong or just that this is a temporary local exception.
