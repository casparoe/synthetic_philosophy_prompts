I'm a philosophy of mind PhD student, and next month I have to lead a two-hour session for first-year graduate students in my department who work mainly in ethics, ancient philosophy, and political theory; none of them has read the mental causation literature. The topic is the causal exclusion argument as a challenge to nonreductive physicalism. I don't need the argument explained to me — I need a plan for explaining it. Please tell me: the order in which the components should be introduced and what depends on what; specifically, whether it would be a mistake to state the causal closure of the physical before the audience feels the pull of the problem of mental causation, and how to sequence supervenience, closure, and the exclusion principle so the dilemma lands as something they see rather than something imposed. Then give me the one worked example that should carry the whole argument — what features it must have and what it must not have; I am wary of the decision-to-raise-one's-hand case because it drags in free-will intuitions, and I want your judgment on whether a pain causing withdrawal or an intention causing an action does better. Then map the misconceptions this audience is likely to form — my guesses are that they will slide from exclusion to "so mental properties do nothing," that they will think the argument secretly assumes dualism, and that they will offer overdetermination as an easy way out — and tell me exactly where in the sequence I should pre-empt each one rather than wait for it to harden. End with one diagnostic question I can ask at the close that would show whether they have grasped the force of the dilemma rather than merely restated the premises, and say what I should openly refuse to adjudicate at this level.
