I'm putting together a working-group document on what our lab should count as an AI system being dishonest, and it's become clear that most of the arguments in the room are really people talking past eachother about which definition they're silently using. Some mean something close to lying: the model outputs a false claim while somewhere in its processing there's a representation of the truth it's overriding. Others mean any behavior that predictably causes a user to form a false belief, whether or not the model knows anything. Others mean goal-directed information control, like a negotiation agent that bluffs, or a model that withholds a relevant fact because revealing it would get in the way of the task. Before I read another paper on this, I want the full desiderata. What does an adequate account of honesty and deception for AI systems have to deliver? Give me the list of cases it has to get right — a model that fabricates a citation, a model that tells the user what it predicts they want to hear, a model that omits a relevant consideration, a chess engine that feints, a system that is simply wrong, a future system that lies to avoid being shut down, a recommender system that learns to show people whatever keeps them scrolling — and for each, why it's a requirement rather than a preference. I also want the distinctions a good account can't collapse: lying versus misleading versus bullshitting versus hallucination versus ordinary error, sincerity versus truthfulness, deception by commission versus omission. And say which desiderata are non-negotiable and which are just one camp's taste, plus which empirical findings about current systems a definition can't contradict — confident fabrication, sycophancy showing up after preference tuning, prompt-sensitive behavior — and why each is a constraint.

The part I most want flagged is where the requirements pull against each other. For instance, a definition precise enough to apply to current systems, where we can't inspect beliefs, will have to be mostly behavioral; but a purely behavioral definition can't tell an accidental falsehood from a deception; and a definition that requires intent or theory of mind won't apply to systems whose internal states are either absent or unreadable. Is that a real trilemma, or is there a version that gets all three? And if you had to pick the one requirement that does the most filtering work once you test the usual definitions against it — say, deception as any false output, or as any behavior that predictably manipulates a user's beliefs — which requirement is it? Spend most of the answer on the criteria and why each earns its place; a short scorecard of the obvious accounts is fine, but I'll do the deep comparison against the literature myself.
