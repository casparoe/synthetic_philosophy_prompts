Me and my friend got into this argument in philosophy club and we built something that seems to prove too much, and I cant find the exit. We were arguing about whether you should set your confidence to match objective chances. The principle seems obviously right: if you know the objective chance of a coin landing heads is 0.9, and you have no special inside info, your credence that it lands heads should be 0.9. Call that the deference principle.

Then we tried a toy case. Imagine a tiny universe with exactly ten coin tosses, full stop, and the chance of heads on any toss is just the proportion of the ten tosses that land heads — so chance is nothing over and above the actual pattern of outcomes (the 'Humean' picture, which we both liked because it doesn't make chances into spooky extra facts). Suppose the chance of heads is 0.9, so nine of the ten tosses land heads and one lands tails. You've watched the first nine tosses and they all came up heads. The tenth toss is about to happen. Here's the argument:

(1) You know the objective chance that toss #10 lands heads is 0.9.
(2) By the deference principle, your credence that toss #10 lands heads should be 0.9.
(3) But you also know the first nine tosses were heads, and you know the chance being 0.9 just means nine of ten tosses land heads. So exactly one toss is tails, and since the first nine are heads, toss #10 has to be tails.
(4) So your credence that toss #10 lands heads should be 0.
(5) Contradiction.

The obvious fix is to say the chance fact is 'inadmissible' because it's a global fact that's partly about the future, so the deference principle doesn't apply. But that fix feels like it proves too much, or at least guts the principle: the whole point was to tell us what to do when we learn a chance, and now we're saying you can't use it exactly when you learn a chance. And if we instead say any info that's partly about the future is inadmissible, then the principle never applies to real people, since we always know alot of things about how the future will go.

I want to keep the deference principle and maybe ditch the Humean toy model, but I honestly don't see how that helps, because the contradiction seemed to come just from knowing the outcomes, not from where chances come from. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
