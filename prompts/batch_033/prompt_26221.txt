ok so I've been thinking about the surprise exam paradox and its variants, and I want to figure out what my intuitions actually commit me to. here are my verdicts on a bunch of cases — they're not all consistent and I know it, but I want to see if there's a principle that gets most of them and tells me which ones I have to give up.

Case A (the classic). A teacher announces on Friday: "There will be exactly one exam next week, Monday through Friday, and it will be a surprise — meaning you won't be able to deduce the day of the exam from this announcement before the exam happens." The student reasons backward: it can't be Friday, because if it hasn't happened by Thursday night I'd know it's Friday. Eliminate Friday. Then it can't be Thursday, because if it hasn't happened by Wednesday night and Friday's eliminated, I'd know it's Thursday. And so on, eliminating all days. The student concludes no exam can be given. On Wednesday the teacher gives the exam, and the student is surprised. My verdict: genuinely paradoxical. The reasoning feels airtight but the conclusion is false. Something deep is going on, not just a sloppy equivocation.

Case B (two-day week). Same setup but the week is just Monday and Tuesday. Student eliminates Tuesday, then Monday. Teacher gives the exam on Monday. Student is surprised. My verdict: still paradoxical, but I can almost see the flaw — it feels like the student's reasoning about Monday depends on having eliminated Tuesday, but the elimination of Tuesday depended on getting to Monday night, which never happens if the exam is Monday. But I still can't fully articulate why the reasoning fails in a way that doesn't also feel ad hoc.

Case C (one-day week). Teacher says: "There will be a surprise exam tomorrow." Student reasons: if there's an exam tomorrow, I now know it's tomorrow, so it won't be a surprise, so the announcement can't be satisfied, so there won't be an exam. Teacher gives the exam the next day. Student is surprised because they'd convinced themselves there wouldn't be one. My verdict: not paradoxical. This is just a self-undermining belief. The student's reasoning is obviously bad — the fact that you've talked yourself into believing there's no exam doesn't make the exam unsurprising when it happens.

Case D (self-referential reformulation). Teacher says: "There will be an exam next week, and you cannot prove from this announcement which day it will be." Assume "prove" means prove in the student's formal system, which is strong enough to encode statements about provability. My verdict: this isn't the same paradox anymore — it's now a Gödel-type thing. The announcement might be true but unprovable, which is interesting but not the same puzzle. So: not the surprise exam paradox, or at least a fundamentally different beast.

Case E (the teacher is known to lie sometimes). Same as Case A, but the students know the teacher lies about exam dates roughly 20% of the time. My verdict: the paradox dissolves. The backward induction doesn't go through because at each step you can't be certain the teacher was telling the truth. Not paradoxical, just a straightforward epistemic situation.

Case F (multiple exams). Teacher announces two surprise exams next week, same definition of surprise. Student tries backward induction and concludes no exams can be given. Teacher gives exams on Tuesday and Thursday. Student is surprised both times. My verdict: still paradoxical, same structure as A, maybe even harder to dismiss because the induction is more complex.

Case G (surprise means "you won't know the night before"). Same as A but "surprise" is defined as: on the evening before the exam, you won't yet know it's the next day. So the student can figure it out the morning of. My verdict: not paradoxical. The backward induction fails at the first step — you can't eliminate Friday because on Thursday night you wouldn't know it's Friday (you only find out Friday morning). The whole chain breaks. This feels like a clean dissolution, and it makes me suspect the original paradox depends on a specific (maybe implausible) reading of "surprise."
