So I've been trying to get my head around this whole AI-safety-via-debate thing, and there's a specific sub-problem that I feel like I almost understand but not quite. The basic setup, as I get it, is: you have two AIs arguing in front of a human judge. One argues for the correct answer, one argues against it. The human can ask questions. The idea is that even if the human couldn't have figured out the truth on their own, the adversarial dynamic flushes it out — because if the lying AI says something false, the honest AI can point out exactly where the lie is.

But then there's this worry about "obfuscated arguments." The idea being that a clever enough liar doesn't just say false things — they construct arguments that are technically valid in some sense but hide the crucial misleading step in a way the human judge can't spot, even when the honest AI tries to flag it. And I've seen people talk about this as if it's maybe the central hard problem for the whole debate paradigm.

What I'm confused about is: what are the actually different *kinds* of obfuscation people worry about here? I've seen mentions of steganography-like hiding, and of arguments that exploit the judge's inductive biases, and of cases where the issue is computational rather than informational — but I don't have a clear map of how these relate to each other or which ones are considered the real dealbreakers. Like, is the worry that the honest debater literally can't *point to* the hidden flaw? Or that they can point to it but the judge won't *understand* the pointing? Or something else entirely? And how does the debate structure — with cross-examination and the ability to break things down into sub-questions — actually fare against each of these? Would love a walkthrough that distinguishes the different failure modes here rather than just lumping them all as "obfuscation."
