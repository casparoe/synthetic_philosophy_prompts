I recently joined the acquisitions committee of a mid-sized art museum, and in preparation I read a well-regarded popular philosophy book that includes a chapter on aesthetic testimony. The author summarizes the debate as follows:

"The core question is whether you can gain aesthetic knowledge from other people's say-so. Pessimists hold that you cannot: to know that a painting is beautiful or a piece of music is sublime, you must experience it yourself—this is often called the acquaintance principle, and it traces back to Kant's claim that aesthetic judgment must be based on one's own feeling rather than on concepts or the reports of others. Optimists about aesthetic testimony reply that this is an untenable form of epistemic snobbery. We rely on critics, curators, and friends for aesthetic guidance constantly, and it would be bizarre to deny that we know anything from them—surely you can know that the Rothko Chapel is worth visiting without having been there. The debate has largely been resolved by distinguishing two things: you cannot acquire the aesthetic experience itself through testimony, but you can certainly acquire justified aesthetic beliefs from reliable testifiers. The pessimist's insight survives only as the modest reminder that testimony cannot replace firsthand experience if your goal is to appreciate the work yourself."

I need to know how much of this I can trust, because my committee work will require me to vote on acquisitions based partly on curators' written reports about works I have not seen in person. Is this summary a fair representation of the philosophical landscape? Please give me exactly five points, each a sentence or two, identifying what is accurate, what is misleading or overstated, and what a philosopher working on aesthetic testimony would push back on. No introduction or conclusion.
