I've been collecting my own reactions to decisions under ambiguity, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, they add up to. Here are my verdicts.

(1) Urn A has 50 red and 50 black balls; Urn B has 100 balls, but I'm not told the proportion of red to black. I prefer a $10 bet on red drawn from A to the same bet on red drawn from B, and I also prefer the same bet on black drawn from A to the same bet on black drawn from B.

(2) If I'm told Urn B was filled by tossing a fair coin separately for each ball, I'm indifferent between the urns, both for red and for black.

(3) If I'm told only that Urn B contains between 45 and 55 red balls, I still lean toward Urn A, but only barely.

(4) If I'm told Urn B is either all red or all black, with nothing indicating which, I prefer Urn A even more strongly than in case (1).

(5) Yet if you ask me to state a single fair price for a bet paying $10 on red from Urn B in case (1), I say $5, and I would call one-half the fairest single probability of red.

Is there a coherent account — I have heard the phrase imprecise probabilities but do not know whether it fits — that gets all of these, or close? Please state the principle precisely, say which of my verdicts motivates each part of it, tell me what it predicts about a case I have not listed, and be honest about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and whether I should overturn that verdict or complicate the principle. Please write your answer in American English.
