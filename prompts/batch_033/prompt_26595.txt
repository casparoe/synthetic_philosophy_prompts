I'm a machine learning engineer working on a project that involves learning general rules from sparse observations — think of it as training a classifier where the hypothesis space is rules like "all X are Y." A colleague pointed me to Hempel's raven paradox, and now I can't stop thinking about it in ML terms.

For those who haven't encountered it: the paradox starts with the hypothesis "all ravens are black." This is logically equivalent to "all non-black things are non-ravens." So observing a black raven confirms the hypothesis — but by the same logic, observing a non-black non-raven (a white shoe, a red apple) also confirms it. This seems absurd: my white sneakers shouldn't tell me anything about ravens. Yet the logic seems impeccable. Philosophers have been arguing about this since the 1940s, and the main responses include appeals to background knowledge (we already know most things aren't ravens, so a white shoe provides negligible confirmation), appeals to the data-generating process (we didn't sample shoes to learn about ravens), and more radical proposals that reject the logical equivalence move or the Nicod criterion itself.
