I've been reporting on a neurotechnology startup that plans to offer what they call "gradual uploading." The procedure works like this: over the course of several months, a patient's biological neurons are replaced one at a time with artificial neurons — nanoscale devices that precisely replicate the input-output behavior of the original cell. The patient remains conscious throughout and reports, at each stage, feeling like the same person with the same memories and personality. At the end, the skull contains a fully artificial brain, and the removed biological neurons have been cryopreserved.

Now a rival company has proposed a follow-up service: take those preserved neurons, thaw them, and reassemble them into a working biological brain — restoring the original organic substrate. The question is: which brain does the patient wake up in?

An argument has been circulating in the philosophy-of-mind corners of my reading that seems to show the answer is "both" — which cannot be right. It runs like this:

(1) If you replace a single neuron in my brain with a functionally identical artificial one while I am conscious, I survive the operation. Afterwards, I am still me — the same person.

(2) If I am the same person after each step in a sequence of operations, then I am the same person after the whole sequence. If step 1 preserves me, and step 2 preserves me, and so on through step N, then the person at the end of step N is me.

(3) Therefore, after all N neurons have been replaced one at a time, the person with the artificial brain is me.

(4) Now consider the biological neurons. Losing a single neuron to a micro-stroke does not kill the person — the biological brain minus one neuron is still me. So at the first replacement, the biological remainder, if it could be kept alive and functioning, would still be me.

(5) By the same transitivity reasoning as in (2), if we removed neurons one at a time while keeping the biological remainder alive and functioning at each step, the biological brain at the end of the process — the very one the rival company proposes to reassemble — would also be me.

(6) But the artificial brain and the reassembled biological brain are two numerically distinct entities. One person cannot be identical to two distinct things.

(7) So the premises that seemed harmless — that one-neuron replacement preserves personal identity, and that identity is preserved through a series of identity-preserving steps — lead to a contradiction.

I find myself stuck. The argument's steps each feel plausible, but the conclusion is unacceptable. I can see several places one might push back: perhaps premise (1) is false and replacing even a single neuron destroys the original person, replacing them with a copy. Perhaps the transitivity assumed in (2) does not hold for personal identity in the way it holds for the identity of objects. Perhaps the move in (4) and (5) is illegitimate because the biological neurons were not kept functioning as a unified brain during the procedure, so the reassembly case is not analogous to the gradual replacement case. Or perhaps the whole setup smuggles in a false picture of what a person is.

I want to know which premise or step you would reject, and what else you have to accept or give up as a result. If you reject (1), you seem committed to saying that none of us survives the natural turnover of cells in our bodies — which happens constantly, if more slowly in the brain. If you reject (2), you seem to be saying that "same person" is not a transitive relation, which is a strange thing to say about identity. If you reject (4) or (5), you need to explain why the biological brain's claim to being me is weaker than the artificial brain's claim, given that the underlying reasoning looks parallel. Walk me through the options.
