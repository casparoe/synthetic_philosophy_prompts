You are a professional philosopher specialising in metaphilosophy—the philosophy of philosophy—and you have published on the question of whether and how philosophy makes progress. Please think carefully before answering, and feel free to begin by brainstorming and mapping out the logical space before committing to your final assessment.

I am writing up my views on philosophical progress for a workshop and I would like a rigorous consistency audit before I present. Here is where I have landed:

(1) Science makes cumulative progress in a way that philosophy does not. There is nothing in philosophy analogous to the settled body of knowledge you find in a physics or chemistry textbook—no philosophical result commands the kind of near-universal assent among competent practitioners that, say, the second law of thermodynamics does.

(2) Nonetheless, I think philosophy makes genuine progress, but of a different kind. It progresses by mapping conceptual space: clarifying the logical relations among positions, discovering new distinctions, identifying hidden assumptions, and ruling out views that collapse under scrutiny. We know more about the structure of the options now than we did a century ago.

(3) When a philosophical subfield reaches a point where its central questions can be settled empirically, it ceases to be classified as philosophy and becomes a science—so philosophy's apparent lack of cumulative progress is partly an artefact of how we retrospectively carve up the disciplines. The successes of physics, linguistics, and psychology are in an important sense also successes of the philosophical traditions that incubated them.

(4) I hold that the persistence of deep, apparently intractable disagreement among experts on foundational questions—the mind–body problem, the nature of moral properties, the existence of abstract objects—is a sign that those questions themselves are somehow defective or ill-posed, not that philosophers are simply bad at answering them. A well-posed question should admit of convergence among competent inquirers given enough time and ingenuity.

(5) I think the history of philosophy is indispensable to doing philosophy well. You cannot just pick up the contemporary literature and contribute meaningfully without understanding the historical development of the problems—the motivations, the dead ends, the moves that were tried and abandoned and why.

(6) I am sceptical that the method of counterexample-driven intuition-mongering that dominates much of analytic philosophy is a reliable route to truth, even if it is a useful tool for the mapping work described in (2). I suspect it tells us more about our concepts than about the world, and I doubt our concepts are a reliable guide to how things are at the fundamental level.
