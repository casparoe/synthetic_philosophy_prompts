Consider the nonidentity problem: when a policy or choice affects which particular individuals come to exist, and those who do exist have lives worth living, it is difficult to claim that any of them has been harmed — had the policy not been enacted, they would not have existed at all. The problem is usually taken to challenge person-affecting ethical theories, which locate harm and wrongdoing in effects on particular individuals. Jeremy Bentham's classical utilitarianism, by contrast, is impersonal: it evaluates actions by the total sum of pleasure and pain they produce, without regard to whose pleasure or pain it is. What would Bentham have made of the nonidentity problem? Would he have regarded it as a genuine philosophical difficulty, or as a confusion that an impersonal utilitarian can simply sidestep? If he would have dismissed it, was his dismissal too quick — does the nonidentity problem expose any difficulty within classical utilitarianism itself, or does it only trouble views that reject it? If he would have taken it seriously, how might he have responded?
