I'm teaching a political philosophy course and we're reading Robert Penn Warren's *All the King's Men* alongside Simone Weil's "On the Abolition of All Political Parties." Weil argues that political parties are structurally incapable of serving truth or justice: a party exists to grow and capture power, and this telos inevitably subordinates individual conscience, intellectual honesty, and the common good to collective discipline and electoral advantage. Read Willie Stark's trajectory through Weil's lens. Where does the novel dramatize Weil's diagnosis — the way party machinery hollows out the idealism that brought Stark to power, the way loyalty to the party replaces loyalty to truth? And where does the novel complicate or resist Weil's conclusion that parties must be abolished rather than reformed? I want a reading that takes both the novel and Weil's argument seriously, and that is willing to say whether the novel offers something Weil's essay misses — for instance, a picture of political life that acknowledges the inescapability of collective organization even while showing its corruptions.
