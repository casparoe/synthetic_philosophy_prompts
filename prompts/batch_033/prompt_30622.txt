Lately I've been going back and forth with two friends about whether thought experiments in philosophy are a way of learning something new or just a way of dressing up what you already believed. One of them keeps reaching for a piece of plumbing to settle the matter: he says thought experiments are 'intuition pumps,' and that a pump only moves fluid that is already there — it doesn't create water — so a thought experiment can't generate new insight, it can only mobilize intuitions the thinker already had. My other friend says the metaphor, taken seriously, cuts the other way: a well-built pump can move water uphill, against the direction it would naturally flow, and that is exactly what a careful thought experiment does to an intuition — it pushes it somewhere it wouldn't have gone on its own. I've seen the phrase used all over the literature, and I want to know how much argumentative weight it can actually bear before I concede either point.

I'd like you to audit the plumbing. Treat 'intuition pump' as an analogy rather than a neutral label, and work through what corresponds to what: the pump itself, the fluid, the pressure or energy source, the pipes and valves, priming, leaks, and the uphill/downhill direction of flow. Then separate the disanalogies that matter for the two conclusions above from the ones that are merely incidental. I'm especially interested in whether the pump metaphor quietly imports features that thought experiments lack — for instance, that a pump doesn't alter the composition of what it moves, that it needs an external power source, and that it is causally indifferent to what it pumps — and what those imported features do to each friend's argument. If the metaphor is doing illicit work, say what a better analogy would be and why it avoids the problem.
