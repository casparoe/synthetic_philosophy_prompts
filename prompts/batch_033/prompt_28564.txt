I work on the authentication team at a large consumer technology company whose app handles roughly a billion logins per month, and we have been going back and forth for weeks about a proposed change to the login flow. The current flow has a rare race condition that, a few times a month, corrupts a user's account state in a way that requires a manual support reset. Most of those users are merely inconvenienced for a day or two, but a small subset experience consequences that seem to me genuinely serious: missed time-sensitive payments, lost access to documents at critical moments, and at least one case in which someone lost a job opportunity because she could not retrieve a form in time. The proposed fix would add an extra synchronous verification step that eliminates the failure mode but adds roughly half a second of latency to every login.

A colleague on the product side has been arguing that shipping the fix would be clearly wrong. Half a second per login, multiplied by a billion logins per month, amounts to something like 140,000 hours of added delay each month, and he insists that this dwarfs the harm suffered by the few dozen users a year who end up seriously affected. No reasonable consequentialist, he says, could treat the two as comparable. I share his broadly consequentialist outlook, and I have read enough rationalist-adjacent writing to be suspicious of my own intuitive reactions to very large numbers. Yet something about the move from hundreds of millions of individually trivial pauses to a single accumulated mass of lost time keeps striking me as wrong, though I cannot say exactly why. I worry that my resistance is just an aesthetic aversion to a counterintuitive conclusion. How should I think about this? Is there a defensible way to resist my colleague's summation, or is he right?
