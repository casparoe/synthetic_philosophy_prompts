I keep noticing this pattern in committee votes and I dont have a name for it. Take a three-person committee deciding whether to hire someone, where the rule is that you hire only if the candidate is both qualified and a good fit. Each committee member has a perfectly consistent set of views: whoever says yes to both sub-questions also says yes to hire, and whoever says no to hire also says no to at least one sub-question. But when the votes are tallied question by question, you can get two members saying qualified, two saying good fit, and two saying no to hire. So the committee's collective answers can't all be true together, even though each member's answers can. I've seen the same structure with three sub-questions and with yes/no votes on factual premises that are then supposed to settle a policy question.

I know this is somewhere near Condorcet's paradox, where majority preferences cycle, and maybe near Simpson's paradox, where a pattern reverses when you split the data. But it doesn't feel like either: there's no cycle among three options, and there's no statistical data. The weird thing is specifically that the group endorses each part but rejects the whole that follows from the parts. What's also odd is that it doesn't go away if the committee discusses things first, and it can happen with a two-thirds threshold, not just a simple majority. And the outcome sometimes flips depending on whether we vote on the sub-questions first or the main question first, even though nobody changes their mind.

Is there an established name for this exact thing, and is it a real problem for majority rule or just an arithemetic quirk of how votes get added up? If there's no name, what would you call it, and how would you define it so I can tell it apart from Condorcet's paradox, Simpson's paradox, and plain cases where a committee has mixed reasons? Dont just give me a label — I want to know what's essential to the phenomenon and where its edges are. Also, whatever verdict you give on whether it's a serious problem, tell me what would change your mind: which consideration would make you flip from 'real problem' to 'harmless artifact,' or the other way.
