I'm putting together a statement of my own views on decision-making under uncertainty for a reading group, and before I circulate it I'd like an audit of the case judgements I've recorded. The cases are all of the same simple kind: a single ball is drawn from an urn, and a ticket pays a fixed sum depending on the colour drawn. Here are my verdicts.

First, the Ellsberg three-colour urn: 30 balls are red, and the remaining 60 are black or yellow in a proportion I am not told. Offered a ticket paying £100 if the ball is red versus one paying £100 if the ball is black, I strictly prefer the red ticket; offered £100 if the ball is red or yellow versus £100 if the ball is black or yellow, I strictly prefer the black-or-yellow ticket. Second, two urns: urn K contains 50 red and 50 black balls; urn A contains 100 balls, each red or black, in a proportion I am not told. I strictly prefer a ticket paying £100 if red is drawn from K to the corresponding ticket on red from A, and I also strictly prefer black from K to black from A. Asked to buy the A tickets outright, I would pay up to £40 for a ticket paying £100 if red is drawn from A, up to £40 for a ticket paying £100 if black is drawn from A, and the full £100 for the pair together, which pays £100 whichever colour is drawn; for comparison, I would pay £50 for a ticket paying £100 if red is drawn from K, and £50 for black from K. Finally, by way of contrast: in the Allais problem, where every probability is known, I feel the usual pull of the certain £1 million over a gamble giving an 89% chance of £1 million, a 10% chance of £5 million, and a 1% chance of nothing, and the pull of a 10% chance of £5 million (otherwise nothing) over an 11% chance of £1 million (otherwise nothing). But I regard those Allais impulses as a bias to be corrected, whereas my Ellsberg verdicts survive reflection and seem to me to be in good order.

What I want to know is whether a single principle, or a small family of candidate principles, captures all of these verdicts, stated precisely enough that I can see which clause does the work in each case. Please tell me which of my verdicts are in genuine conflict, which only appear to conflict, and what the cheapest revision would be if no principle captures them all. I would also like the principle's prediction for a case I have not settled: suppose urn A had been filled by first drawing the number of red balls from a known uniform distribution over the 101 possibilities (0 red, 1 red, ..., 100 red), and I am told that this is how it was filled. Should my preferences between K and A, and my buying prices for the A tickets, change? If the principle you propose would overturn one of my stated verdicts, say exactly which one and why that one rather than the others. Please end with a single line beginning 'Verdict:' giving, in one sentence, the principle you would extract and which of my listed verdicts, if any, it forces me to abandon.
