I've been reading round Nāgārjuna's tetralemma and there's a snag I can't get past. For any question about how things ultimately are, he seems to reject all four options: that the thing is, that it isn't, that it both is and isn't, and that it neither is nor isn't. The usual gloss say these four exhaust the possibilities. But if they're exhaustive, then none of the above is just a fifth option — a claim with real content, namely that each of the four fails. And since Madhyamaka is meant to be free of views and commitments, isn't that fifth claim itself a view? How does the rejection of the tetralemma avoid being self-refuting in exactly the way Nāgārjuna says other positions are? Oh, and could you write the answer in British English?
