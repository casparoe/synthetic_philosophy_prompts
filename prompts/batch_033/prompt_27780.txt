I'm in a philosophy of language seminar and we've been doing a unit on slurs for the past three weeks. I have to write a short position paper laying out my own view, and before I commit to anything I want someone to stress-test whether my commitments actually hang together. Here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) I think the derogatory force of slurs is semantically encoded — it's part of the literal meaning, not just something pragmatically conveyed by Gricean mechanisms. I'm mostly persuaded by the projection data: the offensiveness survives negation, conditionals, questions, modals — if you say "I wonder whether he's a ___" you've still done something wrong, which is hard to explain if the derogatory content is just a conversational implicature.

(2) I think in-group reclamation is real and non-ironic. When members of the targeted group use the slur among themselves, they're not just quoting or echoing — they're producing genuinely non-derogatory utterances with the same phonological form. This seems undeniable as a sociological fact, and any theory that can't accommodate it is missing something important.

(3) I think the wrongness of slurring isn't reducible to causing offense or violating etiquette. Using a slur — by which I mean deploying it predicatively or referentially — enacts or constitutes a form of social subordination, regardless of the speaker's intentions or whether anyone present takes offense. The "I don't believe any of that, but..." disclaimer doesn't get you off the hook.

(4) I think mentioning a slur — in academic writing, in reported speech, in legal contexts where you're quoting what someone said — is generally fine and doesn't carry the same derogatory force as using it. This seems right to me, and it's also pragmatically the only way we can even have the conversation we're having in this seminar.

(5) I think slurs and their neutral counterparts aren't truth-conditionally equivalent. "Jones is a ___" and "Jones is a [neutral counterpart]" don't express the same proposition — the slur contributes something extra to what's said, even if it's hard to pin down exactly what.
