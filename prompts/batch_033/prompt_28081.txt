At our decision theory research seminar last month, two PhD students got into a dispute about the Pasadena game that I have not been able to settle to my own satisfaction. The game, for context: you flip a fair coin repeatedly until it lands heads. If the first heads occurs on the nth toss, you receive (–1)^(n–1) · (2^n / n) dollars. So the payoff alternates in sign and grows in magnitude — you win two dollars if heads comes immediately, lose two dollars if it takes two tosses, win roughly $2.67 if it takes three, lose four dollars if it takes four, and so on.

(1) Marcus argued that the fair price of the game is ln 2, about sixty-nine cents. He noted that the probability-weighted sum of payoffs is the alternating harmonic series, which converges to ln 2, and that this is the natural valuation since the sum respects the temporal order of the coin tosses. He claimed that anyone who would turn down a free play of the game — or refuse to pay, say, fifty cents for it — was behaving irrationally.

(2) Julia replied that Marcus was mistaking conditional convergence for genuine convergence. The alternating harmonic series does not converge absolutely; the sum of the absolute values of its terms diverges. In standard probability theory, an expected value exists only when the relevant integral converges absolutely. Since the Pasadena game fails this condition, she argued, it has no expected value at all, and Marcus's willingness to pay rests on an artifact of summation order.

(3) Marcus countered that the summation order is not arbitrary but fixed by the structure of the game itself — the tosses occur in a definite temporal sequence, and any valuation that ignores this structure is throwing away normatively relevant information. He added that a decision theory that falls silent on the Pasadena game is simply incomplete: real agents face choices among gambles like this, and "I have no view" is not an acceptable answer from a theory of rational choice.

(4) Julia responded that the Riemann rearrangement theorem shows the problem goes deeper than summation convention. Because the series is conditionally convergent, one can partition the underlying state space differently and obtain any real number as the "expected value," or make it diverge to positive or negative infinity. She argued that this reveals a genuine indeterminacy in the gamble's value: the game does not merely lack an expected value in the technical sense; it lacks a well-defined value of any kind. Paying to play it amounts to picking one rearrangement and treating it as privileged without justification.

(5) Marcus answered that the rearrangement objection is mathematically correct but normatively irrelevant. No one is offering the rearranged gambles; they are offering the Pasadena game as actually specified. He argued that if we refuse to evaluate any gamble with a conditionally convergent expectation, we must also refuse to evaluate many infinite-horizon decision problems that economists and decision theorists routinely analyze using precisely the same kind of natural ordering of states. Biting that bullet, he said, costs far more than accepting ln 2.

The seminar ended before they could continue. I have my own leanings but I cannot confidently say who has the better of it on each point. Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: for each of the five numbered claims, assess its strength on a spectrum from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and explain what — if anything — the speaker is missing or overstating. Then give your overall verdict: which position is more defensible, and what is the philosophically soundest way to think about the Pasadena game?
