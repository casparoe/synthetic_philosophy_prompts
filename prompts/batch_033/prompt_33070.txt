I just spent an evening arguing with a friend about the doomsday argument, and I walked away less struck by the argument itself than by the structure of our disagreement — which, as far as I can tell, perfectly mirrors the state of the literature. The doomsday argument has been debated since the 1980s, and the moves on both sides are thoroughly mapped. The core reasoning is straightforward: if you treat your birth rank among all humans who will ever live as a random sample, then finding yourself born relatively early in the sequence of all humans is more likely under a "doom soon" hypothesis than a "doom late" one, and you should shift your credence toward doom soon. The disagreement is not about the conditional probabilities — everyone grants that P(early rank | doom soon) exceeds P(early rank | doom late). The disagreement is about whether the self-sampling assumption that generates those probabilities is justified in the first place.

My friend held that the self-sampling assumption is simply the principle of indifference applied to self-locating uncertainty: among all possible observers you could be, you should treat yourself as equally likely to be any of them, absent further information. Rejecting it because you dislike the conclusion, he said, is motivated reasoning. I held that the principle of indifference is empty until you specify a reference class — "all humans," "all sentient beings," "all observers with my epistemic situation" — and the doomsday argument's conclusion depends entirely on that choice, which no formal principle settles. We went in circles, and I realized we were not even sure what kind of disagreement we were having.

What I want is a diagnosis of the disagreement itself, not a verdict on the doomsday argument. Is this a substantive disagreement about the correct formalization of self-locating belief, one that could in principle be resolved by a better theory of anthropic reasoning? Is it a disagreement about the reference class problem — and if so, is the reference class problem the kind of thing that has a solution, or is it more like a choice of priors that no argument can compel? Is it a verbal dispute in which "probability" means something different to the two camps — one using it as a guide to action, the other as a measure of evidential support? Or is there a hidden empirical disagreement that neither side has made explicit? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress? A formal uniqueness result about reference classes? A new thought experiment that isolates the self-sampling intuition? Or should we accept that the doomsday argument is a Rorschach test for bedrock intuitions about indifference, and that further argument is pointless?
