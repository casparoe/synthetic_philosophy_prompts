I'm trying to sort out what I think about reasons, and I keep going in circles. Here's where my gut lands on a bunch of cases:

Case 1: Owen wants a gin and tonic. He sees what he reasonably takes to be gin and tonic on the counter. It's actually petrol and tonic. Does Owen have a reason to drink it? I say no — that's easy.

Case 2: Same setup, but the glass really does contain gin and tonic. Owen has no idea it's there and walks past it. Does he have a reason to drink it? I want to say... not really? Or at least, not in the same way. He can't act on it.

Case 3: Maria has been raised in an insular community that teaches her that her only value is to obey her husband. She has internalized this completely; her deepest desires and commitments are all structured around it. She has no desire to leave, no latent dissatisfaction, no flicker of rebellion. Does she have a reason to leave the marriage? My gut says yes, and I'm pretty firm about it. But I can't see where that reason hooks into her actual motivations.

Case 4: James is severely depressed. He has no desire to get out of bed, see friends, or do anything he used to enjoy. He would, if he got out of bed and went for a walk with a friend, feel somewhat better and be glad he did it — this has been true every time in the past. But right now, in his current motivational state, he genuinely doesn't want to. Does he have a reason to get out of bed? I say yes.

Case 5: A man — call him Carl — enjoys torturing small animals. It's a deep and stable preference. He has no sympathy for the animals, no desire to be a good person, no fear of getting caught. He's on a desert island with a stray cat. Does Carl have a reason not to torture it? I say yes, absolutely. But again, I can't find the hook.

Case 6: Aisha has never tried playing piano and has no particular desire to learn. But if she were to try it — take a few lessons, practice for a month — she would discover a deep love for it and it would become one of the most rewarding parts of her life. (We can stipulate this counterfactual is true.) Does Aisha have a reason to take piano lessons? I lean yes, but much less confidently than for Maria or James.

Case 7: Back to Owen. He wants a gin and tonic, the glass contains gin and tonic, and he knows it. Does he have a reason to drink it? Yes, obviously. This is the clear case.

So my verdicts seem to pull me toward external reasons in cases 3, 4, 5, and maybe 6 — but then cases 1 and 2 pull me back toward something like an internalist constraint. And case 6 feels different from 3 and 5 in a way I can't articulate.

Is there a single principle that gets all of these? If not, which verdict should I give up, and what's the best fallback principle? And what does your preferred account say about a case I haven't mentioned: someone who has a desire that they only have because of a factual error (e.g., they want to vote for a candidate because they falsely beleive the candidate supports a policy they care about) — do they have a reason to vote for that candidate?
