I'm working on a longform piece about constitutional design, and one metaphor I keep running into — from legal scholars, political theorists, and even judges — is the idea that a constitution is a kind of Ulysses contract. The story, as you'll recall, is that Ulysses, wanting to hear the Sirens' song without steering his ship onto the rocks, orders his crew to bind him to the mast and plug their own ears with wax. He gives them strict instructions not to release him no matter how much he begs, knowing full well that his future self will be temporarily deranged by desire. The analogy, as it's deployed in constitutional theory, runs something like this: a democratic people, aware of its own susceptibility to panic, factional passion, or short-sightedness, enacts constitutional constraints — a bill of rights, supermajority requirements, an independent judiciary — that tie its own hands against future majorities. On this view, judicial review that strikes down a popular law isn't antidemocratic; it's democracy's own earlier precommitment being honored, just as the crew's refusal to untie Ulysses honors his earlier, rational self.

I can see the appeal, but I'm also aware that analogies do work beneath the surface, and I want to figure out how much work this one is actually doing. So my question is: audit the Ulysses-constitution analogy. Give me a mapping of what corresponds to what — who or what plays the role of Ulysses, the crew, the mast, the Sirens, the wax, the rope, the ship, the journey — and then identify where the mapping breaks. Which disanalogies are merely cosmetic, and which ones matter for the normative conclusion the analogy is meant to support (namely, that constitutional precommitment is democratically legitimate even when it frustrates a present majority)? Does the conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying more weight than it can bear? And if the analogy turns out to be misleading in important respects, is there a better one — or should we just do without the metaphor and argue the thing straight?

One more thing: if the whole framing of the question rests on a confusion — if, say, the analogy was never meant to do normative work in the first place, or if I'm importing assumptions about what democratic legitimacy means that make the question ill-posed — please say so. I'd rather have the premise corrected than get a polite answer to a broken question.
