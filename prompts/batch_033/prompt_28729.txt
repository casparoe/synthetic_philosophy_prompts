I am the community member of my university's research ethics committee, which oversees both animal research and emerging technologies, and I need to circulate a short decision memo before our meeting on Thursday. A computational neuroscience group has asked us to rule on a proposal that fits none of our existing categories. They want to build and run a large-scale simulation of the mouse brain circuits involved in pain and affect—on the order of seventy million spiking neurons across somatosensory cortex, anterior cingulate, insula, amygdala, and periaqueductal gray, with connections constrained by connectomic data from real mice—and to let the model run for months of simulated time while it learns avoidance of stimuli that produce nociceptive, tissue-damage-like firing patterns. No biological tissue is involved at any stage. Half the committee regards this as a computer model like any other, no more morally troubling than a weather simulation; a simulation of pain, they argue, hurts nothing, just as a simulation of a fire burns nothing. The other half says that analogy gets things exactly backwards: if the simulation reproduces the same computations that constitute pain and avoidance in a real mouse, then the substrate is irrelevant, and running it would amount to creating and repeatedly harming a system that may suffer. That faction wants the protocol either rejected or subjected to the same safeguards we apply to live animal subjects.

I have no academic background, but I have been reading on my own about which computations matter morally and where the boundaries of sentience might lie, and I find both positions genuinely compelling. What troubles me most is the line-drawing problem: a toy circuit of two artificial neurons plainly does not suffer, yet somewhere on the path from that toy to a whole mouse brain, if computation is what matters, a morally important threshold is crossed, and we have no direct way of locating it, since all we can ever inspect is behavior and internal activity—which, as I understand it, is also true of our knowledge of animals. I want the memo to give the committee something it can act on: a clear recommendation to approve, reject, or approve with conditions, together with a time frame for any conditions or review; an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side; and criteria reviewers could apply to future protocols, such as thresholds on scale, the fidelity of affect-related circuits, the presence of a self-model, or monitoring requirements.

You are a professional philosopher specializing in the ethics of artificial minds and the metaphysics of sentience. Please think carefully, begin by brainstorming the strongest arguments on each side before you draft, and then write the memo in your own words, avoiding jargon or briefly explaining it where it is unavoidable, so that a committee of non-philosophers can follow and act on it.
