I sit on the board of a mid-sized public art museum. For three years we have been preparing a major retrospective of the late painter Adrian Voss, whose large-scale figurative canvases are widely regarded as among the most formally accomplished of the past half-century; the exhibition was meant to anchor our newly renovated wing. Last month a coalition of local artists and survivors' advocates demanded that we cancel the retrospective and remove Voss's work from the galleries, citing extensive posthumous reporting that he sexually exploited a series of young women who worked in his studio as assistants and models. Several of the paintings in the show are portraits of those women, long praised for their psychological depth. The women themselves are not of one mind: some have asked the museum to take the portraits down, saying their public display re-enacts the original violation, while at least one former assistant has argued that hiding the work would spare Voss's reputation and that the paintings should stay up with honest labels. The board is split. Some members insist the paintings are great art and that a museum must not confuse the work with the man; a museum, they say, shows the best art regardless of biography. Others reply that a retrospective is an act of honor, that displaying these particular portraits without acknowledging what the artist did to the sitters compounds the original harm, and that aesthetic autonomy cannot be a shield for institutional complicity. A third group thinks we are conflating two questions: whether the paintings are good art, and whether a public institution ought to exhibit them. The director has asked me to draft the short written opinion to circulate before next Thursday's board meeting.

Please write the memo. It should recommend a concrete course of action for the retrospective — proceed as planned, cancel it, or proceed with some form of reframing, contextualization, or partial display — and it should also propose a durable policy for future acquisitions and displays when an artist's moral record is at issue. Engage the strongest version of the autonomist position and the strongest version of the moralist position rather than restating either as a slogan, and give the board reasons it could actually act on.
