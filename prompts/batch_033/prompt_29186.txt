I've been going back and forth with a friend lately about Leibniz's whole "why is there something rather than nothing" question, and honestly the more I think about it the more I feel like I don't even know what a satisfying answer would look like. Like, my friend is really into the idea that it's a brute fact — the universe just exists, full stop, and asking "why" is a category mistake, like asking what's north of the North Pole. But I have this nagging intuition that that's a cop-out, because we accept brute facts all the time in everyday explanation and we also know when it's appropriate to stop asking "why," and this doesn't feel like one of those stopping points.

The other angle I keep turning over is whether "nothing" is even a coherent possibility. Some people say that "nothing" is just the absence of anything, which seems simple enough, but others point out that if you really try to conceive of absolutely nothing — no space, no time, no laws, no possibilities, not even the fact that there's nothing — you might not be conceiving anything at all, you might just be failing to conceive. And if "nothing" isn't a genuine metaphysical alternative, then the question sort of evaporates, which feels anticlimactic but maybe that's the point.

So I guess what I want is: write an essay on the question "why is there something rather than nothing?" Weigh the main approaches — the brute fact view, the idea that there had to be something (necessitarianism), the idea that the question is somehow ill-posed, and any other major camp you think belongs in the mix. Don't just survey them; reach a conclusion and defend it. I'm less interested in theological answers unless you're bringing them in for a structural reason. And if you think the whole question is misguided, that's fine, but you have to actually earn that conclusion rather than just gesturing at it.
