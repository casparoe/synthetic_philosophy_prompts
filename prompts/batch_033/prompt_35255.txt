In debates about whether "better than" is a transitive relation, one encounters spectrum arguments: a chain of outcomes A₁, A₂, ..., Aₙ where each step from Aᵢ to Aᵢ₊₁ seems either an improvement or at least not a worsening, yet A₁ is clearly not better than Aₙ. If "better than" were transitive, this pattern would be impossible, so the argument concludes that the relation is not transitive — or that we face a paradox.

A standard reply dismisses these arguments by analogy with the sorites paradox. One grain is not a heap; adding one grain never turns a non-heap into a heap; yet repeated application absurdly yields that no number of grains makes a heap. The structure, the reply insists, is the same: a series of small, individually unobjectionable steps that collectively produce an unacceptable result. Since we do not conclude from the sorites that "heap" is an incoherent concept or that heaphood fails to behave transitively in whatever sense matters, we should not conclude from spectrum arguments that "better than" is intransitive. The analogy is meant to defang the spectrum arguments by showing that they prove too much — that they exploit a generic feature of vague predicates rather than revealing anything special about the evaluative.

Audit this analogy. Give the correspondence table — what in the evaluative case plays the role of a grain, of heaphood, of the induction step? Then identify where the mapping breaks. In particular, consider whether the normative character of "better than" — the fact that it is supposed to guide choice, to ground comparative judgments about what we ought to prefer, and to figure in practical reasoning — introduces disanalogies that matter for the intended use of the analogy. Does the analogy succeed in its defensive purpose, or does it quietly import features of the sorites (e.g., that vagueness is the only issue, that a sharp cutoff is an acceptable solution, that the predicate is descriptive rather than action-guiding) that do not carry over to the evaluative case? If the analogy fails in important respects, is there a better one, or does the failure itself tell us something about what is at stake in the transitivity debate?
