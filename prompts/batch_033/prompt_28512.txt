I've been teaching a course on personal identity and applied ethics, and we have just finished a unit on advance directives — the problem of whether, and on what grounds, an earlier competent self has the authority to bind a later self whose values, preferences, or cognitive capacities may have shifted in ways the earlier self did not fully anticipate. The standard cases are dementia directives and psychiatric advance directives, and the standard moves in the literature — Dworkin's appeal to critical interests, Dresser's scepticism about the authority of the precedent self, Jaworska's argument that the capacity to value is what matters — are by now familiar to my students. I want to give them something less tidy: a case study that dramatises the underlying tension rather than simply illustrating a pre-existing position.

I am considering using Michel Gondry's film *Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind* (2004, screenplay by Charlie Kaufman). For anyone who has not seen it: after a painful breakup, Clementine undergoes a procedure to erase all memories of her relationship with Joel; Joel, on discovering this, impulsively decides to do the same. The bulk of the film takes place inside Joel's mind as he relives his memories in reverse while the erasure procedure runs, and partway through he realises he does not want to lose these memories after all. He attempts to resist the procedure from within his own dreamscape, but the earlier, waking Joel has already consented, and the procedure cannot be stopped from the inside. The film thus stages a literal conflict between an earlier self who authorises an intervention and a later self — emerging even as the procedure is underway — who repudiates it.

I would like a reading of the film through the philosophical literature on advance directives and the authority of the earlier self. In particular: does the film's structure (the erasing self versus the remembering self) map cleanly onto the earlier-self/later-self distinction as it figures in the bioethics literature, or does it introduce disanalogies that complicate the application? Does the film take a discernible position on whether Joel's earlier consent ought to bind his later resistance, and if so, is that position philosophically defensible? And finally, what — if anything — does the film add to the philosophical debate that the standard arguments and thought experiments do not capture? I am less interested in whether the memory-erasure technology is realistic than in whether the film, taken on its own terms as a narrative, illuminates something genuine about the authority problem that a seminar room argument might miss.

Please write your answer in British English.
