Here is an argument I have been unable to find my way out of, and I would like a diagnosis.

(1) A rule for revising beliefs is acceptable only if the final state it recommends depends on the total evidence received, not on the order in which pieces of evidence happened to arrive.
(2) Jeffrey conditionalization is the standard rule for revising one's credences when the evidence is uncertain — for example, when a witness's report makes you 0.8 confident that the butler did it rather than certain.
(3) If Jeffrey conditionalization is correct, then two successive Jeffrey updates should commute: applying the rule to one piece of uncertain evidence and then to another should leave the same final credences as applying them in the reverse order, provided the same new probabilities are supplied at each step.
(4) But Jeffrey updates do not commute. In a toy case: begin with P(A∧B) = 0.4, P(A∧¬B) = 0.1, P(¬A∧B) = 0.2, and P(¬A∧¬B) = 0.3; updating first on the partition {A, ¬A} with P(A) = 0.7 and then on {B, ¬B} with P(B) = 0.7 leaves P(A∧B) at about 0.58, whereas the reverse order leaves it at about 0.60.
(5) So an agent who updates by Jeffrey conditionalization can end up with different credences after receiving the same uncertain evidence, merely because the evidence arrived in a different order.
(6) Therefore, by (1)–(5), Jeffrey conditionalization is not an acceptable rule for revising beliefs on uncertain evidence — which seems to prove too much, given how entrenched the rule is.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would most like to keep (1), since order-invariance seems nearly definitional of responding to evidence rather than to the accidents of its arrival; but I do not see how to reject the rest without either abandoning the standard rule or denying that it may be applied more than once.
