I am a PhD student in formal epistemology with a background in proof theory, and I convene a reading group on AI alignment and the limits of self-reference. For nearly a year the group has been stuck on one dispute, which also runs through a corner of the alignment literature, and I would like a diagnosis of the disagreement itself rather than a verdict on which side is right.

The dispute concerns the so-called Löbian obstacle to self-trusting agents. One side treats Löb's theorem as a genuine constraint on any system that reasons about its own reasoning. Their claim, roughly, is that a sufficiently strong consistent formal system cannot derive every instance of the principle that if φ is provable by the system then φ. Löb's theorem shows that deriving this principle for a given φ would already force the derivation of φ itself, so deriving all instances would amount to proving every sentence, which a consistent system cannot do. On this view, an agent cannot establish in full generality that its own future reasoning is sound, and a self-modifying agent that wishes to build a successor and then defer to the successor's conclusions faces a principled barrier: it cannot prove in advance that the successor's proofs are trustworthy.

The other side replies that this is an artifact of an implausible idealization. The obstacle, they say, appears only because the agent is modeled as a logically omniscient, consistent proof system equipped with a provability predicate, and because self-trust is represented as a sentence to be proved. Real agents, or any system we would actually build, are resource-bounded, uncertain, and do not reason by enumerating proofs in arithmetic. For such an agent, self-trust is better understood as a prior, a policy, or a property of its design, and Löb's theorem is silent about whether that prior, policy, or design is rational.

My working hypothesis is that the two sides are largely answering different questions under one label: one is asking what a consistent formal system can prove about itself, while the other is asking what it is rational for an embedded, bounded agent to expect of its future self, and the term 'self-trust' is doing different work in each. But I am not confident. The first side may be making a substantive claim that any adequate model of a self-modifying agent must preserve the proof-theoretic limitation, and the second side may be denying exactly that.

What I would like from you is a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is: a verbal disagreement about the meaning of 'self-trust'; a disagreement about which idealizations are harmless and which are not; a hidden formal question, such as whether Löb-like limitations survive in probabilistic or resource-bounded settings; or a difference in what the two sides are trying to explain. Given that diagnosis, please say what would actually count as progress — a theorem, a model, a distinction — or explain why we should expect the impasse to persist.
