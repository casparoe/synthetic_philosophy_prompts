Construct a thought experiment in which a decision has the formal structure of Pascal's mugging — a small cost for a tiny probability of an enormous payoff — but the expected-utility recommendation to pay is intuitively correct, while a bounded-utility response (which caps the value of any outcome at some finite maximum) recommends against paying and is intuitively wrong. Constraints: the scenario must be realistic, with no muggers, no science fiction, and no astronomically large numbers. The payoff must be stated in concrete terms (money, health, or similar). The probability must be well-defined and not dependent on trusting a stranger's claims. The bounded-utility cap should be set at a level that is high enough to be plausible as a general cap but low enough to block the original Pascal's mugging — and it must be this same cap that misfires in your scenario. After the scenario, walk through the expected-utility calculation and the bounded-utility calculation, showing exactly where they diverge. Identify which feature of the case is doing the work to make the bounded-utility response come out wrong. Then explain what a defender of bounded utility would say to resist the verdict — for instance, by arguing that the cap should be set higher, that the case is not genuinely analogous to the mugging, or that the intuitively correct answer is really being driven by something other than expected utility — and assess whether that reply succeeds. Finish by saying whether a complexity-prior response to the mugging (which discounts hypotheses in proportion to their complexity rather than capping utilities) would side with expected utility or with bounded utility in this case, and why.
