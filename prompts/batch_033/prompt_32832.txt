I sit on the clinical ethics committee of a major teaching hospital. A case has been referred to us, and I need to draft a written opinion with a concrete recommendation before our meeting next week.

The patient, a 42-year-old woman, has suffered from severe, treatment-resistant major depressive disorder for over two decades. She has tried multiple classes of antidepressants, several courses of psychotherapy, and two rounds of ECT, all with only partial and transient relief. She is now being offered a trial of adaptive deep brain stimulation (aDBS) targeting the subcallosal cingulate. The clinical team is optimistic: early-phase data suggest response rates above 60% for patients with her profile, and the technology adjusts stimulation parameters in real time based on neural signatures of mood.

The ethical problem is this: the patient is refusing the treatment. She is not refusing all treatment — she is willing to continue her current medication regimen and attend therapy — but she is refusing the aDBS trial specifically. Her stated reasons are thoughtful and articulate. She says that her depression, while painful, has shaped who she is over more than half her life. Her values, her relationships, her sense of what matters — all of these have been formed in the context of this condition. She does not know, and cannot know, what kind of person she would be without it. She describes this as a transformative choice she is not willing to make. She worries that she would become someone who does not share her current commitments, someone who might even look back on her current self with contempt or pity. She has written a short statement to this effect and has been evaluated by two psychiatrists, both of whom find her to have decision-making capacity.

Her family — her husband and adult daughter — see things differently. They argue that her refusal is itself a manifestation of the depression: that the illness is distorting her reasoning, making the status quo seem safer than it is, and that the very fact she cannot imagine a life without depression is evidence of how thoroughly the illness has colonized her perspective. They point out that if the treatment works, the post-treatment person would almost certainly be grateful to have undergone it, and would not choose to return to the depressed state. They have formally requested that the committee authorize the treatment over the patient's objection, citing the principle of substituted judgment (what would the patient want if she were well?) and the principle of beneficence.

Our hospital's policy on competent refusal states that it should generally be respected, but it allows for exceptions when the refusal appears to be a direct product of the condition being treated and when the treatment is low-risk and high-benefit. The aDBS trial carries non-trivial surgical risks (infection, hemorrhage) and the possibility of personality changes that are precisely the point of contention.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate. It should recommend a concrete course of action — whether to authorize the treatment over the patient's objection, to respect her refusal, or some intermediate step — and it should engage seriously with the strongest arguments on both sides, including the family's substituted-judgment argument and the patient's transformative-experience argument. Do not merely restate the legal standard for capacity or the policy text; grapple with the underlying philosophical difficulty.

Before writing the opinion, take time to think through the case carefully, considering the strongest considerations that pull in each direction. You are a senior clinical ethicist with expertise in both medical ethics and the philosophical literature on transformative experience and personal identity.

At the very end of your opinion, state your bottom-line recommendation in the following format on its own line: \answer{...} where the content is a short phrase — either "authorize treatment," "respect refusal," or a specific intermediate recommendation.
