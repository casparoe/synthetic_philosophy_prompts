I'm on my university's academic integrity board, and we've got a case that's genuinely got me twisted in knots. I need to draft the written opinion I'll circulate before we vote on Thursday, and I'd love your help.

Here's the situation. A grad student in our biology department — let's call her M — discovered that her advisor had been fabricating data in at least four published papers. She found this out because she was doing the actual lab work and noticed that the numbers in the manuscripts didn't match what she'd recorded. She tried the normal channels: she talked to her department chair, filed a complaint with the university's research integrity office, and waited. For months, nothing happened. The advisor is a big name, brings in serious grant money, and the investigation was dragging. Meanwhile he submitted two more papers with faked data, and his other grad students were building their dissertation work on results she knew were fraudulent.

So M took matters into her own hands. She used her lab computer access to log into her advisor's account — he'd given her his password ages ago for scheduling shared equipment — and pulled the raw data files that showed the fabrication. She sent them anonymously to the journal editors and to a reporter at the university paper. Once the evidence was out, the investigation moved fast. The papers are being retracted, the advisor is facing serious sanctions, and the students who'd been building on the fake data are now being helped to redirect their projects.

But here's the rub: M clearly violated the university's computer use policy and the academic integrity code. Unauthorized access to a faculty member's files, bypassing formal reporting procedures, disclosing confidential material to the press — these are all violations, and the integrity code doesn't have a "but I had good intentions" exception. The question before our board is what sanctions, if any, she should face. Some of my colleagues want a formal letter of reprimand in her file — they say rules are rules, and you can't have grad students deciding for themselves when it's okay to hack into faculty computers. Others say she should get no sanctions at all, maybe even a quiet commendation, because she exposed serious fraud that the system was demonstrably failing to catch.

What's keeping me up is the weird structure of the thing. The rules she broke — the computer use policy, the reporting procedures, the integrity code itself — exist to protect the integrity of research. That's their whole point. But in this case, her violation of those rules protected the integrity of research more effectively than following them would have. If we sanction her, we're enforcing a norm that, in this particular instance, would have prevented the exposure of exactly the kind of misconduct the norm is meant to safeguard against. That feels almost paradoxical. But if we don't sanction her, we're saying the rules don't apply when someone has a sufficiently good reason to break them, which seems to undercut the whole point of having an integrity code in the first place.

I need to draft the board's decision memo — a concrete recommendation on sanctions (or no sanctions) with reasons laid out clearly, and the memo has to honestly engage the strongest argument on the losing side rather than just caricaturing it. It needs to be something we can actually vote on Thursday.
