I'm a novelist and I sit on the board of a small prize for literary translation. We're deadlocked and I need to circulate a memo before Thursday's meeting, so I'd like you to draft it for me.

The case: we got two translations of the same sixteenth-century French lyric, a tiny get-well poem with a singsong, almost nursery-rhyme feel — three stanzas, only two rhyme sounds, very short lines, and a refrain that closes each stanza. The first translator kept all of that. Same stanza shape, same rhymes, same refrain; it reads like an English nursery rhyme. The catch is that to make it work she changed a fair amount of the sense. A line about the sick girl's fever becomes a line about the weather, an image of the speaker's worry gets cut entirely, and a few lines are padded with filler ("oh," "so," "my dear") just to make the meter come out. The second translator rendered every line's sense with real care, so you can see exactly what the French says, but he dropped the meter, the rhyme, and the refrain; his version is a quiet free-verse poem with a translator's note explaining what the form was doing.

Half the board says the first is the only real translation in the running, because the form is part of the poem's meaning — a get-well poem that doesn't sound like a rhyme is a different kind of object, and you can't claim to have translated the poem if you've thrown away the very thing that makes it a poem. The other half says the first is a lovely English poem "after" the French, not a translation: she invented lines the poet never wrote, and the prize is for fidelity to what the poem says, not for writing a new poem that happens to share a rhyme scheme. One of them keeps saying the second translator at least respects the reader enough not to pretend.

The prize's stated criterion is just "the best translation into English," which is no help. I need a memo that recommends a concrete decision — award to the first, award to the second, split the prize, or give no award — and that honestly engages the strongest point on the losing side, because which ever way we go half the board will be angry. And if you think the board is framing the whole thing wrong, say so, but still give us something we can vote on.
