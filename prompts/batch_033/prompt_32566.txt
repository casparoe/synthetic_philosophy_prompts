i'm in an epistemology seminar and we've been on the Meno problem for two weeks — why is knowledge more valuable than mere true belief? i've been trying to sort out my intuitions by going through cases. here's where i've landed:

1. knowing the way to larissa is better than having a true belief about it, and not just because the true belief might wander off. even if both get you there, knowing feels like it has something extra.

2. the stopped clock case: i look at a clock that's broken (stopped at 3:00), it happens to be 3:00, so i form a true belief about the time. this is justified (clocks are usually right) and true, but it's not knowledge. this feels genuinely worse than knowing — but is it worse than just guessing correctly? i want to say yes, it's worse than a lucky guess, because at least the guesser knows they're guessing. but i'm not confident.

3. a friend correctly believes her partner is faithful. she believes this because she's pathologically trusting and ignores all evidence. she happens to be right. i don't think this is just as good as knowing it. but i'm not sure why — the belief is true, it makes her happy, and it's not like she's hurting anyone.

4. but then: someone correctly believes they're in remission, and they're right by sheer luck (lab mixup, but they actually are in remission). this belief lets them enjoy their final months. i find myself not wanting to say knowing would be better here. the true belief is doing the job.

5. knowing trivia — say, knowing that canberra is the capital — feels basically identical in value to having a true belief about it from a vague memory. i can't get worked up about the difference.

6. if someone spends serious time and money to turn a true belief into knowledge (e.g., enrolls in a course to confirm something they already correctly believed), i feel like they've wasted their resources unless the belief was about something important. but if the belief was important, suddenly the investment seems reasonable.

is there a principle that captures most of these, or at least clarifies where i'm being inconsistent? and what does it say about a case i keep going back and forth on: you have a true belief that your kid is safe at school, held only because you can't bear to think otherwise, and you happen to be right. is that more like the spouse case or the cancer case — or is it its own thing?
