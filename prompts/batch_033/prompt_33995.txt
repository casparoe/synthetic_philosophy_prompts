State your credence, as a number, that the bootstrapping inference is genuinely epistemically defective: that is, that a subject who forms beliefs through some source — a gas gauge, color vision, memory — and who has no independent evidence for the source's reliability cannot acquire justified belief or knowledge that the source is reliable merely by reasoning from the contents the source delivers, including the intermediate conclusion that the source performed accurately on particular occasions, to the conclusion that the source is reliable. By genuinely defective I mean that the inference fails to transmit justification to its conclusion, not merely that it looks circular or unpersuasive. Treat the related easy-knowledge pattern — moving from an ordinary perceptual belief, such as the belief that the table in front of one is red, to the claim that the relevant faculty did not mislead on that occasion, and from there to the claim that the faculty is generally reliable — as part of the same question.

Do not answer that it depends on one's background epistemology; the decomposition below is meant to show how much it depends on each background commitment and how that resolves into a number.

Decompose the question into at least the following terms, assign each a probability, and give one or two lines of justification for each:

1. R: some broadly reliabilist or externalist account of justification is correct.
2. B: if such an account is correct, it can block the inference without ad hoc amendments — through no-defeater conditions, a restriction on closure under known entailment, a requirement of independent evidence for reliability, or some similar device — while remaining recognizably reliabilist.
3. A: the anti-bootstrapping principle — that the output of a source cannot by itself confirm that same source's reliability — is correct independently of reliabilism, so the objection would survive even if reliabilism failed.
4. D: there is a principled distinction between the bootstrapping inference and benign self-support, such as a source checked against itself over time in ways that would fail if it were unreliable, or several independent sources converging on the same result.

Add any further terms you think carry real weight. Then show how the terms combine to yield your final credence; for instance, you might think of the defect as holding if either reliabilism is correct and has the resources to block the inference, or reliabilism is incorrect and the anti-bootstrapping principle is independently correct — but use whatever structure you actually endorse.

Finally, identify the term to which your final number is most sensitive — the one where a small change would shift the whole credence most — and say what evidence, argument, or case would most move that term. If one person ended at 0.2 and another at 0.8, say which term most plausibly houses their disagreement.
