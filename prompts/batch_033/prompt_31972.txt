I'm running a philosophy reading group on conditionals, and we've been at a stalemate for weeks. Two members—let's call them the Suppositionalist and the Propositionalist—have agreed to attempt an adversarial collaboration, and I've been asked to draft the joint document. Here's the lay of the land.

The Suppositionalist holds that the probability you assign to an indicative conditional like "If it rains, the match will be cancelled" simply is your conditional probability of the match being cancelled given that it rains. On her view, conditionals are not propositions with truth conditions; they are devices for expressing conditional credences, and it makes no sense to ask whether they are true or false. She thinks this fits neatly with how we reason with conditionals in everyday life and with the Ramsey test. The Propositionalist thinks conditionals are ordinary propositions with truth conditions—they are true or false, full stop—and that the Suppositionalist's view cannot handle embedded conditionals like "It's not the case that if it rains, the match will be cancelled" or "Either it rains, or if it rains the match will be cancelled." He also keeps pointing to a family of formal results, due to Lewis, that seem to show something stark: if you treat conditionals as events in a probability space and also insist that their probability equals the relevant conditional probability, the whole thing collapses—the probability function becomes trivial, and you can't condition on new information without becoming probabilistically incoherent. The Suppositionalist replies that this is precisely why she doesn't treat conditionals as events in a probability space in the first place, and the Propositionalist retorts that then there's no account of how conditionals embed under negation or in Boolean compounds.

The arguments have been going round in cirlces, and I suspect they partly agree on more than they realise and partly disagree on something that is neither a verbal dispute nor a settled matter.
