I'm the editor of a local news website serving a town of about eighty thousand people. We publish daily, and our reporting covers council meetings, magistrates' court, planning disputes, school controversies, and the occasional fatal accident. At least once a week I have to decide whether to include someone's name, address, medical history, family circumstances, or past offences in a story. The law gives us a floor — defamation, contempt, and the occasional reporting restriction — but that floor is low, and most of the decisions I face are legal either way. At present we make these calls by instinct and by loose analogy with the Editors' Code of Practice, but I find that two editors in the same room can reach opposite conclusions and neither can articulate why. I want something more systematic.

Please write a decision procedure I can run on any piece of personal information we are considering publishing — a sequence of questions with clear stopping rules, grounded in whatever the philosophy of privacy can tell us about why privacy actually matters. I am not asking for a summary of media law; I want the ethical reasoning the law does not supply. The procedure should distinguish between different kinds of personal information (intimate facts, public-facing facts, facts about past wrongdoing, facts about location, facts about family life) and between different reasons for publishing (public accountability, the public's right to know what happened in a public place, the newsworthiness of a person's own actions). It should flag the points where the procedure cannot decide and editorial judgement must take over. I need to be able to run it in fifteen minutes, and it should be honest about which steps are judgement calls dressed up as procedure.

Once you have written the procedure, please run it on two cases I faced last year. (1) A teacher at a local secondary school was disciplined five years ago for a minor safeguarding infraction — a failure to follow a reporting protocol, not any harm to a child. The regulator cleared him after a review, and he is now applying for a headship at a school in the next town. A parent who knew about the old case has tipped us off and wants us to publish before the hiring decision is made. (2) A man in his seventies died in a single-vehicle crash on the high street on a Saturday afternoon. The crash closed the road for three hours and displaced a market that runs every weekend. His family have asked us not to name him, saying he was a private man who would have hated the attention. We have his name from a witness. For each case, tell me where in the procedure the decision is reached, what the answer is, and whether the procedure gives an answer I will be uncomfortable with.
