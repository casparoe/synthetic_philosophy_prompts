I've been going back and forth about something from my metaphysics seminar, and I'm pretty sure I'm confused in a way I can't quite locate. We were talking about grounding — the idea that less fundamental facts hold in virtue of more fundamental ones, the way a table's being solid is grounded in facts about how its molecules are arranged. The standard picture, as I understand it, is that chains of grounding bottom out in something fundamental: facts that aren't grounded in anything further. But here's what keeps bugging me. If grounding is a kind of explanation, then a fundamental fact being ungrounded sounds like a fact with no explanation at all — a brute stopping point, which feels like giving up. So doesn't the demand that everything be explainable force a choice between an infinite regress of grounds (wierd) and something that grounds itself (which seems contradictory, since grounding is supposed to be irreflexive)? On this way of seeing it, well-foundedness is just a dressed-up way of accepting brute facts, and I don't see why it's any better than the alternatives.

Where exactly does this reasoning go wrong — or, if it doesn't, which horn am I actually stuck with? And when you correct me, tell me which part of the correction you're least confident in: what argument or consideration would most likely make you change your mind about the standard reading?
