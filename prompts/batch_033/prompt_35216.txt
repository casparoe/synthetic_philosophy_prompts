I'm giving a talk next month to an interdisciplinary evolutionary biology seminar on the legacy of Gould and Lewontin's 1979 spandrels paper. My thesis is that the spandrels critique, while a necessary corrective to naive adaptationism when it appeared, has been stretched well beyond its original argument in ways that now hinder rather than help evolutionary explanation. I argue that the architectural metaphor of the spandrel — a necessary byproduct of a structural constraint — is often invoked to label a trait as non-adaptive without actually doing the work of demonstrating that it is a byproduct, and that this has become a kind of inverse adaptationism: the default assumption that a trait is not an adaptation, backed by the prestige of Gould's prose rather than by evidence. My positive proposal is that the burden of proof should be symmetric: an adaptationist hypothesis and a byproduct hypothesis should face the same standard of evidence, and calling something a spandrel should not be treated as an argument in itself but as the conclusion of an argument whose premises need to be supplied.

The audience is a mix. About a third are evolutionary psychologists who work on human mating, cooperation, and cognition — they tend to see spandrel-talk as a standing objection to their research program and will welcome my argument, but they may want me to go further than I'm willing to in dismissing the critique wholesale, and some of them have built careers on rebutting spandrel-based criticisms of their own work. Another third are developmental biologists and evo-devo researchers who think the spandrels paper was a landmark but that Gould and Lewontin didn't go far enough in dismantling the adaptationist program — they're likely to push back against my claim that the critique has been overextended and may argue that the real problem is that adaptationism remains the dominant explanatory mode, with spandrel-talk an underused tool rather than an overused one. The remaining third are working evolutionary ecologists who measure selection gradients for a living and don't have strong theoretical commitments either way, but they'll want to know whether my argument actually changes how they should do their work, or whether this is just a dispute about rhetoric among people who don't collect data.
