Two people in my department got into it over lunch about this thought experiment they'd read, and I'm honestly still turning it over. The case is: a couple knows they carry a genetic condition that'll give any child they conceive now a painful, lifelong illness — not fatal, but rough. Their life would still be worth living. If the couple waits a year, they can do IVF with screening and have a *different* child, one without the condition. They decide not to wait and have the child now.

Maya said the couple wronged the child. Jordan said that doesn't even make sense: the only way *that* child could exist is if they conceive now. If they wait, a different sperm and egg meet, a different person is born. So the alternative for this child isn't a healthy life — it's never existing at all. And since their life is worth living, you can't say they were harmed. You can't make someone worse off than they would otherwise have been when the "otherwise" is nonexistence.

Maya said that's a neat logical trick that lets people off the hook for doing something obviously wrong. If your theory of harm says this couple didn't harm their child, then your theory of harm is missing something.

Jordan said: maybe what they did is wrong, but it's not wrong *to* the child. It's an impersonal wrong — like wrecking an ecosystem, or making the world contain more suffering when you could've made it contain less. The child isn't the victim; the wrongness doesn't attach to a particular person.

Maya pushed back: the child is the one living with the pain, the one missing school, the one in and out of hospitals. If there's wrongness here, it's wrongness to that child. Saying it's "impersonal" is just rewording the problem, not solving it.

Jordan said: okay, but if you insist it's wrong *to* the child, you need to explain how. Point to the moment they were made worse off. The child can't point to any moment where they were fine and then became worse off — they were born with the condition. And they can't say "I'd rather not exist" because their life is worth living. So what's the harm?

Maya said the harm is in being brought into existence with a lower quality of life than the parents could have given you — not compared to *your* counterfactual, but compared to the child they *could* have had. Jordan said that's not how harm works; harm is always comparative for the same person. Maya said that's exactly the assumption she's questioning.

They went around on this for twenty minutes and eventually both looked at me like I was supposed to break the tie. I had no idea what to say.
