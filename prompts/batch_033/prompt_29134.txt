I recently resigned from the executive committee of my local constituency party, after two years of watching intelligent people — myself included — defend positions they privately thought were mistaken, because the party line required it. A friend pressed Simone Weil's 1943 essay on me, and it landed with the force of a diagnosis I hadn't known I needed. I now want to present Weil's argument to my political reading group: a dozen people, none of them academic philosophers, but all of them thoughtful and politically active, with strong party loyalties of their own. The session runs ninety minutes.

I am not asking you to explain Weil's view to me. I am asking you to plan how I should explain it to them. What idea has to be in place before the argument can even get a grip? What single example or case should carry the whole thing — and what must it have and lack for that to work? Which misconceptions will this audience in particular bring or form, and at exactly which point in the sequence should I head each one off? (I suspect at least three: that she is merely complaining about excessive partisanship, that her target is only authoritarian parties and not democratic ones, and that she is offering a naïvely anti-political call to transcendence.) What should I leave out at this level and openly say I am leaving out? And what question can I put to them near the end that would tell me whether they have understood the structural claim — that the defect is in the party form itself — rather than merely nodding along?

Please give your answer in British English.
