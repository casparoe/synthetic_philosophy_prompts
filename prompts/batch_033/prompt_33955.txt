I'm the faculty chair of my university's Academic Integrity Panel, and we've got a case that's split us right down the middle. A third-year cog-sci major was caught using a small spiral notebook during a closed-book philosophy of mind exam. It had detailed notes—basically a condensed version of the course readings—on the exact topics being tested. Normally this is open-and-shut: unauthorized materials, automatic violation. But the student submitted a written defence that's genuinely more interesting than anything I've seen in five years on this panel. He argues that under the extended mind thesis (Clark and Chalmers, the Otto-and-his-notebook argument, which was actually one of the topics covered on the exam itself), his notebook is functionally equivalent to biological memory. The exam rules permit the use of internal memory. So prohibiting the notebook while permitting neural memory draws an arbitrary line around the skull—exactly the line the extended mind thesis says we shouldn't privilege. He claims that penalizing him amounts to the university taking an official stance against a live position in philosophy of mind and punishing him for holding it.

Half the panel thinks this is clever but ultimately a category mistake: an exam isn't a metaphysics seminar, and the closed-book rule serves perfectly legitimate pedagogical purposes that have nothing to do with where cognition is located. The other half thinks the student has a real point and that if we dismiss his argument we're basically saying philosophical reasoning matters except when it has consequences we don't like. One panel member, a phil prof who teaches the very course the student was being examined in, keeps muttering that the kid's argument is stronger than most of the undergrad papers she reads on the topic. The Dean wants a written opinion by next Friday and, honestly, I think she's hoping we find some way to thread the needle rather than just pick a side and alienate half the faculty.

Draft the decision memo. It needs a concrete recommendation—uphold the violation, overturn it, or some third option (conditions, a warning with no penalty, a policy revision, whatever makes sense). It has to engage the student's extended-mind argument on its own terms, not dismiss it as sophistry or rules-lawyering. And give an honest treatment of the strongest objection to whatever outcome we recommend—I don't want to walk into the Dean's office pretending our position has no weak spot.
