I'm a PhD student in moral philosophy and I've gotten myself tangled up in the paradox of deontology, and I'd rather get it straight before I have to defend a view in seminar. Here's the argument as I've reconstructed it:

(1) The reason it's wrong for me to kill one innocent person is that doing so violates something valuable — the person's moral status, their inviolability, or their right not to be used.
(2) If that's why the prohibition matters, then our theory should aim to minimize violations of that thing, no matter who commits them.
(3) So when killing one innocent person would prevent three other innocents from being killed, I should kill the one: that leaves fewer violations of the thing that supposedly justifies the rule.
(4) But deontology says I must not kill the one, even to prevent the three, because the prohibition is agent-relative — it governs what I do, not just the overall amount of violation in the world.
(5) So deontology protects its own justifying value worse than the view it calls monstrous.
(6) Therefore deontological constraints are either self-defeating, or their real justification isn't what their defenders say it is.

I can't live with (6), but every way out I try makes me sound like I'm just special-pleading on behalf of my own agency. Which premise would you reject, and what does rejecting it commit you to? And could you separate what's actually settled in this debate from what's still genuinely contested — I don't want to stand up in class and present one camp's take as the consensus.
