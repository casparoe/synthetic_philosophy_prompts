I have been working through the decision-theoretic discussion of Pascal's mugging, and I am struck by how often two quite different responses are treated as interchangeable. One response is to cap the utility function, so that no single possible outcome can contribute more than a finite amount to expected utility. The other is to adopt a probability threshold, refusing to consider outcomes whose probability falls below some small value. Both are meant to stop a mugger from extracting a small certain payment in exchange for a tiny probability of an enormous payoff, but they do not actually do the same work: a cap limits the damage any single outcome can do, whereas a threshold dismisses certain offers before their payoffs are ever weighed.

I would like a thought experiment built to the following specification. Construct a decision problem in which these two fixes come apart: an agent whose utility function is capped at a generous level pays the mugger, while an agent with the same underlying utility function and the same probabilities but a reasonable probability threshold refuses the same offer. The capped agent must be acting as a correct expected-utility maximizer given the cap, not making an arithmetic error. The probability must be small enough that the situation is recognizably a Pascal's mugging rather than an ordinary favorable lottery, and the threshold must be low enough that it is a genuine precaution rather than a blanket refusal to think about anything improbable. After presenting the scenario, show the expected-utility calculation for each agent, identify the feature of the case that makes the two fixes diverge, and explain how a defender of utility-capping would respond to the claim that the capped agent has still been mugged.

Finally, say whether the divergence can be produced with payoffs and probabilities that feel like real money and real credences, or whether any case meeting the constraints forces either the cap or the threshold to be implausible. I am particularly interested in whether the capped agent's willingness to pay can be made non-trivial without setting the cap so low that the fix stops being recognizable as the standard bounded-utility response.
