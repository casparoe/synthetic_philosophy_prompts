The Frege-Geach problem, first clearly articulated by A.J. Ayer in Language, Truth and Logic, is an objection to expressivist theories in metaethics. Expressivism holds that moral judgments like "stealing is wrong" do not express beliefs that can be true or false, but rather express non-cognitive attitudes — paradigmatically, emotions of approval or disapproval. So when I say "stealing is wrong," I'm not reporting a fact about the world; I'm expressing my disapproval of stealing.

The problem arises because moral sentences can be embedded in complex linguistic contexts where the speaker is clearly not expressing the attitude in question. For instance, consider the conditional: "If stealing is wrong, then getting your little brother to steal is wrong." The speaker who utters this conditional is not thereby expressing disapproval of stealing — they might be entirely neutral about the antecedent. Yet the sentence "stealing is wrong" appears as a component of the conditional, and it seems to have the same meaning there as it does when asserted on its own. This creates a puzzle: if the meaning of "stealing is wrong" is to express disapproval, how can it retain that meaning in embedded contexts where no disapproval is expressed?

This is a serious challenge because it threatens the core expressivist claim that moral language gets its meaning from the attitudes it expresses. If the meaning of "stealing is wrong" shifts depending on whether it's asserted or merely embedded, then expressivism hasn't really given a compositional semantics for moral language. The problem also extends to moral reasoning: if expressivism can't account for how moral terms behave in embedded contexts, it's unclear how expressivists can explain the validity of moral arguments like "If stealing is wrong, then getting your brother to steal is wrong. Stealing is wrong. Therefore, getting your brother to steal is wrong." This argument is clearly valid, but expressivism seems to require that the meaning of "stealing is wrong" differs between the conditional premise and the categorical premise, which would make the argument commit the fallacy of equivocation.

The standard response, developed by Simon Blackburn, is quasi-realism. Blackburn argues that expressivists can "earn the right" to talk as if moral judgments have truth-conditions by showing that the attitudes expressed by moral judgments have the right kind of structure to support a deflationary notion of truth. In particular, Blackburn argues that moral attitudes are not just simple emotions but have a complex structure that allows them to be embedded in logical relations. By treating moral judgments as expressing commitments to systems of norms rather than simple occurrent emotions, expressivists can explain how the meaning of moral terms remains stable across embedded and unembedded contexts.
