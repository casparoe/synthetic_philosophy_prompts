I've been trying to write a piece on hard choices for the magazine — the kind where it's not just that you don't know which option is better, but where it genuinely feels like the options aren't on the same scale, so there's no fact of the matter to be ignorant *of*. Ruth Chang's work got me interested in this, and I thought I'd sit down and actually articulate my own verdicts on a bunch of cases to see if there's a coherent position underneath them. Reader, there may not be.

Here's the list I've been kicking around. (1) Fire rescue: my partner is in one room, two strangers in another, I can only get to one. I don't think there's a correct answer about which choice is better, and I don't think I'd be making a mistake either way. Genuine hard choice. (2) Career fork: one path prevents a great deal of suffering (say, global health logistics), the other creates a great deal of beauty (say, directing films). Again, no fact of the matter about which is the better life — pick either and you haven't erred. (3) Trivial vs. catastrophic: preventing one person's moderate headache versus saving a child's life. Not a hard choice at all — the latter just *is* better, full stop. So not everything is incommensurable. (4) Aggregation puzzle: a world where millions suffer moderate chronic pain versus a world where one person is tortured horribly for decades. I find myself thinking the one-person-tortured world is worse, even if the summed-up suffering in the moderate-pain world is greater. So I seem to reject simple aggregation across persons, at least for suffering. (5) Promises and strangers: upholding a serious promise I made to a friend who has since died, versus preventing a minor inconvenience to someone I've never met — the promise wins and it isn't close. (6) Same setup, different stakes: the promise would only save me a minor inconvenience, and breaking it would save a stranger's life. Now saving the life wins, and again it's not close. So apparently the same two values — promissory obligation and general beneficence — can be incommensurable in one context but not in another, depending on how lopsided the stakes are. (7) The meta-verdict: I think that if you face a genuinely hard choice and you just flip a coin, you're doing something wrong — you're treating a moment that calls for the exercise of agency as if it were arbitrary. But I *also* think that deliberating forever between options that are truly incommensurable is irrational in its own way, a kind of paralysis dressed up as thoughtfulness. That pair of beliefs seems unstable.

What I want to know is whether there's a principle, or at least a tight cluster of commitments, that gets all of these verdicts. If there isn't, which ones should I abandon, and what does the cleaned-up view say about a case I haven't listed — say, a democratic electorate choosing between two policy packages that prioritize entirely different kinds of value?
