News organizations, style guides, and many universities follow a standard practice: when a slur is relevant to a story, they refer to it by a euphemism or a bowdlerized spelling rather than print or say the word itself, even when the story is precisely about someone's use of that word. Courts in the same society often do the opposite: a slur introduced as evidence in a harassment, discrimination, or hate-crime case is quoted verbatim in the record, on the grounds that the exact expression is part of the conduct being evaluated. Meanwhile, many of the same institutions that police slurs also tolerate, or at least hesitate to punish, reclaimed use of those words by in-group members. Take these three practices as evidence of an implicit theory of slurs. What must slurs be like — in their semantics, their pragmatics, or their status as taboo words — for this combination of practices to make sense? Reconstruct the implicit view of where a slur's harm or offense is located, distinguish what the practices strictly presuppose from what they are merely compatible with, and identify the deepest tension among them. If one of the main rival views of slurs were adopted instead, which of these practices would be hardest to defend, and why?
