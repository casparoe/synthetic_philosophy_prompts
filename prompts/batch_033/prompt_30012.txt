I'm making a 'which theory of manipulation do you actually hold?' quiz for my philosophy blog, and I want it to actually discriminate between the live philosophical accounts of where manipulation ends and legitimate persuasion begins, not just let people pick whichever label sounds nicest. Could you write the quiz for me: eight to ten questions, each one a concrete little case — a car salesman who flatters you, a doctor who frames a risk as 90% survival rather than 10% mortality, a friend who gives you three good reasons to move to their city but leaves out that they'd benefit, a boss who says nobody on the team has ever refused weekend shifts, a public health ad built around a grieving family, a default organ-donation opt-out, that sort of thing — with two or three answer options each. The options should be reactions to the case (is it manipulation, is it fine, does it depend on something specific) rather than little theoretical statements.

Then give me a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto the main positions, built from the distinctions first rather than from a list of names: whether the influence is hidden from the target, whether it works through the target's reasoning or around it (flattery, fear, framing effects), whether there's deception or withholding, whether the options themselves are rigged, and whether the target would endorse the whole process if they knew exactly what was going on. The leaves I'd like to see located somewhere in that space are: manipulation as covert influence, manipulation as bypassing or subverting rational capacities, manipulation as a kind of pressure that leaves no acceptable alternative, manipulation as deception-adjacent, and the deflationary view that there's no principled line and 'manipulation' is just a loaded word for persuasion we don't like. I dont need you to be exhaustive about the literature, but please flag any leaves your questions can't separate (I suspect covert influence and bypassing rationality come out near-identical in cases, since hiding the influence is often how you get around someone's reasoning) and say which combinations of answers are unoccupied, and whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. The audience is blog readers with a bit of philosophy background, so the questions should be intuitive but the scoring key can be a little more technical.
