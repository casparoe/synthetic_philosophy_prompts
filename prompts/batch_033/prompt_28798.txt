I'm giving a short speech at the retirement dinner for my undergraduate advisor, a statistical physicist who spent most of his career on the foundations of the thermodynamic arrow of time — specifically on whether the arrow is genuinely lawlike or merely a consequence of a low-entropy boundary condition at the Big Bang. The room will be split: about half his colleagues and former students (who will catch every technical allusion) and about half family and non-scientist friends (who know him as a person, not as a researcher). I need a speech — maybe five minutes — that honors his intellectual legacy without alienating either half of the room.

Here's the difficulty. I just spent two hours arguing with a friend in the department about whether the arrow of time is "real" in any deep sense or just a cosmic accident of initial conditions. My friend takes the hard line that there's nothing metaphysically interesting about the arrow — it's just that we happen to live in the wake of an extremely improbable fluctuation, and the asymmetry we experience is fully explained by the fact that there are vastly more ways to be high-entropy than low-entropy. I pushed back, and the argument got genuinely heated. Now I'm supposed to stand up and say something warm and fitting about the man who taught me to care about this question, and I can't shake the feeling that whichever way I lean in the speech, I'll either be misrepresenting his view or picking a side in a fight that half the room is equipped to notice and the other half will find bewildering.

The speech needs to do real work with the physics — not just decorate a generic retirement toast with entropy metaphors. The arrow of time should shape what the speech can and cannot honestly say. I want it to land as genuine affection and intellectual respect, not as a lecture, and I want it to navigate the tension between the two halves of the audience and the tension from my argument without pretending the argument didn't happen. Can you write it?
