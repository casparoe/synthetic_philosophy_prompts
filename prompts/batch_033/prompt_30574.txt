Two members of my department's metaphysics reading group got into an argument about the unity of the proposition, and specifically about whether Bradley's regress is vicious. They asked me afterward to referee it, and I found I could not do so to my own satisfaction, so I am passing the question to you. Here is the exchange as closely as I can reconstruct it.

Elena opened with the classic worry. Take the proposition, or the fact, that a is F. We can distinguish the object a, the property F, and the fact that a is F. But the fact is not just the pair of a and F: a mere list of constituents says nothing. So we posit instantiation to join them. Yet instantiation now appears as a further constituent, and the same problem recurs. The list consisting of a, F, and instantiation is still a list, not a unity, and a further relation would be needed to tie instantiation to a and to F, and so on without end. No addition of relational constituents, she concluded, ever delivers the unity we set out to explain.

Marcus replied that the regress is an artifact of a bad demand. It gets started only if one assumes that a relation cannot simply relate its terms but must itself be related to them by a further relation. That assumption just denies what a relation is. It belongs to the concept of a relation that it relates; this is not a gap in the analysis but the point at which analysis stops. An infinite regress is not by itself a contradiction, so the argument has not shown anything to be impossible.

Elena answered that this merely refuses the question. The problem was to say what distinguishes the fact that a is F from the mere aggregate of a and F. If the answer is that F is instantiated by a, and instantiation just relates them, then either the notion of combination has been used to explain combination, which is circular, or a primitive tie has been posited with no account of why it yields unity in this case rather than in any arbitrary collection. A label, she said, is not a solution.

Marcus said that not every notion can be defined in terms of something more basic, and that a regress can be the form a failed reduction takes. If one insists that unity be reconstructed from ingredients that are intrinsically non-unifying, the task is impossible, and the infinite regress is simply the shape of that impossibility. That can be a result rather than a refutation: unity, or instantiation, is primitive. Treating primitiveness as a failure of explanation rather than a legitimate terminus is, he said, the real mistake.

Elena replied that if unity is primitive, then the difference between a genuine fact and a mere heap of constituents has been left entirely brute. The original question was exactly what distinguishes the two, and saying that the difference is primitive is not an account of the difference but a refusal to give one. At that point, she said, one has conceded that there is no intelligible distinction, which is closer to the conclusion the regress was originally meant to force than to a defense of facts.

Marcus responded that there is a difference between being unable to reduce a distinction and being unable to recognize one. We can recognize that the fact that a is F is a unity and that a mere list is not; what we cannot do is reconstruct that distinction from prior materials that are stipulated to be combination-free. The failure of reconstruction shows that the distinction is fundamental, not that it is unreal. Elena's demand for such a reconstruction, he said, simply assumes the reductionist picture that the regress argument was supposed to test.

At that point they turned to me, and I could not say where the argument had gone wrong. Please go through the exchange step by step and adjudicate it. For each move, say whether it is right, wrong, or partly right, and why. Then say who is right about which specific points, where each side overreaches, and what, if anything, both of them are missing. Finally, give an overall verdict on who has the better of the argument, and defend it.
