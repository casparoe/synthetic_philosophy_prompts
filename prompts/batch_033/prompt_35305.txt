I am a novelist, and in the course of researching a character — a physicist who, late in his career, becomes convinced that individual consciousness is an illusion and that all minds are manifestations of a single underlying awareness — I have repeatedly encountered a philosophical move that I cannot name, and I would like your help identifying it.

The move runs as follows. A writer develops a careful argument that the separateness of persons is a kind of cognitive error: at the deepest level, there is only one subject of experience, and the apparent boundaries between minds are like the apparent boundaries between eddies in a stream — real enough at the surface, but not divisions in the water. The argument is presented as a conclusion that rational inquiry compels. Then, often without signalling any shift, the writer changes register: they address the reader as a distinct individual, draw ethical implications about how we should treat one another, and write as though there are still multiple epistemic agents who need to be persuaded of a truth that, if accepted, would dissolve the very separateness of persuader and persuaded. The practical stance of the prose — its pronouns, its exhortations, its implicit picture of author and reader as two minds converging on a proposition — remains untouched by the metaphysics it professes.

I am not asking whether the metaphysics is correct, or even whether it is coherent. I am asking whether this specific pattern — the oscillation between a monistic metaphysics of mind and an irreducibly pluralistic mode of argument and address — has been discussed by philosophers under a recognized label. If it has, what is the label, and how precisely do philosophers distinguish this from neighbouring phenomena like performative contradiction, the paradox of self-reference, or the broader tension between eliminativist theories and the manifest image? If it has not, what would you call it, and where would you draw its boundaries?
