I'm writing an essay on Daniel Dennett's idea that Darwin and Turing each performed what he calls the strange inversion of reasoning: the reversal of the traditional assumption that competence must be explained by comprehension. A friend of mine, who has read more of this material than I have, offered me the following summary of the view:

"Before Darwin, the only way anyone knew to explain competence — an organism that is good at something, a well-designed eye or a wing — was to posit an intelligent designer who comprehended the design. Darwin inverted this by showing that natural selection can build up genuine competence gradually through an entirely mindless, mechanical process, with no understanding anywhere in the causal chain, so comprehension is a late product of evolution rather than its source. Turing performed the same inversion for the mind: he showed that a machine made of individually stupid parts, each blindly following rules, can do arithmetic, play chess, and in principle converse well enough to fool a human interrogator, all without comprehending anything. The upshot is that what we call understanding is not what drives our intelligent behavior; it is a kind of user illusion generated by underlying mindless competences, and in the end there is no real comprehension in the universe, just competences all the way down. Dennett's conclusion is that the conscious mind is a user illusion and that consciousness itself does no real causal work."

I find the Darwin part and the general shape of the Turing part plausible, but I'm suspicious of the last stretch, where the summary slides from comprehension not being the source of competence to there being no real comprehension at all — and I can't tell whether that slide is actually in Dennett or is my friend's gloss. I'd like you to act as a philosopher of mind and biology who knows Dennett's actual position, the Darwinian and Turing material he is drawing on, and the standard misreadings of both. Please begin by restating in your own words what you take Darwin's inversion and Turing's inversion each to be, so I can see whether the summary has the core right even where its wording slips. Then go through the summary sentence by sentence and tell me which parts are accurate, which are defensible simplifications, and which are wrong or overstated; for each questionable part, say what a careful defender of Dennett would object to and what the more precise claim would be. Finally, address whether the closing claim — that consciousness is a user illusion that does no real causal work — actually follows from anything correct in the summary, or whether an extra step has been smuggled in. I'd like to come away with a formulation of the inversion that I can trust and defend in an essay, not just a list of errors.
