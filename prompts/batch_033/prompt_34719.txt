I'm in a philosophy of art reading group, and last week we read a couple of papers on forgery and aesthetic value. The discussion got derailed by a long argument between two members of the group — Clara and David — and none of the rest of us could settle it. I'd like you to adjudicate it, move by move. Please approach this as a professional philosopher specializing in aesthetics, and think carefully through each step before rendering a verdict. It's fine to start with some brainstorming to get the lay of the land before you commit to an analysis.

The argument started when we were discussing the van Meegeren forgeries of Vermeer. Clara said the following:

(1) "If a forgery is visually indistinguishable from an original — and I mean genuinely indistinguishable, even by the best experts with the best equipment — then the forgery and the original provide exactly the same aesthetic experience. And since aesthetic value just is the capacity to provide valuable aesthetic experiences, the forgery and the original have equal aesthetic value. The premium we place on the original is pure fetishism about history."

David responded:

(2) "That can't be right, because if you learned tomorrow that your favorite painting was a forgery, your experience of it would change. You'd look at it differently — you might even feel betrayed. So the two objects don't provide the same aesthetic experience after all, once you know their histories. And since we're talking about informed viewers, not people kept in the dark, the histories are part of what's experienced."

Clara came back:

(3) "The change in experience you're describing is a causal effect of learning a fact, not a feature of the painting's aesthetic properties. If I feel sad looking at a painting because I just learned my cat died, my experience has changed but the painting hasn't gotten worse. The fact that knowledge of forgery causes distress doesn't mean the forgery is aesthetically worse — it means I'm having an emotional reaction to a non-aesthetic fact."

David replied:

(4) "But the cat example is disanalogous — your cat has nothing to do with the painting. The forgery's history is a fact about the painting. And our aesthetic practices treat facts about an artwork's origins as internal to its appreciation. We don't think someone is fully appreciating a work if they think it's a postmodern pastiche when it's actually a Renaissance piece. History isn't external to aesthetic appreciation; it's constitutive of what the work is."

Clara then shifted ground:

(5) "Fine, let me grant that historical facts can matter to appreciation. But you still haven't explained why a perfect forgery is worse. If the forgery is so good that it fools everyone forever, then the fact that it's a forgery never enters anyone's experience. At that point you're saying the work has less value because of a fact nobody ever encounters. That's like saying a medicine is ineffective because of an ingredient nobody can detect."

David responded:

(6) "That's an epistemic point, not a value point. The fact that nobody knows the painting is a forgery doesn't mean it isn't one, and it doesn't mean the painting lacks the property of being a forgery. Value can depend on mind-independent facts. If I unknowingly marry a con artist, my marriage is worse even if I never find out — because the relationship lacks the property of genuine mutual commitment, which is part of what makes a marriage valuable."
