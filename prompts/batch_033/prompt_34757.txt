I'm a provenance researcher at a mid-size auction house, and after two restitution claims in one year the directors want a reusable due-diligence procedure for deciding weather a consignment's chain of title is clean enough to sell. They specifically want it built on Nozick's entitlement theory — justice in acquisition, justice in transfer, and rectification of past injustice — and not on any patterned or end-state view of who should own what now, so a lopsided distribution alone shouldn't trigger a red flag. Write me the step-by-step procedure I can actually run on a file: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where a cold trail should block the sale versus bump it to a person, and which steps are moral calls the theory can't make for us. Then run the procedure on three files: a painting looted in the 1940s that's since been sold twice in apparently voluntary transactions, a Benin bronze taken during an 1897 punitive expedition and later donated to a museum, and an antiquity with no recorded history before 1970 that the current seller bought in good faith. Make sure to flag the step where the procedure tells us something the directors won't want to hear, and say plainly what Nozick's framework leaves unsettled — especially how much rectification is owed when the wrong is old or the original owners are gone.
