In the seminar I'm taking on the ethics of killing and letting die, we have been collecting a professor's judgments on a series of stylized cases. So far she has judged the following: (i) a surgeon may not kill one healthy patient to harvest organs for five; (ii) a bystander may not push someone off a footbridge onto the track, even to stop a runaway trolley from killing five; (iii) a physician may withhold a ventilator from a competent, terminally ill patient who refuses it, while foreseeing that the patient will die, provided the physician's aim is to respect the refusal; (iv) a nurse who deliberately omits to turn a bedridden patient so that the patient dies, in order to inherit, acts impermissibly; and (v) a doctor may direct a scarce treatment to five patients rather than to one, while foreseeing that the one will die, provided the one is not used to benefit the others.

Next week's case is the standard switch case: a bystander can divert a runaway trolley onto a side track where it will kill one person, thereby saving five on the main track, where the one's death is foreseen but in no way intended and the one is not used to stop the trolley. Predict her verdict. Identify the principle or principles that best fit her record so far, state which further principles fit it equally well, say what each predicts about the switch case, and specify the case you would put to her next to discriminate among the principles that survive her answer — in particular, one in which the person's body is needed to stop the trolley though the death is still, by stipulation, foreseen rather than intended.
