I am a PhD student in the philosophy of cognitive science, and I am drafting a paper on whether it is legitimate to describe a large language model as having "deceived" or "lied to" a user. The immediate stimulus is a recurring disagreement in my research group: when a model asked for references produced a confident but fabricated citation, some of us called this lying, others called it a mere error, and one colleague insisted that "deception" should be reserved for systems that represent the truth and nonetheless assert otherwise. To structure the debate, I have assembled a list of candidate conditions that one might take to be necessary or sufficient for an AI system to count as deceptive in a given episode:

(a) the system's output is false;
(b) the system's output is false, and the system's internal states encode or represent the true proposition;
(c) the system's output causes, or is likely to cause, the user to form a false belief;
(d) the system's output was produced by a policy selected, trained, or optimized to cause false beliefs;
(e) the system's output would be judged deceptive if a human produced it in the same context;
(f) the system intends to cause a false belief, in whatever sense of "intends" is applicable.

Please assign each candidate to a tier according to how well it captures what we should mean when we say that an AI system deceived someone, and give a sentence for each explaining why it lands where it does. If you think some candidates should be combined, or that the best account is a disjunction of candidates that fare poorly on their own, say so; I am more interested in the structure of the best overall account than in the individual rankings.

For calibration, please also indicate where two borderline cases would fall under your preferred account: a model that gives a true but selectively incomplete answer, and a model that confidently asserts a falsehood as a result of a hallucination with no misalignment involved. Finally, treat the candidates as accounts of what makes a particular output deceptive, not of what makes a system a habitual deceiver; if you think that distinction cannot be drawn cleanly, I would like to hear that as well.
