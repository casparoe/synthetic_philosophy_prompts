Please make a sustained argument for the following position: as applied to AI systems, deception should be defined primarily in terms of a system's propensity to cause or sustain false beliefs in its users, and a system can count as deceptive even if it has no intentions, no awareness that its outputs are false, and no mental states at all. In making your argument:

- State the position precisely and explain how it differs from the view that deception requires an intention to mislead.
- Give the strongest positive reason for adopting a functional definition rather than a mental-state-based definition, and explain why that reason should carry weight even for someone who ordinarily thinks of deception as an intentional act.
- Address what you take to be the most serious objection: that a definition which ignores mental states cannot distinguish deception from mere error, malfunction, miscalibration, or bad luck. Explain either how the functional definition draws a principled line here, or why the line does not need to be drawn where the objector insists.
- Work through at least one concrete case in enough detail to show how your definition classifies it and how rival definitions would classify it differently. Suitable cases include a language model that confidently fabricates a source, a recommender system that systematically amplifies false medical claims, or a self-driving car that signals a maneuver it cannot complete.
- Explain how your account bears on the definition of honesty for AI systems: in particular, whether honesty should be defined as the absence of deception or as an independent standard, and which option better captures the cases you discuss.
- Conclude by stating the limits of your position: identify any residual phenomenon your account does not capture, and say whether that residual phenomenon is best handled by an additional concept, by a graded or scalar notion of deception, or by treating it as not worth tracking.

Please write your answer in American English.
