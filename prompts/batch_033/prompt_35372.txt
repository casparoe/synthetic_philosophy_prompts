I've spent the last year or so trying to build a working personal philosophy out of Stoicism — mostly the modern revival through Irvine, Pigliucci, and Holiday, but with some engagement with the original texts (Epictetus, Marcus Aurelius, Seneca in translation). I'm now at the point where I can state my commitments fairly precisely, and I'd like someone to audit them for consistency before I go around recommending this package to others. Here they are:

(1) The dichotomy of control is foundational: my flourishing — the only thing I should ultimately care about — depends entirely on what is up to me, namely my judgments, my choices, and my character. Health, wealth, reputation, the well-being of people I love, and large-scale political outcomes are all externals. They are "preferred indifferents" worth pursuing, but I should never make my happiness conditional on obtaining them.

(2) I hold the Stoic theory of the passions: anger, grief, fear, and even intense elation are cognitive errors — they consist in the false judgment that something external is genuinely good or bad. The only appropriate affective states are the eupatheiai (wish, caution, and a kind of quiet joy), none of which involve assenting to the proposition that an external matters for my flourishing.

(3) I accept the Stoic commitment to cosmopolitanism: I have a duty to act justly and beneficently toward all human beings, not merely toward my in-group, and a life well-lived requires discharging that duty in practice, not just affirming it in principle.

(4) I adopt the reserve clause (hupexairesis) for all action aimed at externals: when I pursue an outcome — a friend's recovery, a political cause, a professional goal — I do so while maintaining inner indifference to whether the outcome actually comes about. My success consists in the quality of my effort and intention, not in the result.

(5) The sage is a regulative ideal I will never reach, but the path of the prokoptôn (the one making progress) is coherent: I can approximate these attitudes asymptotically, and each step is a genuine improvement over the last.

I have a persistent sense that (2), (3), and (4) are rubbing against each other and against (1), but I can't tell which frictions are genuine and which dissolve under analysis. For instance: if grief over a friend's suffering is a cognitive error because it treats an external as bad, what exactly is the psychological fuel for the cosmopolitan duty in (3)? And does the reserve clause in (4) let me double-count — feeling virtuous for effort while preemptively discounting failure — in a way that makes a mockery of genuinely trying? Go through these five commitments and identify which pairs are in tension, rank the tensions by severity, and tell me which single revision would do the most to clean things up. If the problem is that the modern popularized version of Stoicism papers over difficulties the original texts took seriously, I want to hear that too.
