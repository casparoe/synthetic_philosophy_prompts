I'm an undergraduate in a metaphysics course that has just introduced the special composition question, and I'm organizing a study group to work through the primary readings. The problem I keep running into is that members of the group already use words like "object" and "exists" in different ways, which makes discussion collapse into people talking past one another. I want to hand out a short sorting quiz — "Which answer to the special composition question do you actually hold?" — that lets each of us locate ourselves by how we respond to concrete cases, before any labels are on the table.

Could you write the quiz? I'd like nine questions, each a short scenario about whether some things compose a further object, with three mutually exclusive fixed response options tailored to the case. For instance, for two people shaking hands and then letting go, the options might be: a composite exists only while they are in contact; no composite exists at any point; a composite exists and persists after they separate. Include a one-line gloss at the top of the quiz explaining "composite object" without invoking any theory. The scenarios should be chosen so that the response patterns separate seven leaves: mereological nihilism; universalism; the contact view; the fastening view; the cohesion view; the organicist answer associated with van Inwagen, on which some things compose something just when their activity constitutes a life; and brutal composition, on which there are facts about composition but no informative answer to the question. Please make sure at least these cases appear: two people shaking hands and then separating; a living tree and that same tree immediately after it dies; a severed arm; a pile of sand; two stacked bricks; a wooden chair with glued joints; and a cloud of water droplets. Add two further cases of your choosing wherever the seven leave two positions under-separated. The quiz must not ask respondents to identify a position by name or definition; every question should be answerable by someone who has never heard the labels. Then provide the scoring key. Build it from the logical space rather than from the list of names: first lay out the nine questions and the combinations of answers they allow, then locate each of the seven positions in that space. Flag any answer pattern that no named position occupies and briefly diagnose why — because the pattern is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because it is a real but merely unnamed view. Also flag any two leaves the nine questions cannot separate, and state the one additional case that would separate them. Finally, give brutal composition a special line in the key: if a respondent's pattern matches no systematic view, or if they accept that composition facts are brute, the key should sort them to brutal composition rather than forcing a fit.
