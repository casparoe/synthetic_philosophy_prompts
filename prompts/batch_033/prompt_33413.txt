Benjamin Franklin famously designed a personal system for attaining what he called moral perfection. He drew up a list of thirteen virtues—temperance, order, resolution, sincerity, and the like—and kept a small book in which each virtue had its own page, with columns for the days of the week. Each week he gave one virtue his strict attention, leaving the others to their ordinary chance, and each evening he recorded a black spot for every fault he detected, so that the week's virtue was supposed to finish with a clean line. After thirteen weeks he had completed one course through the list and began again, giving each virtue four concentrated weeks a year. He hoped the spots would eventually disappear and leave the book clean, but he found the project far harder than expected. Order in particular resisted him, and he added humility to the list only after a friend told him he was widely thought proud. By his own later account he never achieved moral perfection and doubted he ever would, though he still believed the discipline had made him a better man.

I keep returning to this because the method resembles bookkeeping more than moral education as philosophers usually describe it, and I want to know what it commits him to. Reconstruct the theory of virtue and moral improvement that this practice presupposes. Does it presuppose that the moral life can be decomposed into discrete, independently trainable virtues; that a vice is a countable lapse rather than a disposition of the whole person; that self-examination is reliable enough to keep an accurate ledger of one's own faults; that moral progress is cumulative, the way clearing a debt is? Or is the ledger merely a heuristic, compatible with several rival theories of character? Is the practice internally consistent—above all in treating humility as one more item to be checked off, when the clean ledger seems to reward exactly the self-monitoring pride that humility would forbid? And if the presupposed theory were replaced by a view on which the virtues form a unity, or on which self-improvement runs through institutions, friendships, and habits rather than private accounting, what would have to change in the practice, and what would a regimen built on that rival theory look like?
