I retired from a career in experimental physics and have since developed an interest in philosophy of mind and the foundations of mathematics. I now serve on an advisory panel that reviews proposals to deploy AI systems in mathematical research — automated theorem proving, conjecture generation, and the like. The Lucas–Penrose argument (that Gödel's incompleteness theorems show the human mind cannot be a Turing machine, because we can see the truth of a Gödel sentence that no formal system we might instantiate could prove) comes up repeatedly in our deliberations, but always as a debating point rather than as something we can operationalize.

I would like you to produce a decision procedure — a sequence of questions, tests, and branching conditions — that my panel could apply to a candidate AI system in order to determine whether it is subject to the kind of limitation Lucas and Penrose describe, and what follows for its suitability in mathematical research. The procedure should be something a technically literate panel without specialist training in mathematical logic could run, though it may require the assistance of a logician at certain steps. It must be explicit about:

- Which steps depend on knowing the system's formal specification (and what to do when that specification is unavailable or the system is a black box).
- Where the procedure itself rests on premises that are philosophically disputed — e.g., whether a system that can prove its own Gödel sentence via a reflection principle counts as escaping the argument, or whether an inconsistent system is a live possibility — and what alternative branches a skeptic might insist on.
- Where human judgment enters and cannot be eliminated by the procedure.
- What the procedure is unable to tell us, even when run correctly.

Please also apply the procedure to two hypothetical systems:

(a) A large language model trained on a corpus that includes textbooks on mathematical logic and the Lucas–Penrose debate itself. The model, when prompted, correctly states Gödel's theorems, asserts that the Gödel sentence for Peano arithmetic is true, and claims to "understand" why. It has no accessible formal specification.

(b) A neuro-symbolic system whose designers disclose that its deductive core implements exactly primitive recursive arithmetic. Its learning component sits on top of this core and can propose new axioms, but the deductive engine only sanctions what follows from PRA plus whatever axioms the learning component has most recently fixed. The designers argue that because the learning component can add axioms the deductive core cannot reach on its own, the system as a whole escapes the Lucas–Penrose argument.

For each, tell me what the procedure would conclude and, crucially, what it would leave unresolved.
