Here's the deal. For the last four or five years I've been pretty deeply embedded in a research community that takes a heterodox line on a couple of major questions in epistemology — think something like rejecting the standard Bayesian picture of evidence in favor of a more radical alternative. The group is tight: we've got a reading group that meets every two weeks, an active Slack with maybe thirty people, a yearly workshop, co-authored papers, the works. And what's been nagging at me is that I've gradually noticed I almost never encounter serious pushback on the core claims anymore. When I pick up a paper from outside the group that's supposed to be a critique of our view, I usually spot what I take to be a basic misunderstanding of our position within the first few pages, and I put it down. When my grad students push back in seminar, I give them careful answers, but if I'm honest with myself I'm not really *engaging* — I'm treating it as a teaching opportunity, a chance to show them how the view handles the objection, not as a moment where I might genuinely learn that I'm wrong. A colleague from the political science department, who I respect a lot, told me over coffee last month that she thinks I'm in an echo chamber. She said it kindly, but it stung.

I've been turning this over and I genuinely can't tell whether I'm being irrational. On one hand, it seems like any mature research program reaches a point where the central claims are just taken as background and you stop re-litigating them with every interlocutor. That's not pathological — that's just how inquiry works when you've made progress. Newtonian physicists don't reopen the question of whether F=ma every time someone walks in the room. On the other hand, the patterns look exactly like the sort of thing the echo chamber literature describes: I've cultivated a set of heuristics for quickly dismissing outside views, I notice I only really trust the epistemic judgment of people inside the group, and I feel a real social friction — a kind of queasy discomfort — at the thought of publicly conceding that one of our core claims might be wrong. The social cost feels high, and I don't think that's just my imagination.

So my question is: am I in an echo chamber, an epistemic bubble, both, or neither? And more importantly — what should I actually *do* about it? I want concrete advice, not just "be more open-minded." Should I force myself to read hostile papers all the way through, even when they seem to be missing the point? Should I seek out a formal debate with a critic?
