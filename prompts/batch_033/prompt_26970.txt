I just spent two hours arguing with a friend about Frank Jackson's Mary's room thought experiment, and I came away convinced of something that now strikes me as quite disconcerting. Here is where I ended up.

If Mary knows every physical fact about color vision — all the neuroscience, all the physics of wavelength processing, every functional detail of the visual system — and yet she still learns something new when she first sees red, then there must be facts that are in principle inaccessible to the kind of third-person, objective investigation that science relies on. The experiment seems to demonstrate a principled ceiling on scientific explanation: some truths about the world simply cannot be captured by the methods of the natural sciences.

But if that is right, then how do we distinguish, in advance, which phenomena fall on which side of the line? Presumably we do not need to run a Mary-like thought experiment for every domain of inquiry. So what is the criterion that tells us when scientific investigation will be adequate and when it will necessarily leave something out? Or is the conclusion instead that no domain is fully captured by science, and qualia are merely the most vivid illustration of a universal limitation?
