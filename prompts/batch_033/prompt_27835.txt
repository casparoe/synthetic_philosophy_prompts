I am a novelist, and the book I am drafting turns on a scene in which a woman acts badly under conditions that ensure she could not have done otherwise, yet she seems to me blameworthy in the fullest sense. Working through the scene has led me to accept the Frankfurt-style view: an agent's moral responsibility for an action does not depend on whether she could have done otherwise, but on the actual sequence by which the action came about — her own deliberations, judgments, and choices. The familiar architecture matters to me: a counterfactual intervener stands ready to force the action if the agent is about to diverge from it, but the agent proceeds on her own and the intervener never acts; the intuition is that she is responsible anyway, so the principle of alternative possibilities is false.

I do not want to have talked myself into a position that costs me nothing. I would like you to tell me, candidly, what would count against this view. Specifically: what evidence, arguments, cases, or conceptual distinctions should move me away from it, sorted by how decisive each would be — from fatal to merely awkward? I am less interested in the stock objections as slogans than in a precise map of where the position is exposed. Among the things I have heard mentioned but do not understand well enough to weigh are the flicker-of-freedom strategy; the dilemma defense, which claims that Frankfurt cases either presuppose determinism or fail to close off every alternative; the charge that the cases depend on an antecedently stipulated identification of the act with its actual-sequence causes; and the broader suspicion that the relevant sense of could have done otherwise has been narrowed until the principle is easy to reject. Please say which of these a committed Frankfurtian would concede as counting against the position, which they would dismiss as not touching it, and on what grounds.

I would also like an honest assessment of whether the position, as typically defended, has been insulated against refutation — and whether, if it has, that insulation is a vice in a view of this kind or simply the normal condition of a conceptual claim. Finally, tell me which single consideration, if established, would do the most to change my mind, and which of the defeaters you personally find most persuasive.
