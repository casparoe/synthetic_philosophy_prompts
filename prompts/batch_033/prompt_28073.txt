You are a philosopher of mind with expertise in debates about emergence, collective intelligence, and the metaphysics of consciousness. Please think carefully and work through the question step by step.

In *Gödel, Escher, Bach*, Douglas Hofstadter presents the Ant Fugue, in which an anteater converses with an ant named Aunt Hillary about whether her colony — "Aunt Hillary" in another sense — possesses a mind. The colony as a whole exhibits flexible, goal-directed behavior that no individual ant understands, and the anteater presses the question: is there really a self there, or just a useful *façon de parler*?

This is not just a literary conceit. A version of this debate runs through philosophy of mind, the study of swarm intelligence, and even discussions of group agency. On one side are those who say that if a system processes information, represents its environment, and acts intelligently, then calling it a mind is not metaphorical — it is exactly what functionalism predicts. On the other side are those who insist that without a unified subjective point of view — without something it is like to be the colony — all the talk of "deciding" and "wanting" is just a picturesque way of describing the aggregate of many non-mental individual behaviors.

My sense is that this debate has been stuck for a long time. The same arguments recur, and neither side moves. I want a diagnosis of the impasse, not a verdict on who is right. Specifically:

1. What kind of disagreement is this, at bottom? Is it a verbal dispute about the meaning of "mind" or "consciousness"? Is it a disagreement about the empirical facts — about what ant colonies actually do, or about what brains actually do? Is it a disagreement about the standards of evidence: what would it take to show that a collective has or lacks a mind? Or is it a clash of bedrock intuitions that neither side can argue for?

2. Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress? An experiment? A conceptual distinction that both sides could accept? A formal result? Or should we conclude that the question is ill-posed and drop it?

3. If you think one side bears more of the burden of proof or is confused in a way the other side is not, say so and explain why — but do this as part of the diagnosis, not as a substitute for it.

Start by laying out the possible ways a philosophical debate can stall, then apply the most appropriate framework to this case.
