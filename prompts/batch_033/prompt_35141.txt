I'm a backend engineer, not a philosophy person, so bare with me. Years ago I read this thing — Hofstadter maybe, can't remember the book — where a character called Aunt Hillary is, I think, an ant colony, and she argues with some skeptic about whether the colony itself has thoughts and feelings even though none of the individual ants do. It stuck with me, and it keeps coming up at work, because I build distributed systems: hundreds of little worker processes, none of which "knows" anything, but the system as a whole does stuff that sure looks like planning. Lately I've tried to actually read up on emergence and collective minds, but everything I find just argues for one answer — yes the colony has a mind, no it doesn't, it's only a metaphor — without ever stating the rules of the game.

So before I read one more thing, I want the spec. What would a theory of when a collection of individually mindless parts counts as having a mind have to deliver, to be good enough that I could use it to decide whether the systems I build are even in the running? Give me the full requirements list, with the case or consideration behind each one: the phenomena it has to explain (like why it feels so natural to talk about a colony "deciding" to move), the cases it has to get right (an ant colony, a human brain made of neurons, a corporation, a trained neural net, a flock of birds), the distinctions it can't collapse (like "processes information" vs "has a mind", or having a mind vs simulating one, or the parts' properties vs the whole's properties), and the results it can't contradict (I keep seeing things like "no homunculus" and "no spooky new substance" invoked, but I can't tell which of these are actual constraints and which are just vibes). Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just one camp's preferences, and tell me whether any subset of them can't be jointly satisfied — if the spec itself is impossible, that's the part I most want to see. Then, briefly, which single requirement does the most to knock out the popular answers.

Since I'm going to repeat this to my team: for each part, say how confident you are in it, and call out the one requirement you're least sure about.
