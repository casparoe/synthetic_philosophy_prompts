I coordinate search-and-rescue for a county in the Cascades. A few times a year we get a call where one crew has to choose between two groups of people in trouble and the groups are different sizes — say a fast-rising river or a fire cutting off two drainages, and we can only get to one before dark. Our standing protocol is "save the most lives," but I've been reading Taurek's "Should the Numbers Count?" and now I'm not sure that protocol is as obvious as we treat it. His line, as I understand it, is that you can't just add up the losses of different people — five people's suffering isn't five times one person's, because no single person experiences the total — and that fairness might require giving each person an equal chance of rescue rather than automatically heading to the bigger group. I want you to turn that into a decision procedure my field teams can actually use. These are volunteers with no philosophy background, they're making the call in minutes, and whatever we do will be picked over afterwards by a review board and probably a lawyer. The procedure should have the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good-enough answer to each one before moving on, where it stops with a no, and where it explicitly says "this is a values call, a person has to make it, not the checklist." Be honest about where a Taurek-style procedure is going to conflict with our usual save-the-most-lives instinct and where it'll misfire or go silent. Then run it on three worked cases: (1) one hiker versus four hikers, same injuries, same weather, same confidence we can get to either site; (2) one hiker we're nearly certain we can reach versus five who are maybe reachable if the water holds; (3) one adult versus three children, everything else equal. At least one of those should come out in a way my people are going to hate, and you should say so.
