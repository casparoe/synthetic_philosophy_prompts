For decades now, philosophers have been arguing about what Nelson Goodman's "grue" paradox actually shows about induction, and the debate appears to have been frozen for at least the last thirty or forty years. The setup, to be explicit: define "grue" as applying to an object just in case it is examined before some future time t and is green, or is not examined before t and is blue. All our evidence that emeralds are green — every examined emerald has been green — equally confirms the hypothesis that all emeralds are grue, which entails that emeralds first examined after t will be blue. The question is what this tells us about the nature and justification of induction.

The positions have settled into several stable camps. One, following Goodman himself, holds that the paradox shows induction cannot be justified by purely formal or syntactic rules; we need a substantive notion of "projectible" predicates, and the fact that "green" rather than "grue" is entrenched in our actual inductive practice is what breaks the symmetry. A second camp argues that the paradox dissolves once we notice that "grue" is gerrymandered in a way that "green" is not — it fails to pick out a natural kind or carve nature at the joints, and that metaphysical difference is why we are entitled to prefer green-hypotheses. A third, broadly Bayesian, says the paradox is no threat because rational agents have prior probabilities that already favor green over grue, and the evidence simply updates those priors; the puzzle only looks like a puzzle if one demands that priors themselves be justified by induction. There are also skeptics who take the paradox to succeed in showing that induction has no rational justification, and deflationists who regard the whole thing as a verbal trick with no deep lesson for inductive practice.

What I find striking is that these positions were all essentially on the table by the 1970s, and the arguments for and against each have been refined but not fundamentally altered since. People rarely change camps. My question is: what kind of disagreement is this, really? Is it a substantive disagreement about the nature of confirmation — one that could in principle be resolved by a better theory of confirmation — or is it a disagreement about what we want from a theory of induction in the first place? Are the camps answering different questions under a single heading (one about justification, another about the description of scientific practice, another about metaphysics)? Or is this a case where the starting intuitions are so fundamental that no argument could shift them, and we should expect the impasse to be permanent? I want a diagnosis that engages with what each side actually says, and an honest answer about what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis.
