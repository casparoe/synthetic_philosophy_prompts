**Exam question:**

In *Naming and Necessity*, Saul Kripke argues that there are necessary truths that can only be known a posteriori, and that the notion of rigid designation is central to this claim. Explain what a rigid designator is, how rigid designation supports the existence of the necessary a posteriori, and what this shows about the relationship between metaphysics and epistemology. Illustrate your answer with at least one of Kripke's own examples.

**Student answer:**

A rigid designator is a term that designates the same object in all possible worlds. Proper names, like "Aristotle," are rigid designators because they pick out the same individual across every possible scenario. Definite descriptions, by contrast, are typically non-rigid: "the teacher of Alexander the Great" picks out Aristotle in the actual world but could pick out someone else in a world where Aristotle never taught Alexander. Natural kind terms like "water" and "gold" are also rigid designators, according to Kripke, because they designate the same underlying kind in all possible worlds.

Rigid designation supports the necessary a posteriori in the following way. Consider the identity statement "Hesperus is Phosphorus." Both "Hesperus" and "Phosphorus" are proper names and therefore rigid designators. Since they both rigidly designate the planet Venus, and since identity between rigidly designated objects holds in every possible world where those objects exist, the statement "Hesperus is Phosphorus" is necessarily true. But we came to know this truth only through empirical observation—ancient astronomers had to go out and discover that the evening star and the morning star were the same celestial body. So here we have a truth that is necessary (true in all possible worlds) yet a posteriori (knowable only through experience). The same structure applies to "water is H2O": both terms rigidly designate the same substance, making the identity necessary, but it took empirical science to discover it.

This shows that the traditional philosophical assumption that necessity and apriority always go together is mistaken. Philosophers from Hume to the logical positivists had assumed that all necessary truths are knowable a priori and all a posteriori truths are contingent. Kripke demonstrates that these are independent distinctions: necessity is a metaphysical notion about how things are in all possible worlds, while apriority is an epistemological notion about how we can come to know something. The necessary a posteriori arises when we have an identity between rigid designators that we can only verify through experience. Conversely, Kripke also argues for the contingent a priori—truths we can know without experience but that could have been otherwise—which further reinforces the independence of the two distinctions.

In summary, rigid designation is the mechanism that allows Kripke to pry apart the metaphysical and the epistemic: it shows that what is necessarily the case need not be knowable from the armchair, because the connections between our terms and the world are fixed by how things are, not by how we happen to learn about them.
