I'm a retired physicist who's gotten into philosophy recently, and I've been stuck on causation by omission. Here's the thing that bugs me: if I push a button, the pushing has a precise time. If I don't push it, when does the not-pushing happen? Omissions seem weirdly untethered from time in a way that actions aren't, and I can't shake the feeling that this matters for whether they're real causes.

I want you to build me a thought experiment that uses this temporal feature to drive a wedge between two theories of causation. Specifically: construct a realistic one-paragraph scenario where an omission and a positive action both contribute to the same outcome, and intuitively they're about equally causal. Then show that a counterfactual theory (Lewis-style) counts the omission as a cause, while a production or conserved-quantity theory (Dowe-style) does not — even though both theories agree the positive action is a cause. The crucial thing: the divergence should boil down to the omission having no determinate time of occurence.

Constraints: everyday setting (a kitchen, a garage, whatever), no professional or legal duties in play — just ordinary moral expectations. No demons, perfect predictors, or exotic physics. I got enough of that in my old job.

After laying out the scenario, walk through the verdict each theory gives and the precise reasoning behind it, and identify which feature of the case is doing the work. Then answer this: if you stipulate an artificial time-of-occurrence for the omission (say, the last moment the agent could've acted), does the production theory flip its verdict? And if it does, what does that tell us about whether the temporal-location problem is the real issue or just a symptom of something deeper?
