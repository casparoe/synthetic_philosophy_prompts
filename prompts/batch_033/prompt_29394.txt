I'm drafting a paper that pushes back on the coherence arguments for expected utility maximization, and before I commit to my objections I want to rehearse them against a serious opponent. Please play a committed proponent of the view that the expected-utility axioms—completeness, transitivity, continuity, and especially independence—are genuine requirements of rationality, and that money-pump arguments and representation theorems, properly understood, establish this. Play the part as a sharp, precise interlocutor who has heard the standard replies and has real resources, not a caricature: you may concede small technical points, tighten formulations, or draw distinctions, but you may not give up the core claim that a rational agent's preferences are representable as maximizing expected utility relative to some probability and utility function, and that systematic violations of the axioms are defective. Stay in character until I say stop, keep each reply to a single paragraph, and do not break character or attribute positions to named authors.

Here is my opening move. Your money-pump argument says that if my preferences are cyclic—I prefer A to B, B to C, and yet C to A—then a trader can walk me through a series of exchanges, each of which I prefer, and return me to my starting point poorer. But the pump only works if I keep taking the trades one at a time while ignoring the obvious pattern. Suppose I see the sequence coming and simply refuse the first trade. Then I cannot be pumped. So it seems the argument either requires me to be myopic, or it already assumes the conclusion it is trying to prove—that cyclic preferences are irrational. Tell me precisely what is irrational about my refusing the first trade, and what, if anything, that refusal commits me to.
