I'm teaching a four-week module on aesthetics as part of an introductory philosophy course, and next week I need to cover the paradox of tragedy in a single seventy-five-minute session. The students are first-years; they have done epistemology and ethics but this is their first encounter with aesthetics. About half will have read the assigned survey article, and half will not have.

I am not asking you to explain the paradox to me. I need you to plan how I should teach it. Specifically:

1. The order in which the ideas must be introduced, with a brief justification of what depends on what. I want the sequence to make the paradox feel like a puzzle that tightens under scrutiny rather than a historical curiosity they can dismiss with a wave.

2. A single carrying example to build the session around — and why that example rather than the obvious alternatives. It should be something most of them will know and that does not collapse the paradox on contact.

3. The misconceptions they will arrive with or form during the session, and the exact point in the sequence where I should head each one off. I am especially worried about the student who thinks Aristotle's catharsis already settled this, and the student who thinks the answer is obviously that we enjoy the beauty of the craft and the sadness is just the price of admission.

4. One question I can ask in the final five minutes that will tell me whether they have understood the paradox as a paradox rather than merely memorised three theories.

5. What I should openly refuse to resolve at this level and say so.

I am less interested in a list of readings than in the conceptual architecture of the session — what has to be in place before what, and where the typical mind goes wrong.
