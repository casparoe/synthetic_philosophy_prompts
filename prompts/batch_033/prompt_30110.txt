I'm a junior on my school's student government constitution commitee, and we're rewriting the whole constitution this year, so this thing will govern every school-wide vote for the next few years. We keep getting stuck on the same question and nobody can settle it: who actually counts as the student body for voting? Like, do freshmen get to vote on the budget that mostly affects upperclassmen? Do students who transfer in halfway through the year get to vote on rules that were proposed before they arrived? Do graduating seniors get a vote on rules that only kick in next year, when they'll be gone? Our school shares a building with a small arts academy, and sometimes we make decisions about shared spaces (cafeteria, auditorium, the courtyard) — do their students get a vote on those? The annoying thing is that every time we try to answer it, we feel like we're already assuming an answer: to decide who gets a say, we'd have to already know who the "we" is. Our advisor just says be fair, and the principal signs off at the end, so we need something more defensable than vibes.

We meet for an hour twice a month and none of us has any philosophy background, so the procedure has to be something actual high schoolers can run in a meeting, and it has to produce reasons our advisor and the principal will accept as real, not just "philosophy says so." You're an expert on democratic theory and the boundary problem. Please write us a decision procedure: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each one, where the procedure should stop with a no, and where it should hand the decision to a person because it's a values call rather than a fact we can look up.

Before you commit to the procedure, brainstorm two or three candidate principles we could build it on (I know there's stuff like the idea that everyone affected by a decision should get a vote, and the idea that everyone who has to live under the rules should get a vote), say which one you're building on and why, and be upfront about what choosing it rules out. Then give the step-by-step procedure. Then run it on at least three cases: graduating seniors voting on next year's rules, arts academy students voting on shared cafeteria hours, and transfer students voting on a budget passed mid-year. I want at least one of those to come out in a way that will probably annoy people — if the procedure just tells us whatever we already wanted, it isn't doing any work. And tell us honestly where the procedure will misfire or can't settle the question.

Finally, say how confident you are in the whole thing, and single out the specific point you're least sure about.
