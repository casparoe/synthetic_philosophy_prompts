I teach an upper-level undergraduate game theory course for philosophy students, and I have just spent two weeks on dominance, iterated elimination, and the beginnings of rationalizability. In office hours this week I noticed that almost every student who came in was operating with a purely pure-strategy picture of dominance: they could see that a strategy is dominated when some single alternative strategy does better against every possible choice of the other players, and they treated mixed strategies as a later, separate topic about randomization, not as part of the same elimination procedure. I want to run a session this week that dislodges that picture before I state the formal definition of iterated strict dominance with mixed strategies allowed, and I want the students to feel the elimination procedure as something they themselves are driven to perform in order to predict each other, rather than as a definition I impose afterward.

Design a runnable in-class game for roughly twenty students and a 75-minute block. The game should be simple enough to fit its payoff matrices on one handout — I would prefer no more than three or four pure strategies per player — but structured so that pure-strategy elimination stalls partway and mixed-strategy elimination does real work: at least one pure strategy should be strictly dominated by a randomization over other pure strategies while no single pure strategy dominates it, and after that strategy is removed, the same kind of reasoning should remove at least one further strategy. Give me the rules, the materials, and a payoff scheme that does not require real money (course points, candy, or standing in the class are all fine), along with a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the formal notions — pure versus mixed dominance, the iterated elimination procedure, and the epistemic assumptions about common knowledge of rationality that each round of elimination is quietly using. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how I should handle it, and be frank about which feature of the formal idea the game cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in the wrap-up. I am not looking for a lecture or a described example; the students should run into the problem through the rules and incentives before it is named.
