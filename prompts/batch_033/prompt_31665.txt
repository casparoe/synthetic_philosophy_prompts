Suppose Jeremy Bentham, late in his life, were presented with a detailed and technically credible account of the project David Pearce calls the hedonistic imperative: the proposal to use genetic engineering, pharmacology, and other biotechnologies to phase out the capacity for suffering throughout the living world, in humans and non-human animals alike, replacing pain with graded signals of well-being so that no sentient being ever again experiences anything worse than mild discomfort. Assume Bentham is persuaded that the technologies would work. The question is what his philosophical verdict would be, not whether he could have anticipated the science.

To anchor your answer in Bentham's actual commitments, work through at least the following:

1. Value theory. Bentham was a hedonist about value: pleasure and the absence of pain are the only intrinsic goods, and pain and the loss of pleasure the only intrinsic evils. Would he see the abolition of suffering as the direct, even obvious, fulfillment of his principle of utility, or would he resist it because his calculus presupposes pain as a standing possibility against which pleasure is measured?

2. Motivation and sanctions. Bentham held that pleasure and pain are the springs of action, and his legal and political program ran on sanctions — physical, political, moral, and religious — that work by threatening pain or promising pleasure. If biotechnology removes the capacity for severe suffering, what happens to the machinery of sanctions? Would Bentham treat this as a welcome simplification of the problem of governance, or as the destruction of the only reliable levers for producing socially useful conduct?

3. Means and ends. Bentham's own tools were codes, punishments, inspection, publicity, and institutional design. Would he regard direct biological intervention as a natural extension of his reformist project, or as a confusion of categories — an attempt to alter the subject when the proper object of reform is circumstances, laws, and incentives? Does his well-known insistence that animal suffering matters morally push him toward endorsing the project for non-human animals, or did he see the relief of animal suffering as a task for law and custom rather than for the modification of organisms?

4. The flatness worry. Would Bentham be moved by the concern that a life without suffering would lose its spur to exertion and achievement, or that beings incapable of anguish would be, in some important sense, diminished? Does anything in his conception of happiness as a surplus of pleasure over pain suggest that he would, or would not, take that worry seriously?

5. Scope. Pearce's project is intended for all sentient life, including future generations and non-human animals. Bentham's utilitarianism was universalistic in principle but practical in focus, aimed largely at the legal arrangements of his own society. Would he extend the imperative across species and generations, or would he impose limits that Pearce would reject?
