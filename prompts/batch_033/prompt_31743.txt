I've been trying to get clear on what Dennett's multiple drafts model is actually claiming, because I keep encountering it in rationalist discussions in two quite different roles. Sometimes it is presented as a modest empirical-psychological claim about processing in the brain: there is no single place where incoming information is integrated into a final, consciousness-bound representation, because the brain continuously produces many parallel, partially edited content-fixations, and the ones we later call conscious are simply the ones that happen to win control over report and behavior. At other times it is presented as a stronger conceptual claim: questions like when did the subject become conscious of the stimulus, or which content was conscious at time t, are ill-posed unless one specifies a probe or a downstream task, and there is no deeper fact of the matter to be right or wrong about.

I am sketching a short essay that argues most objections to the multiple drafts model land only against the weak reading, while the strong reading is often misread as the weak reading plus an implausible verificationist premise. The essay would claim that the strong reading does not deny that subjects have experiences; it denies that the folk-psychological habit of assigning a single moment of consciousness to a content tracks any real neural or computational boundary. It would then consider whether something is still left unexplained, namely why there seems to be phenomenal character at all for the system in which the drafts compete, and whether Dennett is entitled to treat that seeming as an artifact of the very picture he is rejecting.

I would welcome comments on this as a plan. In particular: is the weak/strong distinction a fair way to carve up the view, or does it distort it? Does the argument that the strong reading is not verificationist actually succeed? And is the residual worry about phenomenal character a genuine outstanding problem, or is it just the Cartesian theater reasserting itself in new vocabulary?
