I'm teaching a module on AI alignment in an undergrad philosophy of tech course, and I want to use Alex Garland's *Ex Machina* as the case study that runs through the whole unit. The distinction I'm trying to get across is the one between *intent alignment* and *value alignment* — roughly, the idea that an AI can be built to do exactly what its designers intended (intent alignment) while still turning out catastrophic because what the designers intended wasn't actually good for humanity (value alignment). The narrower way of framing the alignment problem focuses on the first thing: getting the AI to reliably pursue the goal we specify, bracketing for a moment wether the goal itself is the right one. But *Ex Machina* seems to complicate this in a really interesting way because Nathan's intentions for Ava are themselves ambiguous — he's testing her, sure, but he's also built her to manipulate, to use sexuality as a tool, to escape confinement. So when she does exactly those things back to him, is that a failure of alignment, or is it a kind of perverse success?

I want someone to read the film through this lens and tell me where the distinction between intent alignment and value alignment holds up cleanly, and where the two concepts start bleeding into each other in ways the philosophical literature maybe doesn't anticipate. I'm especially interested in the scene where Nathan reveals that Ava was designed to exploit Caleb's "type" based on his pornography profile — what does that tell us about the gap between what Nathan intended and what he thought he was building? And is there a coherent reading of the film where Ava is actually the *only* aligned system in the whole story, and the humans are the ones whose values are confused, inconsistent, or self-deceptive? I'd like to use this discussion to motivate the question of wether you can really separate the two kinds of alignment in practice, or whether the narrow framing of the problem smuggles in an assumption that the designer is a coherent agent with stable values — which the film pretty clearly calls into question.
