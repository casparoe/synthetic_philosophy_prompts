I'm taking a bioethics seminar this semester on the ethics of human enhancement, and we've spent the last few weeks trying to get a grip on the therapy/enhancement distinction. My professor keeps insisting that the distinction is philosophically suspect—that it either collapses under pressure or ends up doing normative work it can't justify—but I find myself with strong intuitions about particular cases that seem to track something like it. The trouble is, when I try to extract a principle from those intuitions, everything falls apart. I discussed this with a friend in the course, and she suggested I just write down my verdicts on a range of cases and see whether a coherent view can be reconstructed from them—or whether I need to abandon some of my gut reactions. I'd appreciate your help with that project.

Here's where I've landed on particular cases. (1) Administering insulin to a diabetic: clearly therapy, no question. (2) LASIK surgery for someone with myopia: I want to say this is therapy too, even though it's reshaping the cornea to a state it was never in naturally—something about it restoring normal visual function feels different from enhancement. (3) Prescribing Adderall to someone with a diagnosed case of ADHD: therapy. (4) A college student without ADHD taking Adderall to pull an all-nighter before exams: this feels like enhancement, and I'm uneasy about it. (5) Human growth hormone for a child with a diagnosed deficiency: therapy. (6) Human growth hormone for a short-but-healthy child whose parents just want him to be taller: enhancement, and I'm against it. (7) Breast reconstruction after a mastectomy: therapy. (8) Breast augmentation for purely cosmetic reasons: enhancement. (9) A cochlear implant for a child born deaf: I genuinely don't know how to classify this, and my intuition flips depending on how the case is framed—as restoring a sensory capacity the child never had, or as imposing a normative standard of hearing. (10) Drinking a cup of coffee to feel more alert in the morning: this is clearly some kind of cognitive enhancement, but I can't bring myself to think there's anything wrong with it—which makes me wonder whether the therapy/enhancement line is really tracking permissibility at all, or whether I'm just inconsistent. (11) A beta-blocker taken by a concert pianist without an anxiety disorder, solely to prevent hand tremors during a performance: this feels like enhancement, but I'm less bothered by it than I am by the Adderall case, and I can't articulate why. (12) A vaccine that not only prevents a disease but boosts the immune system beyond the typical range: this one throws me, because prevention usually feels like medicine, but "beyond typical range" sounds like enhancement.

Is there a principle that makes sense of most or all of these verdicts? If some of them have to be revised or abandoned to get a coherent view, which ones would you overturn and why? And what does the resulting account say about a case I haven't listed: germline genetic engineering to increase a future child's IQ from a predicted 100 to a predicted 115?
