I'm preparing for a metaethics seminar and want to pressure-test my expressivist view before I present. Please act as a senior philosopher who works on the semantics of normative language — someone sympathetic to expressivism but who knows exactly where its weak points are. Think carefully and work through the tensions systematically rather than giving a quick verdict.

Here are my commitments:

(1) Expressivism about moral language: moral sentences like "stealing is wrong" do not express beliefs or represent moral facts; they express non-cognitive attitudes — roughly, disapproval of stealing.

(2) Compositionality: the meaning of a complex sentence is a function of the meanings of its constituent parts and their mode of combination.

(3) Moral sentences embed unproblematically in complex sentences. "If stealing is wrong, then getting your little brother to steal is wrong" is perfectly meaningful, and "stealing is wrong" means the same thing in that antecedent as it does standing alone.

(4) When a sentence appears in the antecedent of a conditional, it is not asserted. If I say "if P then Q," I have not thereby asserted P, nor have I expressed whatever attitude P would express on its own.

(5) Modus ponens is valid for moral arguments. From "stealing is wrong" and "if stealing is wrong, then getting your little brother to steal is wrong," I can validly conclude that getting your little brother to steal is wrong.

I'm worried these five cannot all be true together, but I'm not sure exactly where the tension lies and how severe it is. Go through these and tell me which pairs are in tension, which only look like they are, and what the least costly revision would be. If there's a standard name for the problem I'm running into, tell me — but I'm more interested in the diagnosis than the label.
