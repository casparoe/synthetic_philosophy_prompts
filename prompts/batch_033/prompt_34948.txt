I've spent years reading rationalist blogs and forums, so the Dutch book argument for probabilism is something I've absorbed rather than studied formally. Recently I tried to write the argument out in my own words to see exactly what it assumes, and the reconstruction I ended up with seems to prove far too much: it appears to make it rationally mandatory to accept fair gambles at arbitrary stakes, which would condemn any coherent Bayesian who declines to bet their savings on a fair coin. I don't believe that conclusion, and I don't think the Dutch book argument is a fraud, but I can't find the step where my reconstruction goes wrong.

Here is the argument as I've reconstructed it.

(1) The Dutch book theorem is correct: any assignment of degrees of belief that violates the probability axioms admits a finite set of bets, each with nonnegative expected value according to those degrees of belief, whose net payoff is negative in every possible state.

(2) The Dutch book argument draws a normative conclusion from this theorem. An agent with incoherent degrees of belief regards each bet in the book as fair or favorable, and so is rationally committed to accepting each of them. Otherwise the bookie could not ensure the bets are taken and the sure loss would never materialize. The agent is therefore committed to accepting the whole package; and since the package loses in every state, the agent's degrees of belief are irrational.

(3) The principle that licenses the move from regarding each bet as fair or favorable to being committed to accepting the whole package cannot be restricted to incoherent agents. Whether you are committed to accepting a bet depends on your evaluation of that bet, not on whether some other bet you also accept happens to combine with it into a sure loss. So the principle is general: if you regard each bet in a finite set as fair or favorable, you are rationally required to accept the entire set.

(4) But applied to a coherent agent, this principle is absurd. Take a coherent Bayesian with degree of belief one-half in each of two independent fair coins. She regards an even-money bet on each coin as fair. By the principle in (3), she is rationally required to accept any package of such bets at any stake, including a package that risks her entire savings for zero expected monetary gain.

(5) Yet it is not rationally required to stake your savings on fair coin flips. Declining fair gambles is permissible; for an agent with diminishing marginal utility for money, declining large fair gambles is positively required.

(6) So the general principle in (3) is false, and with it the inference in (2). The Dutch book argument therefore proves too much: the very move that makes the sure loss irrational also makes ordinary gambling-aversion irrational. But the argument is surely tracking something real about probabilism.

I would most like to keep (1) and the conclusion that probabilism is a genuine norm of rationality. Which premise or step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If the problem is that I have misstated what the Dutch book argument needs to assume, I want to know what the correct assumption is and whether it still delivers probabilism.
