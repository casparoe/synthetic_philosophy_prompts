I'm writing to ask you to help me settle something that came up in a long argument I had last night. A friend and I spent hours going back and forth about Dennett's three stances, and I realized I'm not at all sure what I actually think about the ontology of real patterns — whether there's a fact of the matter about which stance is "correct" in a given case, or whether the whole apparatus is just a pragmatic taxonomy of explanatory strategies with no deeper metaphysical payoff.

The argument went like this. My friend started by pressing me on a simple case: a chess program running on a laptop. When I predict its next move by reasoning about its programming (the design stance), and my friend predicts it by attributing strategic goals (the intentional stance), and we both get the same prediction right, is one of us more correct about what the system *really* is? I said no — they're both real patterns, and the question of which is "more real" is a bad question. My friend pushed back: what if the laptop is running on a substrate we built to be radically interpretable, so that the intentional stance works perfectly, but there's also a complete physical description we could consult? Is there any fact of the matter about which description carves nature at its joints? I said no, but I could feel myself losing confidence.

Then my friend raised what I thought were harder cases. Consider a slime mold that solves a maze to reach food. The design stance (it was shaped by natural selection for foraging efficiency) and the intentional stance (it "wants" the food and "navigates" toward it) both yield good predictions. But the physical stance is harder to apply in practice, even though we know there's a physical story. Does the relative inapplicability of the physical stance — the fact that it's computationally intractable for us — mean the design or intentional stance captures something more real about the system? Or is that just a fact about our cognitive limitations? I said it's about our limitations, but then I couldn't explain why I was so confident about the chess program case.

Next: a thermostat. The intentional stance is basically useless there — "the thermostat believes the room is too cold" gets you nothing that "the bimetallic strip bends at this temperature" doesn't give you more precisely. But the design stance works fine. Is the thermostat a case where the intentional stance fails to pick out a real pattern, or is it just a very simple intentional system whose beliefs are so impoverished that the stance isn't worth adopting? I lean toward the former — I think there's just no real intentional pattern there — but my friend said that if I go that route, I owe an account of where the line is, and that line is going to look arbitrary.

Then we got to the truly hard one: a person with a delusion. Suppose someone has Capgras syndrome and believes their spouse has been replaced by an impostor. The intentional stance says: attribute the belief and predict behavior accordingly. But the design stance — understood broadly as the stance that attributes subpersonal mechanisms — says: there's a dissociation between the face-recognition system and the affective response system, and that's what's doing the causal work. Are these competing explanations, or are they descriptions at different levels that somehow coexist? And if the intentional stance systematically fails to predict the person's behavior (because the delusion doesn't integrate with their other beliefs in the way rational belief-desire psychology would predict), does that mean the intentional stance is picking out a pattern that's only partially real, or a pattern that's real but locally disrupted by a design-level malfunction? My instinct is to say both stances are picking out real patterns, but the intentional pattern is fragmentary in a way that the design pattern isn't, and I don't know how to make that precise in Dennett's framework.

My friend's final challenge was this: suppose we encounter an alien artifact whose behavior we can predict perfectly using the intentional stance, and also perfectly using the physical stance, but the two stances attribute wildly different causal histories to the same behavior — the intentional stance says the artifact moved left because it wanted to avoid a predator, while the physical stance says it moved left because of a particular cascade of particle interactions. Are these two descriptions of the same real pattern, or are they picking out different patterns? And if the intentional stance is tracking a pattern that's real, does that pattern's reality depend on the fact that there's some physical-level description (even if we don't know it) that would underwrite it? I found myself wanting to say yes, but that seems to make the physical stance metaphysically fundamental in a way that undermines the whole point of the real-patterns view.

So here's what I'm asking. I've laid out my reactions to these cases — the chess program, the slime mold, the thermostat, the Capgras patient, and the alien artifact. I want to know whether there's a principled way of drawing the distinction between cases where the intentional stance tracks a real pattern and cases where it's just a useful fiction, and if so, what that principle is. I also want to know whether my verdicts, taken together, commit me to something I haven't noticed — for instance, to the claim that the physical stance is metaphysically privileged after all, or to the claim that there's no fact of the matter about which stances are "correct" in any given case. If my verdicts can't all be saved, which ones should I give up, and what does the resulting view look like when applied to cases I haven't considered?
