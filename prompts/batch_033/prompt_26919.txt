A friend walked me through this at the pub and I can't shake it. It seems to prove that a teacher can never give a surprise exam, which is absurd — I've sat enough of them myself. The argument runs like this:

(1) On Monday, a teacher announces: "There will be exactly one exam next week, Monday through Friday, and it will be a surprise: you will not be in a position to know, the night before, that the exam is the next day." The students trust the teacher completely and know the teacher never lies.

(2) Suppose the exam were on Friday. By Thursday evening, the students would know that no exam has occured Monday–Thursday, leaving only Friday. They would then be in a position to know the exam is on Friday. But that contradicts the surprise condition. So the exam cannot be on Friday.

(3) Now suppose the exam were on Thursday. By Wednesday evening, the students would know that no exam has occurred Monday–Wednesday. They also know, from (2), that Friday is ruled out. So only Thursday remains. They would be in a position to know the exam is on Thursday, contradicting the surprise condition. So the exam cannot be on Thursday.

(4) The same reasoning eliminates Wednesday, then Tuesday, then Monday. Every day is ruled out. The teacher's announcement cannot be satisfied.

(5) So a surprise exam, as announced, is logically impossible.

(6) But teachers do give surprise exams. Students are genuinely surprised. So the argument proves too much — it 'proves' that something actual is impossible.

I'm not asking whether the students are being pedantic or whether real teachers are less than perfectly reliable. I want to know: if we grant the idealisations — perfect logic, perfect trust — does the argument still fail? And if so, which step is the culprit? I've heard people say the problem is that eliminating Friday undercuts the students' grounds for trusting the announcement, so the whole chain collapses. Others say the surprise condition isn't the sort of thing you can reason backwards about because it's self-referential in a funny way. And some say the reasoning is fine but the teacher can still give the exam because the students' knowledge isn't closed under the deduction used in step (3). I want a diagnosis that actually locates the mistake, not just a hand-wave about 'real life is messier'. And tell me what giving up that step costs — does it mean we have to revise how we think about knowledge, or about the logic of public announcements, or about backwards induction?
