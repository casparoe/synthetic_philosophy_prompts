I chair the Rules Committee of a university faculty senate. Each spring the senate elects a six-member executive committee, and when more than two candidates stand for a seat the current rule is a plurality vote with a runoff if no candidate reaches forty percent. The committee agrees this rule has to go, but we are split three ways. One faction wants instant-runoff voting, on the ground that it uses the full ranking and guarantees a majority winner; a second wants approval voting, on the ground that it is simple, cheap, and less likely to encourage tactical voting than ranked methods; a third wants a Condorcet method — ranked ballots, electing whichever candidate beats each rival in head-to-head comparisons — on the ground that it best captures majority preference. A senior colleague keeps invoking the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem to short-circuit the debate: under the standard assumptions, with three or more candidates, voters free to hold any complete ranking, no dictator, and no candidate excluded in advance, no rule that turns those rankings into a single winner is strategy-proof, so every ranked method on the table can be gamed. The approval-voting faction replies that the theorem is stated for rules defined on complete ranked preferences, and approval voting asks voters a different kind of question, so the theorem does not settle its status — though they grant that approval voting, too, can be manipulated in some scenarios.

Please draft the short decision memo I will circulate before our next meeting. It should recommend one concrete voting rule for multi-candidate senate elections, with enough detail about the ballot and the counting procedure that the senate office could implement it, and it should explain what the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem does and does not imply for our choice. I want the memo to treat the strongest consideration against the recommended rule as a serious one: if the recommendation is to stop treating strategy-proofness as the decisive criterion, say why the appeal to the theorem is not merely a technicality and then why it should not carry the day. The bottom line should be a rule the committee could adopt, not a survey of options.
