I write a column for a general-audience science-and-ideas outlet, and I'm working on an explainer about Dennett's three stances — the physical stance, the design stance, and the intentional stance — and his claim that the patterns these stances pick out are real enough that taking the intentional stance toward something is not just a cute fiction or loose talk. My editor wants me to not bury the lede, but I know that if I open with the abstract payoff my readers will bounce. So I need you to plan the explanation for me, not write it out: the order of ideas and what depends on what, one carrying example and what it must have and must not have, the misconceptions to expect and exactly where in the sequence to head each one off, what to leave out at this level and say so, and one check at the end that would show a reader actually got it.

The audience is smart but non-philosophical, and the piece is maybe 1,500 words or a 12-minute video. For the carrying example I was leaning toward a chess engine, maybe a thermostat, but I'm open — I just want to know what the example has to do for me. I'm a little worried a thermostat invites "but obviously it doesn't really believe anything." The misconceptions I'm already scared of are: "so the intentional stance means it literally has beliefs and desires?", "the design stance assumes an actual designer, so it's a bit creationist?", and "the physical stance is the only real level and the other two are just useful fictions." I cant quite see how to sequence things so the real-patterns payoff lands without sounding like I'm backtracking on all three stances. Also tell me what I should openly refuse to explain at this level. For the final check, maybe a fresh case or a question would be better than just asking readers to label something.
