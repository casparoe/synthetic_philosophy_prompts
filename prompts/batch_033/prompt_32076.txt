I keep running into the same problem when I read discussions of the limits of free speech: people invoke Mill's harm principle as though it settled the matter, but they plainly mean different things by it. One person treats it as ruling out only paternalistic and moralistic coercion; another treats it as a demanding causal test that a given act of speech must be shown to produce concrete injury before it may be restricted; a third treats it as licensing restriction whenever enough people are genuinely distressed. I want to use the principle to discipline exactly these disputes about legal restrictions on speech in a liberal society, and before I read any more theories or applications I want the specification on its own terms: what any adequate theory or interpretation of the harm principle has to deliver. Write the desiderata. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement — for instance, the distinction between offense and harm and why it cannot be collapsed; the difference between self-regarding and other-regarding conduct and why not every indirect effect may be allowed to erase it; the distinction between harm and moral wrong, so the principle does not quietly become legal moralism; the problem of risk, cumulative, and statistical harm; the question whether the principle governs only state coercion or also social pressure and censure; whether it states a necessary condition only or also a sufficient one; the competence clause for children and those incapable of self-government; consent to harm; and the baseline against which a harm is measured. Where you think two desiderata cannot be jointly satisfied — I am thinking especially of the tension between taking indirect and cumulative harms seriously and still excluding moralistic or paternalistic restrictions, since almost any conduct affects someone somewhere — say so, and say which side you would give up first and why. Mark each requirement as non-negotiable or as a preference of a particular camp, and tell me which single requirement does the most work in knocking out the standard positions. Then, briefly, how the main rival readings (a strict libertarian reading, an offense-principle reading, and a moral-wrongs or legal-moralist reading, at least) do against the list; keep that scorecard short, since I want the criteria themselves to carry the weight.

If the request is misposed — for example, if you think the harm principle is too indeterminate or too contested to generate a non-question-begging list of requirements, or that the harm/offense distinction is itself one of the things a theory must argue for rather than a datum it must respect — tell me that directly and say what should be asked instead. I would rather have the framing corrected than get a polished list that papers over the problem.
