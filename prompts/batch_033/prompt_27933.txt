I help set the research agenda at a foundation that funds conceptual work on AI value alignment, and we are putting together a two-year program on a question we think is systematically under-served: not how to align an AI with human values in the abstract, but whose values an aligned system should serve, and how. We can fund the equivalent of four or five full-time researchers across philosophy, political theory, and formal value aggregation. We need a short strategy memo that identifies the open questions at the intersection of value aggregation and liberal neutrality that are most worth prioritizing, and that defends an ordering among them.

Here is the angle I want the memo to take seriously, though you are free to argue against it. My working view is that the liberal neutrality tradition is a poor fit for the AI case if it is imported wholesale, because its central constraint was designed for the coercive state: a state may not justify its laws by appeal to the superiority of some conception of the good. An aligned AI, by contrast, is not a coercive state; it is a default-acting agent embedded in everyday life, sometimes representing or assisting one person, sometimes needing to act where the user is silent, and sometimes acting on behalf of a collective such as a firm, a public service, or a heterogeneous user base. At the same time, the standard aggregation toolkit from social choice and welfare economics was built mainly for voters and discrete policy choices, not for a single system that must continuously infer, aggregate, and act on the values of many persons, including people with conflicting values within a single person, future and possible people, non-human animals, children, and people who never opted in. My suspicion is that the most interesting questions lie where those two literatures fail together, but I want your own judgment about where the field should put its effort.

Please give me a memo that states each candidate question precisely enough that two researchers would agree on what would count as progress; says why existing positions in political philosophy and value aggregation leave it open; explains which questions are upstream of which others; assesses tractability for philosophers rather than ML researchers or economists; and names at least one question that looks pressing but that we should deliberately deprioritize. I am less interested in a list than in the defended ordering: the criteria you use, how you trade off importance against tractability and neglectedness, and what philosophical or empirical developments over the next two years would cause you to re-rank. Do not bother with citations; I will supply the literature myself.
