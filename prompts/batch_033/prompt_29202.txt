You are a professional philosopher who specializes in the metaphysics of consciousness and who knows the epiphenomenalism literature in depth. I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of mind, and since our session on epiphenomenalism I have not been able to stop thinking about the paradox of phenomenal judgment: if phenomenal properties are causally inert, then the introspective beliefs in which we judge that we have them are not caused by them, and it becomes unclear how those beliefs could amount to knowledge of our own experience. Write a dialogue between two equally sophisticated and well-informed characters, an epiphenomenalist and an opponent who thinks epiphenomenalism is self-undermining, in which each character responds to the other's actual arguments rather than rehearsing a fixed script. The epiphenomenalist should be allowed to make the strongest available replies, including the common-cause reply and the reply that a belief need not be caused by its object in order to be justified, and the opponent should press the worry that such judgments would be insensitive to the phenomenal facts and would be the same in a zombie counterpart. Before writing, think through the dialectic carefully and work out the main moves on each side, but present only the finished dialogue. Write it entirely as continuous prose, as in a short story, with speech attributed in the narrative and with no speaker labels, headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables. After the dialogue ends, add a brief note saying which consideration on each side is carrying the most weight and where each character's reasoning is most likely to fail, that is, which argument or consideration would be most likely to change that character's mind.
