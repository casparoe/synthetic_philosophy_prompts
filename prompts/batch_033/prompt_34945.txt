So I roll a fair six-sided die. "If I roll this die, it won't land six." Probability: 5/6. Obviously.

Same die, rolled under a cup where I can't see it. "If it landed even, it landed six." Probability: 1/3. Equally obvious.

Now: "It's not the case that if I roll the die, it will land six." I'd say probability 5/6 — same as the first one. The negation of a conditional just seems to be the conditional with the negated consequent, probabilistically at least.

Here's where it gets messy. "Either if I roll the die it lands six, or if I roll it it lands odd." That disjunction feels like a logical truth to me — it's gotta have probability 1. But if each conditional's probability is the conditional probability (1/6 and 1/2), the disjunction can't be higher than 2/3 unless they overlap in some weird way. But they don't overlap — six and odd are mutually exclusive outcomes. So something's gotta give.

Then there's nested ones: "If I roll the die, then if it lands even, it lands six." I want this to be 1/3 — same as "if I roll even, it lands six." But I don't even know what the inner conditional means when it's embedded like that. Does it have truth conditions? If not, how am I assigning it a probability?

And one more: "If it rains tomorrow the picnic is cancelled, and if it doesn't rain the picnic isn't cancelled." This conjunction seems perfectly meaningful and has a probability (high if the organizer's sensible, low if they're flakey). But I can't get that probability just from the two conditional probabilities, can I? They have different antecedents.

I know about Adams's thesis — that the probability of a conditional is the conditional probability — and I know Lewis showed it leads to triviality if conditionals express propositions. But I can't tell whether I should (a) keep Adams's thesis and deny conditionals express propositions, (b) keep conditionals as propositions and give up Adams's thesis, or (c) something else. What's the most defensible package here, and which of my verdicts does it force me to abandon?
