I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind, and I've been working through the literature on collective mentality and group minds. Recently I've been stuck on an argument that I constructed myself, which now strikes me as proving far too much — but I can't locate the error with confidence, and I'd like your help diagnosing it.

The argument concerns the possibility that an ant colony might possess genuine mental states — beliefs, desires, perhaps even consciousness — in virtue of the complex functional organization of the colony as a whole, even though no individual ant has anything we would recognize as a mind. This is, of course, the idea Hofstadter explores through the character of Aunt Hillary in the ant fugue from *Gödel, Escher, Bach*. The argument I built runs as follows:

(1) If an ant colony has a mind, then the possession of a mind is a matter of exhibiting the right kind of functional organization — roughly, information integration, flexible goal-directed behavior, and adaptive responsiveness to a changing environment — since that is all a colony has going for it beyond the behavior of individual ants.

(2) But many systems exhibit functional organization of this kind: a market economy processes information and adapts to shocks, a bacterial biofilm coordinates movement and resource allocation, a human crowd at a sporting event exhibits emergent collective behavior that no individual planned, and the internet as a whole routes information and reorganizes itself in response to damage.

(3) If possession of a mind is a matter of functional organization, then by parity of reasoning, all of these systems have minds.

(4) That conclusion is unacceptable: we do not think economies or crowds are subjects of experience, and we do not think they have beliefs or desires in anything but a metaphorical sense.

(5) Therefore an ant colony does not have a mind.

(6) But a human brain is also a system of individually non-conscious parts (neurons) whose coordinated activity gives rise to mental states.

(7) So by the same reasoning that rules out ant-colony minds, we should conclude that human brains do not have minds — or else we must identify some principled difference between the two cases that does not simply beg the question by appealing to biological chauvinism.

Something has clearly gone wrong if the argument threatens to eliminate human minds. My question is: which premise or inferential step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I am interested in what the standard positions in philosophy of mind — functionalism, biological naturalism, panpsychism, eliminativism, and so on — would each identify as the misstep, and what each position has to sacrifice to block the conclusion. If you think the argument is valid and the real work is in choosing which premise to abandon, please price the alternatives.
