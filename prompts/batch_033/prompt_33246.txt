I'm writing a paper that evaluates counterfactual theories of causation (Lewis, Halpern and Pearl) against interventionist theories (Woodward), and I've realized I need to step back and ask what either kind of theory is supposed to do. Give me the desiderata: the full set of requirements that any adequate theory of causation must meet, with the case or consideration that motivates each, and mark which are non-negotiable constraints on any theory and which are parochial commitments of one research program. I am especially interested in whether any subset of the non-negotiable requirements is jointly unsatisfiable — that would be a finding in itself, and it's the part of the answer I most want to see.

The requirements should cover at minimum: actual causation (token-level, what caused what on a particular occasion), type causation (what it means for one kind of thing to cause another), and the relationship between the two. Include the standard problem cases — early and late preemption, symmetric overdetermination, double prevention, omissions, and switches rather than just pushes — but also address structural constraints: transitivity, the relationship between causation and counterfactual dependence, whether causation is a relation between events or between variables taking values, and whether a theory of causation must also serve as a theory of causal explanation or whether those can come apart. For each requirement, press on whether it really is a requirement or just a feature of the examples the literature happens to have fixated on.

Begin with the list itself, with no preface or commentary. Then give the case behind each requirement. Then address the joint-satisfiability question and identify which single requirement does the most to force a choice between the counterfactual and interventionist approaches.

Finally, tell me where your classification of requirements as negotiable versus non-negotiable is most likely to fail — which argument or consideration, if it went the other way, would rearrange the list.
