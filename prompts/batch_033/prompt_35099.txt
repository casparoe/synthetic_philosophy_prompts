I'm a PhD student writing up my thesis on phenomenal consciousness and I keep going back and forth on whether qualia are a coherent posit or whether Dennett's basically right that the whole notion is a mess. I thought I'd write down my gut reactions to the standard cases and see if there's a principle that gets all of them, or most.

Here are my verdicts:

1. Inverted spectrum (the classic one, not the behavioural version). It seems perfectly conceivable to me that someone could have their red-green qualia swapped while everything else — behaviour, functional role, discrimination capacities — stays the same. I don't think this shows qualia are non-physical, but I do think it shows they're something over and above the functional facts.

2. Mary's room. I think Mary learns something when she leaves the room. But I'm not convinced what she learns is a new fact about the world. It feels more like she acquires a new ability or a new way of representing facts she already knew. So I half-agree with Jackson and half-agree with Lewis.

3. Chase and Sanborn. You know the case — two coffee tasters, Chase says the taste of coffee has changed over the years, Sanborn says it tastes exactly the same but he's gone off it. I think Chase is right about his own experience and Sanborn is right about his. There's a real difference between a change in qualitative character and a change in one's liking of it. This seems to me to show that qualia and evaluative reactions are distinct.

4. Absent qualia / philosophical zombies. I can conceive of a zombie twin — somone physically identical to me but with no experience at all. But I strongly suspect this is only conceivability in the sense of ignorance, not in the sense that tells us anything about metaphysical possibility. So: conceivable, but I doubt they're possible.

5. Blindsight. I think blindsight shows that there can be visual information processing without phenomenal visual experience. That seems to support the idea that qualia are a real extra ingredient, not just information processing in disguise.

6. The "what it's like to be a bat" question. I think there's definitely something it's like to be a bat. But I also think there's probably something it's like to be a thermostat, if we're being honest — I just don't think that's a deep philosophical revelation, it's more a fact about how we apply the phrase "something it's like."

7. Dennett's tasting-room case (the one where he keeps asking you to describe the taste of beer and you run out of things to say). I think Dennett's right that we can't introspect fine-grained qualia with any reliability, but I don't think that shows they don't exist. It shows our access to them is lousy.

So those are my verdicts. Is there a coherent position on qualia that gets all of them, or close? I'm particularly interested in what the resulting view says about whether qualia are a coherent posit at all — and which of my verdicts I'd have to drop if the answer is no. I'd also like to know what this position predicts about a case I haven't listed: someone who has perfect colour discrimination (they can distinguish all the same chips as a normal trichromat) but reports that colours don't look like anything to them — they just have a brute "this one's different from that one" judgement. Would my pattern of verdicts say this person is having colour qualia or not?
