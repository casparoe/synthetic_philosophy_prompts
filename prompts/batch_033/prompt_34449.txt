I retired from a career in physics a few years ago and have been spending my time reading philosophy, particularly decision theory and the foundations of probability. I have been trying to work out my own views on the Pasadena paradox and related puzzles about undefined expected values, and I would like a consistency check before I commit to writing anything up. Here is roughly where I have landed.

First, I am a committed expected value maximizer. I think the rational choice in any decision problem is the one that maximizes expected utility.

Second, I am not troubled by the St. Petersburg paradox. I accept that some gambles have infinite expected value, and I think we should simply be willing to pay any finite amount for them. Attempts to bound the utility function to avoid this strike me as ad hoc.

Third, I think the Pasadena game is a perfectly well-posed decision problem. The coin is fair, the payoffs are specified for every outcome, and there is no ambiguity about what might happen. Yet standard expected value theory cannot assign it a determinate value. This shows that expected value theory is incomplete as it stands.

Fourth, I think the source of the problem is specifically that the expected value series is conditionally convergent. The series converges to the natural logarithm of two, but because the convergence is conditional rather than absolute, the terms can be rearranged to yield any real number, or to diverge, by the Riemann rearrangement theorem. This means the sum is not well-defined independently of the order of summation.

Fifth, I think the right fix is to require absolute convergence for expected values. A gamble has a well-defined expected value only if the sum of probability-weighted utilities converges absolutely. When it does not, expected value theory is simply silent — the gamble has no expected value, and the question of what it is rational to pay has no answer within that framework.

Sixth, I think that when expected value theory is silent, we should fall back on other principles — specifically, stochastic dominance. If one gamble yields at least as good an outcome as another in every possible state and a strictly better outcome in at least one, then the first is rationally preferable, regardless of whether the expected values are defined.

Seventh, I find myself drawn to the idea that the natural ordering of states in the Pasadena game — by the number of coin flips, which reflects the actual temporal structure of the game — is not merely conventional. Under this ordering, the expected value is the natural logarithm of two, and I am tempted to say that this is the true value of the game, even though the series is only conditionally convergent.

Eighth, I also think that two gambles that differ only in how we label or order the states are the same gamble and should be evaluated identically. If you simply permute the labels on the outcomes, you have not created a new decision problem.

I have a nagging sense that some of these pull against each other — particularly the fifth, sixth, seventh, and eighth — but I cannot quite articulate the tensions clearly. I would like you to go through these commitments and identify which pairs are genuinely in tension, which are only apparently so, and how severe each tension is. If there are tensions, I would like to know what the cheapest revision would be — which commitment I should give up, and what I lose by doing so.
