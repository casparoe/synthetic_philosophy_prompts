I work at a mid-size AI lab and my team has been given a weirdly open-ended mandate: we have about five people and roughly two years to work on "machine ethics" — making sure our systems reason about ethical questions in a way that's defensible, or at least not catastrophic. The leadership isn't super specific about what that means, but they're serious about the resources. We're not an academic group; we can build prototypes, run experiments with LLMs, and collaborate with the product teams, but we're not going to be publishing in philosophy journals. Think of us as an applied research team embedded in an engineering org.

I've been reading the literature and it feels like there are a dozen different subproblems — value specification and reward misspecification, moral uncertainty (how should a system act when it's unsure which ethical framework is right?), top-down versus bottom-up approaches, the relationship between ethical reasoning and mechanistic interpretability, whether we should even be trying to get machines to "do ethics" rather than just making them corrigible and deferential, how to handle cases where reasonable humans disagree about the right thing to do, the problem of extrapolating from stated preferences to "true" preferences, and so on — and I can't do all of them. Some of these feel like they've been debated for twenty years without much to show for it; others feel like they might actually be tractable now that we have LLMs to work with.

So here's what I want: imagine you're a senior researcher who's been thinking about this for years. If you had to pick the three or four open problems in machine ethics that are most worth a small applied team's time — not the most philosophically interesting ones, but the ones where progress would actually change how we build systems — what would they be, and in what order? For each one, I want to know: why it's upstream of other problems (if we don't solve this, what else is blocked or moot?), how tractable it is for a team that can build things but isn't going to resolve deep philosophical debates, and what a concrete deliverable at the end of two years might look like — a prototype, a benchmark, a design pattern, a set of interventions that demonstrably change model behavior. Also tell me which problems you'd deliberately deprioritize even though they get a lot of airtime, and why you think they're a trap for a team like ours. I'm less interested in the list than in the reasoning behind the ordering — my manager is going to want to know why we picked these and not those, and "because it seemed important" won't cut it.
