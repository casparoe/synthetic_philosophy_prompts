I'm a novelist, currently writing a book from the point of view of a sapient octopus species, and last month at a convention I ended up trapped between two writer friends of mine who got into it after a panel on nonhuman minds. One, Priya, writes hard SF; the other, Marcus, writes mostly literary fiction but has read a lot of philosophy of mind. I took notes because its basically my whole book's problem, but I couldnt referee the argument then and I cant now. Here's roughly how it went.

Priya: If my octopus character stubs a tentacle, and the damage sets off signals that make her pull back, favor the arm, remember the spot, and later describe the thing as hurting, then she's in pain. It shouldn't matter that her blood is copper-based and her nervous system is spread through her arms. Pain is what pain does.

Marcus: That's too fast. You're listing the effects and then calling the thing pain, but those effects are exactly what could happen with no feeling at all. A robot vacuum backs away from a wall and nobody thinks it hurts. If you define pain by the job it does, you've defined away the part that makes pain matter, the awfulness of it.

Priya: The robot vacuum doesn't have the full profile. It doesn't learn from the bump, it doesn't weigh the damage against its other goals, it doesn't have anything like pain receptors feeding into a whole organized system. My octopus has all of that. Your reply only works if you assume the only way to get real pain is with the exact materials our own nervous systems use. That's like saying only creatures with hemoglobin have blood.

Marcus: I'm not saying only humans can hurt. I'm saying the wiring diagram doesn't settle what the inner state is. Her damage state could be some cold evaluative signal that does the same job but feels like nothing, or feels like an itch, or a sudden urge. The causal role underdetermines the feeling.

Priya: So what would settle it? If behavior, function, and whole-system organization aren't enough, then you can't know that your own mother feels pain either, because all you ever get from her is the causal role. Your view makes other minds unknowable.

Marcus: I might have to live with that. I infer your mother's pain partly because she's built like me and shares my evolutionary history. The octopus shares none of that, so the inference is much weaker.

Priya: That's just chauvinism with extra steps. Shared ancestry is a fact about us, not a fact about what pain is. If pain can only be built out of one kind of stuff, that's a strange, brittle picture of the mind. And it would make half of science fiction impossible, but that's not an argument.

Marcus: It's not chauvinism to notice that biology matters. A state isn't only its function; it's also what it's made of and how it got here. If pain is a natural kind, like water, then maybe its essence includes the underlying structure, and a different structure doing the same job is a different state, maybe one that deserves a different name.

Priya: Then what do you do with a person whose neurons get replaced one at a time by synthetic chips that do exactly the same signaling? If the person still says they hurt and acts the same, do you say the pain vanished at some point, or changed into a different state? Because the material changed completely.

Marcus: That's different. There's continuity, the state was never interrupted, and the later chips got recruited into a system that already had pain. I can say the state was preserved through the change. A newly built octopus, or a freshly switched-on copy, doesn't have that history.

Priya: So now it's not the material, it's the history and the pattern. That sounds like you've given up the material-essence view. If continuity matters, then what's doing the work is how the system is organized and how it got that way, which is exactly what I've been saying.

Marcus: Not quite. I'm saying the history matters only because of what it's a history of: a particular biological state being extended, not a different state being installed. That doesn't mean any old thing with the same wiring diagram gets the same feeling.

They went around like this until the bar closed. I honestly can't tell who's right about what, and it matters for the book: I want to know whether my octopus can be in pain in the same sense we are, and what would make me, or a reader, justified in thinking so. Could you go through the argument move by move and say who's right about which point, where each of them overreaches, and who's more right overall? Oh, and please answer in American English.
