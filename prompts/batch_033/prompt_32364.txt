I'm a graduate student in computer science, and I've been asked to lead a two-hour seminar for advanced undergraduates on the Church–Turing thesis—specifically, on its epistemic status: what kind of claim it is, what evidence supports it, and why it resists being settled as a theorem. The students have all taken a standard computability course, so they can define Turing machines, they know the halting problem is undecidable, and they've heard the thesis stated as "every effectively computable function is Turing-computable." But I've noticed, from talking to them, that their understanding tends to be shallow in a particular way: many treat it as a theorem they somehow missed the proof of, or conflate it with the fact that the halting problem is undecidable, or assume it's a claim about the physical limits of silicon computers. A few of the sharper ones have asked me whether it's even a meaningful claim—if "effective computability" is an informal notion, what would it even mean for the thesis to be false?

I don't want you to explain the thesis to me. I want a plan for how to structure the two hours: the order in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced and what depends on what, the one example or case study I should return to throughout to anchor the discussion, and the specific misconceptions the students are likely to bring or to form during the session, with the exact point in the sequence where I should anticipate and defuse each one. I also want to know what I should explicitly refuse to settle at this level—for instance, whether the physical Church–Turing thesis is a separate question I should bracket or whether it's inseparable from the main discussion—and one question I can pose at the end to check whether they've genuinely understood the epistemic peculiarity of the thesis rather than just learned to state it more carefully.
