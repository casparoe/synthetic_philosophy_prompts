I retired some years ago from a research career in the natural sciences and have recently been reading philosophy with the time and care I could not give it while running a laboratory. This term I am helping to teach a small advanced seminar on formal methods in philosophy, and the first problem set included a question on iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies. I am grading the set now and would like a careful second opinion on one answer before I return it.

The examination question read as follows:

"Consider the following two-player simultaneous-move game, with Row's payoff given first in each cell:

|       | L      | R      |
|-------|--------|--------|
| **T** | (3, 0) | (0, 1) |
| **M** | (1, 3) | (1, 0) |
| **B** | (0, 0) | (3, 2) |

(a) Perform iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies, where a strategy may be eliminated if it is strictly dominated by any pure or mixed strategy of the same player. Show the order of elimination and state which strategies survive.

(b) State which strategy profiles are rationalizable.

(c) Identify all pure-strategy Nash equilibria of the original game, and briefly say whether the result of your elimination is consistent with them."

The student's answer, reproduced in full, was:

"First I checked for pure-strategy dominance. For Row, T gives (3, 0), M gives (1, 1), and B gives (0, 3); no one of these vectors is componentwise greater than another, so no Row strategy is strictly dominated by another pure strategy. For Column, L gives (0, 3, 0) against T, M, and B, while R gives (1, 0, 2); again neither dominates the other. So no pure strategy is strictly dominated.

I then checked whether M is strictly dominated by a mixture of T and B. Let the mixture put probability p on T and 1 − p on B. Against L it yields 3p, and against R it yields 3(1 − p). To dominate M we need 3p > 1 and 3(1 − p) > 1, which give p > 1/3 and p < 2/3. Taking p = 1/2 gives (1.5, 1.5), which strictly dominates M's (1, 1). So M is eliminated.

With M removed, Column's L gives (0, 0) against the remaining rows, while R gives (1, 2); so L is strictly dominated by R and is eliminated. With only R left for Column, Row's T gives 0 and B gives 3; so T is strictly dominated by B and is eliminated. The survivor set is therefore {B} × {R}.

Checking best responses, (B, R) is the unique pure-strategy Nash equilibrium: B is Row's best response to R, and R is Column's best response to B.

One caution: I recall that once mixed strategies are allowed in the dominance test, the elimination procedure can become order-dependent and can delete strategies that belong to some Nash equilibrium, as weak-dominance elimination can. So I would be hesitant to conclude that the other profiles are not rationalizable; the elimination is suggestive rather than definitive."

I would be grateful for a second opinion. Please grade the answer as you would for this course: give it a numerical grade out of 10, and provide the specific comments that justify the grade — which steps earn credit, which are wrong or incomplete, and what the student should have said instead. I am looking for a point-by-point assessment rather than a general impression, and I would rather you not assume that I have already formed a judgment about where the answer goes wrong.
