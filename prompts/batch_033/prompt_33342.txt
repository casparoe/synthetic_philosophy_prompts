I cover physics and philosophy for a general-audience science magazine, and I'm working on a short piece about infinity — specifically the idea of completing infinitely many tasks in a finite stretch of time. While researching I kept running into Thomson's lamp, and one of the pieces I read summarized it in a way that felt a little too tidy to me. The author's version went like this: a lamp is off at noon. At half past noon you switch it on, at a quarter to one you switch it off, at seven and a half minutes to one you switch it on again, and so on, halving the interval each time, so that by one o'clock you have flipped the switch infinitely many times. Since the lamp can't be on at one o'clock (every time it's turned on, it's turned off later) and can't be off at one o'clock (every time it's turned off, it's turned on later), the article concluded that the lamp must end up both on and off at once, which is a contradiction. And the article said Thomson took this to show that supertasks — infinite sequences of actions completed in finite time — are logically impossible, full stop. I get that the thought experiment is supposed to be paradoxical, but the both-on-and-off part struck me as a sloppy way to put it, and the leap from this one lamp to all supertasks seemed like a lot. Before I repeat any of this in my explainer, can you help me sort out wich parts of that summary a philosopher working on infinity would accept, which parts they'd wince at, and whether the general conclusion about supertasks actually follows from the accurate parts?
