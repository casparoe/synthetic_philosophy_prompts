I'm putting together a position paper on consciousness for a philosophy of mind seminar, and I want to check whether my current views form a coherent package before I commit to writing them up. I've been reading the Integrated Information Theory literature — Tononi, Koch, and the philosophical commentary around it — and I find myself pulled in several directions at once. Here's roughly where I've landed:

(a) I think IIT is the most promising scientific theory of consciousness currently on the table. The idea that consciousness is identical to a maximum of integrated information seems to capture something deep about why some physical systems are conscious and others aren't.

(b) I am a physicalist: all facts about consciousness are fixed by the physical facts. I don't think there are any non-physical properties or substances involved.

(c) I think the hard problem of consciousness is real and won't be dissolved by conceptual analysis alone. There's a genuine explanatory gap, and we need a theory that bridges it rather than explaining it away.

(d) I think that if IIT is approximately correct, then rudimentary consciousness is vastly more widespread than common sense suggests — there is something it is like to be a small grid of logic gates with the right pattern of connectivity, even if that something is vanishingly faint.

(e) I reject the idea that there is literally something it is like to be a completely inactive system. So I think Φ must exceed some non-zero threshold for consciousness, though I'm not sure what principled basis there is for setting it at any particular value.

(f) The exclusion postulate — that only the complex with maximum Φ over a given spatiotemporal grain is conscious — strikes me as plausible when applied to non-overlapping systems. But I'm not sure how to extend it to overlapping systems (e.g., when two high-Φ complexes share some nodes), and I suspect it may need revision there.

(g) I think the zombie argument is unsound: the conceivability of zombies does not establish their metaphysical possibility, and I think we can explain why zombies seem conceivable without granting that they are possible.

I'd like you to go through these and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension, which only look like they are, and how severe each tension is. For the genuine tensions, tell me what the cheapest resolution would be and what it would cost. I'm especially interested in whether (a) and (b) can really sit together, given that IIT's methodology starts from phenomenological axioms rather than from the physical facts. And I'd like to know whether (d), (e), and (f) form a stable triad — I have a nagging feeling that if I accept (d) and (e), I'm committed to something that makes (f) harder to hold, but I can't quite articulate it.
