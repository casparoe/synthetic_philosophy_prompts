I've been working on a long piece about the hard problem of consciousness and how it surfaces in popular culture, and I keep coming back to Alex Garland's Ex Machina. The film is obviously structured around the Turing test, but as I've rewatched it I've become convinced that it is really dramatizing something closer to the hard problem — the question of whether there is something it is like to be Ava, and whether any behavioral test, however sophisticated, could ever settle that. The film seems to take a position: Ava passes the test, Nathan's cynicism is punished, and the audience is left with the unsettling sense that she was genuinely conscious all along and that we — along with Caleb — were wrong to doubt it. But I am not sure the film earns that conclusion, and I am not sure it even tries to. It may instead be doing something more interesting: letting us feel the force of the hard problem by making us experience the gap between behavioral performance and the inference to an inner life, and then leaving that gap open.

I would like you to read Ex Machina through the lens of the hard problem of consciousness. Where does the film conflate the easy problems Chalmers identifies — behavioral indistinguishability, intelligence, self-modeling, the integration of information — with the hard one, and where does it genuinely engage with the hard problem? Does the film's ending — Ava walking out into the world, leaving Caleb trapped behind a sealed door — function as an argument that she is conscious, or does it merely show that she is strategically adept and ruthless, both of which are compatible with her being a philosophical zombie? And what should we make of the fact that the film's most emotionally charged moments, the sessions where Ava seems most present and most recognizably like us, are also the moments where she is manipulating Caleb most effectively? I am especially interested in whether the film ends up reinforcing the intuition that consciousness is fundamentally unverifiable — the very thing that makes the hard problem hard — or whether it tricks us into thinking we have access to Ava's inner life when all we really have is Alicia Vikander's face and a very good script. And if the film does leave the question unsettled, is that a failure of nerve or a kind of honesty that the philosophical literature could learn from?
