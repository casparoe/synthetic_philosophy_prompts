I recently read a long-form profile of the effective altruism movement in a general-interest magazine, and one paragraph has been nagging at me because it compresses an entire critical literature into a few confident sentences. The author summarizes the movement's problems as follows: "Effective altruism began as the idea that a person can do an enormous amount of good by taking a high-paying job in finance or tech and donating a large share of the income to the most cost-effective charities, which the movement picks by calculating lives saved per dollar. Critics make three main complaints. First, the institutional critique: EA treats global poverty as a problem of underfunded programs rather than of broken political and economic institutions, and it therefore systematically ignores structural change—policy reform, labor organizing, advocacy. Second, earning to give is a kind of moral laundering: it tells people that a harmful or ethically dubious career is fine as long as the salary is donated, and the movement has never seriously answered the charge that this rationalizes doing harm now for good later. Third, measurability bias: because EA demands quantitative evidence of impact, it only funds interventions with clean, countable outputs, and therefore excludes anything whose value cannot be measured—movement building, cultural change, basic research, political work. What remains is a portfolio of marginal, easily quantified fixes that misses the real causes of suffering."

This matches the loose impression I had absorbed—that EA is technocratic, numbers-obsessed, and politically naive—but I also know that magazine profiles tend to flatten movements into the version their critics describe, and I do not know enough to sort the fair from the caricature. Could you go through that paragraph sentence by sentence and tell me which claims a careful critic of effective altruism would stand behind, which a thoughtful defender would reject as misstated, and which are overstatements of something weaker but defensible? I would especially like to know where the paragraph conflates distinct objections, where it treats a live debate as settled or an objection as unanswered when the movement has in fact responded, and whether the closing claim about missing the real causes of suffering follows from the accurate parts once the inaccurate ones are corrected.
