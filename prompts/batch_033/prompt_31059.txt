I've been rereading I Am a Strange Loop and arguing with a friend about what Hofstadter's claim that the "I" is a self-referential symbol actually commits you to. The argument started as a side fight about whether I should be scared of a Star Trek transporter, and somehow turned into the claim that the whole view collapses. Me and my friend numbered it out like this:

(1) On Hofstadter's view, the self just is a strange loop: a self-referential symbol, a pattern of activity in a symbol system that comes to represent the system that produces it.
(2) Symbols are patterns, and patterns are the kind of thing that can have multiple instances: the same pattern can be tokened in different physical media at the same time.
(3) So if my "I" is that pattern, then a molecule-for-molecule copy of my brain (or a faithful emulation running the same self-referential pattern) would token the same "I" as me, even while I keep on living.
(4) But that seems incoherent: the copy and I would have distinct first-person perspectives, and if you told me one of the two would be tortured, exactly one of them is the one I'd be afraid for.
(5) So the "I" can't just be a pattern, and Hofstadter's strange-loop account leaves out the thing that makes a self a self.

I'd really like to keep (1) or something near it, because it's the only naturalistic story about the self that ever felt right to me, but I also don't want to bite the bullet on there being two of me. Which premise or step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I keep suspecting something slipped between (2) and (3), but when I try to say what, I end up either invoking a soul or making "I" mean something so thin that the original view loses its point.
