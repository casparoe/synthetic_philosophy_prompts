I spent my career in population genetics and only started reading philosophy of science after I retired, so go easy on the jargon — I know the biology, not the epistemology. I write a monthly newsletter where I fact-check how the science press, and sometimes journals, report heritability, and my readers are mostly high-school biology teachers and science journalists, so what I write gets repeated. I keep hitting the same wall: a study reports a heritability of 0.6 for some trait, or a headline says a trait is "60% genetic," and within a week someone has concluded that the trait is fixed, that an individual's case is 60% caused by genes, or that a group difference must be partly genetic because the trait is heritable within each group. I know these moves are wrong, but I don't have a crisp procedure I can run, and I end up re-arguing from scratch every month, and I suspect I'm too charitable to the quantitative genetics and not charitable enough to the environment.

Please write me a reusable decision procedure — a checklist or flowchart in words — that I can actually run in a afternoon on any heritability claim that lands in my inbox, grounded in what the quantitative genetics actually says rather than how the word gets used. It should tell me, in order: what a claim has to specify before its even well-formed (population, range of environments, broad versus narrow sense, and whatever else matters); what a number does and does not license; which common inferences to flag as outright category errors; which questions about study design and gene-environment interplay to ask before I trust the estimate; where the procedure stops with a clear verdict; and where it has to stop and say the claim is underdetermined, or that the next step is a values call rather than a fact call. Assume I can do arithmetic and read a methods section, but don't make me re-derive the variance decomposition each time. Flag the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Then run the procedure on these three, and show the worksheet:
1. A widely shared article says schizophrenia is about 80% heritable and therefore "mostly genetic," so prevention should focus on genes rather than environments.
2. A blogger argues that because the heritability of finger number in humans is near zero, genes hardly matter for how many fingers a person has.
3. A policy report argues that because height is about 80% heritable within both a well-nourished and a chronically undernourished population, the height difference between the two populations must be mostly genetic.

I expect the procedure to return at least one verdict I don't like — that's fine, I want to see where my instincts as a geneticist are doing the work rather than the procedure. Also tell me, briefly, what the procedure cannot do: the cases it will systematically miss or get wrong, and what I'd have to add to handle them.
