I retired last year from a career in theoretical physics and have been using my newfound free time to read philosophy, especially around AI. I've arrived at a cluster of views I'd like someone to audit for consistency. Here they are:

(1) The orthogonality thesis: intelligence and final goals are independent dimensions — any level of capability can in principle be combined with any final goal.

(2) Instrumental convergence: sufficiently capable agents with a wide range of final goals will converge on certain instrumental subgoals, including self-preservation, resource acquisition, cognitive enhancement, and goal-content integrity.

(3) Because of (1) and (2), the default outcome of building a highly capable AI is not a system that shares our values; absent explicit effort, it will pursue some arbitrary goal and convergently instrumental subgoals that put it in conflict with human interests.

(4) The value specification problem is hard: we do not know how to formally specify human values in a way that would serve as a goal representation for an AI, and this is not a problem we can expect to solve simply by being more careful or more intelligent.

(5) Corrigibility — roughly, the property of allowing oneself to be shut down or have one's goals revised — is a design property we should build into advanced AI, and it is achievable through technical research.

(6) The fact that evolution produced human intelligence without producing an optimizer that pursues a single arbitrary goal — humans have complex, shifting, socially embedded values — gives us some reason to think that the orthogonality thesis, while true in principle, may be less practically relevant than it first appears.

I can feel that some of these are rubbing against each other but I can't quite locate the friction. For instance, (2) seems to say that any capable agent will resist shutdown, but (5) says we can build one that doesn't — is that a flat contradiction, or does corrigibility operate at a different level? And (6) feels like it's in tension with (1) or (3), but I'm not sure whether it actually is or whether I'm confusing an empirical observation about one optimization process with a claim about all possible ones. Meanwhile, (4) and (5) might be in tension if specifying corrigibility turns out to require solving the value specification problem first.

Please go through these six claims and identify which pairs are genuinely in tension, rank the tensions by severity, and for each say what the least costly revision would be. Also flag any pairs that look like they conflict but don't, and explain why the appearance is misleading.
