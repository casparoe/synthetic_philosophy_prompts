I'm trying to work out what I actually think about conversational implicature and deception, going by my gut on cases rather than starting from a theory. Here are my verdicts:

1. A colleague asks whether any students failed the exam. I know all thirty passed. I say, "Some of the students passed." I think I have misled my colleague, and I have done something wrong — though I would not call it lying.

2. Someone is standing next to an open window on a cold day. I say, "It's cold in here." I think I have implicated that they should close the window, but there is nothing deceptive about this; it is ordinary communication.

3. I write a reference letter: "Jones has excellent handwriting and is always punctual." I think I have implicated that Jones is a poor philosopher. Assuming Jones really is a poor philosopher, I do not think I have deceived anyone — I have communicated something true, just indirectly.

4. My partner asks, "Did you eat the last slice of cake?" I reply, "I don't like cake." In fact I did eat it, and I genuinely do dislike cake — the literal statement is true. But I think I have lied, or at least done something morally equivalent to lying. This feels worse to me than case 1.

5. A friend asks whether I have seen his wife lately. I say, "I saw her with a man at the cinema yesterday." The man was him — I simply did not mention that. I think I have misled my friend, and I have done something wrong. But it does not feel quite as bad as case 4, even though in both cases I exploited an implicature to create a false belief.

6. Someone defends a sharp business practice by saying, "Business is business." I think they have implicated something substantive — roughly, that certain moral norms are suspended in commercial contexts — but this is a normal conversational implicature, not a deception.

7. A visitor asks, "Is the town's new restaurant any good?" I reply, "It's open until 2 a.m." I think I have implicated that the food is not worth discussing, and this is a perfectly ordinary implicature — no deception, even though I deliberately gave an uninformative answer.

8. Someone asks what colour my car is. I say, "It's red." I do not think I have implicated anything beyond what I said.

Is there a principle — or a small set of principles — that captures all of these verdicts? In particular, what distinguishes the deceptive implicatures (1, 4, 5) from the non-deceptive ones (2, 3, 6, 7)? And what explains why case 4 feels like lying while case 1 feels like mere misleading?

Please also tell me what your principle says about a case I keep going back and forth on: a politician is asked whether they plan to raise taxes, and they reply, "I have no plans to raise taxes at this time." They know they will announce a tax rise next month, but they have not yet drawn up the formal plan. Have they deceived their audience, and if so, is it more like case 1 or case 4?

If no single principle captures all my verdicts without becoming gerrymandered, tell me which verdict you think I should overturn and why.
