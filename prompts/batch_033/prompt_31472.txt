I recently joined a committee at work that signs off on changes to our building and grounds, and I have caught myself using the word intentionally in ways I cannot yet defend. I have also been reading about what is called the Knobe effect in experimental philosophy, but I want to get past the headline finding and work out which principle, if any, captures my own judgments, because I apply the concept differently from case to case and the differences feel principled even when I cannot state the principle.

Here are my verdicts, as honestly as I can report them. (1) A manager approves a cost-cutting change that she knows will pollute a nearby stream, and she says she cares only about the savings; I say she polluted the stream intentionally. (2) The same manager approves a change that she knows will clean up the stream, and she says she cares only about the savings; I do not say she cleaned the stream intentionally. (3) If the manager in the polluting case regrets the damage and first looks for a less polluting option before approving the change anyway, I still say she polluted the stream intentionally, though with less confidence. (4) If the manager in the cleaning case is delighted by the improvement and publicises it, I become inclined to say she cleaned the stream intentionally, though less confidently than in the polluting case. (5) If the foreseen side effect is morally neutral, say the change will slightly alter the pattern of wear on the lobby floor, I do not say the manager altered the floor intentionally even though she foresaw it. (6) The case that unsettles me is a surgeon who gives a patient a drug that she knows will cause nausea as a side effect; the drug is the only way to save the patient's life, and the surgeon says she cares only about saving the patient. I say the surgeon caused the nausea but did not cause it intentionally. That sits badly alongside (1), since both involve a bad side effect the agent foresees and is indifferent to.

My working hypothesis is that a foreseen side effect counts as intentional when the agent's attitude toward it is one we would criticise, such as indifference to a bad side effect. That handles (1), (2), (5) and perhaps (6), but it struggles with (3) and (4), where the agent's attitude is not indifference yet my verdict shifts anyway, and I suspect the principle may simply be redescribing the pattern rather than explaining it. I would like a precise principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures as many of these verdicts as possible, with each clause motivated by the case it is meant to handle; an honest statement of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and why those verdicts should be overturned; and its predictions for cases I have not considered, such as a manager who approves a change that will make the office less accessible to a disabled colleague as a side effect and is indifferent to that, or a manager who approves a change with a good side effect that she is annoyed about. If the honest answer is that no compact principle can capture these verdicts without gerrymandering, or that my framing of the question rests on a confusion, please say so plainly rather than producing a principle for the sake of it. I would be grateful if you wrote in British English.
