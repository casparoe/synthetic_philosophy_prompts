I'm a retired hydrologist who got into political philosophy late — I've been slowly working through Machiavelli's The Prince in the W.K. Marriott translation, and chapter 25 is where I've gotten stuck. I spent thirty years thinking about rivers, so I expected the river passage to be the one thing I'd understand cold. Instead the more I stair at the chapter, the less I can tell what the actual argument is, and I'd love some help.

Here's the river passage:

"I compare her to one of those raging rivers, which when in flood overflows the plains, sweeping away trees and buildings, bearing away the soil from place to place; everything flies before it, all yield to its violence, without being able in any way to withstand it; and yet, though its nature be such, it does not follow therefore that men, when the weather becomes fair, shall not make provision, both with defences and barriers, in such a manner that, rising again, the waters may pass away by canal, and their force be neither so unrestrained nor so dangerous. So it happens with fortune, who shows her power where valour has not prepared to resist her, and thither she turns her forces where she knows that barriers and defences have not been raised to constrain her."

Then a little later he says success depends on the spirit of the times:

"I believe also that he will be successful who directs his actions according to the spirit of the times, and that he whose actions do not accord with the times will not be successful."

And a bit after that he adds the part that really undoes me:

"But a man is not often found sufficiently circumspect to know how to accommodate himself to the change, both because he cannot deviate from what nature inclines him to do, and also because, having always prospered by acting in one way, he cannot be persuaded that it is well to leave it; and, therefore, the cautious man, when it is time to turn adventurous, does not know how to do it, hence he is ruined; but had he changed his conduct with the times fortune would not have changed."

The Julius II example right after seems to drive the point home: he went at everything impetuously and succeeded only because the times happened to fit that style, and if circumstances had required caution he'd have been ruined, because he could never have deviated from the ways his nature inclined him to.

My problem is that these feel like two different and maybe incompatible accounts of how a person handles fortune. The river says fortune is manageable the way a flood is: when the weather is fair you build defences and canals so the next flood is "neither so unrestrained nor so dangerous." That's a story about durable preparation — virtù (I gather "valour" is the translator's word for it) looks like foresight plus building something that holds no matter how the water behaves. But the spirit-of-the-times story is a completely different model: success is matching your style to the moment, and he immediately says nobody can actually do that, because a man can't deviate from what nature inclines him to do and the cautious man will never turn adventurous in time. So the prescription is "change with the times" and the diagnosis is "you can't." And then the chapter closes with the famous bit about fortune being like a woman who yields to audacity, which sounds like a third piece of advice entirely.

So my question: is there one coherent view in this chapter, and if so, how do the river model and the times model fit together? I can't tell weather he means them as the same view or not. If the lesson is "build levees," that's something a prince can actually do; if the lesson is "match the times," but no one can change their nature, then what's left — just hoping your temperament happens to fit your historical moment? Also, the opening says fortune is "the arbiter of one-half of our actions, but that she still leaves us to direct the other half, or perhaps a little less." I keep reading that as a scientist and wanting to ask what quantity he's estimating. Is he actually committed to a proportion, or is that just a rhetorical way of rejecting determinism without having a real model? I'm not after the scholarly literature — I just want to know whether the chapter can be made to hang together, and how.
