I've been working through an argument about wild animal suffering and habitat destruction, and it seems to prove far too much. I can't locate the mistake.

(1) If an action prevents the existence of lives that are not worth living — lives that contain more suffering than well-being — then that consequence counts morally in favor of the action.

(2) The vast majority of wild animals live lives that are not worth living. Most are r-strategists: they produce enormous numbers of offspring, the overwhelming majority of whom die painfully shortly after birth — from predation, starvation, disease, or exposure — before experiencing anything that could plausibly compensate.

(3) Habitat destruction prevents the existence of wild animals who would otherwise have been born into those habitats.

(4) Therefore, habitat destruction has a consequence that counts morally in its favor: it prevents an enormous number of lives not worth living.

So far, this is just the standard (if uncomfortable) argument that taking wild animal suffering seriously forces us to reconsider the badness of habitat loss. But now the problem:

(5) The reasoning in (1)–(4) does not depend on any feature specific to wild animals. It applies to any population whose members' lives are, on average, not worth living.

(6) There are human populations — for instance, people with severe treatment-resistant depression, or people in conditions of extreme and inescapable deprivation — among whom many individuals judge their own lives not worth living, and some of these conditions are heritable or otherwise predictably transmitted across generations.

(7) By parity of reasoning, preventing the existence of future members of such human populations — through policies that discourage or prevent reproduction — would have a consequence that counts morally in its favor.

(8) But the idea that there is anything morally to be said in favor of preventing the existence of, say, the children of severely depressed people is abhorrent. It is the kind of claim that has historically underwritten eugenic sterilization programs.

(9) So the argument must go wrong somewhere.

I can see several places to push back, but each seems costly. Rejecting (1) means denying that preventing a life of pure suffering is even pro tanto good — but that seems to imply that bringing a child into a life of unremitting torture is morally no worse than bringing a child into a flourishing life, which I cannot accept. Rejecting (2) requires claiming that the brief, painful lives of most wild animals are on balance worth living, which seems empirically indefensible given what we know about r-selected species. Rejecting (5) requires finding a morally relevant difference between wild animals and humans that breaks the analogy — but what could it be? That humans have autonomy? But the argument is about future beings who do not yet exist and have no autonomy to respect. That humans can reflect on their suffering? But that seems to make the human case worse, not better.

I am most inclined to keep (1) and (2) and find a way to block the inference to (7), but I do not know what principle does that work without being ad hoc. Where does this argument go wrong, and what do I have to give up to stop it?
