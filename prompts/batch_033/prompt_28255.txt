I've recently fallen down something of a rabbit hole reading about behavioral genetics, and I'm hoping someone can help me resolve what feels like a contradiction. The trigger was a conversation with colleagues about whether aptitude for programming is innate or learned, which led me to the broader literature on the heritability of cognitive traits. From what I gather, the heritability of IQ in adulthood in developed countries is typically estimated in the range of 0.5 to 0.8 — meaning that somewhere between half and four-fifths of the variance in IQ scores within those populations is associated with genetic variation.

Here's the part I can't get straight. If a trait is highly heritable, I would expect that environmental interventions can only have a modest impact at best — you can't rewrite someone's genome, after all. And yet I keep coming across studies showing that things like intensive early childhood education programs, improved nutrition, or reductions in lead exposure produce measurable gains in IQ at the population level. How can both of these things be true? It seems like either the heritability estimates must be overstating the role of genes, or the intervention studies must be overstating their effects. But I suspect the real issue is that I'm misunderstanding what a heritability coefficient actually licenses you to conclude. Could someone walk me through where my reasoning goes wrong?
