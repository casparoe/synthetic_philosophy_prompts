I'm grading an undergraduate philosophy of language exam. The question was:

"Explain Frege's puzzle about identity statements and assess what it shows about whether the meaning of a proper name goes beyond its referent."

A student wrote:

"Frege's puzzle, set out in 'On Sense and Reference,' is that 'Hesperus is Hesperus' and 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' differ in cognitive significance even though both are true identity statements. If a name's meaning were exhausted by its referent, the two sentences would express the same proposition — the trivial truth that Venus is identical to Venus — and yet a competent speaker can rationally accept one while doubting the other. Frege's solution is to distinguish sense from reference: 'Hesperus' and 'Phosphorus' share a referent but present it under different modes of presentation, and the informative identity statement combines the same referent with two different senses. This shows decisively that the meaning of a proper name must include a sense over and above its referent. A Millian view on which names are directly referential cannot accommodate the puzzle, since it would have to treat 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' as a trivial necessary truth, which it plainly is not. Kripke's later causal theory of reference tries to rescue direct reference by distinguishing necessary from a posteriori truths, but this merely relabels the problem: we still need to explain how a necessary truth can be informative, and only the sense/reference distinction does that."

What grade would you give this answer, and what point-by-point comments justify that grade? Please work through it in detail: what the student gets right, where the reasoning overreaches or conflates distinct positions, and what a correct answer would need to add or qualify.
