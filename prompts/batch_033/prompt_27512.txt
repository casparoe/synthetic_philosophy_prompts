Construct a two-gamble decision problem in the spirit of the Pasadena paradox. A risk-neutral agent must choose exactly one of two gambles, A and B. Both gambles are built from the same randomizing device: an idealized but otherwise ordinary fair coin, tossed until some stopping condition you specify, with every possible outcome paying a finite amount of money. The case should meet all of the following specifications.

1. In the natural order of outcomes (the order induced by your stopping condition), each gamble's expectation series — the sum, over outcomes, of probability times payoff — converges conditionally: it converges, but its positive and negative parts each diverge, so the expectation is of the ∞ − ∞ form and the expected value is genuinely undefined, not merely infinite.

2. Gamble B weakly dominates gamble A: in every possible outcome B pays at least as much as A, and in at least one outcome B pays strictly more.

3. Neither gamble may be the standard Pasadena game, and B may not be obtained from A simply by adding the same constant to every payoff. Design your own conditionally convergent payoff scheme.

4. The case should be as simple as it can be while satisfying 1–3, with no infinite payoffs, no infinitesimals, no non-Archimedean utilities, and no departures from ordinary risk-neutral choice.

The point of the case is to isolate the gap between dominance reasoning and expected value maximization when expectations are undefined; the case fails if it stipulates a verdict instead of generating it from the payoff structure.

After presenting the two gambles, do each of the following.

(a) Show in detail why each expectation is undefined: write out the expectation series, verify that it converges conditionally, and explain why conditional convergence makes the expectation depend on the order in which outcomes are summed.

(b) State the weak dominance argument for choosing B and show that it is valid for your case.

(c) Exhibit the order-dependence explicitly: find one legitimate reordering of A's outcomes under which A's expectation series sums to more than B's natural-order sum, and explain why a dominance-respecting agent should not be moved by this.

(d) Identify the structural feature doing the work. Explain why removing it — for instance, by truncating each gamble to finitely many possible outcomes — restores defined expectations and makes weak dominance and expected value maximization agree.

(e) Say how a committed expected-value maximizer should respond to your case: that the expectation is undefined and the theory is simply silent, that weak dominance should be adopted as a separate axiom, or something else. Defend your response.

Write the entire answer in American English, keep it self-contained, and do not cite or quote external sources.
