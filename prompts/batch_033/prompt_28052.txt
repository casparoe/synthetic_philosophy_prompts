I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, but I keep running into the concept of utility in decision theory and in the behavioral economics material I read for work. I've ended up with a set of views about what utility and preference mean, and I'd like a consistency check before I commit to them.

Here is where I've landed:

(1) Utility is best understood in revealed-preference terms. To say that a person's choices maximize a utility function is just to say that those choices can be represented as maximizing some function, which is in turn just to say that the choices satisfy standard consistency conditions such as completeness, transitivity, and something like the weak axiom of revealed preference. There is no additional claim about some internal quantity the person is trying to maximize.

(2) I also think utility ascriptions should be substantive and explanatory. A statement like "she chose the cake because it had higher utility for her" should tell me something about why she chose it, and it should be the kind of claim that her choices could in principle falsify, rather than being true by definition.

(3) I think people are often irrational in a perfectly ordinary sense: they procrastinate, they reverse their choices when the same options are framed differently, they eat the cake after resolving to eat the salad, and they describe some of their own past choices as mistakes.

(4) At the same time, I think that, at least for ordinary unconstrained choices, there is no fact about what someone "really prefers" that floats free of what they choose. If someone resolves to order the salad and then orders the cake, I want to say the later choice reveals the preference; the earlier resolution was a forecast about future choice, and it was a bad forecast, not a report of a deeper preference that the choice then betrayed.

(5) But I also want to allow that some choices do not reveal preferences at all. Choices made under coercion, addiction, or severe information deficits should not count as evidence about what the person wanted.

My question is whether these five commitments form a stable package. I'd like an audit rather than a lecture: which pairs are in genuine tension, which only look like they are, and how severe is each genuine tension? For each genuine tension, what is the least costly revision, and what does it cost? If there is a single change that would remove the most tension, I'd like to know that too.
