I am a retired physicist who has recently taken up serious study of academic philosophy. I have been working through the literature on infinite ethics and have constructed an argument that I find troubling. I would be grateful if you, as a professional philosopher with expertise in this area, would examine the argument carefully and help me locate the error — or, if the argument is valid, help me understand which premise is least costly to abandon.

The argument:

(1) An adequate ethical theory must be action-guiding: it must be able to compare at least some possible actions and say that one is morally better than another.

(2) The universe may contain infinitely many people. More cautiously, we cannot rule this out on current cosmology, and an ethical theory should not rest its normative claims on a contingent empirical assumption of this kind.

(3) In a universe with infinitely many people, many actions affect the wellbeing of infinitely many individuals. Consider two actions. Action X: leave everything as it is. Action Y: slightly improve the wellbeing of every person in the infinite population by some finite positive amount. Intuitively, Y is morally better than X.

(4) To make this intuition precise, we need a method for comparing the value of two states of the world, each involving infinitely many welfare subjects. The natural approach, borrowed from finite-population ethics, is to assign a real number to each state and compare the numbers. For finite populations, total utilitarianism does this by summing individual utilities.

(5) For infinite populations, the sum of individual utilities is, in general, divergent. Both state X and state Y have infinite total utility. Standard real arithmetic provides no grounds for saying one infinite quantity exceeds another.

(6) One might try to compare them by examining limits. For any finite subpopulation, the utility difference between Y and X equals the size of the subpopulation. As the subpopulation grows, this difference grows without bound. But this does not yield a real-number valuation for each state; it merely tells us that the difference diverges. Moreover, for other comparisons — say, between a world where wellbeing alternates (1, 2, 1, 2, …) and one where it alternates (2, 1, 2, 1, …) — the limit of the difference depends on the order in which we sum, and there is no privileged ordering of an infinite set of people.

(7) One might appeal to hyperreal numbers or other non-Archimedean value structures. But these approaches appear to either violate the principle that the value of two disjoint populations is the sum of their individual values, or else require an arbitrary choice of ultrafilter, which makes the comparison depend on a non-constructive mathematical object that no one has ever specified.

(8) One might abandon the real-valued framework and rely on Pareto dominance: Y is better than X if Y is better for some and worse for none. This handles the comparison between X and Y, but it is radically incomplete: it cannot compare actions that involve tradeoffs between different people, which is the standard case in ethics.

(9) It appears, then, that we face a trilemma: any ethical theory must sacrifice either (i) the ability to compare actions in infinite populations, (ii) basic aggregation principles that work perfectly well in finite cases, or (iii) the intuitive verdict that Y is better than X.

(10) Each horn of this trilemma is painful. Giving up (i) means ethics goes silent in a world that might be our own. Giving up (ii) means rejecting principles that seem close to constitutive of consequentialist reasoning. Giving up (iii) means accepting that an action which makes literally everyone better off is not morally better — which sounds like a reductio of one's theory.

I am not sure which step of this reasoning is the first to go wrong. I suspect the problem may lie somewhere in (4) through (7), but I cannot articulate the mistake precisely. Please walk through the argument, identify the problematic step or steps, explain why they appeared plausible, and discuss what the main philosophical responses — such as value-density approaches, hyperreal-valued utilitarianism, person-affecting views, and causal approaches — must each sacrifice to escape the conclusion. Please write your answer in American English, and take the time to think through the issues carefully before settling on a diagnosis.
