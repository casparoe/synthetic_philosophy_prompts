Two people in my probability reading group got into an argument about the Pasadena game, and they asked me to referee. I couldn't, so I'm hoping you can. The game is this: flip a fair coin until it comes up heads; if the first heads is on the nth flip, you receive (-1)^(n-1) · (2^n / n) dollars, so you gain on odd n and lose on even n.

Daniel opened with: "The fair price is just the expected value, and that's ln 2, about 69 cents. Each possible outcome — first heads on flip n — has probability 2^(-n) and payoff (-1)^(n-1) · 2^n/n, so the expectation is the sum over n of (-1)^(n-1)/n, which is the alternating harmonic series. It converges to ln 2. So the game is worth a bit less than a dollar."

Mara replied: "That series converges only conditionally. In probability theory, an expectation exists only when the sum of the positive terms and the sum of the negative terms are each finite. Here the positive terms are 1 + 1/3 + 1/5 + ... and the negative terms are -1/2 - 1/4 - 1/6 - ..., and both diverge. So the expectation is undefined. The alternating sum lands on ln 2 only because the terms are written in one particular order; by rearranging them you can make the same terms add up to any real number you like. So ln 2 is an artifact of the ordering, not a fact about the gamble."

Daniel came back: "This isn't an arbitrary infinite series that we're free to reorder. The order comes from the game itself: the first possible outcome is heads on flip 1, then heads on flip 2, and so on. You can't reorder the actual outcomes. And the partial sums aren't just an algebraic trick: they are the expected values of the truncated games in which you flip at most N times and receive nothing if heads hasn't appeared by then. Those truncated games have well-defined values, and their values converge to ln 2. So there is a natural sense in which the full game's value is ln 2."

Mara answered: "The partial sums are one regularization among many. The same gamble can be presented by listing the same outcomes in a different order — nothing about the gamble has changed — and then the partial sums converge to a different number or not at all. Since expected value is supposed to depend only on the gamble, not on how someone lists or approximates its outcomes, the limit of one listing's partial sums doesn't qualify. What you've shown is that a particular family of finite approximations has a limit, not that the original gamble has an expected value. The right conclusion is that this gamble has no expected value, full stop."

Daniel then said: "I'll grant that ln 2 isn't an expectation in the textbook sense. But 'undefined, full stop' is unsatisfying. The game is perfectly well specified, and we can still ask what it's worth. If the standard definition can't assign a value, that may just show the standard definition is too narrow, and that we should look for a principled extension — maybe one that respects the natural order of the outcomes — rather than declare the question ill-posed."
