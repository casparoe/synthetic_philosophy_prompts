So I've been going down this rabbit hole reading about Wittgenstein's later stuff — specifically On Certainty and this idea of "hinge epistemology" that some people have developed from it. The basic gist as I understand it: there are certain propositions (like "the external world exists" or "my memory is broadly reliable") that we can't really justify with evidence, but we also can't meaningfully doubt them, because they're the scaffolding that makes doubt and justification possible in the first place. They're not foundations in the Cartesian sense — they're more like the hinges on which the door of inquiry swings. You don't question the hinge while you're opening the door. And if you tried to doubt all your hinges simultaneously, you wouldn't be left with radical scepticism — you'd just be unable to think at all.

Now here's the thing. I'm a backend engineer and I keep having this nagging feeling that I actually understand this perfectly well in my own vocabulary, but I cant tell if I'm mapping it correctly or just pattern-matching. In my world, every system has a set of assumptions it can't verify from within itself. Your kernel trusts the bootloader, your bootloader trusts the firmware, and at the bottom there's a hardware root of trust you just have to take as given — not because it's provable, but because the whole chain of verification has to start somewhere. Similarly, any formal system has axioms it can't prove, a runtime has invariants it assumes but doesn't check, and a type system has soundness properties it can't establish about itself. Hell, even a CI pipeline assumes the git history hasn't been tampered with in ways that `git log` wouldn't show.
