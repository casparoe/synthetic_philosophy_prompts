I'm a cog-sci PhD student and I've tied myself in knots with this argument. It feels like it proves too much but I can't locate the slip.

(1) Take any apparently tangled hierarchy—Escher's hands drawing each other, the mind-brain relation, a self-referential sentence, whatever. You can always step outside the system and describe the whole thing from a level up, where the tangle resolves into a straightforward linear dependence. (The hands are both drawn by Escher; the mental states are realized by neural states; the sentence is written by a person who isn't inside the sentence.)

(2) If a phenomenon can be fully described from a vantage point where it isn't tangled, then the tangledness isn't a feature of the world—it's an artifact of the description you happened to pick.

(3) So no tangled hierarchy is real. They're all just descriptions that went recursive on themselves.

(4) But if tangled hierarchies aren't real, then self-reference isn't real either, because self-reference just is a system containing a representation of itself—a tangle.

(5) If self-reference isn't real, then Gödel sentences, consciousness (understood as self-awareness), and the very sentence you're reading now are all somehow illusory or incoherent.

(6) That's absurd. And worse, if it's true, it undermines the argument itself, since the argument relies on me stepping outside systems and describing them from above—which is itself a move within a system I can't step outside of.

So something's got to give. My gut says reject (2): a higher-level description doesn't make the lower-level phenomenon disappear. But then I worry I'm just insisting tangled hierarchies are real because I want them to be, and I can't say what "real" means here without it sounding like I'm positing spooky emergent stuff. Where does the argument actually break, and what do I have to pay to fix it?
