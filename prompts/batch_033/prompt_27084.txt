You are an expert in decision theory, especially the independence axiom and the empirical challenges to it. I manage a small investment partnership, and I have been keeping a record of one partner's choices in a series of incentivized lotteries we use to calibrate the partnership's risk tolerance. Next week she must make two decisions, and I need to know how she will come down.

Her past choices:

1. Sure $9,000 vs. a 50% chance of $20,000 (otherwise $0): she took the sure $9,000.
2. A 50% chance of $20,000 (otherwise $0) vs. a 50% chance of $10,000 (otherwise $0): she took the 50% chance of $20,000.
3. Sure $9,000 vs. a 90% chance of $10,000 (10% chance of $0): she took the sure $9,000.
4. An 80% chance of $10,000 (20% chance of $0) vs. a 60% chance of $15,000 (40% chance of $0): she took the 80% chance of $10,000.
5. A 50% chance of $50,000 (otherwise $0) vs. a 70% chance of $12,000 (otherwise $0): she took the 50% chance of $50,000.

Next week's decisions are: (i) $10,000 for sure vs. an 89% chance of $10,000, a 10% chance of $50,000, and a 1% chance of $0; and (ii) an 11% chance of $10,000 (89% chance of $0) vs. a 10% chance of $50,000 (90% chance of $0).

Predict the pair of choices she will make. Start by brainstorming the candidate rules that fit her five past choices—expected utility with a concave utility function, rank-dependent or probability-weighting accounts, certainty-effect heuristics, regret-based rules, and anything else that survives contact with the record. Then say which rule best explains the record, which past choices are doing the real work, and what each surviving rule predicts for the two new decisions. If the record genuinely underdetermines the prediction, say so, and identify the single further choice you would most want to see to discriminate among the remaining rules.
