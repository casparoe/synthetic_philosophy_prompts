I just spent two hours arguing with a friend about why we voluntarily watch movies that make us cry. She kept returning to Aristotle — catharsis, the purging of pity and fear — as if that settled the matter. But the more we talked, the less satisfied I was with any single explanation. Here is the practice I cannot get out of my head: millions of people, every day, choose to spend their leisure time consuming fictions that they know in advance will make them feel grief, dread, or sorrow. They do not do this once or twice by accident; they seek it out, they recommend it to others, they form identities around it. And this is not a niche taste — tragedy, horror, tearjerkers, and melancholic music are among the most commercially successful genres across cultures and centuries. If you tried to explain this to a visitor from another planet, you would sound insane: these creatures deliberately induce negative emotions in themselves, pay money for the privilege, and call the result a good use of an evening.

What I want to know is this: what philosophical commitments does this practice presuppose — or rule out — about the nature of emotion, value, and aesthetic experience? My friend's catharsis story is one candidate, but it seems to presuppose a hydraulic model of the emotions that few philosophers or psychologists would now defend. A different view might be that the sadness we feel in response to fiction is not genuine sadness but something else — a quasi-emotion, or an imaginative counterpart — and that the practice therefore does not need to explain why we seek out genuine suffering. But that move has its own costs. Another possibility is that the value of tragic art lies not in the negative emotion itself but in some other good that the negative emotion makes possible — insight, connection, the exercise of certain sensitivities — and that we tolerate the sadness as a price worth paying. And still another is that the sadness itself is valued, perhaps because we are the kind of creatures who can find something worthwhile in the full range of human experience, including its painful parts. Each of these views has different implications for what else we should expect people to seek out, and for whether the practice is rational or merely a quirk of our psychology.

Please reconstruct the implicit theory — or theories — that the widespread practice of consuming tragic art commits us to, distinguishing what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Where the practice is philosophically underdetermined — where several rival theories could license it equally well — say so. And please separate what is reasonably settled in the literature from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this; I do not want a contested view presented as if it were the consensus.
