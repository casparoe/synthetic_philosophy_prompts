I spent my career in biology and only recently got into philosophy and game theory, so forgive me if I use terms loosely. The thing I keep chewing on is the live-and-let-live system on the Western Front in the First World War. In the quiet sectors the same units faced each other across the same strech of no-man's land for weeks or months, and an unofficial truce would grow up: artillery barrages at predictable times, rifle fire aimed high, nobody shooting at the other side's ration parties. The Christmas Truce of 1914 is the famous version of it, but the quieter version lasted on and off in places for much of the war, and it seems to have driven the staffs on both sides half mad; they kept ordering trench raids to stop the men getting too comfortable with each other.

The standard story I keep hearing is that this is a textbook iterated prisoner's dilemma: cooperate by leaving the other side alone, defect by trying to kill them, and the shadow of the future keeps the cooperation stable because the same two units are stuck facing each other. From biology it also looks like reciprocal altruism, maybe tit-for-tat.

What I want is a careful case analysis, not the just-so version. Map the actual situation onto the game as precisely as you can: who is the player here — the individual soldier, the platoon, the battalion? What exactly are the moves and the payoffs, and what is the discount factor in a trench, concretely? Then tell me where the model breaks or quietly does too much work. For one thing, the game was never just between two players: high command, rotation schedules, and trench raids all act like third parties resetting the game. For another, how do you distinguish cooperation held together by fear of reprisal from cooperation held together by something like fellow-feeling, or just by both sides wanting a quiet sector? And what should I take away about the evolution of cooperation from the fact that this cooperation emerged in the middle of an industrialized war and yet stayed fragile enough that the war ground on for four years? Does the episode confirm the theory, or does it mostly show how much scaffolding the theory needs about group boundaries, third-party enforcement, and who the agent is?
