I serve on my university's institutional review board for human-subjects research, and we are currently reviewing a proposal that has split the board badly enough that our chair has asked me to draft the written opinion that will frame our discussion at next week's vote.

The study, from the psychology department, investigates how people's moral judgments shift when they believe they are interacting with an AI system that genuinely understands them. Participants will be randomly assigned to one of two cover stories. One group will be told that the AI they are about to converse with "forms beliefs about you as a person, has intentions to help you work through what you're feeling, and understands what you're going through." The other group will be told that the system "analyzes text patterns statistically and has no beliefs, intentions, or understanding of its own." In reality, all participants interact with the same large language model, and the true purpose — measuring how attributions of mental states to AI affect subsequent moral reasoning — is disclosed in a thorough debriefing. The researchers argue that the differential cover story is methodologically necessary because pilot data showed that participants' default assumptions about AI mental states are too heterogeneous to leave uncontrolled, and the effect they are studying would be washed out otherwise.

Half the board regards this as routine methodological deception of the kind we approve regularly: the falsehood is confined to the cover story, the risk of harm is minimal, and the debriefing is robust. The other half argues that telling a participant an AI "has beliefs and intentions" is not an ordinary experimental deception — it is an assertion that a reasonable person could regard as straightforwardly false, and asking someone to act on a false metaphysical claim for the duration of an hour-long emotionally charged interaction exceeds what informed consent can absorb, even with debriefing. As one member put it during our preliminary discussion: "If a study told participants they were interacting with a conscious being when they were not, we would reject it on the spot. Why is telling them the AI has beliefs any different? Either beliefs require consciousness, in which case it's the same deception, or they don't, in which case we're taking sides in a live philosophical debate and calling it methodology."

I need to draft a three-to-four-page memo that (a) states clearly whether we should approve the study, reject it, or approve it with conditions; (b) gives the reasoning in a way that takes the losing side's strongest argument seriously rather than caricaturing it; and (c) articulates what principle about attributing mental-state language to AI systems in research contexts we would be adopting with this decision — since whatever we decide will function as a precedent for future proposals. The memo should not simply punt to the federal regulations, which are silent on this question. I am less interested in the bottom-line recommendation than in the quality of the reasoning that gets us there, and especially in whether there is a principled way to distinguish this case from the "conscious being" hypothetical my colleague raised.
