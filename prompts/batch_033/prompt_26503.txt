I'm running a grad seminar on reasons and rationality — about 10 students, a bit under two hours. I want to design an activity that makes them feel the pull of the wrong kind of reasons problem before we read the literature on it. You know the basic setup: an evil demon will punish you unless you admire him, so you've got a pragmatic reason to admire him, but it doesn't seem like the *right* kind of reason — it doesn't make him admirable. The puzzle is saying what the difference consists in.

I want the students to generate the puzzle themseves. So I need a set of cases, a procedure, and a debrief. The cases should include both epistemic and practical attitudes — beliefs, desires, admiration, resentment, fear, etc. — and mix in some where pragmatic reasons *do* seem like the right kind (like: you'll get paid if you desire to eat a bug, because that'll help you actually do it). The activity should force them to try to articulate a principle, fail in interesting ways, and then see why the problem is hard.

Give me the case set (maybe 8–10 short vignettes), the step-by-step procedure with timing, debrief notes that map moments in the activity to the philosophical terrain, and a candid note on what predictably goes wrong and which part of the problem the activity can't get at.
