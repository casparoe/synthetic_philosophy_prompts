I'm a retired geologist who started reading philosophy a few years back, and I've stumbled into something that's been eating at me. It's the backward induction argument in the centipede game, and the thing is, it seems to prove way too much and I can't find the exit.

Here's the game. Two players, a pile of money that grows each time someone passes. Player 1 goes first: take the bigger share and end the game, or pass, which grows the pot and gives Player 2 the choice. Player 2 then faces the same decision, and so on for a fixed number of rounds — say 100. At the last round, the player whose turn it is can either take (getting more than the other player) or pass, which ends the game with both getting an equal split that's slightly less than what taking would give. So passing at the last round is just leaving money on the table.

Now the argument, step by step:

(1) At round 100, the rational player takes. There's no future to influence, and taking gets you more than passing. Strictly dominates.

(2) At round 99, the player knows that if she passes, the other player will take at round 100 (by (1)). So passing at round 99 means ending up with whatever you get when the other player takes at 100 — which is less than what you'd get by taking at 99. So take at 99.

(3) At round 98, the player knows the other will take at 99 (by (2)). So take at 98.

... and this unravels all the way back.

(4) Therefore, Player 1 takes at round 1. The game is over before it begins.

Now here's the trouble. The argument doesn't care whether it's 100 rounds or 10,000. It doesn't care about the exact payoffs as long as the structure holds. So it seems to prove that in any finite sequential interaction where each party can grab a benefit at the other's expense and there's a known endpoint, rational players will never cooperate, never build trust, never pass. But that's absurd. People cooperate in finite interactions all the damn time. Business partners stick to agreements on projects with deadlines. Scientists collaborate on papers even though the collaboration will end. You lend your neighbor a tool even though one of you might move next year. It seems perfectly rational to do these things, and a theory that says otherwise has proved too much.

I've been going in circles on this. Is the flaw in (1)? Does it help to say that real players might be irrational, so passing at round 99 is rational because you think the other guy might mess up and pass at round 100? But then rationality depends on expecting irrationality, which feels like it guts the whole concept. Is backward induction itself the problem? If so, what do we replace it with, and does that create worse problems elsewhere? Is there a hidden assumption about common knowledge of rationality that's doing the work, and if we drop it does the whole thing fall apart? And there's a wierd thing I can't shake: even if you show me exactly where the argument breaks, why did it feel so airtight in the first place?

I want to know: which step do you reject, and what's the bill for rejecting it? If you think the argument is valid and the real question is which premise to abandon, tell me which one and what the rest of the theory of rationality looks like after you've given it up.
