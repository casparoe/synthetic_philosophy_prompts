Just got back from a long argument with my friend about Nietzsche's diet-and-health stuff and I cant shake this argument he made. His reading of Nietzsche is basically that your thinking is downstream of your body: what you eat, how you digest, the climate you live in, how much you walk. That a philosophers ideas are a kind of confession of their physiology, and that bad food and bad air don't just make you feel bad, they make you think badly in a way no amount of clever argumentation repairs.

So he lays out this chain:

(1) If Nietzsche's right, then whenever someone holds a philosophical view, what really explains them holding it is their bodily state (diet, digestion, climate, health), not the reasons they'd offer for it.
(2) If someone holds a view because of their bodily state rather than because of reasons, then the fact that they hold it gives us no reason to think it's true.
(3) So on Nietzsche's own view, no philosophical view — including the view itself — can be established by argument; the only real question about a philosophy is whether the philosopher's body was in the right condition when they formed it.
(4) But Nietzsche spends huge amounts of energy trying to argue people out of Christianity, Wagner, Schopenhauer, German nationalism — which is a thing you only do if you think argument can get at whether a view is right.
(5) So either Nietzsche's thesis is false, or his practice refutes it, or he's making an exception for himself that the thesis doesn't permit.

I keep wanting to keep (1) because it does feel like your body conditions what you can even entertain, and a lot of philosophy is post-hoc rationalization. But I can't find the step where this goes wrong, and the conclusion that Nietzsche is just stuck with the contradiction seems too strong. Which premise should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Or is the argument sound?
