I'm a high school student in an optional reading group my teacher runs on philosophy and economics, and after a session on social choice theory I have been turning over a claim attributed to Amartya Sen that I can't quite get straight. As I understand it, Sen argued that no social decision procedure can satisfy three conditions that each look very reasonable: it can handle any profile of individual preferences; it is Pareto-respecting, so that when everyone prefers one alternative to another, the social ranking reflects that; and it honors a minimal kind of individual liberty, where at least two people each have a decisive say over some pair of alternatives within their own private sphere. What unsettles me is how weak that liberty condition seems—it does not ask for a full sphere of protected choices, just one decisive comparison for each of two people—and yet the three conditions still cannot all hold at once. Could you explain, with as much intuition as you can, why this impossibility arises and what it actually shows?

I've seen the standard example with two people and a controversial book, but I'm less interested in that illustration than in the structure of the conflict. My working guess is that the result is less a proof that liberty and efficiency genuinely clash in real life and more a sign that modeling rights as decisiveness over pairs of alternatives is a poor formal model of liberty, though I don't trust that guess. Please be careful to separate what is mathematically settled from what remains philosophically contested among people who work on social choice, and don't present one interpretation of the paradox as if it were the consensus.
