I'm trying to get clear on what I think about cognitive penetration of perception — whether states like beliefs, desires, and knowledge can directly alter perceptual experience itself, as opposed to merely influencing post-perceptual judgment or guiding attention. I've collected my verdicts on a range of cases, and I want to know whether there's a principled distinction behind them.

Here are the cases and my verdicts:

1. Wearing a heavy backpack makes a hill look steeper than it does without the backpack. I think this is a genuine change in visual experience driven by the body's effort-related state, not just a shift in how I report the slant. Verdict: penetration.

2. The Müller-Lyer illusion: the two lines continue to look different in length even after I measure them and know perfectly well they are equal. I take this as a paradigm case where cognition fails to penetrate perception. Verdict: no penetration.

3. An expert radiologist looking at a chest X-ray sees a subtle tumor that a medical student literally cannot see, even though the same pattern of light reaches both retinas. I think this is perceptual learning — the radiologist's visual system has been retuned through experience — and not a case where the radiologist's belief that the patient is high-risk changes what she sees. Verdict: no penetration (it's perceptual learning, not cognitive penetration).

4. In a standard experimental paradigm, a white participant briefly shown a picture of a Black man holding a wallet is more likely to report seeing a gun than when shown a White man holding the same wallet. I think this reflects a change in visual experience itself: racial stereotypes are penetrating perception, not merely biasing a post-perceptual guess. Verdict: penetration.

5. A banana still looks yellow to me under bluish indoor lighting, even though the spectral information reaching my eye would correspond to gray. I think this is color constancy — a built-in operation of the visual system — and not cognitive penetration by my knowledge that bananas are yellow. Verdict: no penetration.

6. When I'm thirsty, a glass of water looks closer to me than when I'm not thirsty. I keep going back and forth on this one. It feels similar to the backpack case, but I'm not convinced the visual experience itself changes rather than my sense of urgency about reaching for it. Verdict: genuinely unsure, lean toward no penetration.

7. In the phoneme restoration effect, a cough replaces the 's' in the word 'legislature,' but I hear the 's' anyway because lexical context fills it in. I think my knowledge of the word is directly shaping auditory experience. Verdict: penetration.

8. Holding a wide-brimmed hat horizontally makes a doorway look narrower than it does without the hat, because the hat changes the body's action possibilities for passing through. This strikes me as structurally identical to the backpack case. Verdict: penetration.

I can see that some of these verdicts are in tension. Cases 1 and 8 involve bodily or action-related states rather than beliefs or desires in the usual sense, yet I count them as penetration, while case 6 seems similar and I hesitate. Cases 3 and 7 both involve expertise or stored knowledge, but I treat one as perceptual learning and the other as penetration. And case 4 involves a high-level social category affecting perception, while case 5 involves a high-level category — banana — that I insist does not penetrate. So what distinguishes them?

What I want: a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures as many of these verdicts as possible. State the principle precisely, show how it handles each case, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and why. Then apply the principle to a case I haven't listed: a subject who believes, on the basis of a reliable cue, that a gray disc on a screen is actually a white surface in shadow — does the disc look white to them, or does it look gray? Finally, if the principle forces me to revise my verdict on case 6, tell me which way it cuts and why.
