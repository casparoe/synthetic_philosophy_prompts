I'm a software engineer, and I keep noticing that I have strong but maybe inconsistent reactions to a certain kind of sentence: "the reason for X is Y" when no actual person ever had Y in mind. Some cases feel totally fine, others feel like just-so stories, and I can't state the rule that seperates them.

Here are my gut verdicts:

- A nature documentary says the reason a gazelle is fast is to escape predators. No gazelle or creator ever had that thought, and it still seems completely right to me.

- Our CI pipeline has a lint rule nobody understands; the engineer who added it is long gone. We tried deleting it once and an invoice bug appeared in production, so we restored it. Now the team says the reason for that lint rule is to prevent that invoice bug, even though the original author wasn't thinking about it and the rest of us only know that removing it breaks stuff, not why. I'm fine with this.

- A teammate wrote a weird caching layer. When I asked why, he said it was to cut latency. He later admitted he'd copied it from StackOverflow and didn't know what it did; the latency explanation was something he invented afterward. I do not want to say cutting latency was the reason for the caching layer, even if it happens to cut latency.

- Our architecture review board approved a design, and each member had their own private reasons for voting yes; no single member could have stated the overall rationale. Later the design turns out to have a surprisingly coherent logic. I'm torn about whether it's right to say the board's rationale was that logic.

- When I think about a stranger in pain, I want to say there's a reason to help them even if no one in the world actually has that reason or acts on it. That feels non-negotiable, but it sure looks like the same free-floating kind of reason I'm suspicious of elsewhere.

My only working theory is that it's okay when the rationale explains why the thing is there or why it stuck around. That handles the lint rule and the caching layer, but it seems to say there's no reason to help the stranger in pain, which I refuse to accept.

Is there a principle — or maybe a small set of distinctions — that captures when it's legitimate to talk about a reason without a reasoner, and which of my verdicts would it force me to drop or reinterpret? Please answer as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, no introduction or conclusion, in American English: (1) state the principle or principles; (2) apply it to the cases I called fine; (3) apply it to the caching-layer case and the board case; (4) say what it predicts about a case I haven't listed; and (5) tell me which of my gut verdicts I'd have to give up or tweak.
