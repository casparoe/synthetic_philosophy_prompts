ok so i'm teaching an intro ethics unit and i want to use le guin's "the ones who walk away from omelas" to get at scanlon's contractualism, specifically the aggregation problem. the story's obviously been done to death with utilitarianism but i think the contractualist angle is actually more interesting and way less discussed.

for anyone who hasn't read it in a while: omelas is a utopian city whose happiness and prosperity depend on a single child being kept in abject misery in a locked basement room. everyone learns about the child once they're old enough. most people rationalize it and stay; a few just walk away, silently, into the mountains, and nobody knows where they go.

scanlon's view (roughly) is that an act is wrong if it would be disallowed by principles that no one could reasonably reject. and he's famously skeptical of aggregating small benefits across many people to justify imposing a serious burden on a few — the whole point of contractualism is that each person has a kind of veto, and you can't just outvote someone's suffering by piling up benefits on the other side of the ledger.

so here's what i want: a reading of the story through scanlon's lens that doesn't just say "scanlon would disapprove of omelas." obviously he would. i want to know where the story puts pressure back on the theory. like: the citizens do have a principle they're acting on — something like "the immense flourishing of thousands justifies the suffering of one." scanlon would say that principle is one the child could reasonably reject. fine. but what principle do the ones who walk away act on? they don't free the child, they don't organize resistance, they don't even say anything. they just leave. is walking away a contractualist response or is it a failure of contractualism to say anything constructive about what you do after you reject the principle? and what about the fact that the child never gets a say — is the problem that the child wasn't consulted, or that the outcome is bad for the child? those come apart in scanlon's framework and i think the story helps you see why that distinction matters.

also: the story makes a point of saying the child's suffering is necessary — the prosperity literally depends on it. it's not a contingent policy choice. does that change the contractualist analysis? scanlon's objection to aggregation isn't that the tradeoff is miscalculated; it's that the structure of justification is wrong regardless of the numbers. but le guin forces you to sit with the "what if it really is necessary" version of the problem, which most philosophy papers dodge by making the tradeoff contingent and therefore avoidable.
