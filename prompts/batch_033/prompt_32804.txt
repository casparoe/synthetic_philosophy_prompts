I've been rereading Ishiguro's Never Let Me Go and I keep thinking there's something about it that the standard readings miss. The standard line is basically "utilitarianism bad, the clones are sacrificed for the greater good and that's horrifying." Which, sure, but the novel spends way more time on the small-scale caring relationships between the characters — Kathy becoming a carer, her relationship with Tommy and Ruth, the way the guardians at Hailsham try (and fail) to genuinely care for the students. It feels like the novel is less about the injustice of the system and more about what it even means to care for someone when the whole structure of your world is built on not seeing them as full persons.

I've been reading some care ethics lately — Gilligan, Noddings, the whole ethics of care vs. ethics of justice distinction — and it seems like it might be a better lens for the novel than the usual rights-based or utilitarian frameworks. But I'm not sure the fit is clean. Care ethics emphasizes that moral obligations arise from relationships and interdependence, not from abstract principles. But the relationships in the novel are themselves shaped by an unjust system — the students form attachments to each other and the guardians form attachments to them, but the whole thing is built on a foundation of exploitation. Can a care ethics framework make sense of that? Does it have resources for distinguishing genuine care from the kind of "caring for" that's really just managing someone toward their death? Like, the guardians clearly think they're caring for the students — they give them art, education, a kind of dignity — but they also never tell them the truth or fight the system. Is that care or is it complicity dressed up as care?

Also, the novel's ending — Kathy standing alone in a field, imagining Tommy, having lost everyone she cared for — feels like it's making a claim about what care can and can't do. Is it saying that care is valuable even when it can't change outcomes? That seems like a core care ethics idea, but the tone is so bleak I'm not sure it's an endorsement. It might be closer to saying that care is all there is, and that's not enough.

I'm trying to write something for a book club and I want to give a reading that takes both the novel and the philosophy seriously — not just using the novel as an illustration of care ethics, and not just using care ethics as a vocabulary to describe the plot. What would a care ethics reading of Never Let Me Go actually look like? Where does it illuminate things the standard readings miss, and where does the novel push back against care ethics or show its limits? And is there a scene or a relationship in the novel that a care ethicist would read very differently than someone coming from a justice or rights framework would?
