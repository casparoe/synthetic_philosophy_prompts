ok so in my civics class we did a mock trial with a three judge panel, and instead of just voting on who wins our teacher made us vote on the legal questions seperately. the rule was that the company is liable only if the contract was valid AND they breached it. the votes came out like this:

judge 1: contract valid, no breach -> not liable
judge 2: contract not valid, breach -> not liable
judge 3: contract valid, breach -> liable

so two judges thought the contract was valid, two thought there was a breach, but two voted not liable, so the company won even tho a majority agreed with each part of the other side's case. my teacher said real courts and commitees vote like this all the time and called it the discursive dilemma.

what i want to know is what this practice actually presupposes about what a group believes. does "the court" have beliefs the way a single person does, or is that just a metaphor? is it a problem that the group ends up saying the contract was valid, and there was a breach, but no liability — like is the group being irrational, or is consistency only a requirement on individual judges? and if we decided the group's final position has to be logically consistent no matter what, what would have to change — would courts have to vote on the outcome first, or stop treating the majority vote on each issue as the court's view? also is there a difference between each judge being rational and the court being rational, and does the practice need the second thing or just the first?
