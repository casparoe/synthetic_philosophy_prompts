Two members of my department got into a protracted argument after a colloquium on the foundations of quantum mechanics, and neither would concede. I took notes because it seemed like a genuinely substantive disagreement, and I would like you to adjudicate it move by move. Here is roughly how it went:

(1) A said that Bell's theorem shows that any theory that reproduces the predictions of quantum mechanics must be non-local — that is, it must allow for influences between space-like separated events. He called this the standard takeaway from the 1964 paper and from the subsequent experimental tests of Bell inequalities by Aspect and others.

(2) B replied that this is too quick. Bell's theorem, she said, rules out the conjunction of three assumptions: locality, realism (or more precisely, the assumption that measurement outcomes are determined by hidden variables that exist prior to measurement), and measurement independence (the assumption that the choice of measurement settings is not correlated with the hidden variables). She said that the theorem only forces you to give up at least one of these, and that while most physicists give up locality, it is logically possible to give up realism or measurement independence instead. So Bell's theorem does not prove non-locality; it proves that you cannot have all three.

(3) A answered that this is technically true but misleading. Giving up realism — in the sense of denying that measurement outcomes have definite values before measurement — is just to accept the standard Copenhagen interpretation, which is already non-local in its treatment of measurement, given the collapse postulate. And giving up measurement independence, he said, is to embrace superdeterminism, which amounts to a conspiracy theory about the universe: it requires that the experimenter's choice of measurement angle is somehow correlated with the hidden state of the particle pair in precisely the way needed to mimic quantum correlations. He said superdeterminism is not a serious option.

(4) B said that A was now arguing about plausibility, not about logical entailment. She said that superdeterminism may be unappealing, but it is not logically ruled out by Bell's theorem, and that 't Hooft and others have taken it seriously. She also said that there are interpretations of quantum mechanics — such as the many-worlds interpretation — that are local and deterministic, and that they avoid Bell's conclusion by giving up the assumption that measurements have unique outcomes, which is a denial of realism in the relevant sense. So the claim that 'Bell's theorem proves non-locality' is false: it proves that certain combinations of assumptions cannot all hold, and different interpretations give up different ones.

(5) A responded that many-worlds is not local in the relevant sense because the branching structure is global and non-separable, and that the only way to avoid non-locality is to accept something even more radical, like superdeterminism or the denial of any objective measurement outcomes. He said that when people say 'Bell's theorem proves non-locality', they mean that the theorem shows that nature cannot be both local and realist in the way classical physics assumed, and that since the experimental violations of Bell inequalities are now well-established, we must accept that the world is not locally real. He said B was making a philosophical point that no working physicist cares about.

(6) B said that the distinction between 'nature is non-local' and 'nature is not locally real' is precisely the philosophical point that matters, and that it is not irrelevant to physics: the difference between, say, de Broglie–Bohm theory (which is non-local but realist) and many-worlds (which is local but denies unique outcomes) has consequences for how we think about the structure of the theory, even if both reproduce the same observational predictions. She said that Bell himself was careful about this distinction, and that the sloppy way the theorem is often summarised obscures what it actually shows.

Please adjudicate this exchange. For each of the six moves, say whether the speaker is broadly correct, partly correct but missing something, or mistaken, and briefly justify your assessment. Then say what, if anything, both parties are missing, and whether there is a fact of the matter about who is more right overall.
