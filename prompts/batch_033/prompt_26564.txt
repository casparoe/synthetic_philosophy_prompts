I help run a tenants' union in my city, and we've developed this informal practice that I'm starting to find philosophically puzzling. When we're planning a campaign — say, pressuring a landlord to fix a building-wide heating problem — we debate strategy and tactics. If someone who owns a rental property themselves (even a small one they inherited) tries to argue for a more moderate approach, they get shut down pretty fast. "You're a landlord, you don't get to tell us how hard to push." The criticism isn't engaged with on its merits; it's dismissed because of who said it. But a few weeks ago, a city councilmember who's been genuinly helpful to us proposed the exact same moderate strategy, and we took it serious, debated it, and ended up adopting parts of it. When I pointed out the inconsistency, the response was: "it's different — the councilmember isn't a hypocrite, she's just making a tactical argument. The landlord is telling us to go easy on landlords."

So the practice seems to be: we filter criticism through the identity and commitments of the speaker, and we treat hypocrisy as a defeater for someone's standing to even be heard. But we don't have a worked-out theory of when exactly someone loses standing, or whether the standing question is about sincerity, epistemic reliability, or something else. I want to understand what theory of standing to blame (or standing to criticize, or moral standing — I'm not sure of the right term) this practice presupposes. Does it commit us to the view that blame is a kind of speech act that requires the speaker to be in a particular normative relation to the content — that you can't invoke a norm you yourself violate? Or could it be grounded in something more pragmatic, like the idea that a hypocrite's criticism is unreliable as a signal of genuine problem-detection? And is the practice stable — can we really have a norm that says "arguments from hypocrites get less weight" without sliding into "arguments from people we don't like get less weight"?
