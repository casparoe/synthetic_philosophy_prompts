I was at a dinner party last week and got into an argument with my friend's husband who works in evolutionary biology. He was going on about how certain moral intuitions — like the sense that parents have special obligations to their own children — are clearly products of kin selection, and therefore they're not really "moral truths" in any deep sense, just evolutionary tricks. I pushed back and said that even if the intuition has an evolutionary origin, that doesn't by itself tell us whether it's a genuine moral obligation or not. He rolled his eyes and said "You can't get an ought from an is, man. Hume settled this like 250 years ago."

I've heard the slogan a million times but I realized I've never actually sat down and figured out what it's supposed to amount to, precisly. Because it seems like there are a bunch of different things someone could mean by "you can't derive an ought from an is" — from a narrow logical claim about what can validly follow from premises, to a much stronger claim about the relationship between descriptive facts and normative conclusions, to something almost metaethical about the nature of moral properties themselves. And I'm not sure which version, if any, actually does the work my friend's husband needed it to do in that conversation.

Can you lay out the distinct precise claims that slogan could be making, from the most modest to the most ambitious, and give me a committed verdict on each one — which are defensible, which are false, and which are just sloppy? And crucially, tell me which reading (if any) actually supports the move he was making: going from "this moral intuition evolved" to "therefore it's not a genuine moral obligation." I want to know if I should apologize or double down.
