I am trying to find out what principle, if any, underlies my judgments about cognitive penetration of perception, and I would like you to extract it from my verdicts on the following cases. Knowing that the Müller-Lyer lines are equal does not make them look equal; I do not think belief penetrates that experience. A trained radiologist sees a faint shadow on a scan as a lesion that I cannot see at all, but I do not count this as cognitive penetration—I take it to be perceptual learning. If I am told that a wine is a New Zealand Sauvignon Blanc, it tastes grassier to me than it did a moment before, and this one I do want to count as penetration. A heavy backpack makes a hill look steeper to me, though I am unsure whether that is cognition or only effort. And being told that the duck-rabbit is a duck makes the duck appear, but I can flip it back to the rabbit at will, and I do not count that as penetration either. Is there one principle that captures all or most of these verdicts? Please state it precisely, say which cases motivate which clauses, and tell me what it predicts about two further cases: an acoustically ambiguous token that sounds like /d/ in one word frame and /t/ in another, and an achromatic image of a banana that subjects report as faintly yellow. Be explicit about which of my verdicts, if any, the principle forces me to abandon.
