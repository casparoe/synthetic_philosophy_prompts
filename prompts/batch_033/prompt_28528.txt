I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I've ended up down a rabbit hole about the old knowing-how vs. knowing-that distinction. The way I understand it, one side says knowing how to do something (ride a bike, debug a race condition) is a fundamentally different kind of thing from knowing that some proposition is true, and that trying to cash out the first in terms of the second lands you in an infinite regress — the classic Ryle-style argument. The other side says no, knowing how is just knowing some proposition like 'this is a way to do it,' and the regress only bites if you insist the person has to be consciously consulting the proposition while acting. I keep going back and forth and I don't have the training to see which moves are load-bearing.

So I want you to translate this debate into the vocabulary I actually think in: declarative vs. procedural knowledge, a type signature/spec vs. an implementation, unit tests as evidence of behavior without access to internals, the trained weights of a model vs. the source code that produced them, knowledge distillation, and the difference between passing a test suite and producing the right output for the right reason. Do each side as faithfully as you can, and then give me a residue report: what translates cleanly, what you had to fudge, and what has no counterpart at all in the software vocabulary. Then tell me whether the residue is a sign that the philosophical distinction is messier than it looks, or that my vocabulary is missing something. I'm partly trying to figure out whether 'the model knows how to add' is a metaphor or literally the same distinction.
