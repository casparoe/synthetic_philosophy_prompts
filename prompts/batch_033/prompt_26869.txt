For a graduate seminar in formal political philosophy, I would like a decision tree that takes as its root the acceptance of the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem and sorts respondents into named positions by identifying which property of a collective decision rule they are willing to surrender or reinterpret in response to the theorem. The tree should treat the theorem not as a problem to be refuted but as a result that forces a choice among its assumptions. Please assume the theorem in approximately this form: if a deterministic, ordinal, single-valued voting rule has an unrestricted domain over at least three alternatives, then either it is dictatorial, its range has at most two alternatives, it fails to select a single winner, or it is manipulable.

The audience is graduate students who have read the theorem but have not yet mapped the space of theoretical reactions, so each branch node must be a concrete question or claim they can answer without already knowing which leaf it leads to. Build the tree from the logical space first: identify the relevant conditions or commitments, ask the separating questions, and then locate the named positions as leaves. Include at least these leaves, though you may subdivide or add where the logic demands it: the dictatorial response; the binary-choice retreat; random dictatorship or probabilistic social choice; domain-restricted strategy-proofness; the no-winner or deliberative view; manipulation-tolerant aggregation; and at least one empty combination, such as the conjunction of non-dictatorship, determinism, single-valuedness, unrestricted domain, at least three outcomes, and strategy-proofness.

Please be definite about the branching order: choose the sequence of questions you think is most diagnostic, and do not merely list several possible trees. For each branch node, state exactly what the respondent is committing to. For each leaf, give a one-sentence statement of the position and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Flag any pairs or sets of leaves your tree cannot separate, and for empty combinations state whether they are empty because incoherent, unattractive, or simply underexplored. Finally, include a short note on where the tree depends on contested readings of “strategy-proofness” or “single winner,” since those terms do real work in the argument.
