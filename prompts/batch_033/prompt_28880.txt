Write a dialogue between two equally well-informed readers of Alan Turing's 1950 paper Computing Machinery and Intelligence, focusing exclusively on the section near the end in which Turing treats extrasensory perception as an objection to the imitation game. One reader argues that this section is methodologically continuous with the rest of the paper: if telepathy is a statistically detectable capacity of biological systems, then a machine could in principle be programmed to display it, and a test that did not control for possible telepathic influence on the interrogator would be a biased experiment. The other reader argues that the section is a failure of epistemic hygiene: Turing relaxes the operational standards he applies elsewhere and gives weight to parapsychological claims whose evidential standing and causal mechanism are too poorly specified to justify altering the test in advance. The dialogue should develop in real steps: each speaker should respond to the other's strongest formulation rather than the weakest, should revise their own view when pressed, and should not talk past one another. Neither should be a strawman, and neither should win outright. By the end, each should state the exact condition under which they would accept the other's interpretation of what Turing was doing. Avoid turning the discussion into a general debate about whether ESP exists; keep it on what Turing's handling of ESP reveals, if anything, about his conception of what a fair imitation game must control for.
