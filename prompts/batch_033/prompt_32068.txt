A colleague in my department has taken to arguing that the whole problem of truth is an artifact of grammar. His preferred analogy is pronominal: the expression "that is true," he says, stands to a sentence roughly as a pronoun stands to a noun phrase. Just as "she" lets a speaker avoid repeating a name while still talking about the same person, "that is true" lets a speaker affirm or deny an entire class of sentences without enumerating them — as in "whatever the witness said is true." From this he concludes that truth is not a genuine property, that there is no correspondence relation between true sentences and the world, and that the substantive theories of truth are trying to analyze something the word "true" was never in the business of naming.

I want you to audit this analogy rather than simply take sides. First, lay out the intended correspondence explicitly: what in the pronominal source is supposed to map onto what in the truth-predicate target, and which features of pronouns are carrying the argument. Second, identify the disanalogies. In particular, consider whether "that is true" really inherits its content from an antecedent sentence in the way a pronoun inherits its referent from an antecedent noun phrase; whether the analogy handles uses of "true" where there is no recoverable antecedent sentence, or where the antecedent is unknown, foreign, or embedded in an opaque context; and whether pronouns and "true" behave alike under negation, quantification, and propositional-attitude reports. Third, distinguish the disanalogies that matter for the deflationary conclusion from those that are merely incidental, and say which features of the pronominal source the analogy quietly imports that the target lacks. Finally, tell me whether the conclusion — that there is no property of truth and no work for a substantive theory of truth to do — survives the audit, and what a better analogy for the deflationist's point would be, if any.
