After rereading the relevant chapters for a course I am teaching on intentionality, I have the impression that the dispute between John Searle and Daniel Dennett over original versus derived intentionality has not really progressed since the 1980s, even though each side has elaborated its view at length. Searle and those who follow him treat artifacts like shopping lists and vending machines as having merely derived intentionality: their states are about something only because they inherit content from the beliefs and desires of the human beings who design, use, or interpret them. Human mental states, by contrast, are supposed to have original intentionality, meaning what they mean without any further interpreter. Dennett resists this contrast by way of the wandering two-bitser, a U.S. quarter-detecting vending machine that is transported to Panama, where local coins physically similar to U.S. quarters trigger the same internal state. Once we see how much of the machine's content depends on context, history, and our interpretive stance, Dennett argues, we should conclude that human intentionality is equally derived, or at least that the original/derived distinction cannot do the work Searle wants it to do.

The frustrating part is that both sides seem to accept the same facts about the vending machine and about human cognition, and both agree that the machine's states do not have the kind of self-interpreting character our own thoughts seem to have, yet neither side moves. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not really about whether a property called original intentionality exists, but about what would count as an explanation of intentionality: Searle wants an account that leaves room for the first-person, irreducible character of meaning and understanding, while Dennett wants an account that is continuous with third-person science and evolutionary history. Is that diagnosis right, or is the impasse better understood as a hidden empirical disagreement about cognition, a verbal dispute about the word intentional, two groups answering different questions under one label, or something else? I would like a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement, supported by what each side actually says, and an account of what, if anything, would count as progress: a distinction, an empirical result, a reformulated question, or a reason to think no progress should be expected here.
