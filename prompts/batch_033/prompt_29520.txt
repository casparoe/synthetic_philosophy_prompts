hey! i'm a phd student in phil of language / formal epistemology, and our department is hosting a speaker next month who's been defending a no-truth-value account of indicative conditionals. her line is that 'if A then C' isn't the kind of thing that can be true or false, and that the right norm for asserting one is Adams's thesis: assertability goes by the conditional probability P(C|A). she's very good at brushing off Lewis's triviality results (the ones showing that if conditionals express ordinary propositions and their probability equals the conditional probability, the probability function collapses into something trivial) by saying they only bite views that treat conditionals as propositions — and since she doesnt, the whole business is somebody else's problem. i'm moderating the Q&A after her talk and i want questions that dont let that move end the conversation.

can you draft 8-10 interview questions, each with a follow-up that anticipates her likely reply and pushes one step further? i'd like at least one question on each of the following:

(a) embedding: once conditionals appear under negation, conjunction, or in the antecedents of other conditionals, her account seems to go quiet, but people still reason with these compounds and assign them probabilities. can something like the triviality results be re-run even for her non-propositional view once embeddings are allowed, or does she just deny that the embedded cases have determinate content?
(b) attitudes: if conditionals have no truth conditions, what is the object of belief when someone believes that if A then C, and what are two people disagreeing about when one asserts it and the other denies it? how does she handle updating on a conditional learned from testimony?
(c) the norm itself: why is the assertability norm exactly P(C|A) rather than the probability of the material conditional or something else, and can she state that norm without quietly helping herself to truth conditions or to a probability of the conditional?
(d) the wider triviality landscape: the later results that try to get triviality-like conclusions without assuming conditionals are ordinary propositions, and the tenability results that show Adams's thesis can be satisfied only if she gives something up. which thing is she actually giving up, and does she read the results as showing her view is incomplete rather than refuted?

also end with one question about what would make her give the view up — what linguistic or empirical data would count against it, since she keeps presenting it as a discovery about English rather than a stipulation.

and keep them specific enough that a room of grad students who've read Lewis and Adams would feel the questions are doing real work, not just 'what do you say about x?'
