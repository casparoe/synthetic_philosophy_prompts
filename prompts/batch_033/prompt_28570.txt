I am writing an essay that reads Denis Villeneuve's Arrival (2016), together with Ted Chiang's source story "Story of Your Life," through the lens of Quine's indeterminacy of translation thesis. The film follows a linguist, Louise Banks, as she attempts to decode the written language of the heptapods; mastering their language eventually allows her to perceive time non-linearly, as they do. Quine's thesis, most famously developed in Word and Object via the gavagai thought experiment, holds that translation is radically indeterminate: multiple incompatible manuals of translation can be consistent with the totality of behavioral dispositions, and there is no fact of the matter about which manual is correct — no deeper semantic reality that a sufficiently careful translator could uncover. The film appears to deny this: Louise's translation work converges on a single, correct understanding of the heptapod language, and that understanding reveals something metaphysically true about the structure of time. But I suspect the relationship is more interesting than simple opposition.

Give me a reading that works through the following questions. Where in the film or story would Quine's argument bite hardest — which scenes or translation decisions are genuinely underdetermined by the behavioral evidence Louise has? Distinguish carefully between the kind of indeterminacy Quine argues for (underdetermination of meaning by behavioral evidence) and the kind of linguistic relativity the film dramatizes (language shaping or enabling cognitive capacities, particularly temporal perception). Does the film actually engage the philosophical problem Quine raises, or does it borrow the trappings of a translation puzzle to tell a story about something else? What does the film get right about the experience of radical translation that Quine's behaviorism misses? And what does Quine get right that the film's optimism about convergence obscures? I want the reading to be honest where the fit breaks down rather than forcing the parallel, and I want it to end with a claim about what the work of fiction adds to the philosophical debate that the arguments alone do not.
