I'm putting together a syllabus for an interdisciplinary seminar on self-reference and strange loops — we're reading Hofstadter, Borges, looking at Bach canons, the works — and I plan to screen Christopher Nolan's Inception as a kind of cinematic case study. The concept I want the class to wrestle with is Hofstadter's claim, developed in Gödel, Escher, Bach around tangled hierarchies and the inviolate level. A tangled hierarchy as Hofstadter describes it is a system in which what initially appear to be distinct levels with a clear direction of causation or control turn out to loop back on themselves: the judge is judged by the accused, the rule-making body is governed by the rules it makes, each hand draws the other in Escher's Drawing Hands. Hofstadter argues that every genuine tangled hierarchy of this kind presupposes an inviolate level — a level that sits outside the loop and enables it without itself being caught up in it, like Escher himself drawing both hands, or the physical hardware on which a self-referential software strange loop runs. The inviolate level is what prevents the loop from collapsing into mere circularity or infinite regress: it is the ground that the loop cannot itself supply.

Inception seems almost designed to test this idea. The film's architecture — dreams nested within dreams, with the persistent danger that you can lose track of which level is base reality — puts the inviolate level under pressure from the start. Cob b's to tem is supposed to serve as a marker of the inviolate level: if it falls, you are awake; if it keeps spinning, you are still inside a dream. But the film famously cuts to black before we see whether the top falls on what Cobb takes to be his return home, and Nolan has suggested in interviews that the point is not the ontological status of that final level but Cobb's decision to walk away from the top toward his children. That final gesture raises the question I want to explore with the class: does Inception ultimately vindicate Hofstadter's claim that a tangled hierarchy requires an inviolate level, or does it challenge it?

I'd like you to give me a reading of the film through the lens of the tangled hierarchy and the inviolate level that I can work into my lecture. Specifically: where does the film seem to endorse the necessity of an inviolate level, and what serves as that level at various points in the narrative (the kick, the to tem, the shared dreamer, something else)? Could the inviolate level in Inception turn out to be something other than waking reality — for instance, Cobb's acceptance of his children as real regardless of the ontological status of the level he occupies, or the emotional truth he reaches by releasing his guilt over Mal? Does the film entertain the possibility of a tangled hierarchy that genuinely lacks an inviolate level, and if so, does it present that as incoherent (a hall of mirrors you cannot escape) or as a different kind of thing from what Hofstadter describes — perhaps a loop that is lived rather than analyzed? I'd like a reading that points to specific scenes and structural features of the film, not just the ending, and that is willing to say where the film resists the Hofstadterian framework rather than forcing it to fit.
