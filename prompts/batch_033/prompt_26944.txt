I'm a working software engineer (distributed systems, some compiler work) with no formal philosophical training. I recently came across two ideas in epistemology — the bootstrapping problem and easy knowledge — and I want them restated in the vocabulary I actually use day to day, not introduced through more philosophical jargon.

As I understand it, one influential view is that a belief counts as knowledge when it is produced by a reliable process, where "reliable" means something like: the process outputs true beliefs at a high enough rate. The bootstrapping problem then asks how a person could legitimately arrive at the belief that a given process is reliable. If the only evidence for the process's reliability is produced by the process itself, the justification looks circular. That sounds to me like a health check served by the system it is supposed to be checking, or a compiler that is used to compile itself and whose successful self-compilation is then treated as evidence that the compiler is correct. Is that all there is to it, or am I flattening something?

The second idea, easy knowledge, seems to involve a closure principle: if you know P, and you know that P entails Q, then you can come to know Q without any further checking. The example I've seen: I look at a wall, it looks red, so I know it is red; since a wall's being red entails that it is not a white wall illuminated by red light, I thereby know the lighting isn't tricking me. That strikes me as deriving a claim about the illuminant from an object label produced by a vision pipeline whose white balance I never calibrated — or as applying a schema-level implication to a sensor reading and treating the result as if it were a fresh measurement. Is that diagnosis right?
