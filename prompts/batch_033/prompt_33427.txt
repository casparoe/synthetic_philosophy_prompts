Marcus pushed back. He said Clara was running together two different things. The original inversions were surprising because they showed how a transparently dumb process (natural selection, or a Turing machine grinding through a program) could yield smart-looking outputs. With LLMs, he argued, we don't actually know whether the internal process is dumb in the same way, because we don't understand the internal computations well enough yet. The model might have developed something functionally equivalent to compressed world-models and rudimentary reasoning — not comprehension in the full human sense, but not the total absence of it either. Calling it "competence without comprehension" assumes exactly what's at issue.

Clara replied that this is precisely the move Dennett warns against: when the system gets impressive enough, people start attributing comprehension to it because they can't imagine how mere mechanism could produce the output. She said the whole point of the inversion is that our intuition about what requires comprehension is systematically unreliable, and Marcus was falling into the very trap the inversion was designed to expose. The fact that we can't trace the LLM's reasoning the way we can trace a chess engine's search tree doesn't license an inference to comprehension inside — it just means the mechanism is opaque to us, and opacity is not evidence of understanding.

A thermostat has it in a trivial sense; a chess engine has more; an LLM might have still more. So we have the emergence of competent behavior from a process that lacked comprehension, which is precisely the inversion. Whether the resulting system "comprehends" in some deflationary sense is a secondary question; the core Darwinian-Turing move is about how you get there from a starting point of zero comprehension, and on that point the LLM story is a clean instance.
