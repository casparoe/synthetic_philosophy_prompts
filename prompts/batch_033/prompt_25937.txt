I'm taking a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of mind, and we've been working through functionalism and its discontents. I've found myself tangled in a version of what I think is called the grain problem, and every attempt I make to escape it seems to pull me deeper into trouble. I'd like someone to walk me through the options with clear costs attached.

Here is the argument as I understand it. (1) If functionalism is true, mental state types are individuated by their causal-functional roles: the network of typical causes, typical effects, and interactions with other mental states. (2) A causal-functional role can be specified at indefinitely many levels of grain. Take pain: at a coarse grain, it is caused by bodily damage and causes avoidance behavior; at a finer grain, it is caused by C-fiber firing under specific conditions and causes specific motor routines via specific pathways; at a still finer grain, it is caused by particular patterns of neurotransmitter release and causes particular muscle contractions. These are different functional profiles, and a physical system can satisfy one without satisfying another. (3) There is no non-arbitrary way to select the correct grain. If you pick a grain fine enough to exclude the systems you do not want to count as having pain — a simple robot that avoids sharp objects — you risk excluding systems you do want to count — a mollusk whose nociception runs on different neural hardware. If you go coarse enough to include everything you want, you let in too much. (4) If there is no non-arbitrary grain, then whether a given physical system instantiates a given mental state depends on an arbitrary choice about how to describe its functional organization. (5) But whether a system is in pain is not an arbitrary matter — it is an objective fact about the system. (6) Therefore, functionalism is false.

Now here is why I said every escape makes things worse. The obvious move is to reject (3) and insist there is a principled grain: the level at which psychological generalizations hold, or the level cognitive science cares about. But the reasoning in (2) through (4) does not trade on anything special about mental states. It applies to any functional kind — being a heart, being a memory system, being a carburetor. If we reject (3) for mental states by appealing to psychology's explanatory interests, then we seem committed to saying that whether something is a heart also depends on our explanatory interests, and that the distinction between a heart and a particular arrangement of cells is interest-relative. That might be defensible, but it is a much larger bullet than I wanted to bite. So the argument seems to prove too much: either functionalism is false, or functional kinds across the sciences are interest-relative in a way that makes many people uncomfortable. I want to keep functionalism — I think multiple realizability is a genuine phenomenon and functionalism is the best account of it — but I also do not want to relativize biological kinds to human interests. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
