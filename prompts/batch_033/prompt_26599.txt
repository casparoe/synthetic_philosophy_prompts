I've been trying to untangle two different objections that people raise against markets in things like kidneys, votes, and disaster supplies. They often get lumped together, but I think they're doing different work, and I want to understand whether they really are distinct and which one does the heavy lifting in different cases.

The first objection is that putting a price on certain goods corrupts or degrades them — the good itself changes in meaning when it becomes a commodity. A vote stops being a civic act and becomes a piece of property. A kidney becomes just another thing to be inventoried and priced. The market doesn't just distribute the good; it transforms what the good is.

The second objection is that the real problem is background injustice. The reason kidney markets are troubling is that sellers would be desperate and poor while buyers would be rich enough to exploit that desperation. On this view, the market itself isn't corrupting — it's the conditions under which the transaction would take place that are the problem. Fix the inequality, and the objection dissolves.

I want to compare these two accounts. Specifically, I want to know: are they genuinely different, or can the corruption account be reduced to the exploitation account once you spell it out? One way to test this is to ask what each account would say about cases where the two come apart. For example, if you could eliminate background inequality for a particular market — say, by guaranteeing that everyone who sells a kidney does so from a position of genuine economic security — would the corruption objection still have force? And conversely, are there cases where the corruption objection seems clearly right but the exploitation objection struggles to explain what's wrong — like vote selling, where the harm seems to be about the integrity of the democratic process rather than about who's buying and who's selling? Use examples like kidney sales, price gouging after natural disasters, and vote selling to work through where the two accounts converge and where they diverge, and say which account you think is more fundamental and why.
