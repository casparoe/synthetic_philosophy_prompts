An article I read recently gave a quick explanation of inverse reinforcement learning for a general technical audience, and I'd like to know how much of it I should trust. The article summarized the approach as follows:

"Inverse reinforcement learning attacks the alignment problem from the right direction. Ordinary reinforcement learning starts with a reward function and learns a policy to maximize it; IRL inverts this, watching an expert's behavior and recovering the reward function she must be optimizing. It is essentially the same logic as revealed preference theory in economics: if you observe enough choices, the chooser's underlying utility function can simply be read off from her behavior. That is why IRL is often called the key to aligning AI with human values — once we have recovered the human reward function from demonstrations, we can hand it to a reinforcement learning agent, and the agent will do what the human actually wants. The remaining obstacles are mainly computational, having to do with the amount of demonstration data and compute required, and modern deep IRL methods have made significant progress on them."

I have a vague sense that this makes the whole project sound more settled than it is, but I don't know enough to say exactly where it goes wrong — or whether it goes wrong at all. Could you walk me through it claim by claim? I'd like to know which claims a careful researcher in this area would accept, which ones they would find subtly misleading or overstated and why, and whether the article's concluding implication — that IRL, once it recovers a human's reward function from behavior, gives us a way to make AI do what the human actually wants — actually follows from the accurate parts.
