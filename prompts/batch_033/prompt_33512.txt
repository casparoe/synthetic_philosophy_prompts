I've been teaching an undergraduate course on rational choice theory for a few years now, and I've found myself defending a fairly hardline position on the sunk cost fallacy: that honoring sunk costs is always irrational — not just usually, not just in the textbook cases, but always, across every context and every specification. By "honoring sunk costs" I mean treating a cost you've already incurred and cannot recover as a reason, in itself, to continue with a course of action, over and above what the forward-looking costs and benefits would dictate. I take the standard view seriously: rational agents should make decisions exclusively on the basis of marginal costs and marginal benefits going forward, and past expenditures are, strictly speaking, irrelevant to the decision at hand.

But I've noticed that the harder I push this line in seminar, the more edge cases my students and colleagues throw at me. Some are easy to dismiss — the person who finishes a bad meal because they paid for it is just making a mistake, and the person who keeps pouring money into a failing project out of pride isn't a counterexample to the normative claim, just evidence that people are irrational. But others are trickier. What about cases where abandoning a project would signal to collaborators or funders that you're unreliable, and the reputation cost is genuinely forward-looking? What about cases where the sunk cost carries information you'd otherwise lack — say, a firm that has invested heavily in a technology and, precisely because of that investment, now has private information about its promise that an outsider couldn't recover? What about the emotional or psychological costs of admitting waste to oneself, which are themselves forward-looking costs? And then there are the deeper, more structural challenges: if you're a creature who knows you'll face future temptation to abandon projects prematurely, might it be rational to cultivate a standing disposition to honor sunk costs as a kind of precommitment device or personal rule, even if each individual instance, considered in isolation, looks like a fallacy?

I want to take these challenges seriously rather than just bat them away with definitional moves. So here's what I'm asking: I hold the view that honoring sunk costs is always irrational. Tell me what would count against it. I want the evidence, arguments, and cases that should move me, sorted by how decisive they'd be — from "merely awkward" to "fatal." I also want an honest assessment of whether the view, as I've stated it, has been qualified in ways that insulate it from falsification (for instance, by reclassifying every apparent counterexample as a case where the cost isn't truly sunk, or where the reason isn't truly the sunk cost but something else). And if it has been insulated, I want to know whether that's a problem for a view of this kind, or whether some degree of insulation is appropriate for a normative principle about rationality.

1. **The view restated** (roughly 150 words): Clarify the position I'm defending, drawing distinctions you think are necessary to make it precise enough to evaluate. Distinguish it from nearby claims (e.g., that honoring sunk costs is merely *usually* irrational, or that it's a *descriptive* fact about human psychology). If there are weaker and stronger versions of the view, specify which one I seem committed to and what would count as abandoning it for a weaker one.

2. **Merely awkward cases** (roughly 300 words): Present the challenges that a defender of my view can accommodate without serious revision — cases that look like counterexamples but dissolve under analysis, or that require only minor clarifications of what counts as a sunk cost or what counts as honoring one. Explain why they aren't more threatening, and what principle of the view allows them to be handled cleanly.
