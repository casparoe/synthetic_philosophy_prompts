You are a scholar of Immanuel Kant's moral philosophy, with particular expertise in his views on duties regarding non-human animals. You are writing for an audience of professional philosophers, so you may assume familiarity with Kant's general framework and can focus on fine-grained reasoning.

Before giving your answer, work through the following in your own reasoning:

First, clarify as precisely as you can what Kant's position on animals actually is. What does he say about the grounds on which we have or lack duties to animals? What is the distinction between direct and indirect duties, and what work does it do? What does he think about the moral significance of cruelty to animals, and what is his reasoning for it?

Second, identify the load-bearing joints in Kant's view. Which commitments are essential to his overall system — for example, the identification of moral status with rational nature, the formula of humanity, the architecture of the categorical imperative — and which are more contingent or empirical claims that he might in principle revise without breaking the system?

Now consider the following challenge:

In the 1970s and 1980s, the contemporary animal rights and animal liberation movement emerged with philosophical foundations quite different from Kant's. Peter Singer, drawing on utilitarian reasoning, argued that the capacity to suffer — not rationality — is the relevant criterion for moral considerability. On his view, excluding animals from moral concern simply because they are not human is a prejudice he called speciesism, structurally analogous to racism or sexism in its arbitrariness. Tom Regan, working within a broadly deontological framework, argued that many animals are subjects of a life — beings with a unified psychological existence over time, with beliefs, desires, memories, and a welfare that matters to them — and that this status confers inherent value, which in turn grounds moral rights, including the right not to be harmed or used as a mere resource for human benefit.

Your task: Imagine Kant could read Singer and Regan. Would he find any of their arguments compelling? Would he revise any of his own views about duties regarding animals, or would he reject their challenges outright? What would his response most likely be, given the deepest commitments of his system?

Do not merely summarize what Kant said. Reason forward from his principles as he might have done, engaging seriously with the specific arguments of Singer and Regan. Be precise about which parts of Kant's apparatus do or do not permit him to accommodate their claims. Where you think Kant would push back, articulate the pushback in his own terms — for instance, in the language of the categorical imperative, the formula of humanity, or the distinction between persons and things. Where you think he might be moved, explain what in his system would have to give way, and whether the revision would be a minor adjustment or a major structural change.
