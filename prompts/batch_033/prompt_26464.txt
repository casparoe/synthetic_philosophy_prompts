I've been reading about scientific revolutions — the shift from Newtonian mechanics to relativity, from caloric theory to thermodynamics, from phlogiston to oxidation — and I keep noticing a move that seems too convenient. When someone wants to defend the idea that science converges on the truth, they describe the old theory as having been "approximately true" or "true in its limited domain": Newtonian mechanics still works at low speeds, caloric theory got the mathematics of heat flow right even though there is no such thing as caloric fluid. But when someone wants to use the same historical episodes to argue that our current theories will probably be overturned too, they describe those old theories as having been false — not just slightly off, but centrally false, their basic posits nonexistent. The same case gets characterized in opposite ways depending on which argument someone is making. I want to know whether this specific tension has a name in the philosophy of science. Is it just a special case of the pessimistic meta-induction, or is it a distinct problem about how we should characterize the content of superseded theories? And if there are positions that try to resolve it — by saying, for instance, that the old theories were false enough for the induction to go through but true enough for realism to survive — I would like to know what those positions are called and whether the resolution actually works.
