I've got a seminar on Stoicism going, and one of my students came in from an ML PhD and keeps saying things like "the dichotomy of control is just what you do when your policy gradient doesn't reach the environment's transition dynamics." It's glib but it's not entirely stupid, and it's made me curious what a real translation would look like.

So here's what I want from you. Take the Epictetan dichotomy of control — his hard claim from the Enchiridion, that what's up to us (eph' hêmin) is exactly our judgments, desires, aversions, and impulses, and everything else is not up to us, including our bodies, reputations, property, careers — and restate it systematically in the vocabulary of reinforcement learning. Use the full MDP vocabulary: states, actions, policies (π), transition functions, reward, value functions, discounting, the whole thing. Go claim by claim through the dichotomy and say: here's what translates cleanly, here's what you have to bend to make fit, and here's what has no RL counterpart at all.

Then do the residue report. I'm especially interested in two things. First: the Stoics claim your judgments and desires are entirely up to you in a way that your body or reputation aren't. In RL, your policy is "up to you" but it's learned from reward that comes from the environment, and the action space itself is environment-given. So does the "completly up to you" claim survive translation, or does the RL vocabulary expose it as overstated? Second, and from the other direction: the Stoics make a normative move from "you only control X" to "you should only care about X." The MDP formalism is, on its face, silent about that — it tells you how to get what you value, not what to value. Is that silence a limitation of the RL framework, or is it correctly identifying that the Stoics are smuggling in a normative premise with no formal counterpart? Be merciless about the mismatches — those are the interesting part.
