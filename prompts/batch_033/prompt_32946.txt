I'm designing a unit for an undergraduate philosophy seminar on the nature and value of privacy, and I want to build one session around Peter Weir's *The Truman Show* (1998). The premise of the film is familiar: Truman Burbank has lived his entire life, from birth, on an enormous soundstage disguised as a small island town, his every moment broadcast live to a global audience without his knowledge or consent. His relationships — his wife, his best friend, his mother — are scripted roles played by actors; his career choices, his fears (a staged childhood drowning makes him afraid of water), and even his spontaneous impulses are shaped by the show's creator, Christof, who intervenes from a control room to keep Truman inside the set. The film's drama turns on Truman's gradual discovery that something is wrong and his final, desperate effort to escape.

The seminar has already covered the main philosophical theories of privacy's nature and value. We have read the control-based view (privacy as the right to regulate access to oneself and one's information), the intimacy-based view (privacy as what makes close relationships possible by creating contexts of restricted access), the autonomy-based view (privacy as a necessary condition for self-determination and the formation of an authentic self), and reductionist or skeptical views that treat privacy as a derivative value, reducible to other goods like liberty, dignity, or freedom from coercion. I want the students to test these theories against the film rather than simply applying them: does any single theory capture what is distinctively troubling about Truman's situation? If not, what does the gap tell us about the theories?

For the session, I need a careful reading of the film through these competing frameworks. Here are the pressure points I am most interested in. First, the control theory says privacy is about whether one chooses what to reveal and to whom. But Truman does not know he is being watched for most of the film. Can one's privacy be violated without one's awareness, or does the control theory imply that ignorance preserves privacy because no unwanted access is experienced as such? Second, the intimacy theory says privacy enables close relationships by allowing people to share things with some and withhold them from others. Truman has what feel to him like intimate friendships and a marriage — but those relationships are not what he takes them to be. Is the harm here a harm to privacy, or is it a harm to something else entirely — deception, manipulation, the denial of knowledge about the basic conditions of one's own life? Third, the autonomy theory holds that privacy protects self-determination. Christof's interventions clearly undermine Truman's autonomy — the engineered fear of water, the staged death and return of his father — but those interventions are causally downstream of the surveillance. Is the surveillance itself the wrong, or is it merely the instrument by which the autonomy-violating manipulations are enabled? Could there be a version of the show that watched Truman passively, never interfering, and would that still wrong him? And fourth, near the end of the film Christof defends the enterprise by insisting that Truman is free — free to leave if he truly wanted to, free because the world he inhabits is safer and gentler than the real one outside. Is this defense philosophically interesting, or merely villainous sophistry, and what assumption about the relationship between privacy and freedom does it trade on?

I would like your reading to treat both the film and the philosophy with equal seriousness — not reducing the film to an illustration of pre-existing theories but using it to stress-test them, and not distorting the theories to make them fit the film. Where the film complicates a theory, say so; where a theory reveals something about the film that a casual viewer might miss, point that out. And if, at the end, you think the philosophical literature on privacy is missing something that the film makes vivid — something about the connection between privacy and the conditions for having a life that is genuinely one's own — I would be very interested to hear what that is.
