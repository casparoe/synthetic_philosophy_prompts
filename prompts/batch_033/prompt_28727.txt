I have been reading about analogy-making programs that operate in a deliberately tiny world — strings of letters — where the system is asked to complete mappings such as abc → abd, and the interesting cases are the ones in which a rigid rule fails. To solve abc → abd : ijk → ijl, the system needs something like the successor relation applied to the rightmost letter; but when the string is reversed, or when the alphabet runs out at an edge, or when several letters group themselves into a unit, the system may have to let a concept change its identity slightly under the pressure of the failed analogy. In one well-known architecture of this kind, Copycat, the system keeps its concepts in a network called a slipnet, in which successor can slip toward predecessor, left toward right, and same toward opposite, with different degrees of resistance. The general phenomenon is conceptual slippage: a concept bending into a neighboring concept when the current interpretation cannot be made to work.

I would like you to write a short story that makes this phenomenon vivid. You may set it in the laboratory of a researcher who notices the system making slippages no one designed; inside the representational space of such a system, where concepts drift and re-form under pressure; or in an ordinary human setting whose categories begin to slip the way letter-string concepts do. Whatever setting you choose, the story should be fiction first and exposition second: the reader should finish with an intuitive feel for what it is like for a concept to slip, not with a definition. Give the story at least one developed character and a genuine narrative turn, and keep it roughly between 1,000 and 2,000 words.
