Last summer I volunteered at a wildlife rehabilitation center, and the experience left me with a puzzle I cannot resolve. The center would go to extraordinary lengths for a single animal: a veterinarian stayed up all night to stabilize a raccoon that had been hit by a car, donors paid for surgery on a hawk with a broken wing, and volunteers hand-fed orphaned squirrels every few hours until they could be released. Everyone involved treated this as obviously worthwhile. Yet the same people seemed entirely unmoved by the vastly larger amount of suffering going on in the same woods — animals starving over a hard winter, dying slowly of disease, or being eaten alive. We would spend a thousand dollars to save one injured animal we had named, but none of us would have spent a fraction of that to prevent an anonymous wild animal from starving, and no one could say why the line fell where it did.

I would like to know what view of moral obligation this practice actually presupposes. Does it presuppose that we only have reason to intervene in suffering we humans have caused, or that only visible, individual suffering matters while anonymous and "natural" suffering does not? Or is the practice compatible with several different views at once, with emotional closeness doing the real work rather than any principled distinction? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, say whether its parts are coherent with one another, and tell me what would have to change about our practices if we instead started from the view that wild animal suffering matters regardless of its cause or visibility.
