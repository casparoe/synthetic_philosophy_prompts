I've spent years working in applied statistics and machine learning, and I keep running into an inferential move that bothers me — but I don't know whether it has a name, or whether there's a serious literature that tries to justify or circumscribe it.

Here is the pattern. Someone wants to estimate the probability of a compound event. They know, or can estimate, the marginal probability of each component, but they have no information about the joint distribution — getting that information would be most of the problem, or would require experiments nobody has run. So they say, in effect: I know the pieces; I don't know how they fit together; independence is the fitting that adds no extra structure beyond what I already know; therefore I'll multiply the marginals and treat that as my estimate.

A clean example: someone wants to guess how likely it is that both n and n+2 are prime. They know the density of primes near n is roughly 1/log n. They have no particular reason to think primality at n and primality at n+2 are correlated in any systematic way — there's no obvious mechanism linking them, aside from the trivial exclusion of small factors. So they treat the two events as independent and estimate the twin-prime density as roughly 1/(log n)². This estimate is not exactly right — there are known correction factors — but it is strikingly close, and it has guided conjecture in number theory for a century.

Another example: a physicist modeling a many-body system knows the single-particle properties but cannot solve the full interacting Hamiltonian. She replaces the interactions with an average field, which amounts to treating each particle as statistically independent of the others except through a shared mean. The approximation is crude, but it often gives qualitatively correct phase diagrams, and it becomes exact in certain limits.

A third: an engineer building a classifier knows the base rate of each feature given the class, but the training set is too small to estimate the full joint distribution. She assumes the features are conditionally independent, multiplies the likelihoods, and the resulting classifier performs far better than she had any right to expect — even when the independence assumption is demonstrably false.

I am not asking about naive Bayes specifically; that is one engineered implementation. I am asking about the underlying inferential move that all three cases share: treating quantities as independent not because independence has been verified, but because it is the joint distribution that adds the least information beyond the marginals one already has. The move seems to function as a kind of null model — the thing you are entitled to assume until evidence of dependence forces something richer.

This move shows up across an oddly wide range of fields — statistical mechanics, analytic number theory, probabilistic method in combinatorics, machine learning, Bayesian prior selection, and ordinary everyday reasoning about compound risks. It works often enough that people keep making it, but it also fails in systematic and sometimes spectacular ways: when there are latent common causes, or selection effects, or when the very process that generates the observations builds in correlations that the marginals alone cannot reveal.

What I want to know is whether this move — defaulting to independence in the absence of joint information — has a name of its own. I am aware of several neighboring concepts: the principle of indifference, maximum-entropy inference, exchangeability, the idea of a random model in number theory, the naive Bayes assumption in machine learning. But none of these seems to name exactly the thing I am pointing at: the practice of treating independence as the epistemically neutral starting point when you have marginal constraints but no joint constraints, together with the question of when that practice is justified and when it is reckless. If there is an established term, what is its precise definition, and how exactly does it relate to those neighbors — does it reduce to one of them, or is it a distinct principle? If there is no established term, what should it be called, and how should its scope and its limits be drawn? I am less interested in the label than in the carving: what exactly is being assumed when someone makes this move, under what conditions is that assumption innocuous, and is there any formal principle that either vindicates it or condemns it as a fallacy?
