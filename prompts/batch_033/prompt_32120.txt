I have been working through the philosophy of mind for the better part of a year now, and I have come to the view that constitutive Russellian panpsychism is the most defensible answer to the hard problem of consciousness. The position, as I understand and endorse it, holds that the intrinsic nature of the physical is phenomenal (or at least proto-phenomenal), and that the unified conscious experience of a human being is literally constituted by the micro-experiences of its fundamental physical parts standing in the right relations — roughly, that macro-consciousness is built out of micro-consciousness the way a molecule is built out of atoms. I take the primary rivals to be standard physicalism, which I regard as structurally incapable of closing the explanatory gap between third-person description and first-person experience, and strong emergentism or substance dualism, which I find metaphysically extravagant and empirically unmotivated. I grant that the combination problem — the question of how many micro-subjects could sum to a single macro-subject, and why this particular set of micro-subjects yields this particular macro-subject rather than a different one or none at all — is the central difficulty for the view. But I am provisionally persuaded that it is a research problem rather than a refutation: we do not yet possess the full story, but the resources are there, and the alternatives are, in my assessment, worse.

I want to know what would genuinely count against this position. I am not asking for a rehearsal of the standard objections I have already encountered in the literature; I am asking what I, as someone who accepts the view, should regard as evidence or argument that would weaken it, force me to qualify it into triviality, or compel me to abandon it altogether. What would have to be true for constitutive panpsychism to be false? Which assumptions could in principle fail — about the nature of subjects, about the metaphysics of composition, about the relationship between microphenomenal and macrophenomenal properties — and what would the world have to look like for each such failure to obtain? Which of these escape routes are live possibilities rather than merely logical ones? I would like defeaters sorted from fatal to merely awkward, and I would like an honest assessment of which of them a committed panpsychist would herself concede as counting against the view and which she would dismiss as question-begging. I am especially interested in whether the combination problem, as it has been formulated in the recent literature, has been set up in a way that insulates panpsychism from empirical refutation — and if so, whether that is a genuine problem for a metaphysical thesis of this kind, or whether panpsychism is simply the sort of view that must be assessed by comparative theoretical virtue rather than by direct test. Finally, I would like to know whether there are specific empirical findings — from neuroscience, developmental psychology, or fundamental physics — that would tell against panpsychism more than they would against its rivals, and whether any such findings have already appeared.
