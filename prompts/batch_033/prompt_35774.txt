I've been working through the debate between prioritarians and egalitarians, and I've encountered an argument that seems to show prioritarianism is an unstable compromise — it either collapses back into the egalitarianism it was meant to replace, or it lacks any rationale for its central claim. I can't tell whether the argument is sound or where exactly it goes wrong.

Here's the argument:

(1) Prioritarianism says we ought to maximize the sum of weighted individual utilities, where the weight on a person's utility is a strictly decreasing function of their absolute level of well-being.

(2) Prioritarianism was introduced to capture the intuition that benefits to the worse-off matter more, while avoiding the leveling-down objection. That objection says: if you hold that inequality is intrinsically bad, you are committed to the absurd conclusion that leveling down — making some worse off while benefiting no one — is in one respect an improvement.

(3) Prioritarianism claims to avoid this because it does not value equality itself; it values benefits to individuals, weighted by how badly off they are. Leveling down never increases anyone's weighted utility, so it is never better.

(4) But now we can ask: what justifies the concave weighting function? Why should a unit of utility matter less, morally, the better off its recipient is?

(5) If the answer is that inequality is bad, then prioritarianism is a form of egalitarianism and the leveling-down objection returns in full force: if inequality is bad, then reducing it is good even when no one benefits.

(6) If the answer is something else — for instance, that the diminishing moral weight of utility is a basic, unanalyzable feature of the moral landscape — then we face a different problem. Without some deeper rationale, the concave function looks arbitrary. Why this particular function and not a slightly different one? And if we are simply positing a brute moral fact about the shape of the value function, why not posit a linear one and be utilitarians? The prioritarian seems to have no answer that doesn't either beg the question or circle back to the badness of inequality.

(7) So prioritarianism either inherits the leveling-down objection (if its rationale is egalitarian) or rests on an unexplained posit (if it isn't). Neither looks like a stable position.

I suspect the move from (4) to the dilemma in (5)–(6) is too quick, but I can't articulate exactly what the third option would be. What would a non-egalitarian, non-arbitrary justification for the concave weighting function look like? And if there isn't one, which horn should the prioritarian take, and what does taking it cost?
