I'm in a grad seminar on epistemic logic this semester and we've spent like four weeks on the logical omniscience problem — you know, the fact that in standard possible-worlds semantics for knowledge and belief, agents automatically know all logical consequences of everything they know, which is obviously bonkers as a model of actual human reasoning. We've read Hintikka's original setup, the Fagin and Halpern awareness models, Levesque's explicit/implicit belief distinction, some of the impossible-worlds stuff, and a couple of the more recent dynamic approaches. But the thing is, everytime someone proposes a fix, the discussion goes in circles because nobody in the room agrees on what the fix is supposed to *do*. The professor keeps saying "you can't evaluate a solution until you know what you're demanding of it," which is fair, but then we never actually sat down and wrote the list.

So I'm trying to write the desiderata before I start my term paper. What does a formal model of belief (or knowledge) actually have to deliver to count as an adequate response to the logical omniscience problem? I don't just mean "don't make the agent logically omniscient" — I want the positive requirements. Here's the kind of thing I'm wondering about: Should the model distinguish between trivial consequences (modus ponens, say) and nontrivial ones (some deep theorem the agent hasn't worked out yet)? Should it require that the agent can eventually reach any consequence given enough time and steps, or is it okay for the agent to be permanently blind to some entailments? Should it model the *process* of reasoning or just the end state of the agent's belief set at a time? Should psychological plausibility be a hard requirement, or is it fine if the model is a normative idealization with some bounded-resource tweaks? And what about computational tractability — is that a genuine desideratum for a *philosophical* theory of belief, or is that someone else's problem?

I also want to know which pairs of these are in tension. Like, if you want the model to be psychologically realistic about how people actually reason, does that rule out modeling agents as having a perfect deductive engine that just hasn't finished running yet? And if you want closure under some weakened notion of logical consequence, does that conflict with allowing agents to have blatantly inconsistent beliefs (which real people definitely do)?

And finally — if you had to pick the single desideratum that gives existing approaches the most trouble, which one is it and why? I don't need a full scorecard, just the pressure points.
