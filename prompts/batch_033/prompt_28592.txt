I've been working through money-pump arguments and have built one that seems to show something I can't accept — that incomplete preferences are never rational. Here it is, with each step looking airtight to me.

(1) A rational agent can have incomplete preferences: for some options A and B, she neither prefers A to B, nor B to A, nor is indifferent between them — the mark of a hard choice between incommensurable goods.
(2) If she neither prefers A to B nor is indifferent between them, then none of her preferences speaks against taking B over A, so taking B rather than A is rationally permitted.
(3) By the same reasoning, once she holds B, taking A rather than B is rationally permitted.
(4) If each choice in a sequence is rationally permitted at the time it is made, then the sequence as a whole is rationally permissible.
(5) So she is permitted to accept both halves of this offer: pay one cent to exchange A for B, then pay one cent to exchange B for A.
(6) But accepting both halves leaves her with A minus two cents, when she could have kept A for nothing. An agent who is rationally permitted to take a sure loss is rationally defective.
(7) Since nothing in this argument depended on which A and B we chose, the defect generalizes: incomplete preferences are never rational.

I'd much rather keep (1) than accept (7), but I can't locate the bad step, and I don't want to give up money-pump reasoning wholesale, since it seems to do real work elsewhere.
