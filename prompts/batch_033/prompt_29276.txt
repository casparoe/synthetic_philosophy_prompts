At a philosophy reading group I attend, two members got into a long argument about Goodman's "new riddle of induction" and I've been turning it over ever since. Here's the back-and-forth as I remember it:

(1) Chen opened by saying the grue problem shows that enumerative induction is empty unless we have a principled way to restrict which predicates we're allowed to project. "All observed emeralds are green" and "all observed emeralds are grue" are both fully supported by the same evidence — every emerald checked so far has been both green and grue — yet they license opposite predictions about emeralds first observed after time t. So induction needs a rule for picking the right predicates, and that rule can't itself be justified by induction without begging the question.

(2) Maria replied that this makes the problem sound deeper than it is. "Grue" is gerrymandered: it's defined as "green if observed before t, blue if not." We have independent reasons to prefer predicates that don't contain built-in temporal indices — reasons about simplicity, about carving nature at the joints, about explanatory power. We don't need a complete metaphysical theory to see that "green" is a better candidate for projection than "grue," and once we have that, the paradox dissolves.

(3) Chen answered that "independent" is doing all the work. Our preference for simple, non-gerrymandered predicates is itself the product of our inductive history — we've had success projecting predicates like "green" and failure with ones like "grue." So appealing to simplicity just pushes the problem back: why should we expect simple predicates to continue to be reliable? That expectation is itself an inductive projection, and Goodman's challenge applies to it just as much.

(4) Maria said that at this point the argument threatens to become self-defeating. If you can't justify projecting "simple" or "natural" from past cases, then you can't justify projecting anything at all — including the meanings of the words we're using to have this argument. The grue paradox doesn't give us a reason to suspend induction; it just shows that induction can't be grounded in pure logic or pure syntax, which we should have expected anyway. It's a result about the limits of formal epistemology, not about the rationality of induction.

(5) Chen responded that this is exactly what Goodman was trying to show, and that Maria is now agreeing with him while framing it as a rebuttal. The "new riddle" was never meant to make us doubt induction — it was meant to show that induction depends on substantive, non-formal constraints on projectibility, and that these constraints can't be given a purely formal justification. Maria's response concedes the central point and then acts like it's trivial.

(6) Maria said it's not trivial, but it's also not as destabilizing as Chen's framing suggests. The constraints we need — that the world has stable regularities, that some ways of carving it up are better than others — are ones we're already committed to in ordinary reasoning and scientific practice. The grue paradox is an elegant piece of philosophy, but it doesn't require us to change anything about how we actually reason inductively. It's a solution in search of a problem.
