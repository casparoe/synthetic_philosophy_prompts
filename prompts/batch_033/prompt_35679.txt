I am an undergraduate in a philosophy of action seminar on weakness of will, and our final meeting is an open format in which each student contributes a short piece that captures something from the course. I would like to contribute a poem, and I have a specific form in mind: a nineteen-line villanelle about the case we have been reading in which an agent plans to take a larger, later reward, then takes a smaller, sooner reward once it becomes imminent, and then, once some distance has returned, prefers the larger, later reward again and regrets the choice.

I would like the form to carry the argument rather than merely illustrate it. The two refrains should be the same agent speaking from two temporal distances: one refrain the far-sighted self's commitment to the later, larger good, and the other the near-sighted self's capitulation to the nearer, smaller good. A reader who knows the problem should be able to tell which refrain is which. The alternation of the refrains through the tercets should track the way a steeply bowed discount curve lets the smaller reward's present value rise and overtake the larger reward's present value as the smaller reward approaches the present, so that the preference reverses without any new information and without any change in the rewards themselves. The final quatrain, where the two refrains are forced together, should leave the reader seeing that the cool and the hot evaluations belong to one temporally extended agent, and that the puzzle is how a single agent can hold both at once — not the simpler picture of reason being shouted down by desire.

The poem should be accurate enough that someone working on akrasia would recognize that it is tracking the distinction between synchronic weakness of will — acting against one's better judgment at the moment of action — and the diachronic preference reversal characteristic of hyperbolic discounting, and it should leave that distinction visible rather than flatly identifying the case as akrasia. It should also stand on its own as a poem for someone who has not read the literature.
