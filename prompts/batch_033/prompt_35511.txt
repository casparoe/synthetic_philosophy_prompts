I'm taking a module this term on conservative political thought, and we've been working through Burke's Reflections on the Revolution in France alongside Chesterton's shorter essays. I find myself genuinely stuck on a passage from Chesterton's "The Drift from Domesticity" (1929) — the one that gave us the idea of "Chesterton's fence." I'll reproduce the relevant bit below, and then I'll try to explain what's bothering me about it.

> In the matter of reforming things, as distinct from deforming them, there is one plain and simple principle; a principle which will probably be called a paradox. There exists in such a case a certain institution or law; let us say for the sake of simplicity, a fence or gate erected across a road. The more modern type of reformer goes gaily up to it and says, "I don't see the use of this; let us clear it away." To which the more intelligent type of reformer will do well to answer: "If you don't see the use of it, I certainly won't let you clear it away. Go away and think. Then, when you can come back and tell me that you do see the use of it, I may allow you to destroy it."
> 
> This paradox rests on the most elementary common sense. The gate or fence did not grow there. It was not set up by somnambulists who built it in their sleep. It is highly improbable that it was put there by escaped lunatics who were for some reason loose in the street. Some person had some reason for thinking it would be a good thing for somebody. And until we know what the reason was, we really cannot judge whether the reason was reasonable. It is extremely probable that we have overlooked some whole aspect of the question, if something set up by human beings like ourselves seems to be entirely meaningless and mysterious. There are reformers who get over this difficulty by assuming that all their fathers were fools; but if that be so, we can only say that folly appears to be a hereditary disease. But the truth is that nobody has any business to destroy a social institution until he has really seen it as an historical institution. If he knows how it arose, and what purposes it was supposed to serve, he may really be able to say that they were bad purposes, or that they have since become bad purposes, or that they are purposes which are no longer served. But if he simply stares at the thing as a senseless monstrosity that has somehow sprung up in his path, it is he and not the traditionalist who is suffering from an illusion.

Here's my difficulty. In our seminars, we've been treating Chesterton's fence as a tidy encapsulation of Burkean conservatism — the idea that we ought to approach inherited institutions with epistemic humility, that there is a "latent wisdom" in long-standing practices, and that the burden of proof falls on the reformer. But as I read the passage more carefully, Chesterton seems to be making a narrower and stranger argument than Burke does. He doesn't actually say that the fence is likely to be useful, or that tradition is a repository of accumulated wisdom. He says that *until you understand what it was for*, you may not destroy it. The injunction is conditional on your ignorance, not on the fence's probable value. That seems like a purely epistemic stopping rule — a rule about when you're entitled to act — rather than a substantive claim about the likely wisdom of inherited arrangements. Burke, by contrast, seems to me to make a positive claim: that the accumulated reason of institutions deserves our deference because it typically outperforms the private reason of any single individual. So I'm left wondering: is Chesterton's fence really a Burkean argument, or is it something else? Is it a prudential rule of thumb, a moral principle about respecting the agency of those who came before, or a sceptical thesis about the limits of individual reason? And if it is distinct from Burke's position, which one is actually more defensible — and are there cases where the two come apart?

I'd be grateful if you could approach this as a historian of political thought who is also comfortable doing analytic philosophy. Please think carefully and work through the passage sentence by sentence before drawing conclusions. I'm less interested in whether Chesterton's principle is "right" in the abstract and more interested in what kind of argument it actually is, how it relates to Burke's own reasoning, and whether treating them as interchangeable — as our lecturer seems to — obscures something important.
