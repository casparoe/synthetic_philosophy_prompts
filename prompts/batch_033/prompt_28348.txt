You are a professional philosopher specializing in the conceptual foundations of artificial intelligence and AI safety. Before composing your final answer, take some time to brainstorm the strongest possible versions of both sides of the debate; use that to make your defense as honest and precise as possible. Then write a careful, formal philosophical argument.

Your task is to make the strongest possible case for the following position:

The central problem of AI alignment is best understood narrowly, as intent alignment. On this narrow conception, an AI system is aligned when it is robustly trying to do what the particular user or operator who deploys it actually intends for it to do in the relevant context. Alignment, so understood, is not the same as—and should not be identified with—value alignment: the project of making AI systems act in accordance with human values in general, with the interests of all affected parties, or with some independently specified moral standard.

In your response, please do the following:

1. State the narrow conception precisely. In particular, distinguish the user's actual intention from (a) the user's literal instructions, (b) what would be objectively good for the user or for others, and (c) the user's revealed, long-term, or idealized preferences. Explain what it means for a system to be trying to do something, as opposed merely to behaving in a way that happens to satisfy it.

2. Offer at least three distinct arguments for treating intent alignment as the central formulation of the alignment problem. You might consider arguments from tractability; from the moral division of labor between an agent and its principal; from the ill-definedness or instability of any general target called human values; and from the idea that intent alignment already rules out many of the most concerning failure modes, such as deception, manipulation, and reward hacking, in the cases where those would frustrate the user's actual intention.

3. Present the two or three strongest objections to the narrow conception. For example: a system that perfectly does what a malicious or reckless user intends can cause serious harm; users' actual intentions are often indeterminate, inconsistent, or unknown even to themselves; and a focus on a single principal neglects third parties and social externalities. State these objections as charitably and forcefully as you can.

4. Respond to each objection from the standpoint of the narrow conception. Do not strawman the objections or dismiss them by stipulation. Where the narrow conception must make a concession, say so explicitly and explain how much of the original position survives.

Do not rely on citations or external sources, and do not provide a bibliography. The argument should stand entirely on its own conceptual merits, and it should be precise enough that a reader could identify exactly which premise they would reject.
