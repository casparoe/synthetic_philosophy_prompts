I'm teaching Borges's "Pierre Menard, Author of the Quixote" in an undergrad aesthetics class next week, and I want to use it to really get at the question of why we value originals over perfect copies or forgeries. The story gets used in philosophy of art a fair bit, but I want to go deeper than just "context matters." The wild thing in the story is that the narrator actually claims Menard's Quixote is *better* than Cervantes's, even though they're word-for-word identical — because Menard had to work so much harder to arrive at it, and because his version carries the weight of all the history between 1602 and the 1930s. Is this just snobbery dressed up as theory, or is Borges onto a genuine point about aesthetic value? And how does it hook into the broader forgery debates — the van Meegeren Vermeers, the fact that we'd feel cheated if we learned a painting we loved was a fake even though nothing about the canvas changed? I want the students to come away seeing that the forgery problem isn't just about money or authentication, but about what kind of property aesthetic value even is. Can you walk me through how to structure that discussion — what the story gets right, where it maybe overreaches, and what a philosopher who *disagrees* with Borges's narrator would say back?
