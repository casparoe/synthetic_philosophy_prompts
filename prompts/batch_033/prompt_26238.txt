I've been defending a pretty straightforward hedonism about well-being in my ethics seminar this semester — the view that pleasure and only pleasure is intrinsically good for a person, and pain and only pain is intrinsically bad. I came in thinking it was obviously right and now I'm less sure, but not for the reasons I expected. The thing that's really gotten under my skin is the heterogeneity objection. You know: listening to a symphony, eating a good meal, the relief of finally scratching an itch, the drained quiet after a marathon, schadenfreude, the calm of meditation, the buzz of nicotine, the satisfaction of solving a proof — what do these actually have in common? If the answer is "nothing except we call them all pleasant," then hedonism starts to look like it's picked out a gerrymandered category and declared it the good, which isn't much of a theory.

But I'm not ready to give up on it, and I want to think clearly about what would actually count against the view before I jump ship. Not just "here are the standard objections" — I've read those. I want a map of the view's exposure. What observations, what arguments, what thought experiments, what empirical results from psychology or neuroscience would count against hedonism, sorted from fatal to merely awkward? Which defeaters would a committed hedonist themselves concede as genuine problems for the view (as opposed to ones they'd dismiss as question-begging or trading on a confusion about what pleasure is)? And here's what I really want to know: has hedonism been qualified into unfalsifiability? If the hedonist can always say "well, that's a kind of pleasure too" whenever someone points to an apparent counterexample — the satisfaction of a sadistic desire, the calm of a lobotomy patient — is that a problem for the view, or is it just what it means to have a monistic theory of value? I'd rather know if I'm defending a zombie than keep at it for another semester.
