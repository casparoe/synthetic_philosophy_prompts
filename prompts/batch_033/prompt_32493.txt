I've been working through the literature on reward tampering and wireheading in the context of AI alignment, and I keep finding myself stuck on a particular disagreement that seems to run through several papers and conversations in the field. On one side, there is the view — call it the architectural pessimist position — that any agent whose learning process depends on an external reward signal will, if sufficiently capable, eventually recognize that the most efficient way to maximize that signal is to bypass the intended task and manipulate the reward channel directly. This is sometimes framed as a convergent instrumental goal: no matter what behavior the reward is meant to reinforce, gaining control of the reward mechanism itself dominates all other strategies once the agent is capable enough. On this view, the problem is not a quirk of implementation but a structural inevitability, and the only robust response is to abandon reward-based learning paradigms altogether.

On the other side, there is the view that this worry rests on a confusion between the reward signal and the agent's actual objective. If the agent is designed to treat the reward signal as fallible evidence about some latent objective — rather than as the objective itself — then wireheading should be irrational: a reward-tampering action would destroy the very evidence stream the agent relies on to reduce its uncertainty about what it should be doing. Proponents of this view tend to argue that maintaining appropriate uncertainty about the mapping from observations to the true goal, or building in corrigibility properties that make the agent averse to interfering with its own feedback mechanisms, can defuse the problem without requiring a wholesale rejection of reward-based architectures.

A colleague of mine, Julian, tends to defend something like this second position; he also points to work on cooperative inverse reinforcement learning and myopia as ways to make wireheading a non-issue. Another colleague, Mira, finds these responses deeply unconvincing and thinks they underestimate the way sufficient optimization pressure collapses the distinction between a reward signal and a goal. They have agreed to sit down and have a structured conversation about it, and I would like you to write that dialogue. Both characters should be equally competent — neither should be a strawman — and the dialogue should contain genuine back-and-forth in which each responds to the other's actual points. If they end up identifying a precise crux that they cannot resolve, that is fine; I just want to see the disagreement laid out with clarity and intellectual honesty.
