I have been reading metaethics on my own, and I keep noticing a pattern I do not have a word for. Here are two representative instances. A powerful being threatens to make your life go very badly unless you admire him. It is hard to deny that the threat gives you some sort of reason to admire him — or at least a reason to try to bring it about that you admire him — and yet, by stipulation, there is nothing about him that makes admiration appropriate; the threat gives you a reason without making him any more admirable. A second instance: someone offers you a large sum of money to desire a saucer of mud for its own sake. Again, you seem to have a reason to desire the mud, but the mud does not thereby seem desirable, and the money does not seem to make the mud any more worth desiring. I have also seen the same shape in discussions of belief, where a bribe to believe something is treated as a consideration of a wholly different kind from evidence that the thing is true, even though both can be reasons to get oneself to believe it.

I want to know whether this family of cases has an established name, and if it does I want the boundaries rather than just the label. In particular: are the desire cases and the belief cases the same phenomenon or merely analogous? What exactly marks the difference between the two kinds of consideration — is it that one bears on the attitude itself while the other bears on the object, or is the real difference elsewhere? And does drawing this distinction commit one to saying that some things are admirable or desirable in themselves, or can the distinction be drawn without that commitment? If there is no established name, I would want a coined one with a definition and boundary cases.

Please structure your response in four sections: (1) Identification, 100–200 words, on whether an established name exists and what it is, with the caveat that a bare label will not answer my question. (2) The core distinction, 300–400 words, giving a precise account of what separates the two kinds of consideration and showing how the admiration, desire, and belief cases each fit. (3) Boundaries and pressure points, 200–300 words, covering the hardest cases for the distinction, including whether the desire cases and the belief cases are really the same phenomenon and whether the distinction presupposes that some things are appropriate objects of admiration or desire in themselves. (4) Verdict, 100–150 words, summarizing how you would define the phenomenon and what it is not.
