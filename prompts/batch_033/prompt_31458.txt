I'm taking a course on medieval Islamic philosophy, and we've spent three weeks on Al-Ghazālī's occasionalism. My professor keeps returning to the slogan "God is the only true cause" as though it settles every dispute — she's used it to dismiss Avicennan secondary causation, to push back against students who ask whether occasionalism makes natural science pointless, and to argue that the debate between occasionalism and concurrentism is merely verbal. The problem is, I'm becoming convinced that "God is the only true cause" doesn't pick out a single thesis at all; it seems to slide between different claims depending on what she needs it to do.

I'd like you to lay out the distinct precise theses that "God is the only true cause" could be expressing, from the most radical occasionalist reading — the one where God recreates the world at each moment and no created thing has any causal power whatsoever, so that fire does not burn cotton, God does — to more moderate positions that might still be summarized with the same words. For each reading, I want to know: (a) what exactly it asserts and what it denies, (b) whether it's a defensible philosophical position, and (c) whether it would actually do the work my professor puts the slogan to — specifically, does it rule out Avicennan secondary causation, does it threaten the intelligibility of doing natural science, and does it collapse the distinction between occasionalism and concurrentism? Ideally, tell me which reading Al-Ghazālī himself probably held (given that he also wanted to preserve human moral responsibility), and which reading my professor is most likely invoking when she uses the slogan as a conversation-stopper.
