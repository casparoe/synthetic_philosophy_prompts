I've been trying to think through Le Guin's "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" using Parfit's nonidentity problem, and I keep going in circles. Here's the setup: the city's happiness depends on one child kept in abject misery, and if the child were freed the whole city would collapse and none of its inhabitants would exist. The nonidentity problem says you can't wrong someone by bringing them into a worthwhile existence, even if you could have brought a different person into a better one. So the citizens might say: we haven't wronged the child because without the arrangement the child wouldn't exist at all, and we haven't wronged ourselves because we wouldn't exist either. But that feels like a perversion of the nonidentity problem, not an application of it. Where exactly does the analogy break? Is it that the child's life probably isn't worth living? Is it that the arrangement is ongoing rather than a one-time choice at conception? And what about the ones who walk away — is their decision philosophically coherent, or are they just opting for a meaningless gesture? I want to know whether Omelas is a genuine counterexample to something in Parfit or whether Le Guin is just working a different moral problem entirely.
