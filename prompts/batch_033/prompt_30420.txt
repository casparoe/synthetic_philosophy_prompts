I am a novelist, and several times a year I face the same decision in a sharper form than most writers admit: a trusted editor or early reader tells me that a scene, a character, or an ending does not work, I disagree, and I must decide whether to revise on their say-so or hold to my own judgment. I know enough about the epistemology of disagreement to see that my own conviction is only one piece of evidence, and that evidence about my reliability — that I am exhausted, that I am attached to what I wrote, that this reader has been right before — is a different kind of evidence that should not be treated as just another first-order consideration. But I also know there is a live dispute over whether it can ever be rational to believe p while believing that my evidence says I am unreliable about p, and I do not know how to bring that dispute to bear on a working deadline. Please write me a reusable decision procedure, grounded in the higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia literature, that I can run whenever a trusted reader and I disagree about a substantive revision. Fix the role and stakes: I am the final authority on the manuscript, revisions are costly in time and confidence, and I need an answer within a few days. The procedure should say which questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it tells me to defer even when I feel strongly, where it tells me to hold firm, and where it must hand the decision back to unaided judgment because no rule settles it. Then apply it to two cases: a developmental editor with a strong track record says my ending is unearned while I am convinced it is the only right ending; and a first-time beta reader I respect says a secondary character acts inconsistently while I think the reader has missed the point of the character. Include at least one case in which the procedure tells me to do something I will not like, and be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.
