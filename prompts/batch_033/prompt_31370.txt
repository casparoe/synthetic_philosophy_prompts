Here's a thing I keep getting stuck on. In criminal law we distinguish between doing something intentionally, knowingly, recklessly, and negligently — the mens rea categories. The whole system of punishment severity is built on this. And courts treat the question "did the defendant act intentionally?" as a factual question for the jury, like any other factual question.

But the Knobe effect from experimental philosophy seems to show that ordinary people's judgments of intentionality are themselves shaped by moral judgments. Show someone a CEO who disregards a harmful side effect, and they say the harm was intentional. Show them the same CEO with the same disregard but a helpful side effect, and they say the help wasn't intentional. The only variable is the moral valence of the outcome.

So what does the legal practice presuppose about the relationship between factual and moral judgment? Does it presuppose they're seperable — that you can determine whether someone acted intentionally without first making a moral judgment about what they did? And if the Knobe effect is right that they're not seperable in ordinary cognition, is the practice in trouble, or does it just mean the law is using a technical notion of intentionality that doesn't have to answer to folk psychology? And if it's the latter, what does that do to the justification for punishing intentional conduct more harshly — if "intentional" in the legal sense doesn't track what people actually mean by the word, why does it matter that it was intentional?
