"Taurek's central argument is that when you must choose between saving one person and saving five, the losses involved are not additive across persons. Each of the five stands to lose their life, and the one stands to lose theirs; there is no additional loss or harm that anyone bears beyond that. Since no individual has a greater claim than any other — each person stakes their whole life, and these stakes are equal — Taurek concludes that the mere fact that five is a larger number than one does not in itself give us a reason to save the five. The fair procedure, he thinks, is to flip a coin, which gives each person an equal chance of rescue. The difficulty with Taurek's argument is that it only works if we accept what Rawls called the separateness of persons, which Taurek explicitly invokes as a premise. But the separateness of persons is a Rawlsian doctrine about distributive justice, not about life-or-death rescue cases, and applying it here is a category mistake. Furthemore, Taurek himself seems to concede the aggregative point when he proposes the coin flip: by giving each person a 50% chance, he is effectively treating the five collectively as five times as important as the one, since the expected number of lives saved by a coin toss is three — which is simply the aggregate of the individual chances. So his own procedure smuggles the numbers right back in."
