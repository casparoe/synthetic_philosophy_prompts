I've been going down a bit of a rabbit hole with experimental philosophy lately (our philosophy teacher mentioned the Knobe effect in passing and now I can't stop thinking about it), and I've came to accept the standard reading: whether people say someone did something 'intentionally' is partly shaped by the moral valence of the outcome, not just there beliefs and desires. The classic case is the chairman who signs off on a project for profit, doesn't care about the environmental damage, and people say he harmed the environment intentionally; flip it so he doesn't care about an environmental benefit, and people say he didn't help intentionally. So the badness of the side effect seems to leak into how we apply the concept of intentional action.

I want to know what would count against that view, properly. Not just 'it didn't replicate' — I want the deeper stuff. Could the whole asymmetry be explained non-morally, say by how statistically normal the side effect is, or by something about how the English word 'intentionally' behaves with good versus bad side effects, and would that count against the claim that morality is doing the work? What experimental results would make a defender of the Knobe effect concede the effect isn't really about moral judgment at all? Is there a version of the case that would be especially awkward for the view — like one where the badness is held fixed and the effect still disappears? And do you think the view has been adjusted over the years so that it risks nothing, and any pattern of results would get absorbed? I'd like the threats ranked from 'this basically kills the view' to 'this is just a bit awkward', with an honest take on how exposed the standard reading actually is.
