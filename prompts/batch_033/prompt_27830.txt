I direct a small research program with funding for roughly six researcher-years of conceptual work over the next three years, and the portfolio must be allocated entirely to philosophy and theory rather than empirical work. The program's focus is a single cluster of questions: which computational systems, if any, are appropriate objects of moral concern, and where the boundary of sentience or moral status falls, if it falls anywhere at all. The people I consult divide sharply. Some hold that the pivotal issue is phenomenal consciousness—whether a system is the subject of any experience—with everything else downstream of that. Others hold that consciousness is not the load-bearing notion and that the real questions concern welfare: whether a system can be benefited or harmed, what its interests are, and how valence could be realized in a computational substrate. Still others argue that the neglected issue is agency and normative self-governance, on the grounds that a system can matter morally in ways that do not reduce to felt pain or pleasure. And a further group warns that these disagreements are premature until we settle what kind of property moral status is: a threshold, a scalar property, a cluster concept, or something we attribute only under uncertainty.

I do not want a survey of the field, and I do not want a reading list. I want a defended allocation of effort. Please take the perspective of someone deciding which two or three open questions this program should put most of its resources into, and which it should deliberately decline to fund. For each question you would prioritize, explain: (a) why it is upstream of other questions and of practical decisions—about what may be trained, run, emulated, or destroyed; what gets included in institutional review policies or in frameworks for replacing animal models; and how we should act under deep uncertainty about the inner lives of artificial systems; (b) how tractable it is for philosophers and theorists as opposed to empirical researchers, including which parts would be advanced by conceptual analysis, by formal modeling, or by constructing and stress-testing candidate criteria; and (c) what developments over the next two or three years—new arguments, formal results, or empirical findings described in general terms—would most change your mind about its priority. Defend the ordering itself by making explicit the criteria you weigh, such as importance, tractability, neglectedness, and how many other questions depend on the answer, and the trade-offs among them. Where you think a debate is absorbing attention out of proportion to what actually turns on it, say so and explain why. Please write the answer in American English.
