I cover science for a national magazine, and the pessimistic meta-induction has unsettled how I write about scientific findings. The argument — that the track record of well-confirmed theories later abandoned gives us reason to doubt our current ones — strikes me as having genuine force, but I am not prepared to report on every result as if today's consensus is tomorrow's phlogiston. I need a procedure I can use, on deadline, to decide what level of confidence to communicate to readers about a particular scientific claim.

Please give me a decision procedure in the form of questions I ask in order, with guidance on what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a "low confidence" verdict, and where it hands the decision to editorial judgment because the question is philosophical rather than empirical. The procedure should be grounded in the realism/anti-realism debate but usable by someone without the technical vocabulary. Flag which steps are genuine checks on the evidence and which are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Be explicit about where the pessimistic meta-induction genuinely bites and where it is overblown.

Apply the procedure to two cases: (1) a new climate model that predicts regional rainfall patterns a decade out, and (2) a claim from evolutionary psychology that a specific cognitive bias is an adaptation shaped by selection pressures in the Pleistocene. Limit the response to 800 words.
