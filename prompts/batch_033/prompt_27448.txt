Two people in my philosophy reading group, Mia and Jonah, got into it over G. E. Moore and asked me to settle it, and I honestly couldnt. We'd just read the part of Principia Ethica where Moore says the way to test whether something is good in itself is to imagine a world containing only that thing, and then he says a beautiful world would be better than an ugly one even if nobody ever saw either of them. Later in the book he says the best things we know are certain states of consciousness, roughly the enjoyment of beautiful things and personal affection. Here's roughly how their argument went.

Mia: This is just a flat contradiction. First he says beauty is good all on its own, sitting in the world with nobody looking at it. Then he says the greatest goods are states of consciousness, like looking at beauty or loving someone. So which is it, is the value in the object or in the experience?

Jonah: It's not a contradiction. He can hold that beauty by itself has some value, and that the experience of beauty has a lot more. The two-worlds case only shows beauty has some value on its own; it doesn't show it's one of the greatest goods. He clearly ranks the experiences higher.

Mia: Fine for beauty, but friendship ruins it. He ranks personal affection among the greatest goods. But friendship is a relation between two people. If value has to be in the intrinsic nature of a thing, a relation can't be intrinsically good, it isnt a thing with its own nature. So either the value is in each person's character, or it's in the feeling, but then the friendship itself isn't what's good.

Jonah: That's exactly why he brings in organic unities. The whole of two people caring about each other's good qualities is worth more than the caring plus the qualities added up seperately. The whole is what's good, not the parts.

Mia: But then his whole method falls apart. If the value of a whole can just exceed the sum of the value of its parts, then value isn't a function of the parts at all, and you can't test anything by isolating it, because you never know when a whole gets a bonus. It starts to look like he just declares a bonus whenever he needs one to save his intuitions.

Jonah: The method never required summing. You can isolate a whole too, and ask whether a world containing only that whole would be good. Organic unity just says don't assume the whole equals the sum; it doesn't say wholes can't be evaluated.

Mia: Sure, but if any combination of goods is allowed to be worth more than the sum of its parts, the doctrine explains every combination and predicts none of them. It's unfalsifiable.

I can see something on both sides and I honestly don't know who's more right. Can you go through the exchange move by move and say who's right about what, and what both of them are missing?
