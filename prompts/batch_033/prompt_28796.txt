Two colleagues in my department got into an argument at a seminar, and neither would let it go. I was asked to referee and found I couldn't separate the strong points from the weak ones cleanly, so I'm bringing the exchange here. It went roughly as follows.

(1) The first appealed to a standard formal result: if two ideally rational Bayesian agents share a common prior and then update on their respective information, and if their posterior probabilities for some proposition become common knowledge between them, then those posteriors must be equal. From this he concluded that persistent, fully aired disagreement between reasonable people cannot happen without at least one of them being irrational—unless we take the escape hatch of "different priors," which, he said, merely names the problem rather than explaining it.

(2) The second replied that the result does nearly all of its work through the common-prior assumption. Priors are subjective starting points; nothing in rationality requires two people to begin with the same degrees of belief before either has seen any evidence. So the theorem shows only that if you stipulate away the main source of disagreement, disagreement disappears. It does not show that persistent disagreement is irrational.

(3) The first came back that if any prior is rationally permissible, then "rational" loses all normative content. For any body of evidence and any conclusion you care to name, there is some prior that, combined with Bayesian updating, delivers that conclusion. Without a common prior—or at least principled restrictions on admissible priors—you can never say that someone's belief is unwarranted by the evidence; you can only say that it differs from yours. That would empty epistemology of its critical force.

(4) The second answered that this is a false dichotomy between a single uniquely rational prior and total permissiveness. One can impose substantive constraints on priors—symmetry principles, calibration to known chances, limits on complexity, computability—without requiring everyone to start from the same distribution. Rationality is about coherence and responsiveness to evidence, not about convergence from identical starting points. And in any case, the first's original claim was stronger than "we need some constraints": it was that persistent disagreement itself signals irrationality, and that does not follow once the common-prior assumption is dropped.

(5) The first responded that the common-prior assumption is just the demand that all differences in posterior belief be traceable to differences in information. If two agents have the same total information and still disagree, then their disagreement has no evidential source; it is pure prior, which is to say pure prejudice. Calling that "rational" merely relabels stubbornness.

(6) The second said that "same total information" is doing suspicious work. Real agents never have literally identical total evidence, since their histories and background theories differ; and even with identical public evidence, different priors can reflect substantive commitments about which hypotheses are simple, projectible, or worth taking seriously. The common-prior assumption idealizes all of that away and then declares the remaining disagreement inexplicable—so the theorem's conclusion is close to a restatement of its premise.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each numbered move, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating. Then say who is more right overall, and what—if anything—both parties are missing.
