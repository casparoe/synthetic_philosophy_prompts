I'm taking a course on the philosophy of mathematics, and we recently spent a session on supertasks — tasks composed of infinitely many steps executed within a finite interval of time. The instructor used Thomson's lamp as the canonical example: a lamp begins in the off state at t = 0, is switched on at t = 1 minute, off at t = 1.5 minutes, on at t = 1.75 minutes, and so forth, with each switching taking half the time of the previous one, so that after exactly 2 minutes infinitely many switchings have been performed. The question, of course, is whether the lamp is on or off at t = 2.

What I find puzzling isn't the mechanics of the setup but the philosophical upshot. Thomson apparently took his lamp to demonstrate that supertasks are logically impossible — that the very idea of completing an infinite sequence of discrete actions leads to contradiction, since the lamp's final state cannot be consistently assigned. But our instructor also mentioned Benacerraf's reply: that the lamp's state at t = 2 is simply underdetermined by the description, not contradictory. On this view, Thomson has described an incomplete specification, not an incoherent one, and so the impossibility of supertasks doesn't follow.

I'm trying to get a sharper sense of what's really at stake between these two positions. If Benacerraf is right that the final state is merely unspecified, does that fully neutralize Thomson's challenge, or does Thomson have a residual point — perhaps about what it means for a task to be well-defined, or about the relationship between a process's specification at each finite stage and its behavior at the limit — that survives Benacerraf's response? And stepping back: what does this particular debate reveal about the broader question of what we're even asking when we ask whether a supertask is "possible"?
