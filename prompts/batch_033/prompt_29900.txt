I'm moderating the Q&A at a departmental colloquium next month where the speaker is a philosopher who's spent her career defending Frankfurt-style cases against the priniciple of alternative possibilities (PAP). She doesn't just think the cases are interesting; she thinks they basically settle the debate, and she's prone to waving off objections as mere tinkering with the details of the example. I need eight to ten questions that won't let her wave things off that easily. No "what's a Frankfurt case" or "so are you a compatibilist" filler.

Specifically, I want at least one question on each of the following, and for each a follow-up that anticipates the reply she's most likely to give:

- the flicker-of-freedom strategy: even in the cases she likes, the agent seems to retain some alternative (a flicker, like deciding to do otherwise or at least showing a sign of it before the interveener steps in), so maybe PAP only needs revising, not rejecting. The follow-up should deal with her likely line that the flicker is too thin to ground moral responsibility.
- the dilemma defense: the intervener has to be triggered by a prior sign of what the agent will decide. Either that sign deterministically guarantees the decision (in which case the example assumes determinism and begs the question against the incompatibilist), or it's merely a reliable indicator (in which case the agent could have done otherwise after all). I want a question that makes her take a stand on which horn she's on, and a follow-up for whichever she picks.
- the prior-sign/bypass worry: if the intervener is keyed to a sign that already settles what the agent will do, hasn't the agent's actual decision been preempted or bypassed too, so the case no longer shows responsibility without alternatives—it just shows a weird case where responsibility is unclear. The follow-up should press her on what the agent is actually doing in the actual sequence.
- sourcehood: a lot of incompatibilists now say what really matters isn't whether you could have done otherwise, but whether you were the source of your action in the right way. Frankfurt cases are aimed at the alternatives part. I want a question that asks what her cases show about sourcehood, and a follow-up if she says they show nothing, since that seems to shrink her conclusion.
- the strength of her conclusion: does she claim alternatives are never needed for moral responsibility, or only that PAP as stated is false? If it's the weaker claim, a lot of her full-stop rhetoric is overblown, and I want the follow-up to push on what exactly survives.

End with a question about what could count against the view: what kind of case or argument would make her give up on Frankfurt-style counterexamples, and is that thing something that could actually be discovered, or is the view unfalsifiable in practice?
