You are a senior scholar in decision theory and the ethics of emerging technology, with particular expertise on the unilateralist's curse and its applications to real-world cases of risky innovation. You are writing for an audience familiar with the basic idea of the curse but who want to see it stress-tested against a detailed historical episode. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming the key structural features of the case before mapping the theory onto it.

Here is the episode I want you to analyze: the creation of the world's first gene-edited human babies by the Chinese scientist He Jiankui, announced in November 2018 at the Second International Summit on Human Genome Editing in Hong Kong. He and his team had used CRISPR-Cas9 to disable the CCR5 gene in embryos, aiming to confer resistance to HIV; the edited embryos were implanted, resulting in the live birth of twin girls. At the time, multiple research groups worldwide possessed the technical capacity to attempt human germline editing. The international scientific community had been actively debating whether and when such a step might ever be justified, with prominent bodies—including the organizing committee of the 2015 International Summit on Human Gene Editing—calling for a moratorium on clinical applications until broad societal consensus could be reached. He Jiankui, operating with limited oversight and without the knowledge of his university's leadership, judged the risks acceptable and the benefits sufficient to proceed. His action was met with near-universal condemnation from the scientific establishment, led to his criminal conviction in China, and prompted the World Health Organization to establish a global governance framework for human genome editing.

Analyze this episode through the lens of the unilateralist's curse. The curse, as formalized by Bostrom, Douglas, and Sandberg, describes a situation in which multiple actors can each unilaterally take an action that imposes consequences on all, and where the group's fate is determined by whichever actor has the lowest threshold for acting—so that even if a majority or the most expert would judge the action net-negative, it goes forward anyway if just one actor disagrees. Your analysis should do the following:

First, map the case onto the formal structure of the curse. Identify the set of actors who could unilaterally act, the action in question, the relevant threshold judgments (what each actor had to believe about the action's net value to proceed), and the mechanism by which a single actor's decision determined the outcome for everyone. Pay attention to whether the curse's standard assumptions—that actors act on their own judgments, that their judgments differ, and that the action is irreversible or hard to reverse—hold in this case, and note any ways the episode complicates the simple model.

Second, say what the curse explains well about the episode and what it misses or misdescribes. Does the curse capture why the existing governance mechanisms (summit declarations, professional norms, national regulations) failed to prevent the action? Does it illuminate something about the relationship between the distribution of scientific opinion and the probability of a norm violation? And where does the curse fall short—are there features of He Jiankui's motivations, the institutional context, the role of state interests, or the nature of the harm that the curse's framework cannot accommodate or that it positively distorts?

Third, run the analysis in the other direction: what does this episode reveal about the unilateralist's curse as a theoretical tool? Does the case confirm the curse's central insight, expose a hidden boundary condition, or suggest that the curse is too thin to guide policy in domains like this? If you had to revise or supplement the curse to make it more useful for analyzing human germline editing and similar technologies, what would you add—and what would you be willing to give up?
