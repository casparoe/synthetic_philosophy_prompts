I've been following the debates about decision theory in the LessWrong-sphere for years now, and I keep coming back to the impasse between updateless decision theory and its critics. The basic UDT move — you should choose the policy that would have been best ex ante, rather than conditioning on your current evidence and then choosing — seems to handle a beautiful range of cases: Newcomb's problem, counterfactual mugging, the smoking lesion problem, all of it. But the critics (and I don't just mean the "but the money is already in the box" crowd, I mean people who genuinely get the project) keep pointing out that UDT is weirdly underspecified in some cases, or that it collapses into something like CDT when you try to nail down the formalism, or that it gives wrong answers in variants of the original puzzles. And the UDT people respond by tweaking the formalism, or appealing to "logical counterfactuals" that nobody can agree on how to formalize, and the whole thing just cycles.

What I want to know is: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it a hidden formal disagreement — would a precise enough definition of UDT settle things, and the impasse is just that we don't have one yet? Or is it a disagreement about what the decision problem fundamentally is: UDT people think the problem is "choose a policy before you have any evidence," CDT-ish people think the problem is "choose an action given your current epistemic state," and they're just answering different questions under one heading? Or is it something deeper, like a clash of bedrock intuitions about what rationality even requires — where one side's starting point is that you should act on the information you actually have, and the other side's starting point is that you should act in a way you'd endorse in advance, and there's no argument that could bridge that gap?

I want a diagnosis of the impasse, not a survey of decision theories. And I want an honest answer about what, if anything, could move the debate forward — a formal result, a new thought experiment, a conceptual distinction — or whether we should just expect it to stay stuck.
