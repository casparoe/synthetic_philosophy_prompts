Question: State Jeffrey conditionalization, explain how it generalizes ordinary Bayesian conditionalization, and identify the central constraint that Jeffrey conditionalization imposes on the transition from the old to the new probability function. Then give one reason this constraint might fail in a realistic case of uncertain evidence.

Student's answer: Jeffrey conditionalization is a rule for updating on evidence that is less than certain. In ordinary conditionalization, one learns some proposition E with certainty and sets P_new(H) = P_old(H | E) for every H. Jeffrey's rule covers the case in which an experience changes one's probabilities over a partition of evidence propositions E_1, ..., E_n without making any one of them certain. If q_i are the new probabilities of the E_i, the rule says:

P_new(H) = Σ_i q_i P_old(H | E_i).

This reduces to ordinary conditionalization when one of the q_i equals 1, since the remaining terms vanish and the formula becomes P_old(H | E). The rule is justified because, once the q_i are given, the probability axioms determine the new probability of every H through the law of total probability. The central constraint is that the evidence partition must be exhaustive and its members mutually exclusive; otherwise the q_i would not sum to one. A realistic failure of this constraint occurs when an experience is ambiguous enough to change how the evidence propositions bear on the hypothesis. For example, after seeing a blurry shape, I might not only raise my probability that the shape is a circle; I might also change my conditional probability that the object is a coin given that it is a circle, because the same visual experience also bears on the lighting conditions.
