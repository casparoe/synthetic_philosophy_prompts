I've been chewing on the absent-minded driver problem and I think I've built an argument that shows either decision theory is broken or there's a sleight of hand somewhere. I'd really like a careful diagnosis, not just the usual "you forgot to condition on your strategy" line unless you can actually make it stick and tell me what it costs.

Here's the setup: a driver plans his route home from the pub. The highway has two exits, X and then Y. He wants the second exit, Y. If he takes X by mistake he gets a payoff of 0 (hazardous road). If he takes Y he gets 4 (home). If he blows past both exits he ends up at a hotel for a payoff of 1. The kicker: he knows in advance that when he's on the road he'll be too absent-minded to tell X from Y, and he won't remember passing an exit already. So he has to adopt a single plan and stick to it.

The argument that unsettles me goes like this:

(1) A plan for this driver is a probability p of continuing at each intersection he reaches, since he can't distinguish X from Y and must use the same rule at both.

(2) From the planning perspective, the expected payoff of a plan p is: probability of taking X (which is 1-p, since he starts at X) times 0, plus probability of reaching Y (p) times the expected payoff at Y, which is (1-p) times 4 plus p times 1. So the total expected payoff is 4p(1-p) + p^2, which simplifies to 4p - 3p^2.

(3) This expression is maximized at p = 2/3, giving an expected payoff of 4/3. So the rational plan from the pub is: continue with probability 2/3 at each intersection.

(4) Now consider the driver at the time of action. He's sitting at an intersection, knows he's at an intersection, but doesn't know whether it's X or Y. He reasons: "I have no information that distinguishes X from Y, so each is equally likely." So he assigns probability 1/2 to being at X and 1/2 to being at Y.

(5) If he exits now, his expected payoff is (1/2)*0 + (1/2)*4 = 2.

(6) If he continues now, he has to consider what happens later. If he's at X and continues, he'll eventually reach Y and, by his plan, will exit with probability 1/3 and continue with probability 2/3, giving an expected payoff of 4*(1/3) + 1*(2/3) = 2. If he's at Y and continues, he gets 1. So the expected payoff of continuing now is (1/2)*2 + (1/2)*1 = 1.5.

(7) Since exiting has a higher expected payoff than continuing (2 > 1.5), he has a decisive reason to deviate from the plan and exit at the intersection he's at.

(8) This reasoning applies at any intersection he reaches. So he'll exit at the first intersection, which gives payoff 0. That's the worst possible outcome, and he knew it from the pub. So the plan he rationally chose from the pub is one he will rationally abandon when he's on the road, even though he has gained no new information. Rational planning seems to be time-inconsistent for a forgetful agent, and the driver ends up predictably screwing himself.

I don't believe that conclusion — it seems to prove too much, because it would mean there's no such thing as a rational plan for anyone with imperfect recall, which is absurd. But I can't locate the exact step that slips. I'd like to keep (1) and (2) if I can, because they're just the standard expected-utility calculation. I'm suspicious of the 1/2 probabilities in step (4), but I worry that rejecting them pushes me into saying the driver should have some weird self-locating belief that depends on his own strategy in a circular way, and I don't know how to make that coherent.
