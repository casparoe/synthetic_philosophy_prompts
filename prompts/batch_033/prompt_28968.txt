Michael Dummett’s work on immigration draws a sharp distinction between a state’s discretionary control over ordinary immigration and its strict obligations toward refugees. Please explain the conceptual basis of that distinction. In particular, address the following tension: if a state may legitimately exclude non-citizens to preserve the character of a national community, why is that same rationale unavailable when the excluded person is a refugee? Dummett seems to treat refugee status as triggering a different kind of normative relation, but what exactly is the feature—being subject to persecution, facing a well-founded fear of serious harm, having crossed a border, or something else—that does this work? Also explain how his account handles the case of people who flee extreme poverty, general lawlessness, or environmental degradation rather than targeted persecution: are they owed anything as a matter of justice, or only as a matter of humanitarian discretion? The goal is not a summary of his policy recommendations, but a reconstruction of the underlying conceptual machinery and its pressure points.
