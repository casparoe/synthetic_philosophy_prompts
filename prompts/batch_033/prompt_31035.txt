So I'm a backend engineer at a mid-size startup and we're having this debate about whether to rewrite our main service in Go or stick with Python. Me and another senior dev, Alex, we've both been here about the same time, similar experience levels, we both read the same benchmarking posts and looked at the same codebase — we even pair-programmed a spike in Go for a couple days to feel it out. We sat down together, went through the pros and cons, and came to opposite conclusions: I think we should migrate to Go for the performance gains and better concurrency model, he thinks the rewrite risk isn't worth it and we should just optimize the Python hot paths.

Here's the thing that's been bugging me, and I've been turning it over in my head all weekend. After we talked it through, I still think I'm right, but I can't point to anything Alex doesn't know or any mistake he made in his reasoning. He's just as smart as me, he's seen the same data, he reached a different conclusion. A part of me feels like the rational thing to do is to become way less confident — like, if a perfectly good thermometer gives a different reading than mine, I should doubt my thermometer, right? But another part of me thinks: I lived my reasoning from the inside, I can see why it makes sense, and the fact that someone else disagrees doesn't automatically make my reasoning worse. If I had to bet the company's money on this (and in a sense I am, since we're both gonna have to commit to one direction), should I stick to my guns or should I basically treat our two opinions as equally likely to be right and flip a coin? And is there a principled way to think about this, or am I just overthinking a normal workplace disagreement?
