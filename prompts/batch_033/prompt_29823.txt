I am a novelist, and I am drafting a book about a manufactured person who demands to be treated as a someone rather than a something. As preparation I have been reading Dennett on the three stances and real patterns, and I keep returning to Philip K. Dick's Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep? as a test case. I would like a committed reading of the novel through that lens — one that argues for a definite interpretation and gives its reasons, rather than laying out the options and leaving me to choose.

Specifically, I want you to read the novel's treatment of the androids, the Voigt-Kampff empathy test, the empathy box, and the closing episode with the electric toad through Dennett's distinction among the physical, design, and intentional stances. Where does each stance succeed or fail in the novel, and what does the novel take to be at stake in adopting the intentional stance toward a replicant? Is the point that the intentional stance tracks a real pattern in the replicants' behavior, or that taking the stance is a choice the patterns themselves do not settle? The novel seems to complicate this from two directions: the androids are literally designed artifacts, so the design stance is not merely a predictive convenience but a fact about their history; and the humans use a machine, the empathy box, to produce the very response the novel treats as the mark of the human, which blurs the line between a real pattern and an artifact. Does the novel ultimately take a position on whether the intentional stance toward androids captures something real, or does it show that the question is badly posed? I want the reading to be faithful in both directions: do not reduce the novel to an illustration of Dennett, and do not force Dennett's view to fit the novel. End by saying what the novel adds to the philosophical debate that the essays alone do not deliver, and note where your interpretation leans on a contested reading of either the novel or Dennett.
