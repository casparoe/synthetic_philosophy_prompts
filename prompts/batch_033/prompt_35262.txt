I've been teaching philosophy of statistics for years now, and I keep coming back to the same frustration: the Bayesian vs. frequentist debate seems to have been going in circles since at least the 1950s, and the replication crisis — which you'd think would be a decisive test of something — hasn't actually settled anything. Both sides claim the crisis vindicates them. The Bayesians say it's because p-values and null hypothesis testing are fundamentally broken; the frequentists say it's because people are misusing their tools, and anyway Bayesian methods have their own problems with subjectivity and model misspecification. And yet, somehow, after all this, the social sciences are still mostly frequentist, the machine learning people are mostly Bayesian (or at least Bayesian-ish), and nobody has convinced the other side.

So here's my question, and it's not "who's right." I want to know what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it a verbal dispute about what "probability" means? Is it a disagreement about which epistemic values matter — error control versus coherence? Is it a hidden empirical disagreement about what would actually happen if everyone switched to Bayesian methods? Or is it, as I sometimes suspect in my darker moments, just two professional communities with different training and different tools, each defending their turf, with the philosophical arguments as rationalizations after the fact?

I'd like a diagnosis that engages with what each side actually says, not a survey of the positions. And I want an honest answer about what, if anything, could actually move the debate forward — a new piece of empirical evidence, a conceptual distinction, a formal result, or whether we should just stop expecting it to resolve and call it a diference of temperament.
