I've been reading a book on the philosophy of emerging technologies, and the author summarizes the gradual-replacement argument for mind uploading roughly as follows:

The gradual-replacement argument shows that uploading preserves personal identity. Suppose we replace your neurons one at a time with silicon chips that perform exactly the same input-output computations. Because each replacement is functionally identical to the neuron it replaces, your conscious experience continues uninterrupted, and at no point do you notice any change. Since there is no moment at which you disappear, you survive the entire procedure. This is fundamentally different from destructive scanning followed by reconstruction, where the original is destroyed and a mere copy is created. The argument thus demonstrates that what matters for survival is psychological continuity rather than biological continuity.

As someone who spent a career in the natural sciences before turning to philosophy, I am accustomed to reasoning about gradual versus abrupt transitions — in phase changes, in speciation, in the onset of turbulence — and I know that arguments turning on the absence of a sharp boundary can be trickier than they look. But I do not yet know the literature on personal identity well enough to pinpoint where, if anywhere, this summary goes wrong. I suspect it is broadly right but may compress or overstate certain claims. Could you go through it and tell me which parts a philosopher working on these issues would accept, which they would qualify, and whether the three conclusions the author draws — that uploading preserves identity, that gradual replacement differs in kind from destructive copying, and that psychological continuity is what matters — actually follow from what the argument establishes?
