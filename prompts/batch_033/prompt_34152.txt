The question I set was:

"Is there a morally significant difference between lying and misleading? Discuss the main arguments on each side of the debate and give your own view."

And here's the answer I got:

"Lying and misleading are both ways of deceiving someone, but they work differently. A lie is when you say something you believe to be false, with the intention that the listener will believe it. For example, if someone asks you where the money is and you say 'I don't know' when you do know, that's a lie. Misleading is when you say something that is technically true but you know the listener will draw a false conclusion from it. For instance, if someone asks whether you ate the last biscuit and you say 'I've been in the garden all afternoon' — which is true, but you did eat the biscuit before you went out — that's misleading.

One argument for why there's a morally significant difference is that when you mislead someone, they are partly responsible for the false belief they end up with. You gave them true information; it was their own reasoning that led them to the wrong conclusion. So the moral responsibility is shared. This is sometimes called the 'self-deception' argument or the 'listener's responsibility' argument. In contrast, when you lie, you directly implant a false belief in them and they have no chance to detect it.

The main argument against a moral difference is that both lying and misleading involve an intention to deceive, and the intention is what matters morally. If you deliberately cause someone to believe something false, does it really matter whether you used a false statement or a misleading true one? The outcome is the same and your intention was the same. Some philosophers go further and say that misleading is actually a form of lying, because the broader definition of lying is just 'intentionally causing a false belief', and by that definition misleading counts as lying too. So the supposed distinction collapses.

I think the second argument is stronger. If we care about honesty, we should care about all forms of intentional deception equally. The fact that you technically told the truth doesn't make you an honest person if you were trying to deceive. What matters is what you were trying to do, not the exact words you used. So I'd say there is no morally significant difference — or at least, any difference is much smaller than people think. The only exception might be cases where the misleading statement is so vague or ambiguous that the listener really should have asked for clarification, but even then, if you knew they'd misunderstand, you're still being deceptive."
