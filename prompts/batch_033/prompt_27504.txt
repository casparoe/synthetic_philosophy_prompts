I've been taking a course on the philosophy of art this semester, and we spent a week on the problem of forgeries. The professor had us read the classic papers — Goodman, Dutton, Lessing — but I found myself more interested in something the readings didn't directly address: the actual institutional practices around forgeries, and what they implicitly commit their practitioners to.

Here's what I mean. When a museum discovers that a painting in its collection is a forgery — say, a work long attributed to Vermeer turns out to have been painted in the 1930s by a skilled imitator using anachronistic pigments — the museum doesn't destroy the object or remove it from public view. But it doesn't leave things unchanged either. It reclassifies the work, typically moving it to a different context: a special exhibition on the history of fakes, a study room, or a display about the history of collecting and attribution. The label changes, the framing changes, and the object is treated as valuable in a different way — as a document of twentieth-century taste, perhaps, or as a cautionary tale about connoisseurship, rather than as a Vermeer. The physical object is identical to what it was the day before the discovery. The same pigments on the same canvas, the same composition, the same visual effect. And yet the museum acts as though the object's identity as a cultural artifact has shifted.

Meanwhile, the art market's response is even starker. The same discovery causes the object's monetary value to collapse — from tens of millions of dollars to perhaps a few thousand — even in a hypothetical scenario where every prospective buyer already knows the full history and the auction is perfectly transparent. No one is being deceived; the price still plummets. Again, nothing physical has changed.

I'm trying to reconstruct the implicit theory of aesthetic value — or of what constitutes an artwork — that makes sense of these practices taken together. Does the museum's continued interest in the object (under a new description) and the market's near-total abandonment of it rest on the same underlying view of why originals matter, or do they presuppose different and possibly inconsistent commitments? And what would have to change in both institutions if we adopted the view — call it aesthetic empiricism — that the value of an artwork resides entirely in its perceivable surface properties, so that a perfect replica just is the same aesthetic object?
