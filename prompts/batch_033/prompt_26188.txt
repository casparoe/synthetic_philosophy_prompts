Two friends of mine got into it at a party last weekend and I've been turning it over since. The topic was expected value fanaticism — the idea that if you accept expected value reasoning, you should be willing to sacrifice any amount of certain good for a sufficiently tiny probability of an astronomically large payoff.

(1) Dan started by bringing up Pascal's mugging: a stranger asks for your wallet, promising to return tomorrow and give you a trillion times the value of what's in it. Dan said anyone who actually hands over their wallet has lost the plot, and that this shows expected value reasoning breaks down when probabilities get small enough. (2) Maya pushed back: she said the thought experiment just shows our intuitions can't handle very large numbers, not that the reasoning is wrong. If you really could get that payoff with that probability, the math says you should take it — your gut recoiling doesn't refute the math. (3) Dan said fine, but the real problem is that for literally any action you might take, there's some tiny probability it produces astronomical value — maybe scratching your nose prevents a chain of events that would have led to human extinction. So if you take fanatical expected value reasoning seriously, you can't ever decide anything; every choice gets swamped by tiny-probability enormous-payoff considerations. (4) Maya said that's a problem of computational intractability, not a problem with the normative theory. We can't calculate everything, but that doesn't mean the right answer isn't out there. (5) Dan came back: "No, it's worse than that. At probabilities like 1 in 10^100, we don't just fail to calculate — the probability isn't even epistemically accessible. You can't distinguish 10^-100 from 10^-105 from 10^-200. The whole framework breaks down because you're not actually assigning credences, you're just making up numbers." (6) Maya thought for a second and said: "Okay, that's the strongest objection, but I think it only shows we need to be careful about how we model uncertainty over small probabilities — it doesn't show we should reject the principle that if you did have a well-defined credence in an enormous payoff, you should act on it. And that principle is what does all the work in arguments for caring about existential risk, the far future, and so on. If you reject it, you probably have to give up on longtermism too." (7) Dan said he was fine with that — maybe longtermism is also built on sand.

They never resolved it and honestly I couldn't tell who had the better of it. Can you go through their exchange move by move and say who's right about what?
