I'm not an academic — I trained as a nurse and have done most of my reading in biology and philosophy on my own time — but a pattern I keep noticing has started to bother me, and I'd like to know whether anyone has carved it up before and whether it has a name.

When I look at how living things cope with problems they have never met before, I seem to find four distinct grades. At the first grade, the individual doesn't adjust at all: a bacterium or a plant facing a new poison survives only if the population happens to contain variants that can tolerate it, and the adjustment happens across generations rather than within a single life. At the second grade, an animal learns, but only by acting and then suffering or enjoying the consequences: my neighbor's dog learned not to touch the electric fence by touching it, and learned a trick through many repetitions with treats and corrections. At the third grade, an animal seems able to do some of its trial and error before acting: a crow or a rat faced with a puzzle will pause, look, and then produce the right move on the first attempt, as though it had run through and rejected bad options internally, so that the mistakes which would have hurt it in the world get made and discarded somewhere inside. At the fourth grade, the trials don't have to be carried out by the animal itself, or even in its own head: a human being can borrow other people's already-tested ideas, and can think with words, sketches, maps, and tools, so that I can benefit from mistakes made by people I have never met, some of them long dead.

I'd like to know whether this four-level picture is an established framework in the study of minds, and whether it has a name. But the label is the least of it. What I actually want is the carving: are these really different kinds of minds or problem-solvers, or just a continuum with a few convenient mileposts? Where are the boundaries exactly, and where do they blur? I keep finding cases that don't sit cleanly on one level — an animal that picks up a trick by watching another animal succeed, a human expert who can do something reliably but cannot put the skill into words, a young child before it has language, a person following a rule they cannot state. I would not be satisfied with a bare name; I want a precise account of how the levels are defined, which of my four grades the standard framework actually recognizes, where my description differs from it, and what the framework has trouble handling.
