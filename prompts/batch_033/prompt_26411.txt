I'm teaching a grad seminar on social epistemology this semester, and we've been working through the conciliationism/steadfastness debate — you know, the whole question of what you should do when you discover that an epistemic peer disagrees with you. The students are getting the abstract arguments fine, but I want to give them a concrete case study to chew on, and I keep coming back to the K-Pg extinction debate from the 1980s and early 90s. I mentioned it offhand last week and a couple students got really interested, so now I want to do it properly.

For those who don't know the geology: in 1980 the Alvarezes dropped their asteroid-impact paper — iridium spike at the K-Pg boundary, shocked quartz, the whole thing. And a bunch of paleontologists and volcanologists pushed back hard, arguing the extinction was gradual and driven by the Deccan Traps eruptions. These weren't cranks on either side; you had serious people like Charles Officer and Gerta Keller arguing against the impact hypothesis, and the Alvarezes and their allies were obviously first-rate. Both camps had the same published evidence — the same iridium measurements, the same fossil records — they just interpreted it differently. The debate dragged on for over a decade, and it only really got settled when they found the Chicxulub crater in '91, which was basically a smoking gun for impact.

Here's what I'd like: I want you to analyze this episode through the lens of conciliationism vs. steadfastness. In particular, give me a JSON object with the following fields:

- "parties": who the epistemic peers were and what exactly they disagreed about
- "shared_evidence": what evidence both sides had access to before Chicxulub
- "conciliationist_verdict": what conciliationism says each side should have done given the peer disagreement (and whether they actually did it)
- "steadfast_verdict": what the steadfast view says each side should have done (and whether they did it)
- "chicxulub_effect": what the discovery of the crater does to the retrospective epistemological analysis — does it vindicate one side in a way that matters for the theory?
- "lesson_for_theory": whether the episode, taken as a whole, is an argument for conciliationism, for steadfastness, or something messier

Don't just tell me "conciliationism says they should have lowered their confidence." I want the analysis to engage with the actual details — the fact that the Alvarezes were physicists crashing into a paleontology debate and whether that complicates peerhood, the role of the gradualist vs. catastrophic reading of the fossil record, and whether the crater discovery is the kind of thing either theory handles naturally or whether it exposes a blindspot in how philosophers think about disagreement. I'm also curious whether the episode pushes toward the idea that conciliationism is more plausible in some domains than others — geology vs. philosophy, say — or whether that's a dodge.
