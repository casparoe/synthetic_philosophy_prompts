I'm a novelist, and my current project has a philosophy professor as a main character — he teaches epistemology and loves tormenting his students with paradoxes. I was trying to get inside his head and fell down a rabbit hole reading about the surprise exam paradox, and now I can't stop thinking about it. Here's the argument as I understand it, laid out the way my character might present it to a first-year seminar.

A teacher announces on Friday that there will be an exam on exactly one day next week, and that it will be a surprise: the students won't be able to deduce the night before which day it is. The students reason backward. (1) The exam cannot be on Friday, because if it hasn't happened by Thursday night, they'd know it must be Friday, and it wouldn't be a surprise. (2) With Friday ruled out, the exam cannot be on Thursday either: if it hasn't happened by Wednesday night, they'd know it must be Thursday (since Friday is impossible), and again no surprise. (3) Repeating this reasoning eliminates Wednesday, then Tuesday, then Monday. (4) So no day can host a surprise exam. (5) Therefore the teacher's announcement is false — a surprise exam is impossible under these conditions. But then Wednesday rolls around and the teacher hands out the exam, and the students are genuinely surprised. So the argument proves something that's manifestly false; we know surprise exams happen.

I'm less interested in the "solution" per se — I know philosophers have been arguing about this for decades — and more interested in the craft of it: if you had to diagnose exactly where the reasoning goes wrong, in a way that would satisfy someone who finds every individual step compelling, what would you say? I want to write a scene where my professor character explains the flaw to a student who's been loosing sleep over it, and I need the explanation to feel earned, not hand-wavy. Also, I'd like you to tell me what argument or consideration would most likely change your mind about your diagnosis — where's the weakest link in your own reading of the paradox? And please, no headings or bullet points or any of that; just write it out as continuous prose, the way you'd explain it to someone over coffee.
