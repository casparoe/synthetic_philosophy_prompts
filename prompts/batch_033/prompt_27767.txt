"The discursive dilemma, first identified by List and Pettit, arises when a group votes on a set of logically connected propositions. The classic example is a three-judge panel deciding a breach-of-contract case. They must rule on three propositions: (P) the contract was valid, (Q) the defendant breached it, and (R) the defendant is liable, where liability requires both validity and breach, so R if and only if (P and Q). Suppose the judges vote as follows. Judge 1: P true, Q true, R true. Judge 2: P true, Q false, R false. Judge 3: P false, Q true, R false. If we use premise-based voting — take the majority on each premise — we get P true (2–1), Q true (2–1), and therefore R must be true. But if we use conclusion-based voting — take the majority on the conclusion directly — we get R false (2–1). So the group's judgement depends on which aggregation procedure we pick, and the two procedures give contradictory results. This reveals a deep problem: majority rule is logically incoherent when applied to interconnected propositions, because it can produce a collective judgement set that is inconsistent even though every individual's judgements are perfectly consistent. The dilemma is essentially the same structural problem as Condorcet's paradox of cyclical majorities, just applied to propositions rather than preference orderings. The upshot is that democratic voting procedures cannot guarantee both collective rationality and respect for individual judgements — something has to give. List and Pettit argue that the only philosophically defensible solution is to abandon majority voting on interconnected issues in favour of deliberative procedures that force groups to confront the inconsistency and revise their judgements collectively."
