I'm teaching the School of Names this term and i've been reconstructing the white horse argument for a lecture. I turned it into a numbered argument and now it seems to prove too much, but I can't find the exact spot where it goes wrong. Here's the version I gave my students.

(1) When you ask for a horse, a brown or black horse will satisfy you; when you ask for a white horse, it won't. So the request for a horse and the request for a white horse are satisfied by different things.
(2) From this the dialogue concludes that the term horse and the term white horse name different things.
(3) And from that it concludes that a white horse is not a horse.
(4) But the very same move would show that a brown horse is not a horse, and that a white horse is not white. Ask for something white and a white wall or white paper will do, but neither satisfies the request for a white horse; so the term white and the term white horse also name different things, and by the same reasoning a white horse is not white.
(5) So a white horse would be neither white nor a horse, and the same would go for brown horses and fast horses, which is absurd.

So either the inference at step 3 is bad, or the conclusion has to mean something other than it seems to, or one of the earlier moves has to go. Wich step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd like to keep the idea that the argument is doing real work about how compound terms pick out kinds, but I don't want to end up committed to the claim that a white horse isn't white.
