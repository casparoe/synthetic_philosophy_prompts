I've been going down a rabbit hole reading about the surprise exam paradox (sometimes called the unexpected hanging paradox). The basic setup: a teacher announces there'll be a surprise exam sometime next week, and the students reason backwards — it can't be Friday because by Thursday night they'd know it's coming, so it wouldn't be a surprise; then it can't be Thursday because they'd know by Wednesday night, and so on. They conclude no suprise exam is possible. Then the teacher gives it on Wednesday and everyone's surprised.

What's bothering me is this: it seems like the students' reasoning is actually perfectly valid at each step, and yet it leads to a false conclusion. Doesn't this mean that deductive reasoning itself can lead us astray in certain situations? Like, if a chain of logical deductions can be flawless step-by-step and still give you the wrong answer, what does that say about the reliability of logical reasoning more broadly? My friend says I'm overthinking it and that the paradox is just a cute puzzle with no deeper implications, but I feel like there's something genuinely unsettling here about the limits of rationality. I guess what I'm really asking is: does the surprise exam paradox reveal a genuine limitation of deductive logic as a way of knowing things, or is there a specific flaw in the students' reasoning that I'm missing?
