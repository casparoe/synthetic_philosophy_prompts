In many scientific fields, it is now standard for journals to require that authors deposit their data and code in a public repository as a condition of publication, and for funding agencies to mandate open-access archiving of the papers they support. Yet in those same fields, the institutional review board or ethics committee that approves a study almost never requires that the approval documents, the risk-benefit analysis, or the deliberations that produced them be made public. The data and the paper are forced into the open; the ethical reasoning that authorized the data collection stays behind a door. What view of transparency, of accountability, and of the division of epistemic labor does this combination of practices presuppose? Is it assumed that ethical judgment is not a form of expertise that benefits from post-hoc scrutiny, or that the relevant public is a different one than the scientific public? Reconstruct the implicit theory that makes this asymmetric treatment intelligible, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and tell me whether the practice would survive if we replaced that implicit theory with one that treats the ethical reasoning behind a study as a public part of the scientific record. I want a committed answer: take a definite position on what the best reconstruction of the implicit theory is, defend it, and say whether the practice is philosophically defensible on that basis.
