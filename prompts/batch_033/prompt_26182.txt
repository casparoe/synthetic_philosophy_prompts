I spent the better part of an evening arguing with a friend about Molyneux's problem — the question William Molyneux posed to John Locke in 1688: whether a person born blind, who has learned to distinguish a cube from a sphere by touch, would, upon suddenly gaining sight, be able to tell which is which by vision alone, before touching them. My friend takes the view that modern cataract-surgery studies have effectively settled the question in the negative: newly sighted subjects perform at chance on cross-modal matching tasks, so the answer is no. I think this is too quick. The empirical results are ambiguous — subjects improve rapidly, some had residual vision, and the testing conditions do not capture the idealized moment Molyneux imagined. We ended up talking past each other, and I suspect we disagree less about the facts than about what the question is really asking.

I want to get clearer on my own view. Please give me your credence that the answer to Molyneux's question, properly understood, is no — that a newly sighted person would not be able to visually identify the shapes without tactile confirmation. But I want the number decomposed. What sub-questions does this credence ride on? Assign a rough probability to each and show how they combine. In particular, I want you to address:

- Whether the cataract-patient studies actually test the question Molyneux and Locke were asking, or whether they change the question in ways that matter — subjects with some residual vision, testing after a delay rather than at the first moment of sight, the fact that the subjects are not the ideal reasoners Locke imagined.
- Whether cross-modal transfer of shape information is the right way to operationalize the philosophical issue, or whether Molyneux's problem is better understood as a question about whether sensory modalities contribute distinct kinds of content to cognition — a question that no single empirical result could settle.
- Whether the answer might depend on which shape properties we mean — three-dimensional geometry versus two-dimensional projected shape, or global shape versus local features — and if so, whether that splits the question into multiple questions with different answers.
- Any other sub-questions you think belong in the decomposition.

Then tell me which sub-question your overall credence is most sensitive to — the one where new evidence or a new argument would shift your number the most. Finally, given that my friend is at roughly 0.9 that the answer is no and I am somewhere around 0.4, tell me which sub-question most likely houses our disagreement.
