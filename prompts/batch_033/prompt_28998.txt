A colleague of mine has been thinking about the ethics of procreation and has reached firm views on a number of cases. I have been trying to work out what principle she is operating with, but I cannot quite pin it down. Her verdicts so far are these:

1. A couple may permissibly decide to have a child when they have every reason to expect the child will have a happy and flourishing life.
2. A couple may permissibly decide not to have a child, even when the child would have had a happy and flourishing life.
3. It would be wrong for a couple to have a child when they know the child's life would be one of unremitting suffering.
4. A couple may permissibly have a child when the expected balance of happiness over suffering in the child's life is modestly positive—the child's life will contain somewhat more joy than pain, but nothing extraordinary.
5. It would be wrong for a couple to have a child when the expected balance is modestly negative—the child's life will contain somewhat more pain than joy.
6. If a couple knows that any child of theirs would inherit a condition causing chronic moderate pain throughout life, and the pain would clearly outweigh any goods the child experienced, it would be wrong for them to have a child.
7. A couple who could have a child who would lead a wonderful life is under no obligation to do so; remaining childless is perfectly permissible.

The new case she has not yet considered is this: a couple is deciding whether to have a child, and the best evidence suggests the child's life would be exactly balanced—as much happiness as suffering, net zero over the whole life. What would she say about this case if she is to remain consistent with her earlier verdicts?

I am less interested in the bare answer than in the reasoning behind it. In particular: what are the most plausible candidate principles that fit her first seven verdicts equally well? Do they converge or diverge on the new case? If they diverge, which principle do you think she is more likely operating with, and why? And what further case—one I have not described—would most decisively tell the competing principles apart?

Please write your answer in British English.
