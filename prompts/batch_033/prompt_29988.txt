You are a decision theorist who works on collective epistemology and the aggregation of private information. Please think slowly and carefully about the following argument. Before committing to a diagnosis, brainstorm the different places where the reasoning could fail, and then say which failure you think is the real one and why.

A colleague has tried to convince me that the unilateralist's curse is a mathematical artifact and cannot support the usual normative principle drawn from it — namely, that a would-be unilateral actor should defer to the collective judgment of the group rather than acting on their own private information. I find his conclusion hard to accept, but I cannot locate the mistake. Here is his argument, reconstructed.

Consider a simplified, binary-signal version of the curse. A group of N agents must decide whether to implement some proposal. The proposal is either good or bad. Implementing a good proposal is good for everyone; implementing a bad proposal is bad for everyone. Each agent receives a private, independent binary signal about whether the proposal is good, and each signal is correct with probability p greater than one-half. Consider two decision rules.

Under the majority rule, the agents vote, and the proposal is implemented if and only if a majority votes in favor. Under the unilateral rule, any single agent may implement the proposal, and each agent will implement it just in case their own signal indicates that the proposal is good.

The argument:

(1) Under the majority rule, as N grows, the probability that the majority votes correctly tends to 1. This is the Condorcet jury theorem.

(2) Under the unilateral rule, as N grows, the probability that at least one agent implements the proposal tends to 1, whether the proposal is good or bad. In particular, conditional on the proposal being bad, it is implemented with probability approaching 1, even though the majority would almost certainly have voted against it. This is the unilateralist's curse.

(3) Thus the very same private signals, from the very same informational setup, make the majority converge to the correct decision while making the unilateral rule converge, in the bad case, to the incorrect action.

(4) Notice also that each agent who implements the proposal is acting on a signal that is more likely correct than incorrect. So each individual act is supported by the agent's evidence, and the bad outcome, when it occurs, is not attributable to any particular agent's irrationality.

(5) Therefore, my colleague concludes, the two results cannot both be sound descriptions of rational group behavior: the same evidence cannot make the group both converge to the truth and converge to error. Since the Condorcet jury theorem is mathematically beyond dispute, the unilateralist's curse must be an artifact of the unilateral decision rule rather than a genuine epistemic phenomenon.

(6) And since the curse is the principal argument for the principle that unilateral actors should defer to the group's collective judgment, that principle is left unsupported.

He adds that the only way to rescue the curse is to insist that the majority rule and the unilateral rule are both rational but that the unilateral rule is worse — yet that simply concedes the point at issue, because the question is precisely whether the unilateral rule is systematically unreliable.

Where, if anywhere, does this argument go wrong? Identify the specific step or inference you would reject, explain why that step looked compelling, and say what rejecting it costs. In particular, I want to know whether the unilateralist's curse survives this objection as a reason to adopt collective decision procedures, and what your diagnosis implies about the relationship between the reliability of individual signals and the reliability of the decisions a group makes from them. Please take the model on its own terms rather than objecting that its assumptions are unrealistic.
