I have a colleague who works on the foundations of probability, and I have been recording how she applies the principle of indifference to cases of complete ignorance. Her recorded verdicts: for a coin with completely unknown bias, the probability of heads on the next toss is 1/2; for a six-sided die with completely unknown bias, the probability of any specified face is 1/6; for the chord problem familiar from Bertrand's paradox, she assigns 1/3 when the chord is chosen by two endpoints picked independently and uniformly on the circumference, 1/2 when a radius and a point along it are chosen uniformly and the chord is drawn perpendicular to the radius through that point, and 1/4 when the midpoint is chosen uniformly in the disk and the chord is taken to have that midpoint. The next case asks for the probability that a chord chosen at random in a circle, with no procedure specified, is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle. Predict her verdict, state the rule or rules she seems to be running, identify at least two rules consistent with all her earlier verdicts that diverge on this new case, give your best guess with the reasons for preferring it, and name the further case that would most cleanly settle which rule she holds. Please write your answer in American English.
