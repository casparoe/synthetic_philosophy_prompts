Two people in my philosophy group got into a long argument about Dennett's tower of minds — you know, the Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, Gregorian thing. I'll call them Ewan and Mira.

Ewan started by saying he found the framework genuinely useful for thinking about what different animals can do. He gave the standard summary: Darwinian creatures are stuck with whatever they're born with and only their species learns across generations; Skinnerian ones can adjust behaviour based on reinforcement within a single lifetime; Popperian ones can run internal simulations and let their hypotheses die in their stead; and Gregorian ones — that's us — use public representations like language to think thoughts we couldn't think otherwise. He said it was a nice way to see human cognition as built on animal foundations rather than a magic add-on.

Mira said the whole thing was question-begging. Her complaint was that Dennett builds the tower so that each floor adds exactly the kind of cognitive capacity that humans excel at, and then acts as if he's discovered something about the natural order. She said it's like defining intelligence as "whatever humans do" and then announcing that humans are the most intelligent — the conclusion was baked into the choice of criteria.

Ewan pushed back: the tower isn't a ranking of intelligence, it's a story about the order in which natural selection had to discover certain design tricks. You can't evolve language-assisted thinking without first having the capacity to evaluate options internally, and you can't do that without first being able to learn from experience. The nesting is a logical constraint, not a value judgement.

Mira said that's exactly what a value judgement disguised as a logical constraint looks like. She pointed out that Dennett's criteria for what counts as "Popperian" are ultimately behavioural — they're about whether an animal acts as if it's testing hypotheses — and that this systematically favours animals whose inner processes produce visible, experimenter-friendly outputs. An octopus might be doing something deeply cognitive that just doesn't look like hypothesis-testing to a human observer, and Dennett's framework would lump it with Skinnerian creatures by default.

Ewan replied that this was a misreading: Dennett's whole point with the Popperian level is that the hypothesis-testing happens internally and needn't be visible at all. The behavioural evidence is just how we'd detect it, not what it consists in. And he said Mira was smuggling in a demand for "deep cognition" that Dennett would reject as a Cartesian residue — the idea that there's some special inner glow of understanding that matters more than what the system can actually do.

Mira said that was exactly the behaviourism she was worried about, and that Dennett's refusal to countenance inner glows is a philosophical choice, not a neutral observation about minds. If you define minds in a way that rules out anything you can't operationalise, you haven't solved the problem — you've just defined it away.

They asked me to settle it and I honestly couldn't. Can you go through their exchange and say who's right about what, and whether either of them is missing something important?
