A colleague of mine has been applying Dennett's tower of generate-and-test—Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures—to classify a range of organisms and systems. So far her verdicts are:

- A bacterium: Darwinian only. Its behavioural repertoire is fixed; selection operates across generations, not within a lifetime.
- A planarian flatworm: Skinnerian. It can be conditioned to avoid a stimulus, but shows no evidence of simulating alternatives before acting.
- A laboratory rat: Popperian. It can run an internal model of a maze before committing to a path, letting its hypotheses die in its stead.
- A chimpanzee: Gregorian. It uses socially transmitted tools of thought—gestures, traditions, rudimentary theory of mind—to bypass the need for firsthand trial and error.
- A current large language model: she placed it outside the tower entirely, on the grounds that it has no persistent self, no agency, and no selection environment of its own.

She is now puzzling over the octopus. Octopuses show sophisticated problem-solving, can learn by observing conspecifics, and exhibit behavioural flexibility far beyond what most Skinnerian creatures manage. Yet they are largely solitary, do not teach their young, and have no cumulative culture of the sort that defines Gregorian creatures.

Predict her verdict on the octopus. Which rung of the tower is she most likely to place it on, and why? If her existing verdicts genuinely underdetermine the answer, identify the competing interpretations of her framework that pull in different directions, say which further case—real or hypothetical—would best distinguish them, and give your best guess as to which interpretation she is running.
