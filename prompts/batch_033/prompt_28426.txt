I serve on the disciplinary panel for my state's nursing board. We review cases where a nurse is alleged to have fallen below the standard of care — medication errors, failure to monitor, stuff like that — and we decide on sanctions ranging from a letter of warning up to license revocation. We handle about 40 cases a year, our panel has five members, and we get roughly 45 minutes per case.

Here's the problem we keep running into, and we have no consistent way of handling it. Two nurses make essentially the same error. Say they both fail to double-check an insulin dose before administering it — same lapse, same departure from protocol. In one case the patient dies. In the other, a colleague catches the error before it reaches the patient, or the patient just happens to tolerate the wrong dose without lasting harm. And our panel splits every time: some members say the outcome has to matter for the sanction, because the nurse whose error killed someone bears a different weight of responsibility than the nurse whose error was caught in time. Others say that's pure moral luck — the sanction should track what the nurse did and could control, not what happened downstream. We've been going case by case with no framework and honestly it's starting to feel kind of arbitrary.

I've been told the philosophical literature on moral luck has something useful to say here. What I'd like is a decision procedure we can actually run — a sequence of questions we ask about each case, in order, with clear guidance on what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure stops and says "this is the outcome-independent sanction," and where it says "now adjust for the outcome, and here's by how much." The procedure has to be something five non-philosophers can get through in 45 minutes. Be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure and where reasonable panel members can still disagree. Flag the kinds of cases the procedure can't handle well.

Then run the procedure on three cases:

Case A: Nurse fails to check a patient's allergy bracelet before administering penicillin. The patient has a documented allergy and goes into anaphylactic shock and dies. The nurse's lapse is clear — checking the bracelet is mandatory and takes ten seconds.

Case B: Nurse fails to check a patient's allergy bracelet before administering penicillin. The patient has no allergy, so nothing happens. The lapse is identical to Case A.

Case C: Nurse fails to check a patient's allergy bracelet before administering penicillin. The patient has a documented allergy, goes into anaphylactic shock, but is resuscitated by the rapid response team and recovers fully after three days in the ICU.
