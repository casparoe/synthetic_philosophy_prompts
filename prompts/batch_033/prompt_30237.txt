I'm a philosophy PhD student poking at the surprise exam paradox and I've realized my intuitions shift around depending on how the case is framed, which is embarassing because it's supposed to be one puzzle. I've written out my verdicts on a bunch of variants below. I'm hoping someone can tell me whether there's a single diagnosis that handles all (or most) of them, what principle or theory of the paradox it corresponds to, and which of my verdicts I'd need to abandon to make things consistent.

Case 1 (the classic setup): Teacher announces on Friday: "There will be exactly one exam next week, Mon–Fri, and it will be a surprise — meaning you won't know the day before that the exam is the next day." The student runs the backwards induction: can't be Friday (Thursday night you'd know, since it's the last day), so can't be Thursday (Wednesday night you'd know, having ruled out Friday), etc., concluding there can be no surprise exam. The teacher then gives the exam on Wednesday and the student is surprised. Verdict: the student's argument is wrong somewhere, and the teacher's announcement can be true. I'm confident about this.

Case 2 (self-referential announcement): Teacher says: "There will be an exam one day next week, and you will not be able to deduce from this very announcement alone which day the exam will be." Now the announcement explicitly references what the student can deduce from it. Verdict: this feels genuinely paradoxical, or at least the teacher simply can't make this announcement truly. Something different is going on here vs. Case 1.

Case 3 (one-day version): Teacher says on Friday: "There will be an exam on Monday, and on Monday morning you won't expect it." Verdict: straightforwardly impossible. If the student believes the teacher they expect it, so the prediction is false. No backwards induction needed — it's like a Moore-paradoxical statement. This makes me think the multi-day structure is doing real work in Case 1.

Case 4 (dense student): Same as Case 1, but the student never performs the backwards induction. On Thursday night they think "well, could still be Friday." The exam is Friday and the student is surprised. Verdict: yeah sure, but this is boring. The paradox isn't about whether some particular person happens to be surprised — it's about whether the teacher's announcement can be known to be true by a student who reasons perfectly. The dense-student case just dodges the puzzle.

Case 5 (no announcement): Teacher gives a pop quiz with zero warning. Student is surprised. Verdict: totally fine, no paradox. The announcement is doing all the heavy lifting.

Case 6 (two-day version): Only Monday and Tuesday. Teacher says "exam one of these two days, it will be a surprise." Verdict: I can actually feel the pull of the student's argument here. If I imagine myself as the student on Monday night, knowing only Tuesday remains, I feel like I would expect the exam — and so it wouldn't be a surprise. Two days feels different from five, and I'm not sure why. Maybe I'm wrong about this one.

Case 7 (student publishes the argument): Before the week, the student publishes the backwards-induction argument in the school paper, concluding there will be no exam. The teacher reads it, laughs, and gives the exam on Wednesday. The student is surprised. Verdict: the teacher can still surprise the student even when the student has made the reasoning fully explicit in advance. This pushes me away from diagnoses that blame the paradox on the student not being able to "hold the whole reasoning in mind" or something psychological about their credences shifting mid-week.

Case 8 (teacher is known to be unreliable): Same as Case 1, but the student knows the teacher lies roughly half the time. Verdict: the student can't even get the backwards induction off the ground, because they can't take the announcement as known. The whole thing dissolves. This makes me think the paradox relies on the student treating the announcement as something like common knowledge.

What I'm after: is there a unified account here? Does the paradox turn on the KK principle (knowing that you know), or on self-reference, or on something about the logic of announcement and belief revision? And which of my verdicts do I have to give up to get a clean theory? Also, if you've got a favorite case I didn't think of that would help sort between the candidate diagnoses, I'd love to hear it.
