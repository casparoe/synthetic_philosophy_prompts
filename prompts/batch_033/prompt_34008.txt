I write a science-and-ideas column for a general-interest outlet, and I want to publish a decision tree that sorts readers by which position on algorithmic fairness their own commitments actually entail, rather than by which label they prefer. The tree should start from a concrete high-stakes setting, such as a risk score used for pretrial release or loan approval, and branch through the distinctions that generate the main impossibility results: whether a score must be calibrated within each group (so that a given score corresponds to the same observed outcome rate in every group), whether it must equalize false-positive and false-negative rates across groups, and what happens to those two demands when base rates differ. Each node should be phrased as a concrete choice a non-specialist can answer without knowing the literature—for example, whether a score ought to mean the same thing in every group, or whether groups ought to experience the same rate of mistaken denials. The leaves should include at least a calibration-first position, an error-rate-balance-first position, an anti-classification or procedural position, and the view that the choice of fairness criterion is itself a substantive value judgment rather than a technical specification. Keep the instrument to roughly ten branch points. Flag any positions the tree cannot separate with these questions, and mark which combinations of answers are jointly unsatisfiable in light of the impossibility results, briefly explaining what the unsatisfiability turns on.
