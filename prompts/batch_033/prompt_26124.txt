My philosophy professor has been working through a unit on reasons, and she's given verdicts on a bunch of cases. I'm trying to reverse-engineer what theory she's actually running. Here's what she's said so far:

- Owen wants a gin and tonic, and he believes the bottle on the counter is gin. It's actually petrol. She says Owen has no reason to drink from it.
- A woman wants to stay healthy and knows jogging would help, but she hates jogging and feels zero motivation to do it. She says the woman has a reason to jog.
- A man has no sympathetic desires, no concern for reputation, no desires that would be furthered by helping strangers — he's just indifferent to others. He walks past a child drowning in a shallow pond. She says he has no reason to help.
- Maria was raised in a cult and her only genuine desire is to obey the cult leader. She has no other motivations that bear on the situation. The leader orders her to harm an innocent person. She says Maria has a reason to refuse.
- A gambler wants the thrill of betting his last savings and has no other relevant desires. She says he has a reason not to bet.

Next week's case: a CEO wants to maximize profits and genuinely doesn't care about environmental harm — it's just not in her motivational set. There's a legal manufacturing process that pollutes a local river but boosts profits. Does the CEO have a reason not to pollute?

I'm less interested in the yes/no than in what rule generates these verdicts. Is she an internalist, an externalist, or something more nuanced? Which verdict is doing the most work to constrain the options, and what would she have to say about the CEO to stay consistent? Also, if you had to design one more case to definitively settle which theory she's commited to, what would it be?
