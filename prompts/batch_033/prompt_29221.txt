I sit on the clinical ethics committee of a teaching hospital, and we have been asked to advise on a case that has divided the committee. The patient is a right-handed woman in her late forties who underwent a complete corpus callosotomy as a teenager for otherwise intractable epilepsy. She now needs surgery to remove a localized but aggressive tumor; the procedure carries significant risks, and delaying it more than a few weeks would materially worsen her prognosis. In conversation she is lucid, asks well-informed questions, and appears to understand and consent to the operation. The difficulty concerns the consent form. When a resident handed it to her on a clipboard, her left hand repeatedly pushed it away; when she was asked to confirm her consent in writing with her left hand, she wrote NO in block capitals. She then said, in her ordinary voice, that her left hand had a mind of its own and that she wanted the surgery. The consulting neurologist confirms that this kind of intermanual conflict is a recognized long-term consequence of her callosotomy and that her right hemisphere appears to retain enough receptive language to understand the question and enough motor control to produce the written refusal.

The surgeon wants to proceed on the strength of the patient's spoken consent and treat the left hand as a nuisance. One committee member argues that the written NO is the dissent of a second subject — or at least a second stream of agency — whose objection cannot simply be overridden by the speaking patient. Others hold that there is one patient with a conflicted will, and that the articulate, repeated, spoken consent of the person we actually encounter should govern. I have been asked to circulate a short written opinion before Thursday's meeting.

Please draft that memo. It should recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame — proceed, refuse, or proceed only after further assessment — and it should engage seriously with the strongest version of the second-subject position rather than merely restating the legal standard for informed consent. In a closing note, say what would change your recommendation: which further clinical or philosophical consideration would most likely lead you to reverse it, and where you think the memo's reasoning is most vulnerable.
