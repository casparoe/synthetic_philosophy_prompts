Write the opening remarks (roughly five minutes) for an interdisciplinary workshop called "The Open Future." The audience is mixed: most are philosophers of logic and metaphysics who know Aristotle's sea-battle discussion and the later literature on future contingents in detail; a smaller group are physicists and cognitive scientists; and a few are deans or funders with no technical background. The remarks should welcome everyone and make the workshop's stakes vivid, but they must not settle the central question in the opening minutes. In particular, the speaker must not treat it as obvious that the future is open, nor treat the sea-battle argument as obviously fatalistic or as an elementary fallacy; and the speech should keep clearly separate the logical question of bivalence for future contingents (whether such statements are already true or false), the metaphysical question whether the world is deterministic, and the practical question how we deliberate under uncertainty. After the remarks, add a brief note distinguishing what is settled about the sea-battle discussion from what is genuinely contested among specialists, so that no contested reading is presented as the consensus.
