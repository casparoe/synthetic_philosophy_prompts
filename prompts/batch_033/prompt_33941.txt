I work as a software engineer on evaluation infrastructure for a large language model company, and I keep getting pulled into a running argument with a colleague on our safety team about debate as a scalable oversight method. He is considerably more optimistic about it than I am, and last week he laid out a version of the case that I have not been able to get out of my head. As I reconstruct it, the argument runs as follows.

(1) Interactive proof systems show that a weak verifier can, by interrogating a prover, come to accept claims it could never have established on its own, and this soundness holds even when the prover is adversarial.
(2) AI debate has that same structure: a human judge who could not establish the answer alone interrogates two systems, one defending the true answer and one defending a false one, each able to challenge the other's claims.
(3) What makes interactive proofs work is not the particular mathematics but the structural fact that a weak verifier can cross-examine a stronger party and catch inconsistencies in its answers.
(4) Debate preserves that structural fact.
(5) Therefore a human judge interrogating two superhuman debaters can, in principle, verify answers far beyond the judge's own competence.
(6) Therefore debate solves scalable oversight in principle, and what remains is engineering: training debaters, keeping judges attentive, building evaluation sets.

I find (6) very hard to accept. It amounts to getting the central problem of alignment almost for free from a mechanism, and I have been burned enough times by arguments that move from clever structure plus superhuman components to safety that I want to know exactly where this one overreaches. But I cannot identify the step that is doing the work, and my colleague insists that rejecting any of (1) through (4) means giving up on the whole research direction rather than just on his strong conclusion. My question is: which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost for the broader case for debate? Be as precise as you can about the difference between the formal setting of interactive proofs and what a human judge actually does during a debate. Please start by naming the step you would reject in one sentence, then justify the choice. I would also like you to say what would most likely change your diagnosis: the consideration or empirical finding that would lead you to point at a different step, and where you think your own reading of the argument is least certain. Keep the entire response under 700 words.
