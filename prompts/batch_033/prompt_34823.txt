ok so I'm taking this grad seminar on moral psychology and we've spent the last three weeks absolutely stuck on the same debate about moral worth. You know the one: what makes an action have moral worth — what makes it creditworthy in the way that matters for praise and blame? The professor is getting frustated and told us to try writing an "adversarial collaboration" document between the two positions that keep butting heads in class. She said we should actually try to produce something both sides could sign, the way theists and atheists have done in some famous collaborations, rather than just another "here's why my side is right" paper.

The two camps in the room are:

Side 1 (call them the "duty-first" view): An action has moral worth iff the agent does it *from the motive of duty* — they do the right thing because they recognize it as morally required. If you help someone just because you feel warm and fuzzy about it, that's nice but it doesn't have moral worth in the strict sense. The paradigmatic case for them is Kant's person who helps others from duty even when all their sympathetic feelings have dried up and they feel nothing but cold indifference — that person's action still has moral worth, maybe even the purest kind.

Side 2 (call them the "right-reasons" view): An action has moral worth iff the agent acts for the features that *actually make the action right* — the right-making reasons. The agent doesn't have to conceptualize those reasons *as duties*. The paradigmatic case for them is Huck Finn helping Jim escape slavery: Huck thinks he's doing something wrong (he believes the prevailing morality that says you should return escaped slaves), but he helps Jim anyway out of friendship and a recognition of Jim's humanity. On the right-reasons view, Huck's action has moral worth because he's responding to the features that genuinely make helping Jim right, even though he misdescribes the moral status of his action to himself.

These two sides keep talking past each other. The duty-first people say "but Huck didn't think he was doing his duty, so how can his action have moral worth?" and the right-reasons people say "but the cold-duty person is just following a rule — aren't they missing the point of *why* helping people matters?"

I need to draft the document they'd produce after actually sitting down and trying to find common ground. Here's specifically what I want:

- A numbered list of substantive agreed claims — stuff both sides can actually sign, not just boring platitudes. Include claims about what moral worth is *not*, about specific cases where both sides agree on the verdict even if they disagree on the explanation, and about what the debate is really about.

- A disagreements table. For each genuine disagreement, give: (a) the precise formulation each side would accept for its own position, (b) the crux — the sub-question that if answered would resolve at least that disagreement, and (c) at least one concrete case or test that both sides agree bears on the crux, with each side's prediction about it.

- Finally, a verdict on whether any part of the dispute is purely verbal. If some disagreements are just about different ways of using the phrase "moral worth," say which ones and why.

At the very end, put the one-sentence summary of the whole thing inside \boxed{...}.
