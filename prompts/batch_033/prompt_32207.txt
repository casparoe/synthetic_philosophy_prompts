I'm a PhD student in cognitive neuroscience, and I keep encountering the same move in discussions about mind uploading and whole brain emulation. The claim is that the brain is, at bottom, a computer — neurons implement something like logic gates, action potentials are effectively bits, and the mind is the software running on neural hardware. From this, people conclude that uploading a mind is a straightforward engineering problem: scan the brain at sufficient resolution, simulate the circuit, and you've got the person running on silicon. I've heard versions of this from computational neuroscientists in my department, from visitors in the neuromorphic computing community, and, most consequentially, from funders and institute directors who treat the conceptual question as closed and the remaining challenges as purely technical.

I want you to audit this analogy as thoroughly as you can. Start with a table of correspondences: if the brain is a computer, what maps to the hardware, the software, the processor, the memory, the clock, the instruction set, the program counter, the compiler, the operating system, and the distinction between stored program and stored data? Then walk through where the mapping breaks down. I'm especially interested in which disanalogies matter for the upload conclusion and which are mere differences of implementation that don't threaten the inference. In particular: does the analogy quietly import features of digital, von Neumann architecture — discrete states, a fetch-execute cycle, the bit-for-bit copyability of software, the clean separation of stored program and stored data — that neural tissue simply lacks, and that would have to be true of the brain for the upload conclusion to survive? And if the brain is not a computer in the ways that count for the upload argument, what is a better analogy, and what does that alternative analogy imply about the feasibility of the projects the computer analogy is currently being used to license?
