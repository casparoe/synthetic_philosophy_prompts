Last week I watched two friends of mine, Maya and Leo, get into a long argument about something that happened to Leo, and I'm still not sure who was right. Leo got stopped outside a gas station by a guy who claimed to be a time traveler stranded in our era; the guy asked to borrow $50 and promised to come back in a week and repay Leo with $1 billion. Leo told him to get lost. When he told us about it, Maya said he'd made an expected-value error: even if you put the chance the guy was for real at one in a million, the expected value of handing over $50 is (0.000001 × $1,000,000,000) − $50, which comes to about $950. She said that's a better deal than almost any lottery ticket, so if Leo has ever bought one, he's being inconsistent — he just let the weirdness of the story override the math.

Leo said the one-in-a-million number was pulled out of thin air, and that his actual credence was "effectively zero," not one in a million. Maya answered that zero is a much stronger claim than "very small" — you'd be saying it's literally impossible the guy was telling the truth — and that expected utility doesn't require exact probabilities, just your best estimate. Leo came back that the theory commits you to absurdity: if the guy had promised $10 trillion instead, the expected value would only get bigger, so a mugger can always name a number big enough to swamp whatever credence you've got. On Maya's principle, he said, he'd be obligated to hand his wallet to anyone with a good enough story, and that can't be a theory of rational action. Maya said that was too quick, because as the promised amount grows, her credence in the story shrinks — a $10 trillion promise is much less believable than a $1 billion one — so the product doesn't automatically grow. Leo said that just means she's tuning her credences to avoid the crazy conclusion, and asked what principle sets how fast her credence shrinks as the story gets bigger. Maya replied that all credences are estimates from evidence, that more extraordinary claims getting lower credences isn't ad hoc, and that the alternative — a hard cap on payoffs or a floor under probabilities — is arbitrary and can be exploited too. Leo finished by saying maybe rationality needs exactly that kind of cap, and if the axioms of expected utility imply he has to pay the time traveler, then the axioms are wrong, not his refusal. Maya said you can't keep expected value for normal cases and drop it when the numbers feel weird; that's just the inconsistency the theory was supposed to prevent.

They went in circles after that, and I honestly couldn't tell who's argument was better. Can you adjudicate this move by move? For each step, say who's right about what, where the other person has the better point, and what both of them are missing. I'm not asking who wins overall — I want the specific moves rated, including the ones that look right but aren't.
