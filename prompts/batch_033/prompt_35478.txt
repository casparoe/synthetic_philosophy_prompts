I'm currently taking a graduate seminar on rational choice and strategic interaction, and we've spent the last two weeks on the backward induction paradox in the centipede game. I find myself going back and forth on whether the backward induction argument is genuinely compelling as a normative guide to play, or whether it somehow goes wrong — and if so, where exactly the reasoning breaks down.

I'd like you to write a dialogue between two characters, both of whom are philosophically sophisticated game theorists, who disagree about whether backward induction yields the correct prescription in the centipede game. Neither character should be a strawman; each should be able to articulate the strongest version of their position and should be given equal space to develop their view.

The characters should, at minimum, wrestle with the following questions:

- Does common knowledge of rationality among the players actually force the backward induction outcome, or does the reasoning implicitly assume something that common knowledge of rationality does not by itself guarantee — for instance, that each player would continue to believe in the other's rationality even after observing a move inconsistent with it?
