I recently read a chapter on the philosophy of death in a popular introduction to philosophy. The author summarizes the Epicurean argument against death being bad for you like this:

"Epicurus argued that death can't be a harm to the person who dies, because you can only be harmed by things you actually experience. When you're alive, you aren't dead, so death hasn't happened to you yet. When you're dead, you no longer exist, so there's no 'you' left to experience the harm of being dead. It's like how you weren't bothered by all the centuries before you were born — you won't be bothered by the centuries after you die either. So death is nothing to fear, and anyone who thinks otherwise is just confused about what it means to not exist."

Something about this summary bothers me, but I can't tell whether the problem is with the summary or with the argument itself. I've heard that philosophers like Thomas Nagel have pushed back against this argument, but I don't know the details. Can you go through this summary and tell me what's accurate, what's a simplification, and what a defender of the Epicurean view would actually say versus what a critic like Nagel would object to? I'm especially curious about two things: whether the analogy to prenatal nonexistence was part of Epicurus's own argument or was added by someone else, and whether the conclusion that we "shouldn't fear death" follows even if we accept the core claim that being dead doesn't harm the person who dies.
