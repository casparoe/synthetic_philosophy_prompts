Analyze the Han van Meegeren affair as a case study in the philosophical problem of fakes, forgeries, and the value of originals. Take the salient facts as given: in the 1930s and 1940s van Meegeren produced paintings in the manner of Vermeer, presented them as newly discovered works by the master, and saw them praised by critics and acquired by serious collectors and museums; after the war the paintings were exposed as forgeries, and the same canvases collapsed in artistic esteem even though their perceptible properties had not changed. Begin by laying out three candidate explanations of why an original might be valued over a perceptually indistinguishable forgery: a perceptualist view that locates aesthetic value in observable features alone; a contextualist view that makes the history of production partly constitutive of a work's aesthetic character; and an achievement-based view that values a work as the record of an artist's actual performance. Then run each account through the episode. For each, say what it implies about the judgments the critics, buyers, and later viewers should have made, both before and after the exposure; which features of the case it handles cleanly; and which features it must strain to accommodate or leave unexplained. Keep the distinct levels separate throughout: the aesthetic value of the painted surface, the market value of the object, the moral assessment of the forger, and the competence of the experts who authenticated the works. Finally, take a position on what the episode shows about the underlying debate: does it confirm the contextualist view, pose a problem for it, or reveal that the opposition between perceptible features and history of production was too coarse from the start? Where a position is ambiguous between readings, state the reading you are employing before evaluating it.
