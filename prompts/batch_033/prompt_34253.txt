I'm a journalist working on a profile of a cognitive scientist — let's call her Dr. M — who's built a career on distinguishing genuine comprehension from mere competence. I've been through her papers, interviews, and our own conversations, and I've collected her verdicts on a bunch of cases. She's not the kind of thinker who states her criterion up front; she works case by case and let's the pattern emerge. But I need to file this piece and I want to nail what she'd say about the obvious next case everyone will ask about.

Her verdicts so far, as best I can reconstruct them:

- Termite mound: mere competence. The colony builds an air-conditioned cathedral but no individual termite has a clue what it's doing.
- Orchid that mimics a female bee: mere competence. Natural selection sculpted the shape, but there's no understanding anywhere in the system.
- Deep Blue beating Kasparov: mere competence. Brute-force search plus hand-coded evaluation, zero grasp of chess.
- Pocket calculator: mere competence. Pure algorithmic crank-turning.
- A human toddler learning her first language: genuine comprehension. She gets what words are for.
- A spider weaving an orb web: mere competence. Fixed action patterns, however beautiful.
- A rat learning a radial arm maze: she hesitated. "I'd lean no, but I'm less confident than with the spider." She was reaching for something and couldn't quite commit.

Now, the case I need to predict her on: a current large language model — the kind that writes passable essays, adapts its tone to the audience, solves novel reasoning problems, and can explain its own reasoning when prompted. Not some hypothetical future AGI, but what we have right now.

I can see at least three or four principles that fit her verdicts equally well. One is about the *source* of the competence: if the design process didn't itself involve comprehension (evolution, gradient descent, blind search), the output is mere competence. Another is about *generality*: if the system handles genuinely novel cases flexibly, it's got comprehension. A third is about whether the system builds and uses *causal models* of the world. And a fourth might be about *phenomenal consciousness* being required for comprehension — though she's never said so outright.

These rules give different answers about the language model. The source rule says no (trained by gradient descent). The generality rule says yes (it's nothing if not flexible). The causal-model rule is ambiguous verging on yes. The consciousness rule says no.

What I need from you: you're an expert philosopher of cognitive science who knows this literature inside out. Think carefully — start by brainstorming what each candidate rule would predict about the LLM case, and which of her verdicts each rule handles most naturally and which it strains to accommodate. Then give me your best prediction of what Dr. M would say, with the reasons that tip the balance. And finally: what's the one additional case I should ask her about in our final interview that would most cleanly discriminate between the rules she might be running?
