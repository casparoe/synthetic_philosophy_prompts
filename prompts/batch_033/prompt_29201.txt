A friend and I spent a long evening arguing about Wittgenstein's account of religious belief, and I want a careful adjudication of the exchange. I'll reconstruct it as fairly as I can, labeling the two positions A and B, and I won't say which side I was on.

A began with a broadly Wittgensteinian line: we misunderstand religious belief when we treat "God exists" as an ordinary factual hypothesis held on evidence. Religious belief, A said, is not a detached conjecture about an invisible entity; it is a picture that organizes a whole life — how a person acts, hopes, grieves, and ranks what matters. Demanding evidence for it, A said, is like demanding evidence that a loved one is trustworthy: it mistakes the role the commitment plays.

B replied that this move abandons the truth question. If "God exists" is not answerable to evidence, then it is not the kind of claim that can be true or false about the world, yet believers take themselves to be asserting something about reality. So, B said, A is either changing the subject or treating religious language as non-cognitive — a view believers themselves would reject.

A answered that it is about reality, but not in the way a physics claim is. The standards for what counts as reasonable, A said, are internal to the religious form of life; there is no neutral standpoint outside all practice from which the question can be settled. Talk of evidence only makes sense inside a practice that has already fixed what would count for or against a claim.

B came back that this makes religious belief unfalsifiable and therefore immune to rational criticism. It also cannot explain actual religious doubt: people lose faith because of the problem of evil or historical discoveries about their scriptures, and those look like evidence weighing against belief. On A's view, B said, such losses of faith would be unintelligible, or mere non-rational shifts.

A replied that those considerations do real work, but only within a life that is already religiously serious: they can make the whole picture ring hollow, much as a relationship can collapse under accumulated disappointments without any single experiment refuting it. That, A said, is not the same as the picture being a hypothesis.

B ended: then religious disagreement is in principle unresolvable, and A has landed in a kind of relativism — true-for-them — which is not what believers mean when they say God exists.

Please adjudicate this move by move. For each move, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating; then say who is right about which specific point, and what, if anything, both parties are missing. Also tell me which argument or consideration, if strengthened, would most likely change your verdict, or where your own reasoning is most fragile. Approach this as a professional philosopher specializing in epistemology and the philosophy of religion: think carefully, and start by distinguishing the different things each side might mean before you judge.
