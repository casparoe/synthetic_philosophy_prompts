My own field is early modern philosophy, and I have repeatedly encountered a kind of disagreement in university governance and public life that I have never been able to characterize precisely. In a faculty meeting last term, two colleagues argued at length about whether the university really exists over and above its members, its buildings, and its legal charter. They agreed on every concrete fact one could list: who was employed, what the budget was, which officers were authorized to sign contracts, what the bylaws required, and what would happen under various counterfactual reorganizations. When I pressed them, they even offered what sounded like the same criterion for 'really exists' — something like: a thing really exists if it is part of the world and not merely a convenient name we give to a plurality. And yet one insisted that the university itself acts, owes duties, and can be harmed, while the other insisted that all such talk is shorthand for the coordinated acts of individual persons, and that only the persons really exist. Neither could state any further observation or argument that would settle the matter, and neither was confused about the facts. I have seen the same structure elsewhere: two economists who agree on every transaction nonetheless disagree about whether 'the economy' is a real thing or a summary; two clinicians who agree on a patient's symptoms nonetheless disagree about whether the syndrome is a real disease or a label for a cluster.

I am not after the dismissive reply that the parties are simply arguing about words. That phrase seems to me to cover several different phenomena, and it is often used to close off a disagreement that the parties themselves experience as substantive. I would be grateful for a precise diagnosis. Is there a term of art for this configuration, and what exactly does it cover? A bare label would not be of much use by itself; what I need is an account of the boundaries — of how such a dispute differs from an ordinary factual disagreement, from two people quietly assigning different meanings to a word, and from a substantive ontological disagreement that cannot be settled by observation. Is there a workable test for recognizing when a disagreement is of this kind, or is the distinction itself less sharp than it appears?
