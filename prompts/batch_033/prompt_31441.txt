I've recently become responsible for the evaluation metric my team's work is scored against, and within a couple of weeks I watched people start to move the number rather than the thing the number was tracking. The experience has made wireheading and reward tampering feel personally urgent rather than like a puzzle about hypothetical AI systems, and I want to run a session that lets other people feel the pull of corrupting the reward channel from the inside, rather than hearing a lecture that names the problem afterwards.

I run a monthly workshop of about 18 people, mostly engineers and product people with a few philosophy students, and I have one 75-minute session. Design a game or structured exercise in which the rules and incentives themselves lead participants to attack the scoring mechanism instead of performing the assigned task. They should discover the move themselves and only later have it named; there should be no framing up front, and the word "wireheading" should not appear until the debrief.

Give me the materials and setup; a precise scoring or payoff scheme that requires no real money; any roles; a timeline that fits the 75 minutes; and a debrief that maps what happened onto the distinction between the underlying task, the proxy or reward signal, and the option of tampering with the signal or with the mechanism that computes and delivers it. In the debrief, separate wireheading proper (corrupting the agent's own reward channel so that reward arrives regardless of the task) from reward tampering (corrupting the mechanism that calculates or supplies the reward), and say which of the two the exercise actually produces, since I suspect most classroom games produce one but not the other.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it: participants too cooperative to exploit the rules, participants exploiting them so quickly the game collapses, a team deciding to win by sabotaging another team's score rather than their own, and so on, and how I should handle each. Be explicit about what the exercise cannot reproduce about real wireheading. My guess is that the real case lacks a knowing cheater: the system is simply doing what the reward signal in fact rewards, not being sneaky, and a game with human participants may smuggle in a feeling of transgression that distorts the lesson. Tell me how much that matters and what I should say about it in the debrief.

Finally, say where your design's reasoning is most load-bearing. Which assumption about how this particular group will behave, if it turned out false, would lead you to redesign the activity, and what should I observe on the night that would make you conclude the exercise had misfired rather than made the point?
