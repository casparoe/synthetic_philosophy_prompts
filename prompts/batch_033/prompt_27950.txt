I teach a third-year undergraduate module on moral psychology at a British university — roughly 18 students, one two-hour seminar. We are three weeks into a unit on Adam Smith, and I want them to feel the force of the impartial spectator before I name it or lecture on it.

Some personal context: last year I found myself in a protracted family dispute over the care of an ageing parent. I noticed that my own reasoning shifted dramatically depending on whether I was thinking as a daughter with a stake in the outcome, or as someone trying to imagine what a stranger with no connection to any of us would say was fair. The second perspective felt more authoritative, but also harder to sustain. When I later read Smith, the idea of the impartial spectator — the imagined observer we consult when we step outside our own partiality — landed with unusual force. I want to design an activity that gives students something like that experience: the shift from partial self-judgment to the perspective of an imagined impartial observer, and the sense that the latter carries a distinctive kind of authority.

Constraints: no lecture up front; they should not hear the phrase "impartial spectator" or any Smithian terminology until the debrief. The activity should work for 18 students in a two-hour slot, including the debrief. No materials beyond paper, pens, and a way to display instructions. The activity should generate real (if low-stakes) ethical reasoning, not just hypothetical posturing — participants should feel the pull of partiality and then the shift that comes from adopting a more impartial standpoint, rather than merely discussing it in the abstract.

Please give me: the full setup and rules; a timeline; the exact prompts or questions displayed at each stage; a debrief script that maps what happened onto Smith's account of sympathy and the impartial spectator (with attention to the move from natural partiality, through mutual sympathy, to the standpoint of the imagined impartial judge); a note on what predictably goes wrong and how to handle it; and an honest assessment of which feature of Smith's view the activity cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator afterwards.

Please write the answer in British English.
