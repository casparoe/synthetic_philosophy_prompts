I've read a bunch about HCH (humans consulting humans) and factored cognition and keep nodding along, but I've realized I don't actually know what criterion I'm running when I say a task "factors." So I want to reverse-engineer the principle from my own gut calls. Setup: by factored cognition I mean the idealized thing where a big cognitive job gets split into chunks small enough that each worker only sees a little slice, plus some shallow integrator that combines the outputs. Assume the workers are competent humans, communication is limited, and the question is whether a decomposition exists that doesn't lose the thing you were trying to do.

Here are my verdicts, as honestly as I can report them:

- Translating a 400-page novel: factorable. One translator per chapter, a glossary, one continuity/style editor at the end; it comes out fine.
- Writing a new novel: not factorable. The voice and the plot have to be held together in one head; if you farm out chapters it reads like it.
- Checking a 10,000-row spreadsheet against source documents: factorable. Pure grind, split the rows.
- Finding a proof of a hard theorem: not factorable. The key move is one insight that only happens when one person has the whole problem in their head.
- Checking a proof that's already written: factorable. Each step can be checked against the lines above it with a small amount of local context.
- Grading short-answer exam questions: factorable. One grader per question, nothing lost.
- Debugging a subtle race condition in a large codebase: not factorable. The bug lives in the interaction between components, and no single slice contains it.
- Designing a programming language: not factorable. Every feature interacts with every other feature; someone has to hold the whole design.
- Writing a two-thousand-line CRUD web app from a detailed spec: factorable. Split the endpoints, the templates, the tests.
- Diagnosing a patient with several interacting chronic conditions: not factorable. The symptoms only add up to one picture if someone holds all of them.

The pair that bothers me is translation vs. novel-writing. Both are long-form text with global constraints — voice, continuity, plot — so why does one factor and the other doesn't? I can feel the difference but I cant state it. Proof-finding vs. proof-checking feels like the same difference in miniature.

Is there a single principle that gets all ten, or close? What does it say about cases I haven't listed — writing a season of a TV show, doing a complex tax audit, synthesizing a research literature, planning a wedding? And which of my verdicts should I give up if the cleanest principle won't cover them?
