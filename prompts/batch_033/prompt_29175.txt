I've been trying to sort out what I actually think about trolley-ish cases, and I've found that my gut is more stable than my ability to say why. Here are the verdicts I keep landing on.

A runaway trolley is heading toward five workers; I can pull a switch and send it onto a side track where one worker is. I think pulling the switch is fine.

Same setup, but I'm on a footbridge and the only way to stop the trolley is to shove a large man off the bridge into it's path. I think shoving him is not fine — not even close.

Now the side track loops back onto the main line before the five; the one worker on the loop is heavy enough that his body would stop the trolley. I think pulling the switch here is not fine, or at least it feels much worse than the plain switch case, even though the body count is the same.

A pregnant woman has uterine cancer; a hysterectomy will save her and the fetus will die as a side effect. I think the hysterectomy is permissible. But a craniotomy — crushing the fetus's skull so she can survive an obstructed labor — I think is not permissible, even though again, one dies and one lives.

In war, a pilot bombs a munitions factory knowing some civilians next door will be killed: I think that's permissible if the number isn't huge. A pilot bombs a civilian neighborhood precisely to break the enemy's will to fight: not permissible, even if the civilian death toll is identical.

I expected the doctrine of double effect to capture all this — bad effect foreseen but not intended, and not the means to the good. But then I hit a snag. A doctor gives a dying patient a big dose of morphine to relieve pain, knowing it will also depress her breathing and may hasten death. I think that's permissible. But the analgesia and the respiratory depression come from the same drug doing the same thing, and I can't tell whether the hastening of death is a side effect or part of the mechanism. If it's part of the mechanism, DDE seems to condemn it, and I don't want to condemn it.

And there's a case I can't settle at all. Suppose the one worker on the side track is someone I personally assured the side track would be clear — I'm the site safety officer. Does my assurance make the switch wrong, or does it just mean I should feel terrible afterward while having done the right thing? What does the principle that handles the other cases say about that one?

So: is there one principle — double effect, or a using-as-a-means vs. allowing-foreseen-harm distinction, or maybe just a killing vs. letting-die asymmetry in a nicer suit — that gets all or nearly all of these verdicts? State it precisely, tell me which of my verdicts it can't accommodate, and apply it to the morphine case and the safety-officer case.
