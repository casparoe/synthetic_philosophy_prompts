I'm giving a talk to a philosophy-and-film group next month and I want to use Groundhog Day to think about tranquilism — the view that only the absence of craving (tanha) and suffering (dukkha) ultimately matters, and everything else is at best instrumentally valuable.

The obvious reading is that Phil Connors' arc is basically a tranquilist journey: he starts out driven by craving (for Rita, for fame, for escape), suffers enormously, and eventually finds peace precisely by letting go of craving. But I'm not sure the film actually endorses tranquilism. In the final timeline, Phil isn't just free of craving — he's actively engaged in helping people, learning the piano, ice sculpting, and pursuing Rita in a non-grasping way. That looks more like a picture of flourishing that includes positive goods, not just the absence of suffering and craving.

So here's what I want: a reading of the film that takes tranquilism seriously as an interpretive lens but also tracks where the film pushes back. Where does the film genuinely illustrate the tranquilist diagnosis (craving as the source of suffering)? Where does it suggest that something beyond the mere absence of craving matters — and would a tranquilist have a good reply? I'm especially interested in the suicide montage: if only the absence of suffering matters, why is Phil's despair during that sequence framed as a problem rather than as a rational response? And the final scene: does waking up next to Rita on February 3rd represent the end of craving, or the fulfilment of it?

I want the reading to be fair to both the film and the view — not just using the film as a stick to beat tranquilism with, and not forcing the film into a tranquilist box it doesn't fit. If there's a genuine tension, say so. And if you think the film ends up being a better argument against tranquilism than for it, I want to hear that too.
