I spent my career in molecular biology, and I have only recently begun reading philosophy of mind with any seriousness. The Searle–Dennett dispute about original versus derived intentionality has held my attention because it reminds me of disagreements in my own field about whether a phenomenon is genuinely emergent or merely an artifact of our descriptive framework. I would like a thought experiment built to order.

Construct a scenario that isolates the tension between the two distinct arguments Searle gives against the possibility of non-biological original intentionality. The first argument, from the Chinese Room, is that syntax is not sufficient for semantics: manipulating symbols according to formal rules cannot by itself produce understanding or semantic content. The second argument, from biological naturalism, is that intentionality is a causal product of the specific biophysical properties of neurons—only brains, or systems with equivalent causal powers, can have original intentionality. These two criteria can, in principle, deliver different verdicts on the same system.

Design two systems. System Alpha consists of living human neurons—a cultured organoid or a slab of cortical tissue—connected to input/output interfaces. Its neural activity is trained entirely through supervised learning to perform a symbol-manipulation task such as logical theorem-proving, where every internal state's significance is assigned by the human trainers. It is, in effect, a biological instantiation of the Chinese Room: the right causal powers are present, but all semantic content is derived from the designers. System Beta runs on conventional silicon hardware, but it is not trained by supervised learning. Instead, it is embodied in a robot that explores a physical environment, develops its own categories through unsupervised learning and reinforcement, and eventually uses symbols to navigate and communicate about its world. No human ever assigns meanings to its internal states; if it has semantic content at all, that content is intrinsic to its own activity.

The question: does either system have original intentionality? By Searle's causal-powers criterion, Alpha should qualify and Beta should not. By the syntax-versus-semantics criterion, Beta seems the more promising candidate—its symbols are grounded in sensorimotor interaction, not in a designer's interpretation—while Alpha looks like pure syntax, even though it runs on neurons. The two criteria pull in opposite directions.

Now add the Dennettian perspective. For Dennett, the distinction between original and derived intentionality is not a real joint in nature; it is a matter of which stance we find useful to adopt. His two-bitser argument says that you cannot accumulate enough derived intentionality to purchase original intentionality, because there is no such thing as original intentionality in the first place—it is derived all the way down, or rather, the very distinction collapses. From this vantage, Alpha and Beta are both systems to which we may or may not usefully adopt the intentional stance, and the fact that Alpha uses biological substrate while Beta does not is a difference in engineering, not in kind.

Please deliver the following: (a) a fleshed-out, one-paragraph description of the scenario, keeping both systems within the reach of current or near-future technology—no science fiction; (b) a walk-through of what Searle's stated position says about each system, highlighting exactly where the two criteria conflict and what a consistent Searlean must sacrifice to resolve the tension; (c) what Dennett's intentional-stance view and the two-bitser argument say about both systems, and whether the biological-versus-non-biological difference can be dismissed as easily here as it is in standard discussions; (d) an honest identification of which feature of the case does the real work, and how a defender of Searle might try to redescribe Alpha or Beta to escape the dilemma without abandoning either criterion.
