For the past two years I have been running a working group on moral aggregation — the question of when, if ever, it is permissible to violate a moral constraint (against harming, lying, breaking a promise, and so on) in order to bring about a substantially larger benefit or prevent a substantially larger harm. We have two members who have been the persistent poles of the discussion, and their disagreement has survived long enough that I no longer think either is simply misunderstanding the other. One holds what I will call the Trade-Permissive view: that moral reasons, including the reasons behind constraints, aggregate across persons and across types of consideration in roughly the way cost–benefit reasoning suggests, so that a constraint can in principle be overridden when the stakes on the other side are large enough — the constraint marks a steep slope on a continuous terrain, not a cliff. The other holds what I will call the Constraint view: that at least some moral prohibitions have a structure that is not merely a strong reason on the balance but a genuinely exclusionary or non-aggregative one — the prohibition is not for sale, even if the price is very high, because the wrongness of the act is not the kind of thing that more good elsewhere can cancel.

They have agreed to attempt an adversarial collaboration, and I have been asked to facilitate. Please draft the joint document they should produce. It should include: (1) A numbered list of claims both parties can sign. These must be substantive — not mere truisms — and should map the terrain they agree on before the fork. For each claim, note briefly why each side accepts it, since their underlying rationales may differ. (2) A disagreements table. For each row, state the exact proposition they disagree about, phrased so that each party would accept the wording of its own position. Include at least four rows, ranging from the abstract (the structure of moral reasons) to the concrete (what to say about a particular stylized case). (3) A cruxes section. Identify the sub-questions such that, if they were settled, at least one party would change their mind on the main disagreement. For each crux, state what each party predicts and what kind of evidence or argument both would agree bears on it. Flag any crux for which they cannot agree on what would count as evidence. (4) A section diagnosing whether any part of their dispute is merely verbal — if so, specify which disagreement dissolves under precisification and which does not.

The document should use concrete cases (at least two) that test the boundary between the views: one where the constraint violation is small and the benefit is large, one where the constraint violation is large and the benefit is only marginally larger, and at least one case involving a non-harm-based constraint such as a promise or a lie. Style the document as something both could circulate to others outside the working group as a fair and precise representation of the state of their disagreement.
