I'm on the committee at my university that decides which courses can count toward the science distribution requirement, and we vote next week on whether to approve a course in evolutionary psychology. I'm supposed to be consistant with our own past decisions, but I can't tell what rule we've been running, and I don't want to just vote my priors.

Here's what we've done in the last two years. Approved: a paleontology course that's all historical reconstruction and can't run experiments (a few people grumbled that you can't rerun the Cambrian, but it passed because it makes specific risky claims about where fossils should turn up); a cosmology course that's purely observational; an evolutionary biology course; and a climate modeling course, which is all simulation and no lab work. Rejected: psychoanalysis, on the grounds that it's unfalsifiable; homeopathy, on the grounds that it keeps failing tests; string theory for non-majors, because it has no contact with data yet; and astrology, obviously.

The new proposal is a survey of evolutionary psychology: mate choice, cheater detection, sex differences, the usual. Supporters say it's just evolutionary biology applied to humans and we already approved that. Opponents say it's psychoanalysis with better branding, adaptive stories that can be bent to fit anything. I can see both.

What should we decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves? Which of our precedents is the closest analog? And if our precedents genuinely underdetermine it, what principle would we be adopting with each choice? Finally, if you had to bring one more course proposal to the next meeting to force the rule out into the open, what would it be?
