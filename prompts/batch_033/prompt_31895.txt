I've been trying to find a name for something and I keep coming up empty, so I thought I'd describe it and see if it's a real thing in the literature or just a thing I keep noticing.

Here's the pattern. Someone learns a skill the normal way, gets competent at it, and then starts using a tool that does part of the work for them — a programmer using an AI code assistant, a translator working from machine-translated drafts, a doctor using a diagnostic support system. At first they check the tool's output carefully, because they can. Over time the tool gets better, or they just get used to it, and they start checking less. They skim. Then they stop checking except for the rare case where something looks obviously off.

So far that's just deskilling, and I know that's a thing. But here's the part I'm actually after. Because the tool is now reliable enough that errors are rare, the person never gets the kind of feedback that would reveal how much their own ability has decayed. The outputs keep being good, so everything feels fine. And gradually their own understanding gets hollowed out and replaced by the tool's competence — but they don't experience it as a loss. If you asked them, they'd say they still know how to do the thing. They'd mean: I can produce the result. What they actually have is the ability to operate the tool and a rough sense of whether the output looks acceptable. They've mistaken the tool's competence for their own, and the mistake is almost impossible to notice from the inside because the transition is gradual and the outcomes stay good.

I've seen this a few times. A doctor friend told me the younger residents she works with sometimes can't reason through a case without the decision-support tool, but they don't think of themselves as not knowing — they think of the tool as just how you look things up now. Another case: I know people who've been using LLMs to write their work emails and reports for a year or two, and they've clearly lost some of their own writing fluency, but they'd never notice because they don't write anything long on their own anymore. The thing that struck me in both cases is that the people involved were smart and confident and would have described themselves as fully competent, and yet if you took the tool away you'd find a real gap they didn't know was there.

Is there an established name for this specific phenomenon? Not just deskilling or skill atrophy — that's the loss itself, but I'm after the way the loss is masked by the tool's continued performance, and the way the person's epistemic confidence stays high while their actual understanding drains away. It's also not quite the Dunning-Kruger thing, because these aren't incompetent people overestimating themselves in general; they were genuinely competent once, and the competence got quietly substituted. And I don't think it's exactly automation complacency either, because that's about failing to monitor a system you're still supposed to be supervising — this is more like the supervisor gradually becoming unable to supervise and not knowing it.

Does this have a name? If it's discussed in philosophy or cognitive science, what are the nearest concepts, and how exactly does this differ from each of them? If there isn't a settled term, what would you call it, and how would you define it sharply enough to separate it from plain deskilling, Dunning-Kruger, and automation complacency? I'm less interested in the label itself than in getting the boundaries right.
