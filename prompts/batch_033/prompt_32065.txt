I've been thinking about Kendall Walton's solution to the paradox of tragedy — the idea that our emotional responses to fiction are not genuine emotions but "quasi-emotions" generated by a game of make-believe. On this view, when I weep at the end of a tragedy, I am not actually sad; I am participating in a pretense in which it is fictionally the case that I am sad. I physiologically resemble someone who is sad, and it is true in the game of make-believe that I am sad, but the mental state itself is not the real thing. Since quasi-emotions are not inherently aversive in the way real negative emotions are, the paradox dissolves: I am not perversely seeking out genuine pain, I am seeking out an imaginative experience that involves simulating pain-like states without the sting. The view has a certain elegance — it sidesteps the need to explain how we could enjoy real suffering — and it connects neatly to Walton's broader theory of representation as make-believe.

I find the view genuinely attractive, but I am not sure I should. I want a systematic map of what would count against it, written by someone who takes the view seriously rather than dismisses it out of hand. What would a defender of the quasi-emotion view have to concede as counting against it? I want the defeaters — empirical findings, phenomenological observations, thought experiments, theoretical considerations — sorted from fatal to merely awkward. Which results in psychology or neuroscience would be awkward for the view, and which would be decisive? Which features of our ordinary engagement with fiction does the view handle well, and which does it handle poorly? I am thinking, for instance, of the fact that we sometimes avoid tragic art when we are already grieving or emotionally fragile, which suggests our responses to fiction are real enough to threaten us; or the fact that we can be genuinely disturbed by a work and still seek it out, which seems to require that the disturbance be real. I also want to know whether the view has been qualified in ways that insulate it from falsification — for instance, if the claim is that quasi-emotions are subjectively indistinguishable from real emotions, then what observation could possibly tell against the view? And if it cannot be told against, is that a problem for a theory of this kind, or is it the sort of thing we should expect when we are theorizing about the metaphysics of fiction?
