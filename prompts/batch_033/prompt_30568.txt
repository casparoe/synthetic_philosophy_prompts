I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I've been reading around decision theory because Harsanyi's aggregation theorem keeps coming up in arguments that utilitarianism has a formal foundation. I reconstructed the following argument from a few sources, and it looks like it proves too much. I can't find the mistake.

(1) For any rational individual, preferences over lotteries satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, so each individual's preferences can be represented by a utility function that is unique up to positive affine transformation (a choice of scale and zero point).
(2) Social preferences, understood as the preferences of a rational, impartial planner, should satisfy the same von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms.
(3) Pareto: if every individual weakly prefers lottery p to lottery q, the social planner weakly prefers p to q; and if every individual weakly prefers p to q and at least one strictly prefers p to q, the planner strictly prefers p to q.
(4) Given (1)–(3), Harsanyi's aggregation theorem says the social utility function must be an affine combination of the individual utility functions: W = a1·u1 + ... + an·un + b, with positive weights.
(5) Since the additive constant b doesn't affect choices, maximizing W is just maximizing a weighted sum of individual utilities — which is weighted utilitarianism.
(6) But weighted utilitarianism is a substantive, controversial moral theory. It cannot be forced on us by consistency axioms plus a Pareto condition that almost everyone accepts.
(7) Therefore at least one of (1)–(3) must be false, even though each looked unobjectionable on its own.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it commit me to? I'd prefer to keep (1) and (3) if I can, but I care more about understanding the price of each option than about preserving any particular premise.
