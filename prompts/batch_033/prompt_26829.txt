I'm a PhD student in behavioral economics, and last week at our department's decision theory reading group, two of my cohort got into a pretty heated argument about the Ellsberg paradox, and I've been turning it over ever since. They were going back and forth for like twenty minutes and I honestly couldnt tell who had the better of it. I'd love someone to go through it move by move and tell me who's right about what.

Here's roughly how it went. Maya started by sketching the classic Ellsberg setup: two urns, each with 100 balls. Urn 1 has 50 red and 50 black. Urn 2 also has red and black balls, but in unknown proportion — could be anything from 0 to 100 red. You pick a color, and then you draw from an urn of your choice; if you draw that color, you win $100. She said she'd bet on Urn 1 no matter which color she picked, and she claimed that's not just some psychological glitch — it's actually rational, because when you don't know the odds you should be cautious. She said ambiguity aversion is a sensible epistemic humility, not a mistake.

Then David jumped in. He said: look, if you prefer Urn 1 when the winning color is red, that means you think your chance of drawing red from Urn 2 is less than 50%. And if you prefer Urn 1 when the winning color is black, that means you think your chance of drawing black from Urn 2 is also less than 50%. But those two probabilities have to sum to 100%. So you're simultaneously committed to P(red) < 0.5 and P(black) < 0.5, which is incoherent. He said this isn't just a technical point — the preferences literally contradict each other, and someone who holds them can be Dutch-booked.

Maya came back with: that argument only works if you assume I have precise, point-valued subjective probabilities. But I don't. My credence about the proportion of red balls in Urn 2 is imprecise — it's a range, maybe [0.2, 0.8] or even [0, 1]. And a rational agent with imprecise probabilities can prefer a bet with a known 50% chance to one where the probability is only known to fall in some wide interval. That's not incoherent, it's just not representable with a single additive probability measure. She said the real mistake is insisting that all uncertainty has to be quantifiable in the same way.

David said fine, imprecise probabilities, but then you've got a different problem. If you've got a set of probability measures, how do you decide between acts? If you go with the maximin rule — evaluating each act by its worst-case expected utility across your set of priors — then you get exactly the Ellsberg preferences, sure. But that rule can lead to choices that are strictly dominated. He sketched a case: suppose you face two acts, and no matter what prior in your set turns out to be right, act A yields a worse outcome than act B. The maximin rule can still pick A, because it only looks at the worst-case for each act separately and doesn't compare them state by state. He said that's a reductio — no decision rule that picks strictly dominated options can be rational.

Maya pushed back: the dominated-choice examples always rely on packaging together bets in ways that don't reflect how decisions are actually made. In the Ellsberg setup, you're making a single choice between two urns. The Dutch book and the dominated-choice arguments both assume you'll accept a bunch of bets simultaneously, but in real life you can refuse the package. She said ambiguity aversion is about what you do in the single-shot case, where the information really is missing, and it's the expected-utility orthodoxy that's unrealistic by pretending all uncertainty is like roulette.

At that point they went around on whether the single-shot / package distinction holds up, and the reading group ended before they resolved it. I've been trying to sort out who had the stronger position at each step. Could you go through their exchange point by point and say who's right about what? And if there's a deeper issue they both missed, I'd like to know that too.
