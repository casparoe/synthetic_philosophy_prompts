Ive been teaching a course on philosophy of mind and we just did Dennett's tower of generate-and-test — you know, the Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, Gregorian progression. The idea that minds evolve by internalizing selection pressures: first natural selection does the testing (Darwinian creatures), then the creature's own actions get reinforced or not (Skinnerian), then the creature can test hypotheses in its head without actually doing the thing (Popperian), and finally the creature can offload hypotheses into the cultural toolkits of language and memes (Gregorian).

Anyway, I'm also teaching Frankenstein next week and it hit me that the creature's development maps onto this ladder almost beat for beat. He starts with raw sensation, learns from pleasure and pain, then from observing the De Laceys he picks up language and moral reasoning — and the tragedy is that he becomes this fully Gregorian mind but is denied any actual place in human culture. I want to do a reading of the novel through Dennett's framework, but I also don't want to just force the book into a neat diagram. Where does the creature not fit the tower? Is there something about his mind — the speed of his learning, his self-consciousness about his own origins, his relationship to Victor — that Dennett's categories can't capture? And what does the novel say about the dangers of a Gregorian mind that has language and culture but no community — is that even a coherent category in Dennett's scheme, or does it break the tower?
