I am writing a novel whose protagonist has pain asymbolia: he reports his pains accurately—where they are, how intense they are, what they feel like—but he is not distressed by them, and he has no impulse to stop them. I want the book's treatment of pain to be more than decorative, so I have been recording my own verdicts on a series of cases. I would like to know what principle, if any, captures them.

1. The protagonist has a dental abscess. He says the pain is a sharp seven in his lower jaw, then returns to his book. He does not flinch, does not want the pain to stop, and would not take an analgesic if offered. Verdict: he is in pain, but this pain is not bad for him.

2. A post-surgical patient on morphine says her pain is unchanged in location and intensity, but that it simply no longer bothers her. Verdict: the pain remains, but it is no longer bad for her.

3. A marathoner has deep muscle soreness the day after a race and finds it satisfying; she is pleased by it and does not want it to stop. Verdict: the soreness is not bad for her.

4. A masochist has arranged to feel an intense, stinging pain that he savors. Verdict: the pain is not bad for him—quite the reverse—though it is unquestionably pain.

5. A person born without pain sensation repeatedly burns and fractures herself and must inspect her body each night for injuries. Verdict: her painlessness is a serious harm, even though she is never subject to the unpleasantness of pain.

6. An acutely anxious patient with a mildly sprained wrist finds the pain unbearable and all-consuming; it ruins her week, although the injury is trivial. Verdict: the pain is bad for her far out of proportion to its intensity.

7. The protagonist's wife insists that his condition is a misfortune even on days when it causes no injury, because something important is missing from his life. I want to agree with her, but I cannot see how to square that with verdicts 1 through 4.

The case I keep returning to is a woman in labor who feels intense pain and wants it to stop, yet says it is the most meaningful pain she has known and that she would not trade it for a painless delivery. Is the pain bad for her?

Is there a principle about what makes pain bad—or about how pain relates to its badness—that captures all or nearly all of these verdicts? If no single principle captures them, which of my verdicts must I give up, and what is the simplest principle that captures the rest? State the principle precisely, show how it handles each case, say what it commits me to about the labor case and the wife's claim, and describe one further case of your own devising that would most sharply test it.
