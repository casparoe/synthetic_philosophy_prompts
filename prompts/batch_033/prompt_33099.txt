I've spent a lot of time thinking about how to size bets when you have an edge, and I've become convinced that the Kelly criterion — maximizing expected logarithmic wealth — is the right answer, not just for gamblers but as a general principle of sequential decision-making under uncertainty. The core intuition that grabbed me is that maximizing expected wealth leads to overbetting and eventual ruin, while maximizing expected log wealth avoids this because it's the unique strategy that almost surely outperforms any other fixed fraction in the long run. I've also come to think the ergodicity arguments — that log wealth is the appropriate ergodic observable for multiplicative growth processes — give this a deeper foundation than just picking a utility function.

I want to know what would count against this view. What observations, arguments, formal results, or edge cases should make me reconsider? I'm not asking for the standard objections I've already encountered (fractional Kelly as a practical matter, uncertainty about the true edge, non-log utility functions). I want the deeper trouble: what would have to be true for the Kelly framework to be wrong or importantly incomplete? Which assumptions are load-bearing in a way that their failure would be fatal, and which would merely be awkward? And I want to know whether the view has been insulated by qualifications — "Kelly is right, but only under these conditions" — to the point where it risks nothing empirically, and whether that's a problem for a view that presents itself as a practical norm.
