I run a product org at a mid-size software company, and every quarter we sit down for a kill/continue review of half-built features, internal tools, and legacy migrations. The company line is always 'don't throw good money after bad,' but I keep seeing decisions where walking away looks cheaper only because you ignore what we've already learned, or the cost of restarting later, or what killing the project does to the team. I want a decision procedure my staff can actually run in a one-hour review, without a philosopher in the room: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enought answer to each, where the procedure stops and says kill, where it says continue, and where it has to hand the call to a person because the step is a judgment call rather than a fact. Ground it in what the philosophy and economics literature actually says about sunk costs — not just the slogan — and be honest about which steps are judgment dressed up as procedure. Then run it on three cases: (1) a feature that's 80% built and just missed its market window, (2) an internal tool that works but took twice as long as planned and now looks redundant with a vendor product, and (3) a rewrite of a legacy system where continuing is mostly about not wasting the migration work already done. Make sure at least one case comes out with an answer I won't like, and say what the procedure can't do. At the end, tell me how confident you are in the procedure overall and which single step you're least sure about.
