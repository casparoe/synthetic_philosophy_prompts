I'm taking a module on Wittgenstein this term and we've been going through the Philosophical Investigations, specifically the bit about language-games and family resemblance. You know, the famous passage where he says don't assume there's one thing all games share — some have competition, some have luck, some have skill, some have teams, and they're all connected by overlapping threads rather than a single essence. I keep thinking about this in relation to the whole esports debate — the one about whether competitive video gaming should be recognised as a sport. Every few years the IOC or some national sporting body agonises over whether League of Legends or whatever belongs in the Olympics alongside athletics and swimming. The arguments on both sides seem to track Wittgenstein's points almost perfectly: one side says esports lack the physical exertion that defines sport, the other says that's an essentialist mistake and sport is a family-resemblance concept where physicality is just one thread among many (and anyway, what about chess, or shooting, or dressage?).

I'm writing a paper on this and I want to use the esports controversy as a case study to test whether Wittgenstein's family resemblance idea actually helps resolve these kinds of boundary disputes, or whether it just redescribes them in a way that sounds clever but doesnt settle anything. Can you analyse the IOC's ongoing esports debate through the lens of Wittgenstein's family resemblance concept? What I'm after is: a careful mapping of the arguments for and against esports-as-sport onto the family resemblance framework, what the framework genuinely illuminates, and where it runs out of steam — specifically, does it give us any reason to include or exclude esports, or does it just tell us that the question "is X a sport?" is itself a mistake? And if the latter, what does that mean for institutions like the IOC that have to draw a line somewhere?
