I've been trying to sort out a problem in moral psychology that's been nagging at me for months, and I'd be grateful for help working through it systematically. The question is whether there's a principled way to distinguish a moral *preference* from a moral *bias*—or whether, when you press on the distinction, it collapses into something else. I don't mean the easy cases, where someone's moral conviction is clearly just rationalising self-interest or group prejudice. I mean the harder ones: convictions that are sincerely held, that concern genuinely moral subject matter, and that the person themselves would defend as moral truths, not mere tastes. In those cases, is there a fact of the matter about whether they're holding a preference or a bias, and if so, what determines it?

I've been recording my gut reactions to a series of cases, and my verdicts are less coherent than I'd like. Here they are.

(1) A person believes that eating dogs is morally wrong, even when the dogs are raised humanely and killed painlessly. They were raised in a culture where dogs are companion animals. They have never meaningfully engaged with arguments from cultures where dog meat is eaten, nor could they articulate a reason beyond 'dogs are not food animals.' My verdict: this is a bias.

(2) A person believes that factory farming of pigs is morally wrong because it causes severe and unnecessary suffering. They were raised in a culture that treats pigs as food. They arrived at this view after reading about farming conditions and reflecting on animal sentence. My verdict: a preference.

(3) A person believes that arranged marriage is morally wrong. They were raised in a culture where romantic love is treated as the sole legitimate basis for marriage. They have never spoken at length with anyone from a culture where arranged marriage is common, nor have they read any serious defence of the practice. Their objection, if pressed, amounts to: 'People should choose their own partners.' My verdict: a bias.

(4) A person believes that polyamory is morally wrong. They were raised in a monogamous culture, but they have close friends who are polyamorous, have read several defences of polyamory, and still find it objectionable. Their grounds are that exclusive romantic commitment provides a depth of trust and mutual understanding that non-exclusive arrangements cannot replicate. My verdict: I'm torn. It feels like a preference, but I worry I'm granting it that status only because the reasoning is more articulate than in the dog-meat case, and the underlying structure—a culturally inherited taboo, defended with post-hoc reasoning—looks similar.

(5) A person believes that hiring one's own child over a better-qualified stranger is morally wrong. They were raised in a culture that broadly condemns nepotism, but they have also worked in family businesses and seen the practice up close. They can articulate a rationale: hiring should track merit because roles affect third parties, and departing from merit is unfair to the better-qualified candidate. My verdict: a preference.

(6) A person believes that genetic enhancement of children—selecting embryos for intelligence, height, or musical ability—is morally wrong. They were raised in a culture that treats children as gifts rather than as projects to be optimised. Their conviction is pre-reflective: they have never read the bioethics literature, and they cannot offer much beyond 'it feels like playing God' or 'it's unnatural.' My verdict: a bias. But I cannot easily explain why this is different from (5), where the person also holds a culturally inherited view that they can articulate, and where I called it a preference.

(7) A person believes that sex work is morally wrong. They have read arguments on both sides, have friends who are or have been sex workers, and have engaged seriously with harm-reduction perspectives. Yet their conviction persists—not because of any particular argument they can state clearly, but because of a deep-seated sense that sexual intimacy ought to be non-transactional. My verdict: I keep going back and forth. Some days it feels like a preference; other days it feels like a bias dressed in the language of moral concern.

I've toyed with a few candidate principles. One is that a moral conviction is a bias when it is primarily sustained by cultural insulation—when the person could not offer reasons that would have any traction with someone who does not share their starting points. That handles (1) and (3) neatly, and it seems to let (2) and (5) count as preferences. But it struggles with (6): the genetic-enhancement sceptic is insulated, but so is the nepotism sceptic in (5) in a different way, and I've called one a bias and the other a preference. A second candidate is that a conviction is a bias when it would not survive exposure to the best arguments and evidence on the other side. That seems to make (4) a preference (since the person has engaged and held firm) and (3) a bias (since they haven't), which is where I was leaning. But it makes (6) a bias only if we assume the person would change their mind upon reading the bioethics literature, which is both speculative and, frankly, a bit elitist: it implies that ordinary people's unreflective moral convictions are biases by default, awaiting rescue by expertise. A third candidate is that a bias is a conviction whose causal origins are mostly cultural copying, whereas a preference is one whose causal origins include genuine responsiveness to reasons. But that's a historical claim about the person's psychology, not something an observer can usually assess, and it seems to make (7) permanently undecidable.

What I'd like from you is this: propose the best principle you can for distinguishing moral preferences from moral biases—one that captures as many of my verdicts as possible, states clearly which verdicts it overturns and why, and then applies the principle to the unresolved case (7). If you think the distinction is ultimately untenable and that we should talk in different terms altogether, you may say that, but you must still explain why the cases pattern the way they do and what alternative framework makes sense of them.
