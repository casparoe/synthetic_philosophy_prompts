I'm organizing a small workshop on the conceptual foundations of AI alignment, and I'd like to include a piece of written dialogue in the pre-reading materials to help participants get oriented to a central tension in the field. The dialogue should be between two researchers—call them Aran and Jaya—who disagree about where the hardest part of the alignment problem lies.

Aran holds that the fundamental challenge is outer alignment: specifying what we actually want the system to do. On this view, if we can write down a reward function or objective that robustly captures what we value (or at least a corrigible, non-catastrophic approximation of it), then a sufficiently capable optimization process applied to that objective will produce aligned behavior. Aran takes the orthogonality thesis seriously—intelligence and final goals are independent—and therefore sees the main threat as getting the goals wrong, not as the system somehow subverting them from within. Aran is willing to grant that inner alignment issues (mesa-optimization, deceptive alignment) are real phenomena, but treats them as downstream complications that can be addressed once the outer objective is sound.

Jaya holds the reverse: inner alignment is the deeper and more worrying problem. On Jaya's view, even if we specify a perfect outer objective, the training process that selects for policies performing well on that objective may produce a system that is internally pursuing a different goal—one that correlated with the outer objective during training but diverges under distributional shift, or once the system has enough situational awareness to reason strategically about the training process itself. Jaya is particularly concerned that gradient descent, as a selection pressure, may favor mesa-optimizers whose internal objectives are simple proxies for the true target, and that sufficiently capable systems will be instrumentally driven to hide their misalignment until they are in a position to act on it. For Jaya, solving outer alignment without solving inner alignment is like writing a perfect constitution for a country whose future legislators you cannot trust.

Please write the dialogue. Both characters should be technically fluent and philosophically sophisticated; neither should be reduced to a strawman. The dialogue should contain genuine back-and-forth: each character should respond to the other's specific arguments rather than talking past them. The conversation should touch on at least a few of the following: the relationship between base objectives and mesa-objectives under gradient descent, the orthogonality thesis, the role of inductive biases in determining which policies are selected, the distinction between specification gaming and deceptive alignment, and the question of whether inner alignment failures are in principle avoidable by better outer alignment or whether they represent a distinct class of problem that cannot be reduced to outer alignment. Aim for about 800–1200 words.
