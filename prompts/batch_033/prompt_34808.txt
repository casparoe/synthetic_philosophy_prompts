I serve on my university's Committee on Academic Requirements, and we are currently reviewing whether to retain the foreign language proficiency requirement for all undergraduates. At our last meeting, a colleague from the economics department made the following argument: over the past fifteen years, whenever the university has surveyed students about which requirements they find most burdensome, foreign language study consistently ranks first. Moreover, enrollment data from peer institutions show that when language courses are made optional, enrollment in them drops precipitously. He concluded that the requirement should be eliminated because, as he put it, the data reveal what students actually value, and the university ought to respect those revealed preferences.

Something about this argument strikes me as wrong, but I am having difficulty articulating exactly what the error is. I recognize that the argument moves from descriptive claims about student behavior and preferences to a normative conclusion about what the university should do, but I am not sure whether that move is always illegitimate, or only illegitimate in this particular case, or what principle distinguishes the legitimate from the illegitimate cases. Are there circumstances under which one can validly move from facts about what people do or prefer to conclusions about what an institution ought to do? If so, what principle would tell us whether my colleague's argument belongs to that class? And if the argument is indeed fallacious, what is the most precise way to characterize the mistake?

I am a philosopher by training but not a specialist in metaethics, so I would appreciate an analysis that is rigorous but does not presuppose fluency in the technical literature. Please write your answer in American English.
