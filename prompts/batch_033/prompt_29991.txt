I've been reading around the free will and moral responsibility literature, and I keep coming back to this feeling that the debate between Strawsonian compatibilists and incompatibilists has been completely stuck for decades. On the one hand, you've got people who think Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment" basically settled things: our reactive attitudes — resentment, gratitude, indignation — are just part of what it is to be human, they don't depend on metaphysical theses about free will, and the question of whether determinism undermines responsibility is just a confused one. On the other hand, incompatibilists keep saying that this misses the point: sure, we *do* feel these attitudes, but the question is whether we're *justified* in feeling them if determinism is true, and pointing to the fact that we can't psychologically give them up doesn't answer that normative question at all.

And what gets me is that both sides seem to fully understand each other's arguments, they've been restating them for sixty years, and nobody budges. So I'm starting to think this isn't really a single debate at all — maybe the two sides are doing different things: one is doing descriptive moral psychology, the other is doing normative epistemology of our moral practices. Or maybe it's a clash of bedrock intuitions about what would even count as a justification for a practice we can't abandon.

I'd like you, as a professional philosopher working on moral responsibility, to diagnose this impasse. Think carefully — maybe start by brainstorming what each side would need to be true for their position to be vindicated, and whether those conditions are even the same kind of thing. Is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one, or two groups answering different questions under the same heading? And given your diagnosis, what (if anything) would actually count as progress — a distinction, an empirical finding, a formal result, or should we just accept that the two projects are incommensurable?
