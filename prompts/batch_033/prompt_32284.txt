You are a philosopher who works on formal epistemology and the problem of logical omniscience, and who can translate technical distinctions into procedures usable by non-philosophers. I am a PhD student in philosophy, and I have been asked to help a regulatory consultancy that reviews safety incidents after the fact. For each incident, the consultancy must decide whether a staff member "should have known" some consequence given the documents, reports, and internal messages they demonstrably had access to. The current protocol veers between two indefensible positions: it sometimes attributes every logical consequence of everything the person ever saw, as if staff were logically omniscient, and sometimes attributes nothing beyond what was explicitly printed, as if no inference is ever compulsory.

Please write a decision procedure, grounded in the critique of logical omniscience, that a non-philosopher auditor can run in under an hour per case and whose output a review board can act on, with the board signing off on any attribution. Before the final procedure, briefly work through the candidate criteria you considered and which you kept or rejected, so the simplifications are justified. The procedure itself should give: the questions to ask in order; what counts as a sufficient answer at each step; where the procedure stops with "not attributable"; and where it hands off to a human because the step is a judgment call rather than a fact. Be explicit about which distinctions from the logical omniscience literature must survive the simplification (for example, closure under entailment versus closure under a bounded set of cheap, familiar rules) and which can be dropped for this audience. Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) an easy attribution, where the conclusion is one step away from two on-task documents; (2) a hard non-attribution, where the conclusion genuinely follows but requires many steps or specialist rules; and (3) a case where the procedure returns an answer the asker will not like — make this a case in which the procedure attributes knowledge even though the employee sincerely and credibly denies having drawn the inference, the sort of outcome I, as someone who takes first-person reports seriously, will find uncomfortable. Finish by stating plainly what the procedure cannot do and where it is most likely to misfire.
