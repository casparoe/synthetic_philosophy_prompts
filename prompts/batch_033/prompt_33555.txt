I am an editor at a philosophy-and-literature journal, and I have been mediating an adversarial collaboration between two translators and theorists of translation whom we shall call F and M. F holds that the preservation of formal constraints—metre, rhyme, stanza shape, sound-patterning—is internal to what a verse translation is trying to do, and that a translation which abandons these constraints has changed the object it is translating in a way that mere semantic paraphrase does not. M holds that formal features are, at most, instrumental to producing an equivalent effect in the target language, and that fidelity to semantic content and register is the non-negotiable core of translation; sacrificing sense for a rhyme is a betrayal of the text.

They have been arguing past each other for two years, and I have asked them to produce a joint statement of an adversarial collaboration that does the following:

1. A numbered list of claims both F and M can sign, stated in language neither would disown. These should include points about what translation is answerable to, what counts as a failure, and what constraints a translator cannot simply ignore.
2. A table of disagreements. For each row, the left column states the disagreement in neutral terms; the middle column gives F's position in wording F would accept; the right column gives M's position in wording M would accept.
3. A section identifying the cruxes: the sub-questions such that, if resolved, at least one party would change their view on some disagreement. For each crux, state (a) why it matters, (b) a concrete translation case or test both would accept as bearing on it, and (c) what F and M each predict the case would show, recorded in advance.
4. A brief concluding note on whether any part of the dispute is merely verbal, and if so, which part and why.

The document should be precise enough that a third party who has never read the correspondence could use it to understand exactly where F and M stand. It should avoid caricature. It should not reduce either position to a slogan. It should be written in British English, and it should engage with the specific difficulties raised by formal constraint—for instance, the difference between a constraint that is arbitrary in the target language versus one that has a natural analogue, and the question of whether a translation that preserves form but distorts sense can nonetheless be faithful to something the original is doing that a sense-for-sense translation misses.
