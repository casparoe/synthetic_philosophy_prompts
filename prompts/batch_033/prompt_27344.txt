I'm a retired physicist — spent my career in condensed matter, not foundations — but I've been reading philosophy of physics lately and I keep coming back to the quantum suicide / quantum immortality thing. Here's my take, and I'd like to know what would count against it.

I think the quantum suicide thought experiment rests on a mistake about what "you" refers to across branches. In MWI, when the trigger fires, the wavefunction branches. In most branches you're dead, in a few you're alive. The claim is that "you" should expect to find yourself in an alive branch, because you can't have experiences in the dead ones. But that seems to treat "you" as a thing that was going to go into one branch or another, and then — surprise — always ends up in the alive one. But there is no such thing. There are just multiple future persons, most dead, some alive, all equally "you" in the branching sense. The dead ones did have experiences right up until they didn't. So why privilege the alive branch as the one "you" will experience? It feels like the arguement is secretly relying on a single-thread view of personal identity that MWI itself undermines.

Anyway, I'm new to this and might be missing something basic. What I'd like is: what are the best arguments that my objection misses the point? And can you separate what's actually agreed on by people who work on this from what's still being fought over? I don't want a contested reading presented as settled.
