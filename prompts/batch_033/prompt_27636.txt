I am a novelist at work on a fantasy novel whose legal system runs on spoken formulas: a contract binds only if the parties pronounce the exact clause before a licensed registrar, a marriage is valid only when the officiant recites the prescribed words, and a royal decree takes effect only when delivered in a particular ceremonial voice. A friend in my writing group, who has read some philosophy of language, insists I am not inventing anything exotic, that this is just Austin's account of performative utterances made vivid. On her telling, an ordinary promise, a christening, or a judge's verdict already works like an incantation: the right words, spoken by the right person under the right conditions, bring a new state of affairs into being, and the world is altered afterward not because the words described anything but because they were said. She uses this to argue that my novel's magic needs no further justification; it is only a dramatization of how real language already operates.

I am uneasy with the analogy, because in my novel the formulas work through a supernatural causal channel, whereas the speech acts I have been reading about seem to depend on convention, standing institutions, and the uptake of other people in ways I only half understand. Before I commit my worldbuilding to the comparison, I want an audit of how far the spell analogy actually carries for performative utterances.
