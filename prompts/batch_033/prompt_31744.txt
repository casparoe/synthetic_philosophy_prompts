You are a philosopher of mind specializing in perception, with a sideline in the epistemology of disagreement. I want you to think carefully about the structure of a debate that has puzzled me for years, and I'm asking for a diagnosis of the disagreement itself, not a verdict on who is right.

The debate is about cognitive penetration of perception—roughly, whether what we believe, expect, desire, or conceptually possess can literally alter the contents of perceptual experience. Not just where we direct our attention, not just how we interpret what we see after the fact, but whether cognition reaches down into the perceptual process itself and changes the phenomenology.

The two sides, as I understand them:

On one side, you have theorists who argue that perception is cognitively penetrable. They point to a range of empirical findings: studies where desires make objects look closer or larger, where racial stereotypes affect perceived skin tone or facial threat, where linguistic categories shift color discrimination, where wearing a heavy backpack makes hills look steeper, and so on. For them, these are demonstrations that perception is richly sensitive to what we think and want, and that the modular, encapsulated picture of perception is empirically false.

On the other side, you have theorists who argue that perception is cognitively impenetrable—that it is a modular, encapsulated system whose outputs are determined by stimulus input plus fixed, innate computational principles. They argue that every putative case of cognitive penetration can be explained away without conceding that cognition alters perception proper. The effect is really on attention (you look at different parts of the scene), or on judgment (you see the same thing but report it differently), or on memory (you encode it accurately but recall it with bias), or it is a pre-perceptual effect (expectation changes early sensory processing but not perception itself), or it is a post-perceptual effect (perception is intact but downstream interpretation is contaminated). For them, genuine cognitive penetration would be a deep architectural claim, and the evidence for it, they argue, does not survive careful scrutiny.

This debate has been running for decades. The same examples get traded back and forth. The same countermoves get deployed. People publish papers, but I rarely see anyone change their mind. My working hypothesis is that the two sides are not really disagreeing about a single empirical question. Instead, they might be operating with different definitions of "perception"—one side draws the perception/cognition boundary in one place, the other in another, and the empirical findings fall on different sides of that line depending on where you draw it. Or perhaps they disagree about what counts as evidence: one side is satisfied with behavioral effects, the other demands neurophysiological mechanisms. Or maybe they are answering different questions under one heading: one side is asking about the functional architecture of the visual system, the other about the phenomenology of experience. Or perhaps there is a hidden empirical question that nobody has managed to isolate cleanly.

I want you to diagnose this impasse. Is it a verbal dispute about the definition of "perception"? Is it a disagreement about standards of evidence? Is it a clash of background assumptions about how to carve up the mind? Are there hidden empirical questions that, if settled, would resolve it? Or is it something else entirely—for instance, a debate where the two sides have different starting intuitions about what a perceptual system ought to be like, and neither side can argue for those intuitions?

And given your diagnosis: what would actually count as progress? Is there an experiment, a conceptual distinction, a formal model, or a reframing of the question that could break the stalemate? Or should we expect the impasse to persist indefinitely, and if so, is that a problem for the field or just a sign that the question, as currently posed, is ill-posed?

I'm asking you to think carefully—start by sketching out the logical space of the positions and the kinds of moves each side makes, then work through the possible diagnoses one by one before settling on the one you find most compelling. And please: if you think my framing of the debate is confused, or that my hypothesis about it being definitional is wrongheaded, or that I have mischaracterized one of the sides, say so plainly. I would rather learn that I am asking the wrong question than get a polite answer to a broken one.
