In a seminar on the philosophy of logic, a colleague argued that Lewis Carroll's parable of Achilles and the Tortoise shows that deductive inference cannot be justified by adding further premises. On their reading, the Tortoise stands for someone who grants every proposition you put forward but still withholds the conclusion, and turning the inference rule itself into an additional premise only triggers an infinite regress. They concluded that deduction must ultimately rest on something non-propositional — a disposition, a practice, or a kind of know-how that is not itself a belief. I am skeptical that the parable can carry that much weight. Please audit the analogy: lay out what maps onto what in the story (who or what corresponds to the reasoner, the inference rule, the regress, and the conclusion's target), identify where the mapping breaks down or quietly imports assumptions about the nature of rules, and assess whether the conclusion — that deduction rests on non-propositional foundations — survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If a better analogy would capture whatever truth the parable is gesturing at, provide it.
