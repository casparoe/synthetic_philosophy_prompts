Here is the passage (I've condensed it slightly but preserved the argument):

> The two-envelope paradox is a puzzle about rational decision-making under uncertainty. You are shown two sealed envelopes and told that one contains exactly twice as much money as the other, though you are not told which is which. You select one envelope at random. Before opening it, you reason as follows: let the amount in my envelope be \(X\). The other envelope must then contain either \(X/2\) or \(2X\), and since I chose randomly, these two possibilities are equally likely. The expected value of switching is therefore \(\frac{1}{2}(X/2) + \frac{1}{2}(2X) = 1.25X\), which is greater than \(X\). So I should switch. But of course the same argument would tell me to switch back if I had chosen the other envelope instead — a contradiction. The standard resolution, endorsed by decision theorists and Bayesian statisticians, is that the reasoning contains a hidden assumption: that the two possibilities are equally probable no matter what \(X\) turns out to be. This amounts to assuming a uniform probability distribution over the possible amounts of money, and since no such distribution exists over an unbounded domain, the assumption is mathematically illegitimate. Once a proper prior distribution over the envelope amounts is specified, the paradox disappears: the expected gain from switching is zero, exactly as symmetry demands. The broader moral is that paradoxes of rational choice frequently arise from careless appeals to the principle of indifference over infinite or unbounded outcome spaces.
