I'm in a philosophy seminar at my high school, and we've been studying the ethics of political obligation and civil disobedience. I've come up with an argument that seems to show that civil disobedience is never justified in a reasonably just democracy. I'm almost certain the conclusion is wrong — it would condemn the lunch counter sit-ins of the Civil Rights Movement, the Salt March, the suffragette protests, and practically every canonical example of justified civil disobedience — but I can't pinpoint the error. Here's the argument, laid out step by step:

(1) In a reasonably just democratic state, citizens have a prima facie moral obligation to obey the law.

(2) Civil disobedience is the deliberate and public violation of a law, undertaken for the purpose of protesting an injustice or bringing about political change.

(3) Therefore, acts of civil disobedience in a reasonably just democracy are prima facie morally wrong.

(4) To override a prima facie duty, one needs countervailing moral considerations of sufficient weight.

(5) In a democracy, all citizens can pursue political change through legal channels: voting, running for office, free speech, free assembly, petitioning the government, and bringing lawsuits.

(6) When legal channels for political change are available, the mere fact that a particular law is unjust does not by itself supply the countervailing weight needed to override the duty to obey — because the proper response to an unjust law in a democracy is to change it through those channels, not to break it.

(7) There are no other considerations that could supply the needed weight that are not ultimately reducible to the fact that the law is unjust.

(8) Therefore, the prima facie wrong of civil disobedience in a reasonably just democracy cannot be overridden, and civil disobedience is never all-things-considered justified.

I want to keep premises (1), (2), and (4) — they seem independently well-motivated. And I find (7) tempting. So I'm looking hardest at (5) and (6), but I'm not sure exactly what's wrong with them, or whether rejecting one of them forces me into a position I don't want to hold. Can you identify the step or steps where the argument fails, explain why the problematic step initially looked plausible, and lay out what I would be committed to if I rejected it? If the best diagnosis actually requires giving up one of the premises I said I wanted to keep, tell me that too.
