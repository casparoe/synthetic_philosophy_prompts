I have been asked to lead a two-hour seminar at a literary festival, for an audience of novelists and poets with no formal background in Chinese philosophy. The advertised subject is the School of Names, and I want to build the session around the White Horse dialogue and its claim that a white horse is not a horse. I do not want to give them a lecture; I want to take them through the argument so that the strange conclusion stops looking like a trick or a translation glitch and starts looking like a genuine problem about how words attach to things.

Plan how I should teach it: which distinction has to be in place first (I suspect it is something about how a term picks out a kind rather than a particular, but I am ready to be corrected), what single carrying example from ordinary writing or description would do the work and what features it must have and lack, and the misconceptions this audience in particular will bring. Novelists are used to treating words as texture, connotation and image, so I expect the word white will be heard as an adjective modifying a noun rather than as a term doing different work; tell me where in the sequence to head that off, along with any other misconceptions you would expect. I would also like one question I can put to them at the end that would show whether they have grasped the underlying problem rather than merely accepted the moves. Finally, tell me what to leave out: I do not want to turn a room of writers into historians of the Warring States period in a single afternoon.
