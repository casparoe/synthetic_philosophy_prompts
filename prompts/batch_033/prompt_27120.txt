I'm organizing a small workshop on anthropic reasoning and decision theory, and I want one of the sessions to model what a serious adversarial collaboration between proponents of the self-sampling assumption (SSA) and the self-indication assumption (SIA) would actually look like. I've recruited two participants — one who has defended SSA in print and one who has defended SIA — and I've asked them to produce a joint document they can both sign. I'd like you to draft that document for them, as a starting point for their discussion. The structure should be as follows. First, a numbered list of claims that both sides agree on: about the formal setup, about what the problem is, about which thought experiments are centrally relevant, about what would constitute a resolution, and about what both sides are committed to rejecting (for instance, both reject the idea that anthropic reasoning is meaningless or that one can simply ignore indexical uncertainty). Second, a table of disagreements: each row should state the point of disagreement in neutral terms, then give the SSA-side formulation of their own view and the SIA-side formulation of theirs, each phrased in a way that its own proponents would accept as accurate and fair. Third, a list of cruxes — the sub-questions on which the dispute turns, such that if they were settled, at least one side would change its view. For each crux, I want: what both sides agree would count as evidence bearing on it, and what each side predicts about that evidence. The presumptuous philosopher thought experiment should feature prominently, since it is a case where the two views diverge so starkly that it forces the issue. But I would also like the document to address the doomsday argument, the sleeping beauty problem, the self-sampling assumption's handling of reference classes, and the question of whether anthropic probabilities should be thought of as epistemic or as something more like objective chances. Finally, I want the document to include a short section at the end where each side states, in their own words, what they take to be the strongest argument for the other side — the thing that gives them the most pause about their own view. The goal is a document that is genuinely constructive, that neither side would feel misrepresents them, and that sharpens the disagreement rather than papering it over.
