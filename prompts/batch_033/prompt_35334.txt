I recently retired from a career in the physical sciences and have been spending my leisure reading philosophy, particularly the literature on free will and moral responsibility. I have been working through the debates around Harry Frankfurt's attack on the Principle of Alternative Possibilities—the thesis that an agent is morally responsible for what she does only if she could have done otherwise. I have drafted a short essay sketching a worry about Frankfurt-style cases that I have not seen articulated in quite this way in what I have read so far, though I suspect it may overlap with existing arguments. I would be grateful for comments on whether the argument holds up, whether it has already been addressed in the literature, and what the best replies to it would be.

---

**A Structural Worry About Frankfurt-Style Cases**

Frankfurt's strategy is elegant. To show that the Principle of Alternative Possibilities is false, he constructs cases in which an agent cannot do otherwise but is intuitively morally responsible. The template runs as follows. An intervener—call him Black—wants an agent, Jones, to perform some action X. Black has implanted a device that allows him to monitor Jones's neural state and, if necessary, to force Jones to do X. Black will intervene if and only if Jones exhibits some prior sign indicating that Jones is about to decide not to do X. If Jones decides on his own to do X, Black never intervenes. In that scenario, Jones could not have done otherwise—Black would have forced him—yet Jones seems responsible for doing X, since he did it on his own.

Much of the subsequent discussion has focused on whether such cases genuinely eliminate all alternative possibilities. The flicker-of-freedom response grants that Jones cannot avoid doing X, but insists that Jones could have exhibited the prior sign or not, and that this flicker is a robust enough alternative to ground responsibility. Frankfurtians have replied by designing ever more elaborate cases in which the prior sign is not under the agent's voluntary control, or in which the intervener acts on a sign that precedes any flicker.

I want to press on a different point. The prior sign is doing a great deal of work in these cases, and I am not sure it can do the work asked of it. Consider the three demands placed on the prior sign:

First, reliability. The sign must reliably indicate what Jones will decide. If the sign is merely probabilistic, Black will sometimes intervene unnecessarily—that is, he will force Jones to do X even though Jones would have done X on his own. In those cases, Jones is not responsible for doing X (he was forced), and the case no longer serves its purpose. Worse, if Jones knows the sign is unreliable in this way, there may be a possible world in which Jones decides to do X, the sign does not occur, and Black does not intervene, yet Jones does X anyway—in which case Jones could have done otherwise after all. So the sign must be nearly perfectly reliable.

Second, temporal priority. The sign must occur before Jones's decision is made. If the sign simply is the decision, or is simultaneous with it, then we have a flicker: Jones could have not-made-the-decision, which is an alternative possibility of precisely the sort the Frankfurtian needs to eliminate. The sign must be a genuine precursor.

Third, causal non-sufficiency. The sign must not be causally sufficient for Jones's decision. If it is, then the case presupposes determinism at the crucial junction, and the incompatibilist will simply deny that Jones is responsible in such a world. The whole point of Frankfurt cases is to show that the Principle of Alternative Possibilities fails even if determinism is false; the case cannot rely on a deterministic connection between sign and decision without begging the question against the incompatibilist.

The difficulty is that these three demands are in deep tension. A prior event that is nearly perfectly reliable as a predictor of a later event, that is not causally sufficient for that later event, and that precedes it in time—this is a peculiar kind of thing. In the physical sciences we are familiar with two broad kinds of predictive relationships. There are deterministic causal connections, where the earlier state plus the laws of nature necessitates the later state. And there are irreducibly probabilistic connections, where the earlier state merely raises the probability of the later state. The Frankfurt case seems to require something in between: a sign that makes the decision overwhelmingly probable without necessitating it, yet is reliable enough that the intervener never misfires in the relevant counterfactual scenarios.

One might try to satisfy these demands by making the sign a necessary condition for the decision without being sufficient for it: the sign is a neural event that must occur for the decision to occur, the decision occurs if and only if the sign occurs, but the sign does not necessitate the decision—there is an indeterministic gap between sign and decision. But then what fills the gap? If nothing does, the connection between sign and decision is a brute correlation, and it is hard to see how it could be reliable enough for the intervener's purposes. If something does fill the gap—say, the agent's reasons, character, and deliberation—then we seem to have a causal chain from sign to decision, and the incompatibilist will say that this chain is either deterministic (in which case the case begs the question) or indeterministic (in which case the sign is not reliable enough).

I suspect, though I am not confident, that this structural problem is fatal to the Frankfurtian project. The intervener's epistemic situation and the causal structure of the agent's decision-making cannot be made to cohere in the way the cases require. If that is right, then Frankfurt cases do not establish what they claim to establish, and the debate over the Principle of Alternative Possibilities must be settled on other grounds.

I would welcome any comments on this argument. In particular:

- Is the trilemma I have identified—that the prior sign cannot simultaneously be reliable, temporally prior, and causally non-sufficient—a genuine problem, or have I overlooked a way of satisfying all three conditions?
- Are there responses in the literature that address this specific worry? I am aware of the flicker-of-freedom debate and have encountered the so-called dilemma defense associated with Widerker and Kane, but my concern is narrower: it is about the metaphysics of the prior sign itself, and whether the three demands on it can be jointly satisfied in any possible world, not merely in the particular cases Frankfurt and his defenders have described.
- Is my framing of the issue in terms drawn from the physical sciences helpful, or does it import assumptions about causation and prediction that are inappropriate in the context of agency and free will? I worry that I may be treating a decision as though it were a physical event like any other, when the whole point is that it is not.

I should add that I am new to philosophy and may well be missing obvious replies, so I would appreciate being set straight if the argument is fundamentally misguided.
