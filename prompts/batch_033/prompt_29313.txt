I've been thinking about a friendship that ended badly: I found that I had strong reasons to go on admiring the person, but none of them seemed to be about the person at all. I'm writing a paper on the wrong kind of reasons problem and I want a case for it. Construct a thought experiment in which a subject has a decisive reason to admire someone, so that the buck-passing account of value — the view that something is valuable just in case there are reasons to value it — would classify that person as admirable, even though the reason is intuitively of the wrong kind. Constraints: the case must contain no threat, bribe, or social pressure; the wrong-kind reason should be state-given in a subtler way, generated by the subject's own projects or commitments rather than by anyone's interference. Keep it to ordinary life (a workplace, a family, or a friendship) and to one paragraph, and do not stipulate that the target is unadmirable — that verdict has to fall out of the structure of the case. After the scenario, walk through what the buck-passing account says and why, identify which feature of the case does the work, say what would happen if that feature were removed, and explain how a defender of the account could try to exclude the case without circularity — or why they cannot.
