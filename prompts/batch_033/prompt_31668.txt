ok so in my alignment reading group theres this guy who keeps bringing up bongard problems as the model for what concept learning is like. you know the setup: six panels on the left that all follow some rule, six on the right that break it, and you have to figure out the rule. his argument is that this is basically what any learning system faces when it tries to pick up a concept from examples — the space of rules consistent with the examples is enormous, and the only reason we humans can solve bongard problems is that we bring a huge stack of implicit assumptions about what kinds of rules are plausible. his conclusion is that if we want an AI to learn human values from examples of good and bad behavior, we cant just feed it data and hope — the inductive biases are doing all the heavy lifting and we need to know what they are and whether theyre the right ones.

i buy the general point about inductive biases but the bongard analogy feels like its doing a lot of work it hasnt earned. in a real bongard problem the examples are hand-picked to isolate a single rule, the rule is crisp and expressible in a short sentence, the feature space is pre-segmented into shapes and relations, and theres a clean yes/no boundary. real concept learning — especially for something like human values — seems messier on every axis. so: audit this analogy. give me the mapping (what corresponds to what), the disanalogies, and which disanalogies actually matter for his conclusion about value learning vs which are just aesthetic. then tell me whether the conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the argument the whole time.
