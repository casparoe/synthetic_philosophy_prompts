I'm a PhD student in a cognitive science program, and my advisor and I have been going in circles about whether the brain literally is a computer or whether "computation" is just a metaphor we've gotten way too comfortable with. I've read the classic papers (Fodor, Pylyshyn, some of the connectionist back-and-forth, a bit of the dynamical systems stuff) but the more I read the more I feel like both sides are talking past each other on what "computation" even means. 

Could you write a dialogue between two people who actually know what they're talking about — one who thinks the brain is essentially a computing device (a computationalist in the sense that mental processes just *are* computational processes implemented in neural hardware) and one who thinks this whole framing is confused and wrongheaded? Set it somewhere natural — maybe they're grabbing drinks after a conference talk that touched on this, or arguing in front of a whiteboard. I want them to really push each other. The computationalist should argue something like: look, the only way we've ever made sense of systematicity and productivity in thought is in computational terms, and the fact that biological implementation is messy doesn't mean there isn't a computational description at the right level of abstraction. The critic should press on whether the hardware/software distinction even applies to brains, whether "computation" has become so promiscuous a term that it covers literally any physical process (so the claim becomes vacuous), and whether representation — genuine aboutness — can be cashed out purely computationally or whether it smuggles in an interpreter. I want them to actually respond to each other's points, not just take turns giving speeches. Neither should be a straw man — I want to come away feeling like I understand why a smart person would hold either view.
