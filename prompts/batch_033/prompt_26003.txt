I'm working on a collection of philosophical short fiction — the kind of thing you might find in a venue like *Sci Phi Journal* — and I'm looking for a story that dramatizes the idea of origin essentialism. For context, origin essentialism is the Kripkean thesis that an individual's origins are essential to that individual: you couldn't have come from a different sperm and egg, because if a different gamete pair had fused, the resulting person would simply be someone else, not you. It's a claim about the metaphysics of identity, but I'm interested in what it does to a person psychologically and existentially when they really sit with it.

Here's the setup I have in mind. The protagonist is a woman in her late thirties who has just learned, through a direct-to-consumer genetic test taken on a whim, something unsettling: her parents underwent IVF, and the embryo that became her was one of several viable ones — the embryologist chose it essentially at random from a petri dish containing her would-be siblings. She had always known she was an IVF baby, but she had never thought through what that meant for the contingency of her existence. The story follows her in the weeks after this realization, as she navigates her relationship with her parents, her own young daughter, and the strange, vertiginous sense that she is simultaneously irreplaceable and radically accidental. She finds herself preoccupied with the question: if a different embryo had been selected, would *she* have existed at all, or would some other person be living her life — sleeping with her husband, raising her daughter, sitting at her desk? And if the latter, what does it mean to be grateful to exist, or to fear death, or to feel that your life is *yours*?

The story should treat the philosophy seriously — the protagonist can reason her way through the implications, not just emote about them — but it shouldn't read like a philosophy paper. I want the idea to emerge through scene, dialogue, and the texture of a particular life. The tone should be quiet and unnerving rather than melodramatic. A few things I'd especially like the story to touch: the difference between epistemic and metaphysical possibility (she didn't *know* which embryo she was, but that doesn't mean she *could have been* a different one); the way origin essentialism interacts with the non-identity problem (can she regret the choice the embryologist made, if that choice is what brought her into existence?); and the strange asymmetry between our attitude toward our past origins and our future nonexistence. But these should be handled lightly — the story comes first.
