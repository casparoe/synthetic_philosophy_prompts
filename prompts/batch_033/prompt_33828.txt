I spent forty years in a physics lab and only started reading philosophy after I retired, so bear with me if my jargon is off. I keep seeing decision theorists say the rational thing is to maximise expected utility, but the more I read about the Pasadena game the less I understand what "expected utility" is even supposed to name there. The way I understand it: it's a coin-flipping game where the payoffs alternate sign and the probabilities shrink just so, with the result that the expectation is a conditionally convergent series — the positive and negative parts each sum to infinity, so the total you get depends entirely on the order you add the terms in, and some orders give no total at all. It only occured to me recently that most of the literature still hasn't faced this head on. Before I read any more proposed fixes, I want the specification. What does a theory of rational choice under risk actually have to get right if it's going to handle games whose expected value is undefined or order-dependent like this? Give me the desiderata list: which cases it must deliver the right verdict on (dominance, finite truncations, mixtures, whatever else you think earns its place), which distinctions it can't collapse, which mathematical results it can't just ignore — with the consideration behind each one. Then tell me which single requirement does the most to knock out the popular fixes, like "just choose a summation order" or "refuse any gamble without a finite, order-independent expectation". And flag any pair of desiderata you suspect can't be jointly met. I care more about the criteria than about scoring named theories against them. Start with your bottom line in the first sentence, no preamble, and don't restate the question back at me. Also, as someone used to measurement error: say how confident you are in each main claim, and single out the point you're least sure of.
