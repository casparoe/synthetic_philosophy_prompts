I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in metaethics, and I'm writing the final exam. We've spent three weeks on expressivism and the Frege–Geach problem, reading Ayer, Stevenson, Hare, Geach, Searle, and Blackburn. I want a single essay question that separates students who can recite the problem from those who understand why it's deep and what constraints a solution must satisfy.

The question should push students past the standard presentation — expressivism says moral utterances aren't truth-apt, so how do they embed in conditionals and participate in modus ponens? — and into harder terrain. For instance: Is the problem fundamentally about compositionality, about logical validity, about attitude individuation, or about something else? Would a solution that assigns systematic semantic values to moral sentences amount to abandoning expressivism? What, if anything, does the Frege–Geach problem show about the relationship between the content of an attitude and the content of the sentence that expresses it?

Please take your time and think through the issues carefully before writing. Then produce:

(1) The essay question, suitable for a 50-minute in-class essay. It should be specific enough that a student who has only memorized the textbook summary cannot earn a high mark, but clear enough that a strong student knows exactly what they're being asked to do.

(2) A model answer of roughly 600–800 words. This should demonstrate the kind of argumentative structure, precision, and engagement with the underlying issues that distinguishes excellent work. It should not read like a summary of the readings.

(3) A marking scheme on a 20-point scale. The scheme should specify what an A-range answer (17–20), a B-range answer (13–16), and a C-range answer (9–12) must contain, naming the specific moves, distinctions, and arguments that separate the bands. It should also flag two or three common student mistakes — for example, confusing the Frege–Geach problem with the problem of moral disagreement, or thinking that giving a compositional semantics for "boo" and "hooray" operators automatically solves the problem — and explain how each mistake affects the grade.
