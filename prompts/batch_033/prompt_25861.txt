I am a philosopher of action currently collaborating with a colleague in machine learning on a project examining whether reinforcement learning agents can be described as acting for reasons. My colleague is an accomplished RL researcher with no background in philosophy, and our conversations keep stalling at a particular juncture: he treats "the reason an agent acts" as equivalent to "what the agent's policy optimizes for," while I want to distinguish between the considerations that actually motivate an agent and the considerations that ought to motivate her — a distinction that, in philosophical terms, maps roughly onto the debate between internalism and externalism about practical reasons.

I would like you to restate the core of that debate in the vocabulary my colleague uses daily. Take Bernard Williams's case for internal reasons — the claim that an agent has a reason to φ only if there is a sound deliberative route from her current subjective motivational set to a motivation to φ — and the principal externalist responses (whether Korsgaard's appeal to what practical identity demands, or the more generic thought that moral reasons bind agents irrespective of their contingent desires). Translate these systematically into the language of reward functions, value functions, policies, state spaces, terminal versus shaped rewards, inverse reinforcement learning, and the distinction between the agent's current policy and the optimal policy given some reward specification. Be precise about where the mapping holds and where it strains.

Then provide the residue report I actually need. If internalism amounts to the claim that all reasons must be expressible as features of the agent's own reward function, and externalism to the claim that there exist reward signals the agent has reason to track even if they are absent from its present reward function, then the debate seems to reduce to a disagreement about reward specification — something my colleague already has well-developed views about. But I suspect the translation fails at a deeper level. Perhaps the notion of a "subjective motivational set" does not correspond cleanly to a reward function because motivations have intentional content and admit of rational criticism in a way that reward signals do not. Perhaps the externalist's appeal to normativity — to what an agent ought to want — has no natural home in a framework built to describe what an agent in fact optimizes. I want you to identify precisely where the RL vocabulary runs out, and to tell me whether that residue reflects a genuine limitation of the RL framework for thinking about practical reasons, or whether it reflects confusion on the philosophers' side that the translation exposes. My hope is to hand my colleague something that makes the philosophical terrain legible to him without pretending the philosophy reduces to engineering.
