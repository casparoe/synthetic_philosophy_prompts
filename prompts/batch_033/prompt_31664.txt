I'm a PhD student in decision theory, and I've been arguing with a friend in the department about the two-envelope paradox for like a week now. The setup is: two envelopes, one contains exactly twice as much money as the other, but you don't know which is which. You pick one, open it, and see $10. The other envelope therefore has either $5 or $20, so if you switch your expected value is (0.5)($5) + (0.5)($20) = $12.50, which is better than the $10 in your hand. So switching looks rational. But the same arithmetic works no matter what amount you see, which would mean you should always switch, even before opening—and then, once you've switched, the same reasoning says switch back. I know the standard response is that the expected value isn't well-defined unless you have a prior over the possible amounts. But here's the part I can't get past: once you've actually seen $10, isnt it just a fact about the situation that the other envelope is equally likely to be $5 or $20? There are only two possibilities, and by symmetry they should get equal probability. So even if the prior is undefined before opening, after conditioning on the observed amount the switch seems forced. My friend says this is exactly where the mistake is, but I can't see why seeing the $10 doesn't pin down the conditional probabilities. Where does the reasoning go wrong?
