I work at a state environmental health agency, and my team has to issue guidance about whether a chemical or exposure should be restricted. The problem we keep hitting: the evidence is a mess. We've got maybe three or four studies — different designs, different populations, some show harm and some don't, and the ones that show harm can't agree on how big the effect is. We can't get a clean probability out of this, and when we try to force one we're basically making up numbers and then doing fancy math on them. Nobody on my team is a philosopher or a statistician — we're toxicologists and epidemiologists who need to make a call.

I've been reading about imprecise probabilities — the idea that when your evidence is weak or conflicting, you should represent your uncertainty with a set of probabilities rather than a single number, and that this isn't a failure, it's being honest about what you know. But everything I've read is theory. I need something I can actually use.

Please write me a decision procedure my team can follow when the evidence won't support a single probability distribution. It should be a step-by-step thing — questions to ask, in what order, what counts as a sufficient answer. It needs to handle the case where we have multiple studies that point in different directions and we can't just pool them. It should say when we can still make a recommendation and when the right answer is genuinely "we don't know enough to say" (which is politically uncomfortable but sometimes true). And it should flag the steps where we're making a judgment call rather than applying a rule.

Then run the procedure on three cases:

1. A new pesticide where we have one large epidemiological study showing a small increased cancer risk, two smaller studies showing no effect, and a toxicology study in rats showing liver damage at high doses. The manufacturer argues the epidemiological finding is chance.

2. A food additive that's been used for decades: we have a huge observational study showing a modest health risk, but no mechanism has been identified, and the effect disappears when you control for overall diet quality. Consumer groups are pushing for a ban.

3. A case where the procedure tells us we can't make a call — pick something realistic where the evidence is genuinely too thin, and show us what that looks like and why we shouldn't pretend otherwise.

For the response structure, I'd like these sections in order:

- The Procedure (about 800 words): the step-by-step, written so someone without stats training can follow it. Number the steps.
- Why This Works (about 400 words): the conceptual justification, connecting each step to the imprecise probability ideas behind it, but keep it accessible.
- Worked Cases (about 1200 words total across the three cases, roughly evenly split): run the procedure on each.
- What This Can't Do (about 300 words): honest about the procedure's limits and the cases where even the procedure can't save us from having to make a judgment call.
