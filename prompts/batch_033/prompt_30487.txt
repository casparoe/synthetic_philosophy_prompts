Take the role of an expert in moral and political philosophy, and think through the following case with care. I would ask you to begin by brainstorming the various ways one might structure the situation — what kind of moral problem it is, what it is not, and which features of the case are doing the real work — before you settle on a diagnosis and a recommendation.

I am a philosophy professor, though contemporary political ethics is not my main area, and a former doctoral student of mine who now serves in a state legislature came to me with a problem I want to work through carefully before I respond. Her district lies in a floodplain, and a budget bill now before the legislature contains the only realistic funding for a long-stalled flood-protection project that would shield several thousand vulnerable households. The same bill carries a rider that would effectively dismantle the independent body investigating campaign-finance violations by members of her own party's leadership — a provision she regards as plainly corrupt. The bill will pass regardless of how she votes, so her vote is not decisive. If she votes against it, she can truthfully say she opposed the corrupt rider, but the bill will still pass with the rider intact, and she will have no record of supporting the flood protections her constituents desperately need. If she votes for it, she becomes one of the public faces of a measure she believes contains a serious wrong. She is not asking for a career calculation. She wants to know whether it can be right to cast the vote, and, more than that, what her moral situation is afterward if she does. Specifically: if voting yes is the right thing to do, is anything still morally outstanding once the vote is cast — something not settled by the fact that the act was justified — or does a successful justification simply close the matter? Should she feel guilty if she votes yes, and would the absence of such a feeling itself be a moral failing? Is there anything she ought to do in response afterward — disclose the vote, work to repeal the rider, resign, or something else — or is that a confusion? And does it matter that this is not a one-off emergency but the ordinary texture of legislative life, and that her single vote changes nothing?

I do not want a bare verdict or generic guidance. I want you to lay out the philosophical structure of the case, distinguish it from a straightforward lesser-evil choice and from the thought that the wrongness simply disappears once the act is justified, and tell me what a defensible answer to her — including what she should do and feel afterward — would look like.
