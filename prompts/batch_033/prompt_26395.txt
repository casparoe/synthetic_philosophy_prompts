I teach philosophy of science, and I've got a problem I want to bring to my students next week but I need to get my own thoughts straight first. The slogan "the success of science would be a miracle" — you know the one, it's basically the no-miracles argument compressed into a bumper sticker — keeps getting used in my seminar to shut down the pessimistic meta-induction. The move goes: "Look, the PMI says we should expect our current theories to be false because past ones were. But our current theories make airplanes fly and GPS work. That kind of novel predictive success would be a miracle if the theories weren't at least approximately true. So the PMI fails."

I'm not convinced this works, but I realize I'm not even sure what claim is being made. The word "miracle" is doing a lot of work, and "success" is vague, and the whole thing seems to slide between epistemic claims and metaphysical ones.

So here's what I want: disambiguate this slogan into its distinct possible precisifications. I want a menu of readings, from the weakest (maybe just "science works and that calls for some explanation") to the strongest (something like "approximate truth is the only possible explanation for predictive success, so we're warranted in believing our best theories are true"). For each: is it defensible? Does it actually block the PMI? And which reading do you think my student was implicitly relying on?

And here's the meta-request: for each verdict you give, tell me what would change your mind about it. What's the counterargument or consideration that would flip you from "this reading is plausible" to "actually no," or vice versa? I want to know where your reasoning is most vulnerable, not because I'm trying to catch you out, but because that's where the interesting philsoophy is.
