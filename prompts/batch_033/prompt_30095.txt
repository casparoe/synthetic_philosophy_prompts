I was at the APA Eastern last month and ended up eavesdropping on two junior faculty going at it at the hotel bar over Moore's paradox. You know the basic setup — sentences like "It's raining, but I don't believe it's raining" which are absurd to assert even though they can perfectly well be true. The question is what explains that absurdity.

The first one — call her Anita — was pushing what she called the assertion account. Her line was that the weirdness of Moorean sentences is entirely about the pragmatics of assertion. When you assert something, you represent yourself as believing it — that's just built into the norms governing assertion. So when you say "P but I don't believe P," the first conjunct represents you as believing P and the second conjunct explicitly denies you believe P. You've contradicted yourself, but the contradiction is between what your assertion implies about your mental state and what you've explicitly said, not between the propositional contents themselves. On her view, there's nothing especially problematic about *believing* a Moorean proposition, if that were even possible — the problem only arises when you try to put it into speech.

The second one — call him Ben — thought this didn't go deep enough. He kept pressing that Moore's paradox isn't just a quirk of conversation. Even if I never open my mouth, there's something constitutively off about my mental economy if I sincerely think to myself, "The dean is embezzling funds, but I don't believe the dean is embezzling funds." He said the assertion account makes it seem like a mere etiquette violation, when really it's more like trying to believe a contradiction — you can't coherently *occupy* that state. For him, the paradox reveals something about the nature of belief itself: that belief inherently aims at truth in a way that makes it impossible, or at least deeply irrational, to simultaneously hold that P and hold that you don't believe P.

Anita pushed back that Ben was conflating two questions: whether the state is rationally criticizable and whether it's genuinely impossible. She granted that believing a Moorean proposition would be irrational, but she insisted that's a different phenomenon from the infelicity of assertion, which is what Moore originally identified. Ben replied that if you can't even coherently believe the thing, then the assertion problem is derivative — the reason you can't properly assert "P but I don't believe P" is because you can't properly believe it, and assertion expresses belief.

Then things got meta. Anita said Ben's move was question-begging because it assumes that the norm of assertion just *is* "assert only what you believe," which is exactly the kind of claim the assertion-account theorist needs to argue for, not presuppose. Ben shot back that every account of assertion has to explain Moore's paradox, and hers does it by building belief-representation into the norms of assertion in the first place, which is no less question-begging.

I had to leave to catch my panel so I never heard how it played out, but I've been turning their arguments over since. Could you adjudicate this step by step? I'd especially like to know whether Anita is right that the assertion problem and the belief problem are genuinely separable phenomena, or whether Ben is right that one reduces to the other. And at the level of the meta-dispute about question-begging — who's got the better methodological point there?
