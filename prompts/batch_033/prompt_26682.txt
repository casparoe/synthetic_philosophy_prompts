I am a PhD student in philosophy of mind, and I have come to accept the conclusion of the private language argument, stated as follows: there cannot be a language whose terms refer to what only the speaker can know and whose meanings are fixed by private ostensive definition, because such a speaker would have no non-circular standard of correctness for reidentifying a sensation; sensation vocabulary instead gets its meaning from public criteria and shared practice.

I want to know what would count against this position. Please give me a map of its exposure rather than a survey of the literature. In particular: which possible cases, empirical findings, or developments in the theory of mental content would be decisive against it, and which would be merely awkward? For example, would a successful account of phenomenal concepts on which inner ostension fixes reference to a type of experience defeat the argument, or only one reconstruction of it? Would evidence from developmental psychology or from isolated individuals tell against the claim that sensation words require public criteria, and if so, how much? Which defeaters would a committed defender of the argument concede genuinely count against it—for instance, the charge that it rests on a verificationist requirement that meaning be checkable—and which would they dismiss as confusing epistemic privacy with semantic privacy? I would personally be moved most by a defeater that shows the conclusion follows only from an optional verificationist premise rather than from anything constitutive of meaning, so please indicate where that kind of weakening is available as opposed to requiring a full alternative theory of mental content. Finally, assess whether the position as stated risks anything at all, or whether it has been qualified into unfalsifiability; and if it has been, say whether that is a problem for a view of this kind.
