I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on the game theory of commitment, and I'm leading a session for a reading group of about 12 graduate students (90 minutes). I want to run a structured activity that makes participants feel the logic of a commitment race — the situation where each party gains by being the first to make its bargaining position irrevocable, but both lose if they both succeed in doing so. I want the structure of the game itself to produce the experience: the pressure to pre-empt, the temptation to escalate, and the risk of mutual disaster. No framing lecture beforehand; they should encounter the problem in their own choices and only afterwards map what happened onto the concept.

Design the activity: rules, materials, a payoff scheme that doesn't require real money, and a timeline for the 90-minute session. The debrief should connect specific moments in the game to the formal features of a commitment race — the first-mover advantage, the cost of delay, the risk of simultaneous commitment, and the way the race differs from ordinary bargaining. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it. Be explicit about which feature of real commitment races the game cannot reproduce and I will have to supply myself. Finally: what is the strongest argument that an activity like this misleads participants rather than illuminating the concept? That is, where is the design most vulnerable, and what consideration would lead you to revise it substantially?
