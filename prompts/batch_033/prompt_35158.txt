I was at dinner with two friends last week, and they got into an argument I still can't resolve. The trigger was small: Clara mentioned she'd been telling people that a new film was a masterpiece, even though she hadn't seen it yet — she'd read several reviews by critics whose taste she has reason to trust and felt confident in her judgment. David pushed back, saying she couldn't genuinely claim the film was good without having seen it herself. What followed was a proper philosophical back-and-forth:

(1) David: You can't know a film is good from reviews alone. Aesthetic judgment requires first-hand experience — you need to encounter the work and respond to it directly. Otherwise you're just reporting someone else's reaction, not making a judgment of your own. There's something second-hand and defective about an aesthetic belief formed purely on testimony.

(2) Clara: That's too sweeping. We rely on testimony for knowledge all the time — I know that Paris is the capital of France because I was told, not because I verified it. Why should aesthetic knowledge be different? If I have good reason to trust a critic's judgment, and they say the film is good, then I can know it's good on that basis. You need to explain what's special about the aesthetic case.

(3) David: Because aesthetic properties aren't like geographical facts. Knowing a film is good isn't just a matter of knowing a bare proposition — it essentially involves knowing what it's like to experience the film. Testimony can't give you that experiential acquaintance. You're mistaking knowing-that for knowing-what-it's-like, and in aesthetics the latter is required for the former.

(4) Clara: But I'm not claiming to know what the film is like. I'm claiming to know that it's good — a proposition. You're the one conflating the two. I can know that a wine is excellent without knowing what it tastes like, just as I can know that a mathematical theorem is true without being able to follow the proof, provided I trust the mathematician. The basis for my belief doesn't have to replicate its content.

(5) David: The mathematical case is disanalogous — there's a proof-independent fact of the matter. But aesthetic value is response-dependent: something is good in part because it merits a certain response from suitably situated subjects. If you haven't had the response, you're missing part of what makes the judgment true. You're in the position of someone who asserts a joke is funny without having laughed.

(6) Clara: That's a substantive metaphysical claim about aesthetic value, not an obvious datum. And even if aesthetic value is response-dependent, that doesn't mean my judgment must be based on my own response. I can judge that the film would merit a certain response from me, based on the testimony of people whose responses reliably track mine. That's still a genuine aesthetic judgment — it's about the work, not about the critics.

At this point they turned to me and asked what I thought. I said I needed time to think about it and changed the subject. But I've been turning it over since, and I'd like you to adjudicate this exchange. Please go through each move — all six — and assess who has the better of it at each step. Where is someone making a genuinely strong point, where are they missing something important, and who ends up more right overall? I'd especially like to know whether Clara's move at (6) successfully answers David's challenge at (5), or whether there's something David should have said in response that he didn't.

Please approach this as a professional philosopher working in aesthetics. Take your time, think through each move carefully, and give me a detailed point-by-point analysis. Don't just declare a winner — explain what's genuinely at stake in each exchange, what each person gets right and wrong, and whether the disagreement turns on a deeper issue that neither of them has fully articulated.
