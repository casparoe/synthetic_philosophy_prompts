ok so I keep noticing something and I can't tell if it's a named problem or just me being confused. Here's the pattern. Take a simple sentence like 'Socrates is wise.' There's Socrates, and there's the property of being wise, but what makes it a *claim* rather than just two items on a list? People say: there's a relation of instantiation, or a copula, or predication, that hooks them together. But then that relation is itself a third thing — so now you have Socrates, wisdom, and the instantiation relation. What hooks *those* three together? If you say another relation, you're off to the races. And if you say the instantiation relation doesn't need hooking — it just does its hooking intrinsically — then why couldn't you have said that about wisdom and Socrates in the first place?

I've seen the same thing happen with relations. 'The book is on the table.' On-ness is a relation that holds between the book and the table. But what makes the relation actually relate to the book and the table? If you need a second-order relation to connect the on-ness relation to its relata, you get the same infinite chain.

I'm trying to figure out if this has a canonical name, and if so, what exactly it covers. Is it specifically about relations, or about predication, or about the unity of propositions, or all three? Does it show up differently in different historical periods? And if there's no single name, what are the nearest concepts, and how do they carve up the territory differently? I'm not looking for a one-word answer — I want to know where this phenomenon starts and stops, what it's regularly confused with, and which versions of it are taken seriously and which are considered dissolved.
