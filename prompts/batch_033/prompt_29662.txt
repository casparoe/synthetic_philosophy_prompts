I'm the land steward for a county conservation district — no biology degree, just a lot of years in the field — and a few times a season we have to decide whether two populations count as one species or two, because it changes what we protect, what we spend money on, and what we tell the board. I've been reading about what I gather is called the species problem: biologists and philosophers can't agree on what a species even is (interbreeding, shared ancestry, looking different, filling a different niche), and it sounds like the field has mostly stopped trying for one definition. So I need a reusable procedure my small team can actually run in an afternoon, using just field observations and old survey data — no geneticist, no lab. It should be a checklist or decision tree: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts, and where it should just say the question cant be settled by the facts we have. Then run it on three cases: a geographically isolated population of a wide-ranging mammal, two plant populations that hybridize where they meet, and one case where the procedure points to an answer my team won't like. Be honest about where the procedure would misfire or quietly smuggle in one contested view of species. And please keep separate what is actually settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this — don't present a contested view as the consensus.
