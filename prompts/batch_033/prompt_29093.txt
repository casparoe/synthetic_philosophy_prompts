I've been developing a novel that involves alternative histories — characters encountering versions of themselves from different possible worlds — and this has forced me to think seriously about how identity works across possibilities. The philosophical debate I've found most useful is the one between counterpart theory and transworld identity, associated with David Lewis and Saul Kripke respectively. As I understand it, the question is what makes it true that I could have been a carpenter rather than a novelist. According to transworld identity, there is a possible world containing me, the very same individual, living a different life. According to counterpart theory, there is a possible world containing someone similar to me who is a carpenter, and this individual is my counterpart — not strictly identical to me, but similar enough in the right respects.

Now, it seems to me that counterpart theory straightforwardly solves the problem of accidental intrinsics — how I can be five-foot-ten in the actual world but five-foot-nine in some other possible world — by saying the individual in that other world isn't strictly me, so there is no contradiction. But I have also heard counterpart theory described as a theory of transworld identity. Yet if the counterpart is not identical to me, in what sense does the theory address transworld identity at all? Is this not simply changing the subject? If I want to know what makes it true that I myself could have been otherwise, and you tell me that someone else, a counterpart, is otherwise, you have not answered my question. So is counterpart theory a genuine alternative account of transworld identity, or is it simply denying that there is any such thing? And if the latter, how can it claim to be in competition with Kripke's view at all, since Kripke is giving an account of something Lewis apparently denies exists? I would appreciate a response in American English.
