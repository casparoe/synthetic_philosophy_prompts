I would like you to compare two rival conceptions of what a transcendental argument can establish: the ambitious conception, on which such an argument can yield conclusions about how the world must actually be, and the modest conception, on which it can yield at most conclusions about how we must think, experience, or represent the world, given capacities we take ourselves to have. Use a single specimen argument throughout—the argument from the possibility of ascribing experiences to oneself to the objectivity of experience will serve well—and do not settle for the coarse contrast that one conclusion is about the world and the other is about our representations; I want the comparison located inside the argument.

Specifically: (1) lay out the specimen argument in a neutral form that both conceptions could accept as their shared starting point; (2) identify the exact premise or inferential step at which the two conceptions diverge, and explain why a proponent of the modest conception regards the ambitious step as illegitimate; (3) state the additional assumption that would be required to license the ambitious step, and assess whether that assumption is something a transcendental argument could itself establish or whether it must be imported from outside—for example, from a verificationist or idealist theory of meaning; (4) discuss whether the modest conception is a stable resting point, or whether it leaves the skeptic with exactly the conclusion the skeptic wanted, namely that the relevant entitlement is psychological rather than epistemic, and say whether the modest conception can still claim a distinctively anti-skeptical achievement; and (5) close by saying which conception you think a transcendental argument must adopt if it is to be more than a report on our cognitive makeup, and what that choice costs.
