I'm a novelist working on a book that turns on a moment of institutional failure in the late Soviet period, and I've been deep in the counterfactual history literature to help me think about which of my plot's "what if" questions are plausible and which are wishful thinking. One scholar whose framework I keep returning to is Elena Kovacs, whose book *The Forking Paths of History* and several articles develop a systematic approach to distinguishing historically inevitable outcomes from genuinely contingent ones. I've compiled her verdicts on six cases she discusses at length, and I want to apply her implicit framework to a seventh case that matters for my novel.

Here are her verdicts, as I understand them:

1. **No Sarajevo assassination, 1914.** Kovacs argues that a great-power war would have broken out within five years regardless. The alliance systems, German naval ambitions, and Austrian-Russian rivalry in the Balkans had created a self-reinforcing pressure cooker; some spark would have set it off.

2. **Alexander the Great dies at the Granicus River, 334 BCE.** She contends that the Macedonian conquest of the Persian Empire would not have occurred. No one else in Philip's circle possessed Alexander's combination of tactical brilliance, risk appetite, and charismatic authority over the army. The whole enterprise, she argues, was a single-point failure.

3. **Lenin never reaches Russia, 1917.** Kovacs believes the Bolsheviks would not have seized power in October without Lenin's will and strategic clarity. However, she also argues that some form of revolutionary rupture was overdetermined given the Provisional Government's weakness and the war's disintegration of state authority. The October Revolution specifically was contingent; a revolutionary regime of some kind was not.

4. **Constantine loses at the Milvian Bridge, 312 CE.** She argues that Christianity's rise to imperial favor was genuinely contingent on this battle. Without Constantine's patronage, she thinks Christianity would have remained a significant but minority religion, and the Christianization of the Roman Empire would not have occurred in anything like the form or speed it did.

5. **Clear weather over the Channel on June 6, 1944.** Kovacs dismisses the weather as a genuine contingency for the outcome of D-Day. Had the invasion been postponed to the next viable tide window, she argues, the Allies would still have established a beachhead; German defensive preparations were inadequate and the force disparity was overwhelming. The weather affected only the schedule, not the result.

6. **The Cuban Missile Crisis without Kennedy.** She argues that the peaceful resolution was contingent on Kennedy's particular temperament and his ability to resist the Joint Chiefs. A different president — or, she suggests, the same president with a different Executive Committee — could readily have produced an escalation to nuclear exchange.

The case I need help with is the Chernobyl disaster of 1986. More precisely: suppose the shift supervisor that night, Aleksandr Akimov, had been replaced by a different, more cautious engineer — one who would not have allowed the safety test to proceed under the conditions that obtained. Would a catastrophic nuclear accident at Chernobyl, or some comparable Soviet nuclear disaster, still have occurred within a decade?

I can see arguments pulling in both directions. The RBMK reactor's design flaw — the positive void coefficient, the graphite-tipped control rods — and the Soviet nuclear industry's culture of secrecy and production-at-all-costs look like precisely the kind of structural pressures Kovacs treats as making an outcome inevitable. But the specific sequence of errors during the safety test was so bizarre — the disabling of multiple safety systems, the operator decisions at dangerously low power — that it also looks like a single-point failure of the kind she associates with contingency.

What I want is this: predict Kovacs's verdict on the Chernobyl question, and lay out the rule or principle she seems to be running implicitly across her first six cases. I suspect there are at least two distinct rules that fit her six verdicts equally well, and that they diverge on Chernobyl. If so, articulate each rule clearly, say what each one predicts about the Chernobyl case, and tell me which rule you think better captures her actual reasoning — and why. Finally, suggest a further historical case, one she has not written about, that would most cleanly decide between the competing rules.
