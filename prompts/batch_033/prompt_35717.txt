I've become convinced by the picture of cognition put forward in the Copycat project and the surrounding work on fluid concepts: the idea that human concepts are not at all like fixed dictionary definitions or rigid symbolic tokens, but are context-sensitive, slippable structures. On this view, what a concept like "successor" or "opposite" or "sameness" amounts to on a given occasion depends on the conceptual neighbourhood it sits in, and the ability to let these meanings stretch and slide—sometimes quite far—is what makes human analogy-making and creative thought possible, rather than being a defect to be eliminated.

I want to know what would count against this view. If the fluid-concepts picture were false, what would the world have to look like? Which psychological results, neuroscientific findings, or even AI developments would be awkward for it, and which would be devastating? Are there cases where the fluid-concepts approach seems to predict slippage that in fact never occurs in human cognition, or where it predicts no slippage but humans routinely slip? I would also like to know whether defenders of this view have insulated it with qualifications that make it hard to falsify—and if so, whether that is a serious problem for a theory of this kind, or whether some insulation is acceptable given the subject matter. Please rank the counterevidence from fatal to merely awkward, and be clear about which challenges a committed fluid-concepts theorist would concede as genuine problems rather than dismiss as misunderstandings.

I would be grateful if the answer were written in British English.
