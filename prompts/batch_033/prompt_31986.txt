I am a novelist, and a piece of craft doctrine I keep encountering has begun to shape the way I write a first-person narrator. The doctrine is that introspection is a kind of inner perception: to know one's own beliefs, desires, and emotions is to turn an inner eye on them and read off what is there, much as one reads off the visible features of a room. On this view, failures of self-knowledge are failures of looking or failures of candor, not failures of the faculty itself. In my circle this doctrine is being used to support a particular claim about fiction: that a sincere and attentive first-person narrator should be presumed authoritative about his own states, and that a character who is systematically mistaken about his own motives is a craft error unless he is written as a liar or a fool. I gather that a version of this picture also underwrites a long philosophical tradition of first-person authority.

I would like you to audit the analogy rather than simply accept or reject it. Give me the correspondence explicitly: what plays the role of the eye, the object seen, the act of looking, the conditions of good viewing, and the possible sources of error. Then identify where the mapping breaks. In particular, tell me which features of ordinary perception the analogy quietly imports — a dedicated organ, a causal chain from object to report, a stable object that exists before the looking, the possibility of illusion — and whether introspection actually has them. Separate the disanalogies that matter for the narrative conclusion from those that do not. Finally, tell me whether the craft claim survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether some other analogy would serve the novelist better, and what it would license instead.
