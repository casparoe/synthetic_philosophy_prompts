Take the role of a philosopher of literature who works on Dostoevsky, and think the issue through carefully before committing yourself. The interpretive debate over the Grand Inquisitor chapter of The Brothers Karamazov has been running for more than a century, and the main positions now look fixed. One side reads the chapter as a devastating internal critique of Christian freedom: the Inquisitor's case—that most people cannot bear the burden of free faith and would trade it for bread, miracle, mystery, and authority, and that the Church had to correct Christ's work—is so strong that Dostoevsky can answer it only with a kiss and silence, which is to say he does not answer it at all. The other side reads the chapter as dramatic irony: the Inquisitor is himself imprisoned in the logic he diagnoses, and the novel's real response is not a counter-argument but the lived faith of Zosima and Alyosha, which the Inquisitor's categories cannot register. My working hypothesis is that the impasse is less about the text than about what could count as an answer to the Inquisitor—whether a philosophical argument can be met by a literary or embodied response. I want a committed diagnosis: is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one, or two camps answering different questions? Given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress?
