I've been trying to extract a single rule for how my credences should track known objective chances, and my verdicts on cases are pulling in different directions. A coin is known to be fair and is about to be flipped: I'm at 50% on heads. The same coin was flipped an hour ago behind a screen, and no one has looked: I'm still at 50%, even though the outcome is settled and the coin is now definitely heads or definitely tails. The same coin, about to be flipped, but a predictor that has been right on the last thousand flips says it will come up heads: I'm at roughly 99%, not 50%. A known-fair die, whether about to be rolled or rolled an hour ago unobserved: I'm at one-sixth for each face. A coin of unknown bias, about to be flipped, that came up heads on seven of ten practice flips: I'm somewhere around two-thirds, but I don't know whether the right number is 0.7 exactly, a bit lower, or whether this case isn't about chance deference at all.

The pair I can't reconcile is the second and the third: in both I have information beyond the stated chance, yet in one I stay glued to the chance and in the other I abandon it. My provisional rule is "set your credence to the chance when you know it, unless you have direct evidence about how the event will turn out" — but I can't say what makes evidence direct without circularity, since the predictor's track record is evidence about the predictor, not literally about this flip, and it still moves me. I'd like you to state the principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that best captures these verdicts; say precisely what it predicts for a case I can't settle; and say honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. The unsettled case: a fair coin has just been flipped behind a screen; I know that if it landed heads a bell would have rung with 90% probability, and that the bell never rings on tails; I haven't heard a bell. Where should I be on heads? If the request rests on a confused premise — for instance, if there is no single deference rule, or "objective chance" isn't something I'm entitled to treat as authoritative in this way — say so plainly rather than fitting a principle to my list.
