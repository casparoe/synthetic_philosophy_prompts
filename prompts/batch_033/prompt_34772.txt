I'm taking a metaphysics course this term, and we've spent the last few weeks on the special composition question — roughly, under what conditions do some things compose a further thing? I've been trying to get clear on what I actually think by working through cases, and my verdicts are pulling me in different directions. I'd like help finding a principle that captures them, or at least diagnosing which ones I should give up.

Here are my verdicts:

(1) A living cat: the particles that make it up compose a single thing — the cat. This one feels non-negotiable.

(2) A pile of sand grains on a beach: the grains don't compose anything further. It's just many things, not one.

(3) A sand dune: I find myself thinking the grains do compose a dune. But a dune just is a pile of sand with a certain shape and a wind-formed causal history. I can't see what metaphysical difference the shape makes, and I'm uncomfortable with the mismatch between (2) and (3).

(4) A cloud: the water droplets compose a cloud. But a cloud is just a scattered collection of droplets with vague boundaries and no internal structure. If a cloud is a thing, I don't see why the sand pile isn't.

(5) Two strangers shaking hands: they don't compose a new object. They're just two people in contact. This feels obvious.

(6) A deck of playing cards scattered across the floor: no composite object — just fifty-two cards. The same deck, stacked neatly in its box: I want to say the cards now compose a deck. But the only difference is spatial arrangement, and letting composition turn on that seems suspiciously superficial.

(7) A swarm of bees in flight: just many bees, no further object. But I've read that some biologists treat ant colonies as single superorganisms, and I don't know whether that should shift my metaphysical verdict or whether I'm conflating two questions.

(8) A human corpse, minutes after death: I think the particles still compose a single thing — the body. But it's no longer a living organism, so whatever principle covered the cat can't simply be "life."

(9) A watch, fully assembled and running: one thing. The same watch, disassembled into all its parts in a box: just parts, no watch. This feels like the cards case all over again.

(10) The rock in my hand: one thing. But I know it's just a lattice of mineral grains, and I can't say what it has that the sand pile lacks — they differ in how strongly the parts are bonded, but is degree of attachment really a metaphysical difference-maker?

I've tried out a few candidate principles — "compose a thing if and only if the parts are fastened together," "compose a thing if and only if the parts participate in a life," "compose a thing if and only if the collection is functionally integrated" — and each one handles some cases cleanly while failing badly on others. I can feel something in the neighborhood, but I can't state it.

I'd like you to propose the best principle you can that captures as many of these verdicts as possible, state clearly which verdicts it forces me to abandon and why, and tell me what it predicts about a case I haven't listed: the particles that currently make up my body versus the particles that made up my body seven years ago, which are almost entirely different. Are those two collections the same thing — me — or is that a different kind of question from the one I've been asking?
