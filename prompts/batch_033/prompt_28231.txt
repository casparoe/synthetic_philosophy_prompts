I run a monthly discussion group for adults at a public library. The group usually has between 12 and 18 people, most with no background in philosophy or economics, and we meet for about two hours in a room with movable tables. For the past year I have been turning over a problem I first noticed in ordinary decisions rather than in books. Whenever a group has to divide something scarce — the one quiet room in a shared house, a small emergency fund among relatives, the last few seats on a crowded shuttle — we have to weigh how much each person's situation matters to them. In practice we often do this by asking people to rate things: how much would this help you, on a scale from 0 to 10. But I cannot see inside another person's experience, and I have come to suspect that these numbers do much less work than we pretend. One person's 7 may be another person's 3, not because they feel less, but because they are using the scale differently. I have since learned that this has a name — comparing well-being or satisfaction across different people — but I have not read the academic literature, and I do not want the activity to depend on any of it.

Please design a runnable activity for my group: 12 to 18 adults, no background required, about 90 minutes, in a room with tables. The activity should make participants run into the problem themselves rather than hear me explain it. By the end, they should feel two things: first, that comparing how much different people want or value something is unavoidable when a group has to decide; second, that every method for doing it — including asking for numerical ratings — quietly assumes a common measuring stick that nobody actually has. I am not looking for a generic exercise about fairness or empathy. Use modest real stakes, such as better seats, snacks, or small gift cards, so preferences are genuine but nobody is harmed. Build in at least one moment where two reasonable ways of using the same information point to different allocations, so the group can see that the numbers alone do not settle the decision. The moment of confusion should be produced by the rules and the results, and the point should not be named until the debrief.

Please organize your answer in exactly the following sections, in this order, with roughly the lengths shown:

- "The idea in one paragraph" (about 120 words): what the activity is meant to make participants feel, and why the design works through doing rather than through an opening explanation.
- "Setup and materials" (about 200 words): room layout, timing, group size, and a full list of materials, including the exact stakes to use and how to keep them modest but genuinely desirable.
- "The activity, step by step" (about 650 words): the complete sequence with timings, the instructions to read aloud at each stage, and what the facilitator should watch for before moving on.
- "Debrief" (about 400 words): the questions to ask afterward, in order, and how to map specific moments in the game onto the underlying problem; include what to do if the group does not notice the problem on its own.
- "What will predictably go wrong" (about 200 words): the most likely failure modes the first time I run it, and how to handle each without giving away the point.
- "What the activity cannot reproduce" (about 150 words): an honest note about which feature of the idea the game cannot capture and what I will need to supply or say myself.
