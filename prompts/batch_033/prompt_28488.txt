I am designing a handout for a graduate epistemology seminar on the lottery and preface paradoxes. The goal is a decision tree that takes a student from their intuitive responses to a sequence of concrete cases and deposits them at a named position in the debate—without the student needing to know any of the names or positions in advance.

Please construct the tree as follows. Begin with a single vignette: a fair lottery with ten thousand tickets and exactly one winner. Ask whether a rational person may believe, of the ticket in their hand, that it will lose. From there, branch by introducing further cases that isolate the key theoretical joints:

- Whether the number of tickets matters (what if the lottery has only three tickets? what if it has a trillion?);
- Whether it matters that the belief is about a specific ticket rather than the whole set;
- Whether it is permissible to believe the conjunction of such beliefs (i.e., that every ticket will lose);
- Whether the stakes of acting on the belief change what is rational to believe (e.g., believing the ticket will lose when deciding whether to discard it versus when deciding whether to bet your house on it);
- Whether the preface paradox—an author who believes each claim in her book but also believes the book contains at least one error—is the same problem under different clothing or a distinct puzzle requiring distinct treatment.

Each branching question must be phrased as a concrete scenario, not as an abstract principle. For example, instead of "Do you accept closure under conjunction?", give the student a case where someone believes P and believes Q under ordinary circumstances and ask whether they may also believe P-and-Q—and then test whether their answer changes when the beliefs were formed on merely statistical grounds.

The leaves of the tree must include at least these positions:

1. Denial that rational belief is closed under conjunction (one may rationally believe each ticket loses while refusing to believe all tickets lose).
2. Denial that high probability suffices for rational belief (the Lockean thesis is false; rational belief requires something stronger, such as knowledge or certainty).
3. Contextualism about belief ascriptions (the standards for "rational belief" shift with conversational context, so the paradox dissolves once we track the context-sensitivity).
4. Pragmatic encroachment (whether one may rationally believe that a ticket loses depends on what is practically at stake).
5. The credal replacement view (the paradox shows that binary belief is the wrong framework; we should theorize in terms of credences instead).

If the logical space contains coherent combinations of answers that no one defends, mark those leaves as "unoccupied" and briefly diagnose whether the position is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. If two positions cannot be separated by any case-based question in the tree, flag that explicitly and state what further distinction (even if not easily operationalized as a vignette) would be needed to pull them apart.

Finally, ensure the tree has at least eight branching nodes and at least six distinct leaves. The audience is philosophy graduate students who may or may not have read the relevant literature; the tree should work for both.
