I'm writing an essay on the philosophical problem of embedded agency — the idea that an agent is always part of the environment it is trying to model and act upon, rather than standing outside it with a God's-eye view. The core tension is that any model the agent builds of its world is itself an object within that world, which creates problems of self-reference, regress, and the impossibility of a fully external standpoint. This has been explored formally in work on logical uncertainty, reflective stability, and the limits of Bayesian reasoning for bounded agents, but I suspect the existential texture of the problem is better captured in fiction.

I want to read Jorge Luis Borges's "The Library of Babel" through this lens. The librarians are embedded agents par excellence: they inhabit a universe that is itself a library containing every possible book, including every possible book about the library, every possible catalog of itself, and every possible refutation of every possible catalog. They cannot step outside the library to verify any theory about its structure; their theories are themselves texts within the library, which means the library already contains the claim that their theory is true and the claim that it is false. The narrator's own speculations — that the library is unlimited and cyclical, that it is a sphere whose true center is any hexagon — are themselves just more books in the stacks, and the narrator knows this.

I would like a reading that takes both Borges and the embedded agency literature seriously. Where does the story dramatize aspects of embeddedness that the formal models miss? Are there moments where the librarians' predicament illuminates something about, say, the problem of constructing a self-referential probability distribution, or the difficulty of a reasoner modeling its own future reasoning? And where does the analogy break down — where does the literary metaphor trade on something that does not carry over to the technical problem? I am especially interested in whether the story offers any hint of a way out, or whether it suggests that the condition of embeddedness is simply inescapable in a way that makes the search for a complete, consistent self-model a kind of beautiful futility.
