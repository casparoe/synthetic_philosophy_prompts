Two friends of mine got into it after their philosophy reading group did Peirce and James on truth, and they came away with totally different loyalties. I was just listening but now they both want me to say who's right, and I honestly can't sort it out. Here's roughly how it went:

(1) Dan started: Peirce has the better view. Truth is what the community of inquirers would converge on in the long run — that ties truth to something outside anyone's convenience. It's what would compel assent if you investigated far enough. James waters this down to whatever is expedient to believe, which makes truth way too subjective.

(2) Mara pushed back: that's not fair to James. He's not saying truth is whatever feels good. He's saying the truth of an idea consists in its practical consequences — what difference it makes in experience. If two theories have all the same observable upshots, there's no factual difference between them. That's not subjectivism, it's a criterion of meaning.

(3) Dan said okay, but James goes further: he says that if believing something helps you live a better life, that counts toward its truth. That's the break with Peirce. For Peirce, what's satisfying to believe is irrelevant — only the endpoint of inquiry matters. James lets the value of believing something enter into whether it's true, and that's a mistake.

(4) Mara said Dan was conflating two different things. James applies the pragmatic maxim to the concept of truth itself: what does it mean to call an idea true? It means it's verifiable and it guides action successfully. But when James talks about the will to believe — in cases where the evidence is genuinely insufficient — he's asking a different question: when is it permissible to adopt a belief, not what makes a belief true. Those are separate arguments, and Dan was running them together.

(5) Dan replied that James himself doesn't keep them separate. In Pragmatism he talks about the belief in God being true because it makes a practical difference in the believer's life. That sure sounds like letting the benefits of believing determine truth, not just permissibility. So if anyone's conflating them, it's James.

(6) Mara said Dan was cherry-picking. The core of James's theory is that truth is a species of the good — true beliefs are those that prove useful in the long run and in the widest sense. But useful doesn't mean makes me happy; it means it doesn't lead to contradiction with experience over time. That's actually closer to Peirce than Dan admits. Peirce says truth is what we're fated to believe; James says truth is what survives the test of experience. The difference is more about whether convergence is guaranteed or just a messy ongoing process.

(7) Dan said that difference matters a lot. Peirce thinks there's a unique answer inquiry converges to; James thinks truth is plural and grows as new experiences come in. That's a real gap, and James's version opens the door to relativism in a way Peirce's doesn't. If truth is just what works for you, then incompatible beliefs could both be true for different people, and that's a problem.

(8) Mara said Peirce's convergence is just a regulative hope — he can't prove inquiry converges, so his view isn't actually more objective. It just dresses the same uncertainty in fancier clothes. And anyway, James wouldn't say incompatible beliefs can both be true; he'd say they haven't been tested far enough yet.

Can you go through this exchange move by move and say who's right about what? I'm especially curious whether Mara's move (4) — separating the theory of truth from the will-to-believe doctrine — is a fair reading of James or whether Dan is right that James himself blurs them. And if both of them are missing something important about how Peirce and James actually relate, I'd want to know that too.
