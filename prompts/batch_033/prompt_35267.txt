I recently moved into a role that requires long-horizon forecasting, and the more seriously I try to justify my confidence that stable past regularities will keep holding, the more I find myself returning to the familiar fable of the chicken: each morning it is fed, each feeding seems to confirm that it will be fed again, and then one morning its neck is wrung instead. A colleague keeps using the fable to argue that inductive inference is simply unjustified — that the chicken's evidence and ours differ only in degree, not in kind, and that our expectation that the sun will rise tomorrow rests on nothing better than the chicken's expectation of another meal. At the same time I have been reading Goodman's new riddle of induction, and it seems to me to be diagnosing something quite different from what the fable diagnoses. I would like an audit of the analogy. Give the correspondence table: what maps onto the chicken, the farmer, the daily feeding, the accumulating evidence, and the fatal morning in our own inductive practices. Then identify the points where the analogy breaks down, and separate the breakdowns that matter for the skeptical conclusion from the ones that are merely superficial. Finally, explain how Goodman's grue example bears on all of this: does it reinforce the chicken-fable's skeptical conclusion, undercut it, or relocate the difficulty somewhere else — and what, if anything, remains of my colleague's argument once both are on the table? If there is a better analogy for what is actually going on in induction, say what it would be.
