Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between a unificationist and a bifurcationist about indicative and counterfactual conditionals. The unificationist holds that indicative conditionals (e.g., 'If Oswald didn't kill Kennedy, someone else did') and counterfactual conditionals (e.g., 'If Oswald hadn't killed Kennedy, someone else would have') share a single semantic core, with the differences traceable to the modal base or accessibility relation together with pragmatics. The bifurcationist holds that the two constructions are semantically distinct — they involve different conditional operators, different truth conditions, or different kinds of modal quantification — and that no unified account survives scrutiny of the embedding data.

The deliverable is the document the two parties would produce after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. It should include:

1. A numbered list of claims both sides can sign. These should be neither vacuous nor slanted; they should capture what is genuinely common ground about the data, the formal behaviour, and what any adequate theory must explain — including the robust difference between the Oswald sentences and the constraints imposed by modus ponens, embedding, and interaction with modals.

2. A disagreements table. Each row should state a single point of contention in neutral language, followed by the unificationist's formulation of their own position and the bifurcationist's formulation of their own position, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording. The table should separate genuine disagreements from disagreements that are merely terminological or that dissolve once the modal base is explicitly parameterised.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. For each crux, state what both sides agree would count as evidence bearing on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. Cruxes should be empirical or formal — things about the behaviour of conditionals under embedding, about probability judgements, about cross-linguistic patterns, or about acquisition — rather than about which theory is more parsimonious or elegant.

4. If any portion of the dispute is judged by both parties to be verbal, identify it and state why.

The statement should be written in British English, and it should be the kind of document both parties would put their names to — not a compromise, but a precise map of where they agree, where they differ, and what would change someone's mind.
