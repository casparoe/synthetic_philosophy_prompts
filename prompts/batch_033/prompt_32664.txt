I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar called "Philosophical Methods" — 18 students, one 75-minute session. We are in the unit on intuition pumps: the idea, associated with Daniel Dennett, that thought experiments can function as persuasive devices whose narrative framing, vividness, and rhetorical structure do at least as much work as their logical architecture, and that this is a feature of philosophical methodology we should understand rather than simply trust or dismiss.

I want an activity that makes students feel how framing shifts intuition, without telling them in advance what the point is. My plan: split the class into two groups, give each group a slightly different written version of the same thought experiment, have them individually record their intuitive judgment, then discuss in their group, then bring the groups together. The two versions should differ only in framing features — order of information, which details are foregrounded, whether a loaded term is introduced before or after the scenario, choice of metaphor, emotional valence — and these differences should systematically push intuitions in opposite directions. When the groups compare, they should discover they were responding to the same underlying structure, and the debrief can then map the specific framing features onto the philosophical analysis of how intuition pumps work.

I need you to design the full activity. Specifically:

1. **The thought experiment.** Pick one where plausible variations in framing can shift intuitions without changing the structural features. It can be a well-known one from the literature, or one you invent, but it must be self-contained enough that students can grasp it from a short handout.

2. **The two versions.** Write them out verbatim as handouts, with the framing differences annotated or explained so I can see why you made each choice.

3. **The procedure.** Step-by-step with timing: how to distribute the versions, whether students write individually first, how to structure the within-group and between-group discussion, and how to reveal that the two groups had the same underlying scenario without students feeling tricked in a way that shuts down engagement.

4. **The debrief.** A plan for the final 20–25 minutes that maps specific moments in the activity onto the philosophical points: what features of the text did the persuasive work, why were the structural features not doing it, what does this tell us about the method of thought experiments in philosophy, and where Dennett's own account of intuition pumps might need qualification or supplementation.

5. **A candid troubleshooting note.** What will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and how to handle it.

The activity should be runnable by someone who has read the Dennett but is not a specialist in the empirical literature on framing effects. Students should not hear the term "intuition pump" until the debrief.
