In my school's philosophy club we have been stuck for an entire semester on the question whether there are genuine moral dilemmas: cases where a person must choose between two incompatible actions and, whatever she does, she will violate a genuine moral requirement. The same two camps keep forming. The first says such dilemmas are impossible: if you ought to do A and you ought to do B, then you ought to be able to do both, and if you cannot, one of the oughts must give way; the anguish after a tragic choice is grief or sorrow, not guilt for having done wrong. The second says this is a tidy theory refusing to look at the cases: in a Sophie's-choice-type situation the person is morally guilty no matter what, and that residual guilt, the moral remainder, shows a real requirement was left unmet. Nobody has moved in months. I am not asking who is right. My working hypothesis is that the dispute is not really about the cases but about what a moral requirement is: the first camp treats 'ought' as something that must be jointly satisfiable, while the second treats it as a source of demands that can outrun what the world lets us fulfill. Is that the right diagnosis? If so, what kind of disagreement is it, verbal, conceptual, or substantive, and what would actually count as progress? If not, what better explains the impasse, and what kind of evidence or distinction would have to be missing for this debate to be stuck the way it is?
