I have been reading Adam Smith's The Theory of Moral Sentiments with a small group, and one piece of language keeps being made to do very different jobs: "put yourself in their shoes." In one exchange, someone used it to mean that before you criticize an action you have to understand the situation as the agent understood it, so you should suspend judgment until you do. In another, it was used much more strongly: once you imagine the person's circumstances, you will see that the action was excusable — as though understanding a person's situation and endorsing what they did were the same operation. In a third, the phrase was turned away from the agent entirely: someone argued that to judge whether a grievance is warranted you should put yourself in the shoes of a well-informed third party with no stake in the outcome. And Smith himself seems to connect the phrase to a specific claim: that we never have direct access to another person's feelings, so the best we can do is imagine what we ourselves would feel if we were in their circumstances — which is not the same thing as feeling what they feel.

I want to know what this one piece of language could actually be asserting. Lay out the distinct precise theses "put yourself in someone else's shoes" might stand for: literally catching or sharing the other person's feeling; imagining yourself, with your own character and dispositions, in their circumstances; simulating the other person's mind with their beliefs, desires, and history; adopting the standpoint of a neutral or impartial spectator; and any further readings you think are genuinely distinct rather than verbal variants. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or a false one, and say what argumentative work it can and cannot do. Then commit: state which of these readings you take Smith to be relying on, which reading the move from "I understand the action" to "the action is excused" actually depends on, and whether that move survives the distinctions you have drawn. I would rather have a definite, argued verdict on the best reading than a survey that leaves the choice to me.
