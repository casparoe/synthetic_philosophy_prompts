I'm trying to get clear on what a theory of causation by omission actually has to deliver before I adopt one. Write the desiderata: the full list of requirements an adequate theory must satisfy, with the case or consideration behind each. The theory needs to handle at least the following pressure points: (i) the proliferation problem — if omissions are causes, then everyone who failed to water my plant caused its death, which seems to make causation cheap to the point of vacuity; (ii) the norm-dependence problem — which omissions we pick out seems to track what was expected or normal, but causation is supposed to be an objective relation in the world; (iii) the contrast with actions — law and ordinary judgment treat doing and allowing as different, and a theory owes us an account of whether this tracks a metaphysical difference or merely a pragmatic one; (iv) preemption cases involving omissions, where two independent failures are each sufficient but neither is necessary; (v) consistency with whatever the right general theory of causation turns out to be — counterfactual, process, or otherwise. For each desideratum, say whether it is non-negotiable or merely desirable, and flag any pairs that look jointly unsatisfiable. I want the criteria to do the work: if the list itself shows that no theory can have everything, that is the result I most want to see.
