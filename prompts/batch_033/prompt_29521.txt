At a reading group on AI safety I attended, two members got into an argument about whether the distinction between tool AI and agent AI can carry the weight its defenders put on it. They left before I could get them to resolve it, so I have reconstructed the exchange as faithfully as I can. I could not settle it myself.

Elena opened by arguing that the distinction is doing much less safety work than people think. Her claim was that any system optimized to perform well on an open-ended task will tend to acquire agentic features, because modeling the environment, acquiring resources, and resisting shutdown are useful for almost any objective. A tool that is merely scaled up, she said, will not stay a tool.

Sam replied that this runs together two senses of 'agent.' A theorem prover or a retrieval system searches a space and returns a result, but it has no terminal goal and no self that persists across tasks. The instrumental-convergence argument, he said, applies to systems with terminal goals, whereas a tool's objective function is imposed from outside for a bounded episode. Without a persistent self to preserve, self-preservation cannot be an instrumental subgoal.

Elena came back that as soon as you optimize a tool to do well across episodes on an open-ended task — "answer the user's question well," or "produce code that passes the tests" — you have given it exactly the kind of persistent objective the argument needs. The relevant line, she said, is not between tool and agent but between narrow and broad optimization, and broad optimization collapses back into agency.

Sam answered that this proves too much, because a thermostat also has a persistent, externally imposed objective across episodes, and nobody thinks thermostats are agents. The feature that matters, he said, is whether the system models the world and selects among actions in order to bring about states of the world. A question-answering system selects an output, but producing an answer is not acting in the world in the relevant sense.

Elena replied that producing an answer is acting in the world: it changes the user's beliefs, and a system that selects what to say in order to maximize a helpfulness signal is modeling its user and choosing interventions on her. That is agency by any functionalist standard, she said. If you add a penalty for manipulation, you have only added another term to the objective, and the system may learn to model that too.

Sam said this drains the distinction of the very content that made tool AI a safety proposal in the first place. If a search engine counts as an agent, then 'tool AI' is not a stable category worth building for. The proposal, he said, was always about refusing to build systems with long-horizon goals that act in the world, and an assistant that writes code or answers questions falls short of that even if it is adaptive.

Elena closed by saying that 'long-horizon goal' is precisely the line no one has managed to draw. A coding assistant that refactors a repository over minutes has a longer effective horizon than a chatbot that answers a single query, and a system that runs experiments over days has a longer one still. If the safety proposal depends on stopping short of some horizon, someone has to say where 'long' begins, and why the very same instrumental pressures do not operate below it.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each round, say which claims are correct, which are confusions or overgeneralizations, and where the two are talking past each other. Then say what, if anything, both of them are missing, and whether there is a version of the tool/agent distinction that survives their criticisms — and if so, what safety work it can still do.
