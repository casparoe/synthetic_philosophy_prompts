I keep running into a line from Epicurus in online discussions and in a book club I joined — or at least a line people attribute to him. The version I see most often goes: "Death does not concern us, because as long as we exist, death is not here. And once it does come, we no longer exist." People deploy it to shut down arguments about whether death is something to fear, or whether a death at twenty is a worse tragedy for the person than a death at eighty. But I've noticed that the people who repeat it seem to mean very different things by it. Some use it to say that death can't be bad for you at all, full stop. Others use it to say only that you shouldn't feel dread about being dead, which seems much weaker — it leaves open that a premature death might still be a misfortune, just not one you should lose sleep over. Still others use it to say something about the metaphysics of harm: that nothing can be bad for you unless you experience it as bad, and death fails that test because there's no subject left to do the experiencing. And I've seen someone use it to argue that a longer life isn't better than a shorter one, which seems like a further leap from the same starting point.

I'm trying to get clear on what this one-liner is actually asserting, or could be asserting. Can you lay out, as precisely as you can, the distinct claims someone might be making when they say "death is nothing to us" or "death does not concern us" — from the narrowest and least controversial reading to the most sweeping — and tell me for each one whether it's plausible, whether it actually follows from the reasoning in the slogan, and whether it's the reading most people are probably reaching for in ordinary conversation? I'm less interested in the historical Epicurus than in what the slogan amounts to when it gets used as an argument-stopper today.

Please format your answer as a numbered list of readings, with a short verdict on each. Keep the whole response to no more than 900 words.
