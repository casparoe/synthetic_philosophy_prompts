Over the course of a reading group on the Frege–Geach problem for expressivism, a colleague has defended an expressivist account of moral language and has committed herself to the following positions. (1) An atomic moral sentence such as 'Stealing is wrong' is not truth-apt, that is, not the kind of sentence that is true or false; it conventionally expresses disapproval of stealing. (2) 'Stealing is not wrong' is likewise not truth-apt, and it expresses a distinct attitude rather than the mere absence of the disapproval expressed by the unnegated sentence. (3) A conditional such as 'If stealing is wrong, then getting your little brother to steal is wrong' does not express disapproval of stealing or of the brother's act; it expresses an attitude whose content concerns the relationship between the attitudes expressed by its parts. (4) The argument from 'Stealing is wrong' and 'If stealing is wrong, then getting your little brother to steal is wrong' to 'Getting your little brother to steal is wrong' is valid, and someone who accepts the premises while denying the conclusion is thereby inconsistent. (5) 'It is not the case that stealing is wrong' and 'Stealing is not wrong' are equivalent. Next week the group will consider the sentence 'Either stealing is wrong, or the sky is blue.' Predict what she will say about whether this disjunction is truth-apt and what attitude, if any, it expresses. I am less interested in the bare prediction than in the reasoning: identify the semantic program or programs consistent with her five positions, say what each candidate program predicts about the disjunction, give your best guess with reasons, and specify a further sentence whose treatment would best discriminate among the surviving candidates. Please write your answer in continuous prose, as you would a letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
