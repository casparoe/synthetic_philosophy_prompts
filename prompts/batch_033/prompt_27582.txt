I am a postgraduate student in philosophy, and I have been working through the accuracy-first programme in formal epistemology for a term paper. The central claim of the programme—defended most prominently by James Joyce and Richard Pettigrew—is that the norms governing rational credence can be derived from the single epistemic goal of minimising inaccuracy, where inaccuracy is measured by a scoring rule that is strictly proper. But as soon as one grants that any strictly proper scoring rule will do, one faces the problem that there are infinitely many such rules, and they disagree in their comparative inaccuracy judgements. The two rules that receive the most sustained discussion are the Brier score and the logarithmic scoring rule.

I have a reasonable grasp of their formal properties. The Brier score for a credence function c at a world w is the sum over propositions of (c(p) – v_w(p))², where v_w(p) is 1 if p is true at w and 0 otherwise; it is bounded, not local, and in the multi-proposition case it is not additive in any straightforward way. The log score is –ln(c(p)) for the true proposition p at w; it is local, unbounded, and strictly proper. I am also aware that the log score is closely connected to information theory and to Bayesian updating conceived as a special case of minimising expected inaccuracy. What I am less clear about is what, if anything, philosophically turns on the choice between these two scoring rules.

Please compare the Brier score and the logarithmic scoring rule as rival accounts of epistemic accuracy within the accuracy-first framework. In your comparison, please address the following: (a) whether either rule can be shown to be uniquely correct given the motivations of the accuracy-first programme, or whether the programme must live with a kind of scoring-rule pluralism; (b) what specific norms of rational credence—beyond probabilism itself—come out differently depending on which rule one adopts; and (c) whether the locality of the log score or the boundedness of the Brier score carries greater philosophical weight when we are modelling the epistemic lives of bounded agents like ourselves. Please write your answer in British English.
