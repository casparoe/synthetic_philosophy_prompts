I recently joined an AI lab working on alignment, and I've found myself adopting what I'd call the prosaic alignment view: that aligning near-future AI systems — including ones substantially more capable than today's — can be achieved by extending current machine learning techniques (RLHF, constitutional AI, scalable oversight, automated red-teaming, and the like), without requiring fundamental new theoretical breakthroughs. I hold this as a practical working view, not a certainty, and it shapes what I work on and what I advocate for. But I'm aware that I might be wrong, and I want to know what that would look like.

What would count against the prosaic alignment view? I want the evidence and arguments that should move me, sorted by how decisive they'd be. I want to distinguish between developments that would be merely awkward — a particular technique fails to scale, a benchmark saturates without real progress — and developments that would be fatal: structural reasons why no extension of current methods can bridge the gap between optimizing for a reward signal and actually doing what we intend. Which of these potential defeaters would defenders of the view themselves concede as genuine risks, as opposed to objections they'd dismiss as misunderstandings of what the view is committed to? And has the view been qualified in ways that insulate it from falsification — for instance, by treating every failure as a prompt to add another layer of oversight rather than as evidence against the approach? If it has been insulated, is that a problem for a view of this kind, given that it functions as an engineering bet rather than a scientific hypothesis? I want an honest, unsympathetic map of the view's exposure.
