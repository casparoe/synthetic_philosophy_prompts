My professor has a habit of punctuating discussions with the slogan "labels change the labeled," using it to push back against any view that treats psychiatric categories as straightforwardly descriptive of mind-independent conditions. But I have begun to suspect that the phrase is doing too much work: it could mean half a dozen different things, from the utterly trivial to the deeply revisionary, and which reading you adopt determines whether the slogan actually refutes anything.

Before I go to office hours, I want a rigorous disambiguation. Please approach this as a philosopher of psychiatry who knows Hacking's work well. Begin by brainstorming the possible mechanisms of "change" at work here, then lay out the distinct precise claims that "labels change the labeled" could be asserting, ordered from the most trivial to the most radical. For each reading, provide: (a) a verdict on whether it is obviously true, plausibly true but substantive, or likely false; (b) whether Hacking's own view commits him to it; and (c) whether it would actually support using the slogan to dismiss purely descriptive approaches to psychiatric classification. Flag which reading you think is doing the real argumentative work when the slogan is deployed in debate, and note any readings that are often conflated but should be kept apart.
