So I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, but I've been going down this rabbithole lately. A buddy of mine is doing a philosophy masters and he kept going on about Hume's is-ought gap — the idea that you can't derive a normative conclusion from purely descriptive premises. No "ought" from an "is." At first I was like, sure, whatever, but then I started thinking about it in terms of AI alignment (which I follow casually). If an AI only has access to facts about the world, and the is-ought gap is real, then the AI can never logically arrive at a moral judgment. But then I realized the same logic applies to humans: we also just take in sensory data about how the world is, and somehow we output oughts. If there's a genuine logical barrier, we're not crossing it either.

So I tried to make the argument precise, and it ended up proving way more than I intended. Here it is:

(1) In any valid deductive argument, an "ought" can appear in the conclusion only if at least one premise contains an "ought." (That's Hume's gap, as I understand it.)

(2) Therefore, to justify any moral claim M, you must derive it from some prior moral claim P, which serves as a premise.

(3) But P itself stands in need of justification, which requires another prior moral claim, and so on.

(4) This justificatory chain has exactly three possible structures: it terminates in an unjustified moral premise (foundationalism), it continues infinitely (infinitism), or it forms a closed loop (coherentism).

(5) Foundationalism fails because an unjustified premise is just a stipulation — you could have stipulated the opposite, and you've given no reason to prefer one over the other. Infinitism fails because finite minds cannot complete an infinite regress of reasons. Coherentism fails because mutual support among claims in a circle provides no independent reason to accept any of them; if A rests on B and B rests on A, the whole thing floats free of any anchor.

(6) So no moral claim can ever be justified.

(7) But premise (6) is insane. "It is wrong to torture infants for amusement" is justified — I am more certain of this than I am of any step in the argument above.

(8) So the argument contains a mistake. But which step fails, and what do you have to give up to reject it?

I'm leaning toward rejecting (1) — maybe Hume's gap is only about deductive logic specifically and there are other ways to justify ought-claims that don't require an ought-premise. But then I'd need an account of what those other ways are, and I don't have one. I've also wondered if (5) is too quick about coherentism — maybe a big enough web of mutually supporting beliefs is different from a simple two-node circle, and that actually does provide some kind of justification. But I don't know how to make that precise. Where does this actually go wrong?
