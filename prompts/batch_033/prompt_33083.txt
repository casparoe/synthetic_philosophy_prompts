I am trying to determine what I actually think about deferring to my own future opinions, and I want the principle behind my reactions, if there is one. The simple principle I am trying to test is the Reflection principle: my present credence in a proposition, conditional on my future credence in it being r, should be r. My reactions below seem partly to support it and partly to undermine it.

Here are my verdicts. If I now learn that in six months, after careful study of the trial record, I will assign credence 0.8 that the defendant is guilty, I think I should now assign 0.8 as well; future-me is better informed and no less careful. If I now learn that tomorrow, after thirty hours awake and a sedative, I will assign credence 0.8 that the defendant is guilty, I do not think I should now assign 0.8, even though tomorrow-me will have one more day of information. If I now learn that next week I will have forgotten a decisive statistical argument and will assign credence 0.4 to a claim I now hold at 0.8, I do not think I should drop to 0.4; next-week-me is missing evidence, not adding any. If I now learn that a year from now, after a neurological illness that leaves my memory intact but makes my reasoning sluggish and suggestible, I will assign credence 0.9 to a conspiracy theory, I do not think I should defer at all. If I now learn that a future self with exactly my present evidence and abilities will have reflected for an extra hour and will assign credence 0.7 to a proposition on which I am now at 0.5, I think I should defer, though I cannot say in a principled way why this differs from the neurological case. If I now learn that a future self will assign high credence to a proposition after being persuaded by arguments I currently regard as unsound, I do not think I should defer, even though future-me will regard himself as better informed.

The two verdicts I cannot reconcile are the first and second: in both, the future self has strictly more information than I have now, yet in one I defer completely and in the other not at all. What principle, if any, captures all or most of these verdicts? State it precisely, say which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and why, and tell me what it predicts for the case I remain unsure about: a future self whose evidence will be strictly better in some respects and strictly worse in others, and whose judgment will be sharper in some respects and duller in others. I also want to know whether the simple principle can be repaired by conditioning deference on something like expected reliability and expected informativeness, or whether the right view must give up on the simple principle altogether.
