I came across the following summary of Scanlon's contractualism and the aggregation problem on a philosophy blog, and I'm trying to figure out how much of it I can rely on:

"Scanlon's contractualism holds that an act is wrong if it would be disallowed by any principle for the general regulation of behavior that no one could reasonably reject. The test is applied from the standpoint of each individual separately: you ask what principles a given person could reasonably reject, given the burdens that principle would impose on her. Because the assessment is individualistic in this way, the reasons of different people cannot be combined or added together. This is what generates the famous aggregation problem. The view implies that the fact that a thousand people would each suffer a minor headache can never outweigh the fact that one person would die, because each of the thousand has only a weak complaint while the one has a maximally strong complaint. Scanlon tries to escape this by saying that in certain cases we should consider the combined force of everyone's complaints after all, but critics have pointed out that this looks like an ad hoc patch that collapses the view into utilitarianism anytime the numbers get large enough."

I'm not a philosopher, and I'd like to know how much of this is fair. Could you go through it and tell me which parts a careful defender of Scanlon's contractualism would accept as accurate, which are misrepresentations or oversimplifications, and whether the charge of ad-hoc-ness is justified? I'm especially curious about whether the blog's claim that reasons "cannot be combined or added together" is actually something Scanlon asserts, or whether that's a conflation the blog is making between the individualist standpoint of assessment and the content of the principles being assessed.
