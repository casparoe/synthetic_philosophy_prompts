I've been reading one of those "big ideas" popular philosophy books — the kind with short chapters and no footnotes — and I came across its summary of J.L. Austin's speech act theory. Here's what it says, more or less verbatim:

"Austin pointed out that some utterances aren't reports about the world but are actions in themselves. When the officiant says 'I now pronounce you married,' she isn't describing a marriage; she's bringing one into existence. Austin called these performatives and distinguished them from constatives, which are ordinary true-or-false statements. Later he realized the distinction couldn't be maintained, so he replaced it with a three-part classification: locutionary acts (the act of saying something meaningful), illocutionary acts (what you do in saying it — warning, promising, requesting), and perlocutionary acts (the effect you produce on your hearer, like persuading or alarming them). The key takeaway is that performatives work only when backed by social conventions — the speaker must have the right standing and the circumstances must be appropriate — otherwise the act misfires. That's why saying 'I bet you sixpence' only counts as a bet if betting practices already exist in your community."

I'm a retired physicist who's gotten into philosophy late, so I don't trust my own nose yet. But a few things bother me. First, the summary makes it sound like Austin naively held the performative/constative distinction and then dropped it when it broke, but my impression — from somewhere — is that Austin was dismantling it from the start and the whole book is a kind of dialectical unfoliding of why it fails. Second, isn't the emphasis on "social conventions" more Searle than Austin? I vaguely recall that Austin focused on uptake — the hearer understanding what's being done — rather than on conventions for illocutionary acts per se, and that this difference actually matters. Third, the summary leaves me wondering why anyone should care: it sounds like mere taxonomy. What was the philosophical payoff supposed to be?

Can you do a careful audit of this summary? Tell me what's accurate, what's subtly wrong or misleading, and what a speech-act theorist would flag if they read it.
