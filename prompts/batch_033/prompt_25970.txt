My advisor is deep into Adam Smith — like, thinks the whole post-Smith tradition is a series of misunderstandings deep. We've spent the semester working through The Theory of Moral Sentiments and she keeps illustrating with cases. I've been writing down her verdicts:

- A colleague asks if you read their draft and you say yes even though you only skimmed it, because you don't want to hurt their feelings. She says the impartial spectator can't enter into this — the benevolent motive is genuine but the aversion to a mildly awkward conversation is wildly disproportionate, so the action flunks the propriety test.

- Someone finds out their roommate has been stealing small amounts of cash from them. They install a hidden camera, confirm it, and then go straight to the landlord to get the roommate evicted without ever confronting them. She says the impartial spectator sympathizes with the resentment but can't go along with the action — the spectator would insist on a private confrontation first, because the harm of eviction includes foreseeable consequences (the roommate becoming homeless, say) that are out of proportion to the wrong.

- A grad student discovers their advisor fabricated data in a grant application, not a publication. The student reports it to the university ethics office. The advisor is investigated and the grant is revoked, meaning the other students in the lab lose their funding too. She says this one is genuinely hard, but the impartial spectator ultimately sides with the student — the wrong is serious enough, and the reporting went through proper institutional channels rather than public exposure. The collateral damage to other students is unfortunate but the spectator doesn't think it makes the action improper, because the student isn't the one causing that harm — the advisor is.

- A nurse in a severely understaffed ward walks off shift two hours early because they're exhausted and terrified of making a fatal error. Patients are left with even worse ratios. She says the impartial spectator can sympathize with the fear and exhaustion but can't fully enter into the action — the foreseeable harm to specific patients from walking off is more vivid to the spectator than the diffuse, statistical risk of error from staying. The spectator would expect the nurse to stay and document the unsafe conditions afterward through formal channels.

Now here's the case I want to run by her next week. A mid-level employee has been documenting their boss's pattern of sexual harassment toward junior staff for over a year. Internal HR complaints have gone nowhere because the boss is tight with senior leadership — the complaints were buried and one junior employee who came forward was managed out. The employee decides to take the documentation to a journalist. The story will almost certainly end the boss's career, and it'll also cause significant reputational damage to the company, which employs a lot of people who had nothing to do with any of this and who might face layoffs if business drops.

I'm trying to work out what she'll say before I bring it up. I feel like her verdicts so far are consistent with at least two or three different interpretations of how the impartial spectator weighs the relevant considerations — something about the relationship between propriety and merit, or about how the spectator handles collateral damage, or about when private remedies count as genuinely exhausted. What are the candidate rules she might be running, which one fits her track record best, and what further case would cleanly separate them?
