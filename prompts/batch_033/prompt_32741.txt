I'm a retired physicist who has spent the last few years working my way into philosophy of language, and I'm giving a seminar next month to a philosophy department where I've been a visiting fellow. I'm defending epistemicism about vagueness — roughly, the view that vague predicates like "is bald" or "is a heap" have sharp boundaries, and the appearance of indeterminacy is just a case of irremediable ignorance: we can't know where the boundary lies, but there is one.

The department I'm presenting to has several philosophers of language and logic, a couple of metaphysicians, and one philosopher of science. The faculty most likely to attend are broadly sympathetic to supervaluationism: they think vague predicates are partially defined, that borderline cases are truth-value gaps (neither true nor false), and that truth is supertruth — truth on all admissible precisifications. They tend to view epistemicism as a non-starter because it seems to require an implausible metaphysics of reference and an implausible epistemology of vagueness.

My own argument, in outline, runs as follows. (1) Supervaluationism gives up bivalence and classical logic, which is a heavier cost than it first appears — it forces revisions to the logic of propositions we use in science and everyday reasoning. (2) The epistemicist's "sharp boundary" is no more mysterious than the sharp boundaries we already accept in physics — phase transitions, for instance, where a continuous parameter yields a discrete change, and where the exact point is often unknowable due to measurement limitations. (3) The supervaluationist's precisifications face a specification problem: what counts as an admissible precisification, and why does "admissible" not itself breed higher-order vagueness? (4) The margin-of-error principle — that knowledge requires a margin for error — explains our inability to locate the cutoff without positing semantic indeterminacy.
