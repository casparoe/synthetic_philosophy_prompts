I run a teen gardening and science club at our community garden — usually around 14 kids, ages 13 to 16, and we get about 75 minutes. I've been reading about heritability on my own lately (started with plant breeding and then fell into the human genetics arguments), and the thing that finally landed for me is that heritability is about the spread of a trait in a group, not about any one individual, and that the same trait can end up with very different heritability numbers depending on how much the environment varies. I want the kids to run into that themselves instead of me standing up front and saying it.

Can you design a hands-on game or simulation where the kids act as plant breeders picking which plants to save seed from, using cheap stuff I can make at home — beans, index cards, paper cups, dice, whatever. I'm imagining two or three "fields" that differ in how patchy or uniform their growing conditions are, and maybe a second round with cloned seeds so all the plants have the same genes. The moment I most want is the one where they confidently save seed from the tallest plants in a patchy field and then realize they were mostly picking the plants that happened to be sitting in the good spots, not the plants with the best genes — and then the flip where the same kind of selection works much better in a uniform field.

I'd like full rules, a materials list, a scoring or betting scheme that doesn't need real money (candy or beans as stakes is fine), and a timeline for the 75 minutes. Then a debrief that maps what happened in the game onto the ideas: heritability is about variation within a population; it isnt a fixed fact about a trait; it changes when the environment changes; high heritability doesnt mean environment doesnt matter or that the trait cant be changed; and differences within a field dont automatically explain differences between fields. Also be honest about what the game can't get across — I know real heritability estimates come from twin studies or SNP math we won't touch, so tell me what I should say out loud afterwards to fill that gap. And tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it. No lecture up front, and I'd rather they don't hear the word "heritability" until after the game has already made the point.
