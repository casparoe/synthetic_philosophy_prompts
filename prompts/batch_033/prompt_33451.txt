You are a philosopher of economics who works on the foundations of game theory. Think the issue through carefully; it may help to begin by making explicit exactly what work mixed strategies do in iterated dominance before addressing the sub-questions.

Game theorists and economists routinely predict play by iteratively deleting strictly dominated strategies, where a strategy counts as dominated if some randomization over the player's other pure strategies yields a strictly higher expected payoff against every combination of the opponents' pure strategies — even when no single pure strategy would dominate it. Reconstruct the philosophical commitments of this methodological practice. In particular: (a) Does mixed-strategy dominance presuppose that players are expected-utility maximizers who can and do randomize deliberately, or can the mixing be given a purely epistemic reading, as a feature of the analyst's model rather than of the player's deliberation? (b) The practice standardly deletes strictly dominated strategies but refuses to delete weakly dominated ones; what does that asymmetry presuppose about how rationality is being operationalized, and is it coherent? (c) Does iterating the deletion presuppose common belief in rationality, and does it implicitly allow or rule out correlation in a player's beliefs about different opponents? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say what would have to change if expected utility were replaced by an alternative theory of decision under uncertainty, or if mixed strategies were interpreted as population-level frequencies rather than individual randomizations.
