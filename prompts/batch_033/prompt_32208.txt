I'm teaching a upper-level undergrad seminar on minds and collectives, and next week we're closing out our unit on Hofstadter's Aunt Hillary — the ant colony that seems to have a mind even though no individual ant does. The students have been going back and forth on whether the colony *really* has phenomenal consciousness or just behaves as if it does, and I want to give them a framework for thinking about the range of possible positions here, not just the binary yes/no.

So here's what I'd like you to do. Below are six positions one could take on whether an ant colony (or a termite mound, or a similar social-insect superorganism) has a mind. Rank them from most to least defensible — not which one you *want* to be true, but which one you think has the best philosophical arguments going for it. Give a sentence or two of justification for each ranking. And be commited about it: I don't want a "well on the one hand, on the other hand" survey. Pick a hill and defend it. (American English, please.)

The positions:

(1) Eliminativist: neither ants nor colonies have minds. "Mind" is a folk-psychological concept that doesn't carve nature at the joints, and applying it to collectives just compounds the confusion.

(2) Individual-only: individual ants have minimal sentience (maybe something like valence-weighted perception), but the colony as a whole has no mental properties whatsoever — the appearances of colony-level intelligence are entirely the result of distributed, algorithm-like behavior.

(3) Functionalist-as-if: the colony instantiates the right functional organization to count as a mind in principle, but it's a degenerate or marginal case — like calling a thermostat a "believer." It's technically true under a liberal enough functionalism but so thin as to be misleading.

(4) Genuine functionalist: yes, the colony has a mind, full stop. If the functional organization is there, the substrate doesn't matter. The ant colony is a real cognitive system with beliefs, desires, and possibly even a rudimentary form of consciousness, in the same way a brain is a real cognitive system made of neurons.

(5) Strong emergentist: the colony not only has a mind but has mental properties that cannot be reduced to or predicted from the properties of individual ants. Something qualitatively new comes into existence at the colony level.

(6) Panpsychist/idealist: the colony's mind is a manifestation of a more fundamental consciousness that pervades nature. Individual ants are already loci of experience, and the colony is a higher-order integration of that experience — not a new mind but a new unification of minds that were already there.

I realize some of these blur into each other — feel free to note that in your ranking. But I do want a linear ordering, even if it's rough.
