I keep running into the same move in the logical uncertainty stuff I've been reading, and I want someone to actually take it apart instead of just nodding along. The move is: logical uncertainty is just ordinary empirical uncertainty about a fact you haven't observed yet, so all the usual Bayesian machinary carries over. You assign a prior over math statements, you conditionalize when you prove or compute something, and your credence in, say, the Riemann hypothesis should behave exactly like your credence that it'll rain tomorrow. This gets used to argue that a credence in an unproven theorem is a perfectly ordinary probability and that decision-making under logical ignorance needs no special treatment — you just do standard expected utility with your logical prior. I've also seen the efficient-market gloss where a market over sentences is supposed to be the logical analogue of a well-calibrated Bayesian, and the two get treated as basically the same picture.

I get the intuitive pull but I suspect the analogy is quietly doing a lot of work. I want an audit. What actually maps onto what — hypothesis, observation, sample space or possible worlds, conditionalization, calibration? A table of correspondences is fine. Then where does the mapping break, and which dissanalogies matter for the intended conclusion (that logical uncertainty needs no special treatment) versus which are just cosmetic? In particular I keep going back and forth about a few things: in the empirical case observations are independent of the hypothesis in a way proofs aren't, since a proof of a theorem is itself a logical consequence of the same axioms; the sample space isn't a set of worlds where the theorem could turn out false; and conditioning on finding a proof seems importantly different from conditioning on an observation, because you can't coherently condition on a proof of a false statement. But I don't trust myself to sort the fatal breaks from the fixable ones. So: does the conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? And if it's bad, what's the better picture of what a credence in an unproven theorem even is?
