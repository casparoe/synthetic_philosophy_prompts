I'm writing a paper on the badness of pain, and I need to get clear on what any adequate theory of pain's badness must accomplish before I evaluate the candidates. The clinical literature on pain asymbolia, morphine dissociation, and related phenomena has made it impossible to just assume that pain's badness is a simple, unitary thing — but I'm not sure how much weight these cases should bear, or what else belongs on the list.

Please produce a structured specification of the desiderata for a theory of pain's badness. I want:

1. A numbered list of requirements. For each: the case, datum, or consideration that makes it a requirement; whether it is non-negotiable or merely a desideratum favored by one approach; and, where relevant, what exactly the requirement rules out.

2. The requirements should address at minimum:
   - The dissociation of sensory intensity from unpleasantness (pain asymbolia, morphine, frontal lobotomy, and the converse — suffering without nociception, as in some forms of depression or grief). What pattern must a theory predict or at least accommodate across these cases?
   - The relationship between pain's badness and pain's motivational role. Is it a requirement on a theory of badness that it explain why pain normally motivates avoidance, or can that be outsourced to a separate account of motivation?
   - Whether pain's badness is intrinsic to the experience or relational (e.g., a matter of the subject's attitude toward the sensation). What would force a theory to one side or the other?
   - The neuroscience constraint: must a philosophical theory of pain's badness be consistent with the known separation of the lateral (sensory-discriminative) and medial (affective-motivational) pain pathways, or is that an inappropriate demand on conceptual analysis?

3. After the list, flag any pairs of desiderata that appear to be in tension — where satisfying one makes satisfying the other harder or impossible. If you think the tension is only apparent, explain why.

4. Identify the single requirement that does the most to narrow the field — the one that, once you take it seriously, eliminates the largest number of otherwise plausible theories.

5. If there is any commonly cited desideratum that you think is illegitimate — something the literature treats as a requirement but that a theory shouldn't have to meet — name it and make the case against it.

I don't need a full scorecard of every theory, but if a particular desideratum is especially awkward for a major view (e.g., pure perceptualism, evaluativism, imperativism, or attitudinal theories), note it briefly.
