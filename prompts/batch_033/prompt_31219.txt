I am a journalist working on a piece about how thinkers have tried to separate lying from merely saying something false. I am reading Augustine's On Lying (De Mendacio), and a passage in section 3 is giving me trouble:

"Wherefore, that man lies, who has one thing in his mind and utters another in words, or by signs of whatever kind. Whence also the heart of him who lies is said to be double; that is, there is a double thought: the one, of that thing which he either knows or thinks to be true and does not produce; the other, of that thing which he produces instead thereof, knowing or thinking it to be false. Whence it comes to pass, that he may say a false thing and yet not lie, if he thinks it to be so as he says although it be not so; and, that he may say a true thing, and yet lie, if he thinks it to be false and utters it for true, although in reality it be so as he utters it. For from the sense of his own mind, not from the verity or falsity of the things themselves, is he to be judged to lie or not to lie."

I can follow the basic point that someone who states a falsehood while believing it is not lying. But I want to be sure I understand the conceptual machinery underneath it. Specifically: why does Augustine pause to distinguish believing from opining before offering his definition, and what does that distinction buy him here? What exactly is the "double thought" attributed to the liar's heart — a contradiction between two entertained contents, a mismatch between mind and utterance, or an intention to deceive? And how much weight should I put on the closing claim that lying is judged "from the sense of his own mind, not from the verity or falsity of the things themselves"? Does that make lying entirely a matter of the speaker's mental state, and what awkward cases follow from that — for instance, self-deception or speaking without attending to what one says?
