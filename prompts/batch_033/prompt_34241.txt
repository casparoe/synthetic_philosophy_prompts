Quick recap of the problem in case you haven't seen it: A driver's on a motorway with two exits that look identical. Exit at the first one: payoff 0. Go past the first and exit at the second: payoff 4. Go past both: payoff 1 (he ends up at a hotel or something). The driver's absent-minded — when he reaches an exit, he can't tell which one it is and can't remember whether he's already passed one. So whatever he decides to do, it's got to be the same rule at both exits: exit with probability p, continue with probability 1-p.

Here's the argument that's bothering me:

(1) Before setting off, the optimal plan is obviously: don't exit at the first, exit at the second. That gets you 4.

(2) Rationality says at each choice point you should maximise expected utility given your beliefs.

(3) At an exit, if you've adopted the plan from (1), you should believe you're equally likely to be at the first or second exit — the plan has you passing through both, so conditional on being at an exit, it's 50-50.

(4) So at an exit: exiting gives (0+4)/2 = 2. Continuing gives — if this is the first exit, you go on to the second and (by the plan) exit there for 4; if this is the second exit, continuing gives 1. So continuing gives (4+1)/2 = 2.5. Continuing beats exiting.

(5) So at whichever exit you're at, you continue. But then you continue at both, get payoff 1, and the plan from (1) never gets executed.

(6) The plan from (1) is unimplementable: you can't follow it, because at each exit the calculation in (4) tells you to continue, and at the second exit you don't know it's the second, so you continue again.

(7) So the only feasible strategies are of the form "exit with probability p", and the best one has p = 1/3, yielding 4/3 — strictly worse than the 4 you'd get if you could somehow commit to the plan.

(8) But that means rational choice theory tells you to aim for 4 while simultaneously making it impossible to get 4 — the theory's own prescriptions stop you from following its own optimal plan.

That can't be right, surely? I've got a feeling the slide happens somewhere around (3) or (4) — something about whether you can legitimately assign equal probability to being at each exit when you know you're following a plan that you also know you'll abandon once you start reasoning at the action stage.
