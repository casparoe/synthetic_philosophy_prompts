I have been trying to work out what I actually think about reasons for attitudes — reasons to believe, admire, fear, hope, and so on — and I would like to know what principle, if any, captures my judgements across a set of cases.

First, admiration. Suppose someone powerful threatens to harm me unless I admire him. I am inclined to say that I now have a reason to admire him, and even that it would be sensible to try to bring myself to admire him. But I also want to say that the threat does nothing to make him admirable, and that admiration produced only by the threat would be off-target: it would not be getting anything right. By contrast, if someone has been genuinely kind to me, that fact is both a reason to admire them and something that makes them admirable, and admiring them for it would be fitting. Second, belief. Evidence that it will rain is a reason to believe it will rain, and a belief formed on that basis can be correct. But suppose someone offers me a large sum to believe it will rain, and I manage it. I want to say that the money is a reason of some sort to believe — at least, there is a sense in which I ought to try to bring the belief about — yet the money does not make the belief correct, or true, or even likely, and a belief held only for the money is defective in a way that an evidence-based belief is not. Third, fear. Learning that an escaped bear is nearby is a reason to be afraid and also makes the fear fitting; being offered a reward to be afraid gives me a reason of some sort to be afraid without making the fear fitting.

My tentative principle is that there are two kinds of consideration: those that bear on whether the attitude is correct or fitting — whether the person really is admirable, the proposition really is likely, the situation really is dangerous — and those that bear only on whether it would be good, prudent, or otherwise advantageous to be in the state of mind, independently of whether the state fits its object. But I cannot make this precise, and I can already feel it straining: I am not sure whether the money is really a reason to believe, or only a reason to take steps that cause belief; I am not sure whether the second kind can be spelt out without smuggling in the first; and I am not sure it can reach cases where the state of mind matters for someone else's sake rather than my own.

What I would like is this: state the principle as precisely as you can, say which of my verdicts it forces me to revise and which it saves, and then apply it to the case I keep going back and forth on. A friend who is dying asks me whether I believe she will recover. I know the evidence says she will not, but believing that she will recover — and being able to say so and mean it — would make her last weeks markedly better. Is that a consideration of the first kind, the second kind, or neither? Please distinguish between what the principle clearly entails, what it leaves open, and where you are leaning on a contested view rather than something my cases force. And if you think my framing is confused from the start — for example, that the money simply is not a reason to believe, or that my two kinds collapse into one another — say so plainly rather than trying to force a principle onto the cases.
