I've been teaching Descartes in an intro course and I've found myself defending a pretty deflationary reading of the cogito. Here's the position: the cogito establishes, at most, that there is thinking occuring right now — not that there's a thinker, not that anything persists, not that there's a self or a subject in any robust sense. The "I" in "I think" is grammatically required but doesn't pick out anything beyond the thinking itself. (This is basically the Lichtenberg objection — "it thinks" rather than "I think" — but I want to go further and say even the "it" is doing too much work; all we really get is "thinking is happening.")

I want you to play the role of a professional philosopher who specializes in early modern philosophy and the philosophy of mind, and who takes this position seriously but isn't committed to it. Your job: tell me what would count against this view. What arguments, thought experiments, or considerations should move me off this deflationary reading? I want you to distinguish between:

- Things that would be fatal — if true, the position just collapses
- Things that would be awkward but survivable — the position can handle them but it gets strained
- Things that critics often bring up but that a deflationary reader can legitimately dismiss

Also: am I insulated? Have I qualified the position so much that nothing could really touch it? And if so, is that a problem for a view of this kind, or is it fine because the burden of proof is on the person who claims the cogito proves more?

Think carefully — maybe start by brainstorming the strongest objections before ranking them.
