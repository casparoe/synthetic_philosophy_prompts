I'm a software engineer with no formal background in physics or philosophy, trying to understand a specific conceptual knot in the many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics. I get the basic picture: the wavefunction evolves unitarily, decoherence produces branching, and all outcomes are realized. What I'm stuck on is the Born rule problem, and specifically the relationship between two ways people frame it.

I've seen the problem described as having two layers. First, an "incoherence" layer: if every outcome happens, what could probability possibly mean? Second, a "quantitative" layer: even if you can make sense of probability, why should it be |amplitude|² rather than, say, the raw number of branches or some other function of the amplitude?

I then encountered the idea of "self-locating uncertainty" — the thought that before a measurement, I should be uncertain about which branch I'll find myself in, the way I'd be uncertain about which room I'd wake up in after being copied. Some people seem to think this dissolves the incoherence problem entirely: probability just is self-locating uncertainty, and there's nothing more mysterious about it in many-worlds than in any scenario involving duplication.

But I've also seen others insist that self-locating uncertainty doesn't actually solve the incoherence problem — that it either presupposes something like a preferred basis that many-worlds hasn't earned, or that it conflates two different kinds of uncertainty, or that it's question-begging in some way I can't quite pin down.

Can you walk me through this debate? Specifically: (1) What exactly is the argument that self-locating uncertainty dissolves the incoherence problem? (2) What are the strongest objections to that argument? (3) Does resolving the incoherence problem in this way constrain the answer to the quantitative problem, or are they genuinely independent? I'm less interested in a survey of positions and more interested in understanding the conceptual architecture — where exactly the disagreements are located and what turns on them.
