I'm a high school student, and I keep noticing a pattern in arguments about shared resources that I can't find a name for. Here is an example: during a drought, my town debated water restrictions. Someone would argue that they should still be allowed to water their lawn because "my one lawn uses a trivial amount compared to the farms upstream — I'm not the problem." On its own, that claim is true: any single lawn is negligible. But if every homeowner says this, the residential total is substantial. I have noticed the same structure in arguments about voting ("my one vote doesn't decide the election"), carbon emissions, and overfishing — each person points out, correctly, that their individual contribution is too small to matter, and uses that to justify opting out of the restraint that would be needed if everyone opted in.

I know about the tragedy of the commons, but that names the outcome, not this specific argumentative move. It also seems different from the free-rider problem: the free-rider is exploiting others' restraint, whereas here the person is not necessarily counting on others to pick up the slack — they are simply pointing to the causal negligibility of their own action. And it is not quite the fallacy of composition, because they are not saying "since each part is small, the whole is small"; they are saying something more like "since my part is small, my participation does not affect the whole." Is there an established term for this pattern of reasoning, and if not, what would you call it, and how exactly does it differ from the nearest concepts?
