For a workshop on the foundations of game theory and AI alignment, two researchers who have been arguing for years — one who holds that program equilibrium dissolves the apparent conflict between individual rationality and mutual cooperation in one-shot games, and one who holds that program equilibrium merely relocates the problem without solving it — have agreed to produce an adversarial collaboration document. The first researcher (call them the optimist) argues that when players choose programs rather than actions, and each program has access to the other's source code, cooperative equilibria become available even in the one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma: each player can submit a program that cooperates if and only if the other program is syntactically identical to itself, or that cooperates conditional on a proof that the other program cooperates, and mutual cooperation becomes an equilibrium. The second researcher (call them the skeptic) argues that this merely pushes the difficulty into the choice of which program to submit from an infinite space, that the cooperative equilibria are fragile under realistic constraints — approximate rather than exact source-code access, bounded rationality, the possibility of self-modification — and that the normative force of the cooperative equilibria depends on question-begging assumptions about how programs are individuated and what counts as a valid proof.

Draft the joint statement these two researchers would produce after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. The document should contain: (1) a numbered list of claims both parties accept, including claims about the formal structure of program equilibrium, about what the standard game-theoretic analysis of the one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma does and does not show, about the relationship between program equilibrium and the Folk Theorem for repeated games, and about what a satisfactory resolution to the problem of cooperation would require; (2) a disagreements table where each row states a precise proposition over which they divide, phrased neutrally, with the optimist's position and the skeptic's position each stated in wording their own side would accept; (3) a list of cruxes — sub-questions such that settling them would cause at least one side to revise its view — with, for each crux, a description of what kind of theoretical result, simulation, or argument both would accept as bearing on it, and each side's prediction about what such an investigation would yield. Pay special attention to whether any part of the dispute turns on different notions of what it means for an equilibrium to be "available" or "selected": if so, state explicitly what each side would need to concede for the verbal component to dissolve. The final line of your response should be a one-sentence summary of the residual substantive disagreement, enclosed as \answer{...}.
