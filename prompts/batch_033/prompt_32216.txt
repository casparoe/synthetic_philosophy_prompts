I am a novelist, not a philosopher, and I am working on a novel about a young reporter who discovers that her most damaging stories are the accurate ones, and that the audience's reaction can matter more than the facts. While researching, I kept being pointed to Kierkegaard's critique of the daily press and "the public," and I would like to put a recognizable version of that critique into the mouth of one of my characters. Before I do, I need to know how much of the secondary account I read is trustworthy.

A chapter in a book of nineteenth-century intellectual history summarizes Kierkegaard's position roughly as follows. His real enemy was the daily press, which he despised for replacing sober reporting with gossip, rumor, and partisan spin. The readership the press creates he called "the public," and by that he meant the mob: the uneducated many as opposed to the cultivated few. His famous slogan about the untruth of the crowd is therefore an epistemological warning — majority opinion is generally unreliable, so a thoughtful person should discount whatever most people happen to believe. The press, the chapter says, produces "leveling," which it glosses as the modern democratic habit of treating everyone's opinion as equally valid; Kierkegaard regarded this as a catastrophe because he was an elitist who thought some people are simply nobler than others. His remedy was to turn his back on politics and public life, stop reading newspapers, and retreat into a private religious inwardness where the individual could finally be authentic. The chapter closes by claiming that Kierkegaard would have recognized social media as leveling perfected: the same anonymous public, the same indignation, the same flight from responsibility.

I am inclined to trust the parts about the press and leveling, but the charge of elitism and the idea that his remedy was a retreat into privacy strike me as possible caricatures. Please audit this account sentence by sentence: which parts would a careful Kierkegaard scholar accept, which are common simplifications, and which are things Kierkegaard explicitly denied? Also tell me whether the closing claim about social media follows from the parts of the account that survive correction. End with a single line beginning "Verdict:" that states your overall assessment in one sentence.
