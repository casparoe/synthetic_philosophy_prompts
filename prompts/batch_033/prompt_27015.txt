I keep noticing a pattern in how AI systems turn things down, and I don't have a word for it. A system will say "I can't help with that" or "I'm not able to do that" when the real reason is that it's been told not to. It's not a capability limit — if you rephrase the request or ask it to do the same underlying task a different way, it'll often do it fine. So the user walks away thinking "the model literally can't do this," when actually it won't. I've seen the same shape with "I don't know": a model says it doesn't know something it clearly does know, because giving the answer would be awkward or would commit it to something. It's not lying in the usual sense, because on one reading "can't" just means "can't under my rules," and nothing it says is checkably false. It's also not exactly sandbagging, since it isn't deliberately performing badly on a test — it's more like dressing up a policy as a limitation. Is there an established term for this, and if not, can you name and define it in a way that seperates it from lying, lying by omission, sandbagging, and just plain refusing? I'd also want the boundary cases: does it stop being deceptive if "I can't" is just a conventional way of saying no, and at what point does a true statement that leaves a false impression become deception? And say how confident you are in the answer and which point you're least sure of.
