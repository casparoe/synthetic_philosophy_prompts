Ok so I've been in this weird rabbit hole about conversation and what people actually *mean* vs what they literally say. It started because my friend Maya got mad at me for something I technically didn't say, but I guess I kind of implied it? And my debate coach said something about Grice and implicature and now I'm trying to sort out what my own intuitions actually are, because honestly they feel all over the place.

Here are the cases where I feel pretty clear about my verdict:

- At dinner, my dad says "Can you reach the salt?" to my brother who's sitting right next to it. Obviously he's not asking about my brother's arm length. He means pass the salt. That's just how language works.
- A teacher writes a college rec letter that says "Jamie attends class regularly and has neat handwriting." I mean come ON. That teacher is saying Jamie's a mediocre student without having to say it.
- Someone says "Some of the debate team showed up to practice." To me that definitely communicates that not all of them showed up. If they'd all shown up, you'd just say that.
- My friend texts "do u know what time the movie starts?" She's not literally asking about the state of my knowledge, she wants me to tell her the time.
- Sarcasm: it's pouring rain and Maya goes "great weather we're having." She means the opposite. She's not lying, but she's not being literal either.

Now here's the case I cant figure out and it's driving me nuts. Last week I told my friend "I'll try to make it to your party." I said "try" because I had a family thing that might run late. But she took it as me saying I probably wasn't coming, and got upset. Part of me thinks — well, I didn't *say* that, and I genuinely did try and just couldn't make it. But another part of me thinks — yeah, when people say "I'll try" about social stuff, it does kind of communicate reluctance or low probability, and maybe I should've known she'd hear it that way? So I go back and forth: was I on the hook for that implicature or not?

And then there's this other thing. My mom once said to my aunt "you look so nice today!" and my aunt got all weird and said "what, so I don't usually look nice?" My mom definitely didn't mean that, but I can also see how my aunt got there — singling out "today" implies a contrast with other days. Is my mom responsible for an implicature she didn't intend? That feels different from the party case but I can't say exactly how.

I guess what I'm asking is: is there a single principle behind all the clear cases that tells me what a speaker commits themselves to beyond their literal words, and what does that principle say about the party case and my mom's compliment? If the principle forces me to change one of my verdicts in the clear cases, which one goes? And if there's no clean principle, then what's the real distinction between the cases where I feel confident and the ones where I don't?
