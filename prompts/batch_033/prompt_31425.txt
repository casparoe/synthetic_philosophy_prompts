So I've been trying to write music lately — nothing serious, just messing around with a DAW — and I keep running into this wall where I can make something that sounds fine but it's just... a copy of something else with the serial numbers filed off. And it got me thinking about what creativity actually is, at the mechanism level.

I picked up Hofstadter's stuff on this, the idea that creativity is basically having a conceptual skeleton — a "theme" — and then finding the right knobs to twiddle to produce variations that are different enough to be interesting but not so different that they lose the thread. The creative move is knowing which knobs matter and how far you can turn them before the thing collapses. And I find this really compelling as a starting point, but I also feel like it's maybe too neat. Like it describes what happens after you already have the theme, but it doesn't tell you where themes come from, or why some knob-twiddles feel revelatory and others feel like a bored student turning in a homework assignment.

Before I go down a rabbit hole reading every theory of creativity from cognitive science, philosophy of art, and whoever else has a take, I want the desiderata. What does a theory of creativity actually have to get right? I mean the full list: the phenomena it has to explain, the cases it has to handle, the distinctions it can't collapse. Not just "it should account for novelty and value," which is what every textbook says — I want the hard cases. The five-year-old who invents a game. The jazz solo that's technically just notes from the right scale but somehow says something. The mathematician who wakes up with a proof. The fact that most creative people go through long uncreative periods. The way a genre produces a run of masterpieces and then goes stale. The difference between a variation that deepens a theme and one that just permutes it.

And I want to know which of these desiderata are non-negotiable — the ones that any theory has to nail — and which are preferences of particular camps. And if some subset of them can't be jointly satisfied, that's the part I most want to see. Like, is there a tension between saying creativity is search in a pre-existing space and saying it involves genuinely breaking the space open? Does Hofstadter's model just sidestep that by focusing on the twiddling and not on where the space came from?

I'm not asking for a scorecard of every theory against the list — I'll do that myself once I got the list. But if there's one desideratum that knocks out the most popular theories, tell me which one and why.
