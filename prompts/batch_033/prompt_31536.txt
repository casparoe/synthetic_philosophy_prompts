I hold the following view about the color phi phenomenon. Two brief lights are flashed in close succession at two locations, the first red and the second green; under suitable spatial and temporal conditions, subjects report not two separate flashes but a single spot that appears to move from the first location to the second and to change from red to green roughly halfway along its path. The disturbing detail is that the reported color change occurs before the green light has actually been presented, so information from the later stimulus must shape the earlier part of the reported sequence. The standard contrast describes two ways this could happen. On Orwellian revision, the subject first has a veridical experience of two distinct flashes, and the moving, color-changing spot is generated afterward by a retrospective editing of memory. On Stalinesque revision, the brain withholds the relevant experience until the second light has arrived, so that the subject actually experiences, from the start, an already constructed moving spot that changes color midway.

My position is that this contrast marks no empirical difference: the two stories are alternative descriptions of the same data, and no possible evidence could force a choice between them. What would count against that? I want a map of the defeaters, sorted from fatal to merely awkward: experimental paradigms that could separate the two accounts, conceptual arguments showing that they make different predictions after all, and commitments from elsewhere in the theory of consciousness that would settle the matter. Include what a defender of my view would themselves concede counts against it, and which alleged defeaters they would dismiss as changing the subject. Then give an honest assessment of whether the view has been insulated into unfalsifiability, and whether, for a view of this kind, that is a problem.
