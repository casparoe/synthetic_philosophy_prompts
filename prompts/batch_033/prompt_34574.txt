I've been thinking about self-deception and trying to work out what I actually believe by testing my reactions to cases. Below are my verdicts on several scenarios. I'd like to know what principle or theory of self-deception best captures the pattern across them, which of my verdicts it forces me to revise, and what it predicts for the cases I'm still uncertain about.

Case 1. A woman's husband has been coming home late, smelling of unfamiliar perfume, and taking phone calls in another room. A friend tells her she saw him at a restaurant with another woman. The wife dismisses this: the friend must have mistaken someone else for him. She does not secretly think he's unfaithful while telling herself otherwise; she sincerely believes he's faithful. Verdict: this is self-deception, and it's clearly possible — people do this routinely.

Case 2. A graduate student has failed to start writing for every deadline across three years. Each evening he tells himself he'll begin in the morning, and in that moment he genuinely believes it. Morning comes, he doesn't start, and the cycle repeats. He is not surprised by his failure, exactly, but neither does he acknowledge to himself that he won't start. Verdict: this is not self-deception. It's weakness of will, or perhaps a failure of self-knowledge, but it's a different kind of thing.

Case 3. A man is passed over for a promotion in favor of a younger colleague. He insists he's not resentful — just concerned about the department's direction. But he's been curt with everyone, his wife notices he clenches his jaw when the colleague's name comes up, and he's started keeping a mental list of the colleague's minor mistakes. He isn't lying when he says he's not resentful; he genuinely doesn't find resentment when he introspects. Verdict: this is self-deception, and it's importantly different from Case 1. In Case 1 the evidence the person is avoiding is external; here the evidence is his own behavior and physiological responses, which are in some sense his own states.

Case 4. A man smokes a pack a day. His father died of lung cancer at sixty-one. He tells people, and sincerely believes, that he won't get cancer because he exercises, eats well, and has "good genes" — his father's case was different, he says. He can recite the statistics when asked. Verdict: I think this is self-deception, but I'm less confident than in Case 1 or 3, and I can't articulate what separates it from ordinary optimistic denial.

Case 5. A researcher has spent fifteen years developing a theory. A new paper presents evidence that, if sound, would refute a central claim of the theory. She reads the abstract, immediately identifies what she takes to be a fatal methodological flaw, and dismisses the paper without reading further. She genuinely believes the paper is wrong. A trusted colleague later shows her that the supposed flaw rests on a misreading and the methodology is actually fine. The researcher had not registered the key details when she formed her judgment. Verdict: this feels like self-deception, but I'm unsure. She didn't first grasp the truth and then hide it from herself; she formed a belief too hastily, on inadequate grounds, because of what she wanted to be true. If that's self-deception, it seems to work differently from Case 1.

Case 6. A man is diagnosed with stage IV pancreatic cancer. His doctors tell him clearly that the five-year survival rate is roughly three percent. He tells his family he's going to beat it and makes detailed plans for a vacation next summer and a career change the year after. He refuses to discuss end-of-life arrangements. He seems to genuinely believe he'll recover. Verdict: I keep going back and forth. Part of me thinks this is self-deception, structurally similar to Case 1. Another part thinks it's something else entirely — hope, or a coping mechanism we shouldn't pathologize by calling it self-deception.

What I'm looking for: Is there a single account of self-deception that makes sense of these verdicts — that explains why Cases 1 and 3 are self-deception, why Case 2 is not, and what to say about the borderline cases? Does the account require that the person literally knows the truth at some level? If so, how does that work in Case 1, where I said the wife sincerely believes her husband is faithful? And if the account doesn't require knowledge of the truth, what distinguishes self-deception from an ordinary false belief that happens to be convenient?
