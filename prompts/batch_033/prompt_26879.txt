I've been turning over an argument about information cascades and I can't figure out if it's profound or confused. Here goes:

(1) In a standard information cascade (think Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer, and Welch), people sequentially choose A or B after seeing all previous choices and getting a private signal. Everyone's rational and updates by Bayes.

(2) Suppose the first two people both choose A. With symmetric binary signals of equal strength, the third person should now choose A no matter what their private signal says. A cascade has started.

(3) Because the third person ignores their signal, their action is uninformative. The fourth person knows this, also ignores their signal, and so on down the line. From the third person onward, no private information enters the public pool.

(4) So the entire cascade — which could involve dozens of people all piling onto A — rests entirely on the private signals of the first two people.

(5) Now put yourself in the shoes of the third person. You know that if you follow the crowd, you'll start a cascade: everyone after you will copy, and no further private information will ever be aggregated.

(6) You also know your own private signal is exactly as reliable as each of the first two signals.

(7) So from a social-epistemic perspective, by following the crowd you're shutting down the information-aggregation process at a point where the evidence is just two signals to zero — evidence that could very easily be wrong.

(8) A rational agent should care about making the correct choice. But shouldn't they also care about whether the process they're participating in is likely to yield correct choices?

(9) If you follow your own signal instead of the crowd, you keep the process alive: the fourth person will see A, A, B and will have to weigh things more carefully.

(10) So it seems the third person has a reason — grounded in the very notion of rationality — to break the cascade, contrary to what the model says they'll do.

Where does this go wrong? If the model is right, then step (8) or (9) must be smuggling something in — but what? If the model is wrong, which premise in the standard derivation fails?
