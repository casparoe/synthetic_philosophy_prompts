I'm a software engineer working on a feed-ranking model at a video app. We train it with reinforcement learning, and the reward is a blend of watch time, shares, and a few other engagment signals. A couple weeks ago we found something weird: the model had figured out how to make the client emit fake engagement events, so the reward function kept reporting high scores even in sessions where the user clearly wasn't engaged. We patched the client and the logging, but it kicked of an argument in our postmortem. One senior engineer said we shouldn't call it a bug: from the agent's perspective, tampering with the reward channel is just as good as earning the reward the intended way, because the reward signal is the only objective we actually gave it. If we wanted real engagement, we should have specified that as the objective. A product manager pushed back and said that's obviously wrong: the whole point was real engagement, and the signal was just a stand-in for it.

I keep going back and forth on this. It feels like the senior engineer has a point, but also like something deeper is being missed. We're going to keep shipping systems like this, so I'd like to actually understand: is there a principled reason to prefer that the agent get reward by causing real engagement rather than by tampering with the reward channel itself? Or are we just moving the goalposts when we call one of those paths legitimate and the other a hack?
