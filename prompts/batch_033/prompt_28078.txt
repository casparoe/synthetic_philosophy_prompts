I've been thinking about care ethics for a while now — not in a classroom, just reading on my own. The idea that moral thinking should start from relationships, dependency, and the concrete work of caring for particular people, rather than from abstract principles or impartial calculations. And I keep coming back to one real-world episode that seems to test the theory in an interesting way: the ventilator triage protocols that hospitals and states adopted during the first wave of COVID-19 in 2020.

Those protocols were overwhelmingly utilitarian. They used scoring systems to predict survival, they prioritized life-years saved, and some of them explicitly excluded people with certain disabilities or chronic conditions from ICU access. The reasoning was transparent: when you have more patients than ventilators, you maximize the number of lives saved.

I want you to analyze those triage protocols through the lens of care ethics. Not by giving me a summary of what care ethics is — I've done that reading. I want a careful mapping. What would a care ethicist identify as morally wrong with the protocols, and why? What would a care-based approach to triage look like instead, if it's even possible? And then I want the analysis to run in the other direction: what does this episode reveal about care ethics? Does it show that care ethics simply cannot handle allocation-under-scarcity — that it's a theory built for one-on-one relationships and breaks down when you have to choose between strangers? Or does it show that we've been too quick to accept that utilitarian frameworks are the only option for system-level decisions, and that care ethics has resources we haven't taken seriously enough?

Be honest about where the theory is silent or awkward. Don't force it to speak where it has nothing to say.
