You are a professional philosopher specializing in epistemology and the norms of assertion. Please think carefully and work through the following problem step by step, treating it as an exercise in reflective equilibrium.

I have been trying to work out what I actually think about when an assertion is proper or improper, and I have collected my verdicts on a range of cases. I would like you to help me find a principle — or a small set of candidate principles — that best captures these verdicts, and to tell me honestly which of my verdicts the principle forces me to give up, if any.

Here are the cases:

1. **The lottery.** I hold one ticket in a fair lottery with five million tickets. Before the drawing, I say to a friend, flat-out, "My ticket isn't going to win." Verdict: improper. Even though it is overwhelmingly likely to be true, I should not flat-out assert it.

2. **The stopped clock.** I walk into a room and glance at the clock on the wall, which reads 11:15. Unbeknownst to me, the clock stopped exactly twelve hours ago. By sheer coincidence, it really is 11:15. I turn to a colleague and say, "It's 11:15." Verdict: improper. The fact that I happened to be right does not rescue the assertion.

3. **The framed suspect.** A detective has overwhelming evidence that Smith committed the arson: fingerprints, a clear motive, multiple eyewitnesses, the works. But the evidence was planted by the real arsonist, and Smith is entirely innocent. The detective, acting in good faith on the evidence, tells the press, "Smith is the arsonist." Verdict: improper. The detective had every epistemic reason to believe it, but the assertion is still wrong.

4. **The nervous witness.** I clearly saw the defendant at the scene of the crime and I know he was there. On the stand, under aggressive cross-examination, I momentarily panic and lose confidence — for a few seconds I genuinely doubt my own memory. Then I pull myself together and assert, "He was there," even though a trace of doubt lingers. Verdict: proper. Or at the very least, far less objectionable than the stopped-clock case.

5. **Moore's paradox.** At a party, someone asserts, "It's raining, but I don't believe it is." It is in fact raining, and the speaker knows perfectly well that it is. Verdict: improper. The defect feels different in kind from the lottery case — it is not that the speaker lacks knowledge, since he knows it is raining. Something about the form of the assertion itself is defective.

6. **The careful doctor.** A physician tells a patient, "Your test results are negative," based on having reviewed the lab report thoroughly. The lab report is accurate, the test results are indeed negative, and the doctor has no reason to doubt them. Verdict: perfectly proper.

7. **The lucky guesser.** A student who has not studied for an exam takes a wild guess and writes in the margin, "The answer to question seven is 42." The answer is in fact 42, but the student had no basis for this. Verdict: improper — but is it improper in the same way as the lottery case, or is something different going on?

Here is what puzzles me. Cases 1, 2, and 3 all feel improper, but for what seem like different reasons. In case 1, I have a true belief based on overwhelming probabilistic evidence, but I do not know it. In case 2, I have a justified true belief but no knowledge (a Gettier-style case). In case 3, I have a justified belief that happens to be false. Yet I judge all three improper. Meanwhile, case 4 feels proper even though my belief was momentarily shaken — I still knew the fact. Case 5 feels improper even though the speaker both knows and believes the truth. And cases 6 and 7 sit at opposite ends but seem to point in the same direction: the doctor knows, the guesser does not.

I am especially interested in whether a single norm — the knowledge norm, the truth norm, the belief norm, the reasonable-belief norm, or something else — can handle all of these, and if not, what the most principled revision looks like. Please do the following:

- State each candidate principle precisely, with the conditions under which an assertion is epistemically proper according to it.
- Walk through how each principle handles the seven cases, noting which verdicts it preserves and which it overturns.
- Identify any pair (or larger set) of my verdicts that turn out to be jointly unsatisfiable under a single simple principle, and explain the trade-off.
- Give your own assessment of which principle, or which combination or refinement, does the best overall job, and state clearly which of my verdicts it requires me to revise.
- Apply your preferred principle to a case I have not listed: someone asserts a mathematical claim they proved ten years ago and have since completely forgotten the proof of, though they remain confident the claim is true, and it is true. Does your principle count this assertion as proper or improper, and does that strike you as the right result?
