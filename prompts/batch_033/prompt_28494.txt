I've been thinking about something I read online about whether being a "moral saint" — someone who is as morally good as it's possible to be — is actually a desirable ideal for a human life. The argument, as I understood it, is that a moral saint would have to sacrifice so many other kinds of value (aesthetic pursuits, personal relationships, humor, excellence in non-moral domains) that their life would be oddly impoverished, and that we shouldn't want to be like them or have everyone try to be like them.

This made me think of The Giving Tree by Shel Silverstein. The tree gives the boy everything — its apples, its branches, its trunk — until it's nothing but a stump, and the book presents this as touching and beautiful. But if you read the tree as a moral saint, the story seems to dramatize exactly the worry: the tree's total self-giving leaves it with nothing of its own, and there's something uncomfortable about that, even within the story's own terms.

I'd like a reading of The Giving Tree through the lens of the moral saints debate. Does the book actually endorse the tree as an ideal, or does it register the cost? Are there scenes or details that complicate a simple reading either way? And what does the book add to the philosophical discussion that the arguments alone don't capture — does fiction get at something here that a paper can't? I'm not looking for a final verdict on whether the tree is good or bad; I want to see the tension explored honestly, including places where the book resists the philosophical lens.
