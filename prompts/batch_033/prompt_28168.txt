I've been working through decision theory on my own — not for a course, just a long-running obsession — and I've landed on a view I find hard to shake: that you should pay up in counterfactual mugging. The scenario, for anyone who needs it: a perfectly reliable predictor shows you that it flipped a fair coin. If the coin had come up heads, it would have asked you for $100, and if you had paid, it would have given you $10,000. The coin came up tails. The predictor now asks you for $100 anyway. My view is that you should pay. The reason is that your decision procedure in the actual tails-branch determines what you do in the counterfactual heads-branch, and the expected value of a policy of paying across both branches is positive — you lose $100 in the tails branch but gain $10,000 in the heads branch. The fact that the heads branch didn't obtain doesn't matter, because what you're choosing is a procedure, not an act whose consequences are restricted to the actual world. The same reasoning extends to XOR blackmail and similar cases.

I want to know what would count against this view — and I don't just mean the standard causal decision theorist's reply that you shouldn't pay for outcomes you can't causally influence. I've already decided that causal influence is the wrong criterion. I mean the deeper challenges: the ones that might actually move someone who finds the initial reasoning compelling. What formal results, what edge cases, what modifications to the scenario would put real pressure on the position? For example, what happens when the predictor is imperfect but merely correlated with your decision procedure — does the view then commit you to paying off anyone who claims to have run a counterfactual mugging on you? Are there Dutch book arguments, or arguments from reflectively inconsistent policies, that tell against paying? Does the view require a metaphysics of counterfactuals — a notion of what you would have done in a branch that never obtained — that turns out to be incoherent or at least deeply problematic when examined closely? And has the view been qualified in ways that insulate it from counterexamples — for instance, by restricting the scenarios in which you should pay to those involving perfect predictors — and if so, are those restrictions principled or ad hoc?

I'd like a committed response. Don't just survey the options; take a definite position on which challenges are genuinely threatening and which are merely awkward, and tell me whether on balance the view survives. If you think it doesn't, say what you think the right alternative is and why. I'm not looking for a literature review — I want to know what you would say if you had to defend a conclusion.
