I am a professor of moral philosophy, and I am presently trying to work through a conceptual difficulty that has been troubling me in the literature on promissory obligation. The difficulty concerns what I will call the problem of normative bootstrapping. When I sincerely utter the words "I promise to φ," I seem to bring a new moral reason into existence — a reason that I did not possess before speaking and that is owed specifically to the promisee. But how can a mere speech act generate a moral obligation ex nihilo? Hume's conventionalist answer is that promising is a socially instituted practice: the convention of promise-making assigns to the utterance a certain normative significance, much as the rules of chess assign significance to moving a piece. But this invites a regress: why does the convention itself bind? If we answer that we have tacitly promised to abide by the convention, we beg the question. If we answer that the convention is binding because it serves mutual advantage, we seem to have collapsed promissory obligation into a general utilitarian duty, and in doing so we lose the distinctive directedness of a promise — the sense in which the obligation is owed to you, the promisee, and not merely to the impartial good.

Scanlon's contractualist alternative tries to escape this difficulty by grounding promissory obligation in a broader moral principle — roughly, that one ought not to disappoint expectations one has knowingly led others to form, where those others would be harmed by relying on those expectations. But this move faces a structural circularity of its own. The reason it is wrong to disappoint the expectation created by a promise seems parasitic on the very obligation the promise is supposed to generate: we think it wrong to disappoint because the promise already bound me, not the other way around. The expectation-based account appears to put the normative cart before the horse.

I would like you to adopt the role of an expert moral philosopher and think through this problem carefully. Begin by reconstructing the circularity worry in your own terms, making clear why it is structurally so difficult to escape. Then evaluate whether any major account of promising — conventionalist, expectation-based, or otherwise — has the resources to break the circle without losing what is distinctive about promissory obligation. I am not asking for a literature review, citations, or bibliography; I want rigorous conceptual analysis. Please keep your response to a maximum of 800 words.
