I've been trying to extract a principle from my own moral intuitions the way I'd extract a specification from a pile of test cases, and I've reached the point where the obvious implementations all fail. I'd like you to help me find the principle — or the small set of candidate principles — that best fits the cases below, and to tell me honestly which of my verdicts any proposed principle has to overturn.

Here are the cases, with my gut verdicts.

1. Sam finds a wallet full of cash and returns it with everything in it, because Sam thinks that is simply what a decent person must do. I judge this morally worthy — full marks.

2. Priya returns a lost wallet because a reward has been posted and she wants the money. The wallet gets back to its owner, but I do not judge Priya's action morally worthy.

3. Lena donates to a famine-relief charity because she has spent time in the affected region and is genuinely distressed by the suffering there. Morally worthy, yes.

4. Marcus donates to the same charity because giving makes him feel good about himself — a kind of warm glow — and he does not think much about the recipients. I am inclined to say not morally worthy, or at most worth very little.

5. Ruth visits a sick friend in the hospital because she is worried about him and wants to comfort him. Morally worthy, yes.

6. Tom visits the same sick friend purely out of a sense of obligation. He would rather not be there and feels no particular warmth, but he believes he must go. I still judge this morally worthy, though not in quite the same way as Ruth's visit.

7. Jordan jumps into a river and saves a drowning child because a news crew is filming and Jordan wants to be seen as a hero. The child is saved, but I do not judge Jordan's action morally worthy.

8. This is the case that breaks my simple theories. Alex, a junior engineer, covers for a teammate's mistake on a client deliverable. Alex has been taught, incorrectly, that covering for a teammate in this situation always violates the company's integrity policy and is therefore wrong. Alex does it anyway, not for any advantage and not to be liked, but because he cannot stand to see the teammate — who has been treated unfairly all quarter — get fired for one error. Alex believed at the time that he was doing something wrong. I still judge that the action had moral worth.

The obvious principle is something like: an action has moral worth if and only if it is done because the agent believes it is the right thing to do. That gets Sam, Tom, Priya, and Jordan right, but it fails Alex outright, and it also seems wrong about Lena, who is not thinking in terms of rightness at all — she is thinking about the suffering. The other obvious principle is: an action has moral worth if and only if it is done for the reasons that actually make the action right. That handles Lena and Alex, but I am not sure it handles Tom, and it seems to require a settled view about what makes actions right, which I do not have.

So my questions are these. Is there a principle — stated precisely, not just gestured at — that captures all or nearly all of these verdicts? If not, which verdict should I give up, and why that one rather than the others? And what would the best candidate principle say about a few cases I have not settled: a nurse who works overtime caring for patients because she promised her late mother she would, without any particular feeling for the patients; a manager who refuses to fudge an audit because he is afraid of going to jail; and a person who returns a lost item because they cannot bear the thought of the owner's distress, without ever framing the act as a matter of rightness or duty?
