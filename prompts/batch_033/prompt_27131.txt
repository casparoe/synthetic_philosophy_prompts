I'm assembling a small anthology of speculative fiction that treats a philosophical problem as the engine of the story rather than as decoration, and I'd like you to contribute one piece. The problem is the one sometimes dramatized as the ant colony that might have a mind: hundreds of thousands of ants, each following a small set of local chemical and tactile rules, together produce colony-level behavior — nest selection, foraging, defense, even something that looks like memory and decision — while no individual ant possesses anything like an overview of what the colony is doing. The question is whether the colony itself could be a genuine subject of experience or thought, over and above the ants that compose it, and what could possibly settle that question from the outside.

Set the story at a remote field station where a myrmecologist and a philosopher of mind have been arguing about exactly this for months. The myrmecologist has spent years studying a single enormous colony and has begun to suspect that its responses to her manipulations are not merely adaptive but anticipatory: the colony seems to model its environment in a way no individual ant does. The philosopher keeps insisting that whatever the colony does, it is only ants following local rules, and that anyone who claims there is something it is like to be the colony bears the burden of proof. Write the story around the moment one of them is forced to revise their view — not necessarily through a single dramatic event; it could be a slow accumulation of small observations, a failed experiment, or a conversation that makes the philosophical issue vivid. The story should make a reader feel the force of both positions, and its plot should turn on the conceptual crux: what would count as evidence that a distributed system has a mind, and what would count as evidence that we are merely projecting one onto it.
