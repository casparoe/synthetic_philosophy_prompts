I'm a novelist — I've published three books, I teach fiction workshops, and I spend most of my waking hours thinking about dialogue, point of view, and what happens between characters in a scene. Recently I've been reading around in philosophy of language, and I keep coming across speech act theory: Austin's distinction between locutionary, illocutionary, and perlocutionary acts; Searle's taxonomy of illocutionary forces; the idea that certain utterances don't describe the world but change it — performatives, felicity conditions, uptake. What strikes me is that novelists already have a working vocabulary for many of these phenomena, though we don't use those terms. We talk about dialogue as action, about what a line of speech does to the power balance in a scene, about promises made and broken, about the difference between what a character says and what they're doing by saying it. We have concepts like subtext, narrative distance, free indirect discourse, interiority, scene versus summary, showing versus telling — all of which seem to be getting at something in the neighborhood of what the philosophers are after.

I'd like you to do a systematic translation. Restate the core claims of speech act theory — Austin and Searle, primarily — in the vocabulary a novelist would use: the craft terms we reach for when we're revising a scene or diagnosing why a stretch of dialogue isn't working. Go concept by concept. But the real deliverable is the residue report: which distinctions translate cleanly into the novelist's vocabulary, which ones you had to distort to make them fit, and which ones have no expression at all in how we think about fiction. I want to know whether the untranslatable residue points to something the philosophers are tracking that novelists are blind to — something we'd write better dialogue if we understood — or whether it points to philosophical distinctions that don't earn their keep once you're actually trying to make language do work on the page.
