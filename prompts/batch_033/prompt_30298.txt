I hold the view that Lewis's triviality results decisively block any attempt to treat the probability of an indicative conditional as its conditional probability. Specifically, I take the results to show that no connective → in a language closed under conditioning can satisfy the Equation P(A → B) = P(B | A) for all A, B with P(A) > 0, unless the probability function collapses to near-triviality — at most four values in the 1976 result, two in the stronger versions. The philosophical upshot, as I see it, is that indicative conditionals do not express propositions with classical probabilities, and the project of giving them a straightforward Bayesian semantics is dead.

I want to know what would count against this conclusion. The results are formal, so counting against them means identifying which assumptions a dissenter could reject, and at what cost. Which premises of the proof are genuinely load-bearing? Which could be given up while preserving something recognizable as the original project? I want the candidate defeaters sorted into three tiers: those that would force a major retreat (the conclusion was wrong, or the result doesn't mean what we thought), those that would require significant qualification but leave the core lesson intact, and those that are technical quibbles with no philosophical payoff. I also want to know which escape routes have been developed into live research programs — things people actually publish — rather than remaining mere logical possibilities. Finally, I want an honest assessment of whether the triviality results have been insulated by reinterpretation to the point of unfalsifiability, and if so, whether that is a problem for a formal result of this kind or just the normal way a mathematical limitation theorem settles into a field.
