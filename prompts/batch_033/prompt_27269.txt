I run a reading group on rationality and decision theory — about 12 people, mostly grad students but a few advanced undergrads, one 90-minute session. I want an activity that makes them viscerally feel the weirdness of superrationality, specifically the kind of reasoning Hofstadter sets up in the Platonia Dilemma and the Luring Lottery. You know: twenty people get a letter, if exactly one person claims the prize they get it, if zero or multiple claim nobody gets anything, and everyone knows everyone else is perfectly rational and got the same letter. What do you do?

I want to adapt this into a live game. Here's the setup I'm imagining: each person gets a slip of paper and has to write either "CLAIM" or "PASS." They can't talk, can't signal, can't see what others are writing. The prize is something modest but real — maybe the group gets a pizza budget and if exactly one person claims, that person chooses the toppings for everyone; if multiple claim or nobody claims, we get plain cheese (which nobody particularly wants). Or maybe extra credit points if its a course. Whatever makes the stakes feel real enough to think hard but not so high that people get upset.

I want the payoff structure to have two variants we play back to back: first the Platonia version (exactly one claimant wins, otherwise everyone loses), then the Luring Lottery version (prize is split among all claimants, so claiming is less risky but still dilutes the reward). The debrief should compare what happened in each and draw out the superrationality argument: in the Platonia version, if you think everyone reasons the same way you do, then you should claim because you'd want the one person to be you... but then everyone claims and nobody wins. The Luring Lottery version is supposed to break that deadlock by making it so that even if everyone claims, at least you get something, which paradoxically might make fewer people claim.

Rules, materials, exact payoff numbers, how to run it without chaos, and a debrief script that maps what happened to the formal idea. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I'm especially worried about one person just claiming as a joke and ruining the Platonia round for everyone, or people finding subtle ways to coordinate (writing slowly, hesitating, etc.).

And in your response, tell me what would change your mind about the design: which assumption about how participants will behave is most likely to be wrong, and what result from actually running the game would make you rethink the whole thing.
