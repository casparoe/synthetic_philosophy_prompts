I'm giving a department colloquium next month defending a version of actual intentionalism about textual meaning. My core claim is that what a text means is partly constituted by what its author intended it to mean — intention isn't just causal background or biographical trivia, but plays a constitutive role in determining meaning. I'm not defending the extreme view that intention exhausts meaning or that a failed intention still determines meaning; I'm defending the moderate view that intention is one determinant among several, and that it has a special default status: when we interpret a text, we are fundamentally trying to recover what someone meant by it, and this orients interpretation even when we also appeal to linguistic conventions, context, genre, and the text's internal features. The view pushes back against the anti-intentionalist consensus in literary theory — the line from Wimsatt and Beardsley through Barthes to Foucault — but also against stronger intentionalisms that make authorial intent the sole determinant.

The audience: a joint event between the philosophy department and the comparative literature program. About half the room will be philosophers of language and mind — some Griceans who are broadly sympathetic, some semantic externalists who think meaning is fundamentally public and convention-based. The other half will be literary scholars, most trained in the anti-intentionalist tradition. A few are sympathetic to intentionalist or post-intentionalist work, but they are a minority. There will also be graduate students from both departments.

Last night I spent three hours arguing about this with a close friend who teaches Victorian literature and is a committed Foucauldian. The argument was productive but bruising. She kept pressing on cases where the author's intentions are genuinely inaccessible — lost letters, dead authors, contradictory self-reports — and on cases where a text has taken on cultural meanings the author could not have anticipated, meanings that now seem more important than whatever the author had in mind. She also pushed hard on the power question: whose intentions get privileged, and what does intentionalism do to readings that resist the author's apparent designs? I came away convinced that these are the objections that will surface in the Q&A, and that my replies to them are not ready.

3. **Objections, ordered by damage potential** (eight objections, ~200 words each): For each objection, give me (a) the objection phrased the way a member of this audience would actually put it — in their vocabulary, with their framing; (b) a two-minute reply; and (c) a candid note on where the reply is weakest, what I should concede rather than defend, and whether the objection is likely to come from a philosopher or a literary scholar. Order from most damaging to least.

4. **The question I'm hoping nobody asks** (~150 words): The objection that cuts deepest against my position, phrased as it would actually be asked in this room, with an honest assessment of why I do not have a satisfactory answer yet and what I should not try if it comes up.
