I spent most of last evening arguing with a close friend about consciousness, and the argument ended with him summarizing epiphenomenalism and what he called the paradox of phenomenal judgment in a way that struck me as persuasive but possibly unfair. He put it roughly like this:

"Epiphenomenalism says the mind is just a byproduct of the brain: your conscious feelings are caused by neural activity, but they never themselves cause anything. That is already strange, but the real problem is the paradox of phenomenal judgment. If your feeling of pain does not cause you to say 'I'm in pain,' then your saying it is produced entirely by brain processes that would make you say it even if you felt nothing. So your judgments about your own experience are not connected to that experience in any way that could make them reliable. But epiphenomenalists arrived at their view precisely by introspecting their own experience and reasoning about it. If those introspective judgments are just brain events with no tie to the experiences they seem to report, then the evidence for epiphenomenalism undercuts itself, and the view makes the very judgments that support it untrustworthy. So epiphenomenalism is self-defeating."

I had been inclined to defend epiphenomenalism, or at least to take it seriously, but I found I could not say exactly where his summary went wrong, if it did. Please go through his characterization carefully and tell me which parts an epiphenomenalist would accept, which parts are overstatements or conflations, and what a defender of the view would say in reply. In particular, does his conclusion that epiphenomenalism is self-defeating survive once the accurate parts are separated from the inaccurate ones?
