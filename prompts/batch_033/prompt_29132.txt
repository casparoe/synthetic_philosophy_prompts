I've been trying to work out whether I'm some kind of negative utilitarian, and I keep hitting the same wall. My working principle is this: the only thing that matters, morally, is reducing or preventing suffering; happiness has no independent value, it's just the absence of suffering. But my gut on a bunch of cases keeps slipping out from under it. I want you to test the principle against the cases and repair it.

Here are my verdicts.

1. I'd press a button that gives one person a slightly worse migraine for an hour to prevent a million people from getting a severe migraine. No hesitation.

2. I wouldn't press a button that causes one person chronic, agonizing pain for the rest of their life in order to create a billion people who live blissful lives. The bliss just doesn't feel like it's on the same scale as the agony.

3. I'd press a button that creates a billion blissful lives plus one stubbed toe, over an empty world. But by my own principle the empty world should be at least as good, maybe better, since the stubbed toe is a bit of suffering and all that happiness is supposed to count for nothing. This verdict feels right and I can't shake it.

4. I think it's wrong to bring a child into existence if you know their life will be on the whole miserable, even if it has some good moments. But if someone is already living that miserable life, I don't think we should end it against their will; continuing is permitted. So creation and continuation seem to work differently for me, and I don't know whether that's compatible with the claim that suffering is all that matters.

5. I'd be bothered by a button that makes everyone slightly less joyful but causes no suffering. Actually I can't even describe that case in my principle's terms, because if happiness has no value, losing it should be a matter of indifference. But it don't feel indifferent.

6. I'd press a button that prevents a million people from being tortured by causing one person to be tortured. That feels like a good trade. But then why does causing one person agony to prevent a million tortures feel so different from causing one person agony to create a billion blisses? Is that a line I can actually defend, or am I just fooling myself?

So: take my principle, test it against these, and repair it. I'm open to a threshold view, a person-affecting view, or whatever else fits the data. State the repaired principle precisely, with the case that motivates each clause. Say what it predicts about a case I haven't listed — for instance, whether I should press a button that creates a life with one hour of intense agony and a hundred years of moderate happiness. And tell me honestly which of my verdicts it can't accommodate, so I know what I'd have to give up.
