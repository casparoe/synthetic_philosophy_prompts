So I'm in this philosophy of science reading group, and we've been working through inference to the best explanation — Lipton, Harman, that crowd. One of the members keeps leaning on this analogy to shut down sceptical worries. They say: look, science is basically detective work. You show up at the crime scene (the data), you gather the clues (the observations), you formulate hypotheses about what went down, and you accept the one that best explains the evidence. We don't tie ourselves in knots about whether detective reasoning is "justified" — we convict people with it, for god's sake. So why get fussy about IBE in science? The analogy is supposed to carry the conclusion that IBE doesn't need any deeper metaphysical or probabilistic foundation; it's just what rational creatures do, and it works.

I'm not buying it, but I'm having trouble articulating exactly where the analogy gives out. I'd like you to do a proper audit. Start by laying out the correspondences explicitly — a table is fine — what in science plays the role of the crime scene, the clues, the suspect pool, the motive, the means, the verdict, the standard of proof? Then walk through the disanalogies, and crucially, separate the ones that matter for the justificatory work the analogy is being asked to do from the ones that are just colourful but irrelevant. I've got a few candidate worries rattling around but I don't know how deep they cut. For instance: detectives usually work with a closed or at least heavily constrained suspect pool, whereas the history of science is full of explanations nobody had dreamed up until someone did — the hypothesis space seems open in a way that matters. Also, detective work leans hard on motives and intentions, which map awkwardly onto physics or chemistry. And there's the point about verdicts: a criminal trial demands proof beyond reasonable doubt and then closes the book, whereas in science the best explanation today can be tomorrow's laughing stock — so the epistemology of "accepting" an explanation seems different in kind. But maybe these are all surface-level and the core structure holds? I need help sorting which disanalogies are fatal and which are cosmetic.

Finally, tell me whether the conclusion — that IBE doesn't need deeper justification because detective reasoning doesn't — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if there's a better analogy out there that does the same work without the baggage.
