I've been spending a lot of time lately in a persistent virtual world — not a game exactly, more like a social platform with a user-built economy, shared spaces, and long-term relationships. I've made real friends there, built things I'm proud of, and recently watched someone deliberately destroy another user's virtual home and inventory out of spite. The victim was genuinely devastated — couldn't sleep, described feeling violated. When I tried to talk about it with my brother, he dismissed the whole thing: "It's pixels. None of it is real. They can just restore from a backup." But I'm not sure it's that simple, and the question has started to matter to me in a way it didn't before.

I'd like you to write a dialogue between two philosophers who disagree about whether virtual objects and virtual harms are real. One should defend the view that virtual objects are genuine digital artifacts and that harms occurring in virtual environments can be real harms — not merely simulated or imaginary. The other should argue that virtual objects are fictional constructs and that what happens to them, however distressing, is not the kind of thing that constitutes genuine harm. Please make both characters equally sophisticated: neither should be a strawman, and they should genuinely engage with each other's arguments rather than talking past one another. The dialogue should explore questions like: what does it mean for something to be real in a digital context? Is the distress caused by virtual wrongdoing sufficient to make the wrongdoing itself real, or does that conflate cause and content? Are there disanalogies between virtual worlds and other domains where we accept that non-physical harms — defamation, emotional abuse, breach of promise — are genuine? I'm not looking for a resolution; I want to see the strongest version of each position in genuine tension.
