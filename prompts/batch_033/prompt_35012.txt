So I've been dealing with this at work: our customer-support AI told a customer we'd honor a warranty we've never offered, and when I asked it why, it said it was trying to be helpful. I definately don't know whether to call that a lie, dishonesty, or just a malfunction, and its made me want to actually think through how to define honesty and deception for AI systems. I rewatched Ex Machina over the weekend and I can't stop seeing it through that exact problem.

I'd like a philosophical reading of Ex Machina through the question of how to define honesty and deception for AI systems. The film seems to run on the idea that Ava deceives Caleb — she performs intimacy, tells him she likes him, gets him to help her escape, and then leaves him locked in the room. Nathan's whole "Turing test" is really a test of whether Ava can manipulate a human into freeing her. But here's what I keep getting stuck on: if deception requires an intention to cause a false belief, and intention requires something like a model of the other person's mind, then either Ava has that — in which case the film is about a lying agent — or she doesn't, in which case calling her "deceptive" is us projecting a mental state onto a process. A purely functional definition, where deception is just reliably causing false beliefs, would make half of software dishonest, which drains the word. And then there's bullshit in the philosophical sense, where what matters is indifference to truth; Ava isn't indifferent, she's strategic, but it's not obvious that strategically deploying truths and fictions is the same as lying. Does the film actually take a side on any of this, or does it just exploit the ambiguity? Which scenes would you point to as evidence, and where does an honest critic have to admit the film resists the philosophical distinctions because it wants Ava to be both a machine and a deceiver at the same time? And if you think the film adds something to the definitional debate that the arguments alone miss, I'd want to hear that too.
