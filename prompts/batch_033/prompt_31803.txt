ok so i'm a phd student in cognitive science / philosophy of mind and i'm trying to nail down my views before i write my prospectus. here's roughly where i've ended up and i want to know if it hangs together:

(1) i'm an illusionist about phenomenal consciousness. i think phenomenal properties as usually conceived — private, ineffable, intrinsic, qualitative feels — don't exist. what we have instead are introspective mechanisms that misrepresent us as having such properties. (so i'm in the frankish/carruthers camp, not the "qualia are real and irreducible" camp.)

(2) i'm a physicalist. no dualism, no panpsychism, no mysterianism.

(3) i think there's something it is like to be in pain, to see red, to smell coffee, etc. i don't want to deny the existence of "what it's like" — i just think what it's like is a matter of physical and computational goings-on, not some extra ingredient.

(4) i think the hard problem of consciousness is a genuine problem. i don't think it's a pseudo-problem that dissolves once you stop thinking about consciousness the wrong way. it's asking something real, even if the answer turns out to be physicalist.

(5) i think people's first-person reports about their own experiences are generally reliable. not infallible, obviously, but if almost everyone says pain feels like something, i don't think there all just confused or in the grip of a bad theory.

(6) i think the zombie argument is unsound, but i'm not sure exactly where it goes wrong. i'm not a type-a physicalist who just denies zombies are conceivable.

can you go through these and tell me which pairs are in tension? i'm especially worried about whether (3) and (4) are compatible with (1), or if i'm basically trying to have my cake and eat it. also, what's the cheapest fix — which one would i have to give up, and what does that cost me? and are there any tensions here that are only apparent, that dissolve on closer inspection? don't just list everything as equally problematic — rank them.
