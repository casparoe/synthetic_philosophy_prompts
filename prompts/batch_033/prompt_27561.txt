I have been collecting my own verdicts on a set of exchanges, and I want to know whether one principle lies behind them, or whether some of my verdicts have to go.

Here are the cases as concretely as I can state them.

1. A healthy adult sells one kidney to a stranger through a regulated exchange. The price is $45,000, paid into an account that can be drawn down over several years. There is a six-month cooling-off period, independent medical and psychological screening, and a guarantee of lifelong follow-up care for the seller. Verdict: permissible, though I am not entirely comfortable with it.

2. In the two days before a hurricane is expected to make landfall, a hardware store raises the price of its portable generators from $500 to $2,000. Verdict: not permissible.

3. A citizen accepts $50 from a campaign organization in exchange for a promise to vote for a particular candidate. Verdict: not permissible.

4. A plasma collection center pays donors $40 per donation, and many working-class donors give plasma twice a week. Verdict: permissible.

5. A woman carries a pregnancy to term for intended parents she did not know beforehand, in exchange for $30,000 above her medical and legal expenses, under a contract that gives her final authority over medical decisions during the pregnancy. Verdict: permissible, with some unease.

6. A person pays a line-standing company to have someone wait in line overnight at a passport office and then hand over the place when they arrive. Verdict: permissible, though I dislike the inequality it creates.

7. A patient near the front of a hospital emergency department queue accepts $500 from a wealthier patient to swap places, even though the wealthier patient's condition is less urgent. Verdict: not permissible.

The tentative principle I keep reaching for is something like this: it is wrong to let willingness to pay determine who gets something that a person needs in order to survive, or that is supposed to be allocated by need or by equal civic standing rather than by price. That principle fits cases 2, 3, and 7, and it fits my unease about 6. But it seems to condemn case 1 as well, since a kidney is exactly the sort of good whose allocation I would not want left to price in an unregulated way, yet I still think the regulated sale is permissible. And it does not explain why 4 and 5 are permissible even though both involve payment for the use of the body by people who are mostly poorer than the buyers.

Is there a principle, or a small set of candidates, that captures all or nearly all of these verdicts? If so, state it precisely and say what it predicts about a case I have not listed: a municipality that auctions off the right to skip the queue for a publicly funded childcare place. If no single principle captures them, which of my verdicts should I overturn, and which should I keep? I am less interested in labels like commodification or repugnant markets than in knowing exactly which feature of each exchange is doing the moral work.
