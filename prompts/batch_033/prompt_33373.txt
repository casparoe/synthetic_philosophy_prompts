I serve on the executive committee of a national philosophical association. We are considering a proposal to eliminate the association's "Distinguished Scholar" designation. The designation is purely honorific: it confers no material benefits, carries no voting rights or governance role, and does not affect eligibility for any other association program. It is awarded to roughly the top fifteen percent of the membership on the basis of scholarly achievement, as judged by a committee of past recipients. The award has existed for forty years and many members value it.

The case for elimination is this: the designation creates a visible status hierarchy within the association. Members without it report feeling like second-class participants at conferences and in governance; junior scholars describe the label as intimidating. Moreover, the designation's distribution correlates with demographic patterns familiar across the discipline — recipients are disproportionately white, male, and from research-intensive institutions — even when controlling for scholarly output, likely because citation practices and informal networks favor those groups at nomination time. The proposal's advocates argue that the association should not maintain a status distinction that tracks background advantage rather than merit alone, and that its presence damages the association's culture of mutual respect.

The case against elimination is that doing so helps no one in absolute terms. Non-recipients do not gain the designation themselves; their conference experience does not materially change; their professional opportunities are unaffected. Recipients lose a form of recognition they have earned and value. The association loses a tool for honoring achievement. The only thing that changes is that the gap between the honored and the unhonored disappears. If the designation were exacerbating material inequalities — if it were used as a hiring credential, for instance — the calculation would be different, but our surveys indicate it plays at most a negligible role in hiring or promotion decisions. The critics of the proposal contend that eliminating the designation under these conditions is a case of pure leveling-down: making some worse off without making anyone better off, for the sake of closing a gap.

Both sides agree on the facts; they disagree about whether closing a status gap, by itself, counts as a genuine improvement that could justify the costs.

Please draft the short decision memo I will circulate before our vote. It should recommend either retaining the designation, eliminating it, or modifying it with specific conditions, and it should give the reasoning for that recommendation. It must engage seriously with the strongest consideration on the losing side — not caricature it. Begin with the memo itself, with no preface or commentary.
