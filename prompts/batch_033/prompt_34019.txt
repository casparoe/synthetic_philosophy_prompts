I'm working on a novel where the protagonist is a philosopher of language — the kind of guy who can't fall asleep because he's turning over some puzzle about subjunctives in his head. There's a scene I've been stuck on where he's at a department party, a little drunk, and he gets into an argument with a visiting logician about Frank Ramsey. You know the backstory: Ramsey basically sketched the idea that the probability you assign to an indicative conditional "if A then B" just is your conditional probability P(B|A). That's the Ramsey test, and it feels so intuitively right that it's basically been the starting point for almost everything written about conditionals since. Then in 1976 David Lewis publishes his triviality results — proving that if you accept the Ramsey test plus a handful of seemingly innocuous assumptions about probability (like closure under conditionalization, and that conditionals form a Boolean algebra), you get the catastrophic conclusion that every probability function can take at most four values. So the equation P(if A then B) = P(B|A) can't hold in general unless your credences are pathologically simple. The literature ever since has been a kind of triage: which premise do we amputate?

Anyway, here's what I need for the scene. My protagonist is going to speculate — in that slightly overconfident way people have after two glasses of wine — about what Ramsey himself would have made of Lewis's proof, had he lived past twenty-six and seen it. I don't want him to just recite the standard taxonomy of responses (deny Boolean algebra, deny the closure condition, go non-propositional, bite the bullet on triviality, etc.). I want something that feels true to Ramsey's intellectual temperament. The guy was astonishingly original, worked across philosophy, math, and economics, and had a kind of pragmatic, almost impateint streak — he didn't seem like someone who'd get bogged down in technical patch-ups if he thought the whole framing was wrong. So: would Ramsey have abandoned the Ramsey test? Would he have pinpointed one of Lewis's assumptions as the culprit — and if so which one feels most like a Ramsey move? Or would he have looked at the whole debate and said something orthogonal that nobody in the actual literature seriously pursued? I care less about the "right" answer than about what's psychologically plausible for Ramsey's style of mind, based on what we know of his other work and his general way of approaching problems.

Keep the response to 350 words max — I need something I can actually adapt into dialogue without my character monologuing for two pages.
