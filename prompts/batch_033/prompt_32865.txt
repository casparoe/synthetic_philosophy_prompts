I spent thirty years in molecular biology before retiring, and about eightteen months ago I started reading philosophy seriously — epistemology and decision theory mostly. One thing that's been nagging at me is Newcomb's problem. For those who haven't stared at it recently: a Predictor with a near-perfect track record puts two boxes in front of you, one transparent with $1,000, one opaque with either $1,000,000 or nothing. The Predictor has already put the million in the opaque box iff she predicted you'd take only that box. You can take both boxes or just the opaque one. The one-boxers say: take only the opaque box, because that's the kind of decision you want to be making, and the Predictor will have known it. The two-boxers say: the money's already there or it isn't, so taking both strictly dominates — you get the extra $1,000 no matter what.

I've now read a dozen papers on this and watched a half-dozen talks, and what strikes me isn't the puzzle itself but the fact that this debate has been going since Nozick introduced it in 1969 and nobody seems to have moved. These are extremely smart people — philosophers, mathematicians, decision theorists — and they all understand the setup, they all see both arguments, and yet they divide into camps and stay there. In my old field, if two groups had the same data and the same formal structure and disagreed for fifty years about what to do, we'd say something had gone wrong with the question. So here's what I want to know: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it a substantive disagreement about rationality, a verbal dispute about what "rational" means, or are one-boxers and two-boxers answering different questions under the same label — one about evidential relevance, the other about causal efficacy? Or is it something else entirely, like a bedrock intuition about free will or time that neither side can argue for? And given your diagnosis, is there anything that could count as progress — a new formal result, a conceptual distinction, a genuine experiment — or is this one of those problems where the impasse is the point and the real lesson is something about the limits of decision theory itself?
