I write about the foundations of mathematics and computing, and I keep coming back to a question about Kurt Gödel. Suppose one could present to Gödel the debate that has developed since his death over the epistemic status of the Church-Turing thesis. On one side, some philosophers and computer scientists now treat the thesis as an empirical claim about the limits of physically realizable computation — a claim that a device exploiting exotic physics might in principle refute by computing a non-Turing-computable function. On the other side, others insist the thesis is a conceptual or definitional claim about the very idea of an effective procedure, so that no physical result could overturn it. How do you think Gödel would have positioned himself in this dispute, given his Platonism about mathematical concepts, his doubts about mechanism, and his own assessment of Turing's analysis of computability? Which of his commitments would push him toward one reading, and where might he resist both camps? Lead with your bottom-line prediction in the first sentence, then defend it. Please answer in American English, in no more than 450 words.
