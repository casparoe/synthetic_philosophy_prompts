I spent 30 years in a wet lab—organic chemistry—and only started reading philosophy after I retired. So forgive me if I'm using the wrong words for things. Here's what's been bugging me.

The big AI labs all train their models with something like RLHF: you show a human two outputs from the model, the human picks which one they like better, and you update the model to produce more of the liked kind. Nobody sits down and checks whether the model's internal reasoning was sound, or whether it "meant well," or what chain of thought led to the output. It's pure outcome evaluation. And this isn't just for chatbots—the same logic is creeping into how we evaluate AI for coding, for medical advice, for legal reasoning. If the output looks right and sounds right, it passes.

Now, if you tried to evaluate a graduate student this way—just look at their final answers, never ask them to show their work, never check whether they actually understand the mechanism or just got lucky pattern-matching—you'd be a terrible advisor. Every scientist knows this. We make students draw mechanisms, defend intermediates, explain why a reaction goes one way and not another. The process matters because the process tells you whether they can handle the next problem. So there's a weird asymmetry: we demand process-level justification from humans but settle for outcome-level approval from machines, even as we hand those machines more and more consequential tasks.

So here's my question. What theory of mind, or of agency, or of evaluation—I'm not sure which category this falls into—does the outcome-only supervision paradigm presuppose? Does it implicitly commit you to the view that there's nothing to understanding beyond producing the right outputs? That seems like a kind of behaviorism about intelligence, and I thought philosophy had mostly moved past that. Or does it presuppose something more pragmatic, like "we don't know how to evaluate internal reasoning yet, so we're doing the best we can, and the philosophy can catch up later"? And if the latter, is that even a stable position, or does the practice itself start to shape what we think understanding is—so that in ten years nobody bothers asking whether the model "really" reasoned because the training regime has made the question feel quaint?

I guess what I'm really asking is: reconstruct the implicit philosophy behind outcome-based supervision of AI systems. Tell me what it strictly presupposes, what it merely fits well with, and where the practice is philosophically underdetermined—where you could hold several different views of mind and still run the same training loop. And then tell me what would have to change, institutionally and technically, if we took process-based views of understanding seriously.
