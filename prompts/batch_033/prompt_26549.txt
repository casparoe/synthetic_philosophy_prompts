I am a novelist at work on a book that turns, in part, on questions about how language carves up the world — how the names we give things shape what we take those things to be. In the course of my research I encountered the White Horse Dialogue (白馬論), attributed to Gongsun Long of the Warring States period School of Names, and I find myself stumped by a particular passage. I would be grateful for a careful explication.

Here is the passage, in the translation by Donald Sturgeon from the Chinese Text Project. The speaker is the Advocate, who is defending the claim that "a white horse is not a horse":

"White does not fix what is white, this can be put aside. 'White horse' speaks of white fixing what is white. That which fixes what is white is not white. 'Horse' does not pick or exclude color, thus a brown or black horse can be brought. 'White horse' does pick or exclude color; brown and black horses are excluded by color, thus only a white horse can be brought. That which does not exclude is not that which does exclude. Thus I say: 'a white horse is not a horse'."

I have read that one interpretive key is whether the Chinese 非 (fēi) means "is not identical to" rather than "is not a member of the category," but this passage seems to be doing something more subtle. The Advocate appears to be making a point about how predicates work: "white" in the abstract does not attach itself to any particular thing, but "white" in "white horse" does — it fixes itself to a specific bearer. And then he seems to say that because this fixed whiteness is not the same as whiteness in the abstract, and because "horse" (which is indifferent to color) is not the same as "white horse" (which is not indifferent to color), the two terms pick out different things.

As a novelist, I am especially interested in what this passage implies about the relationship between a general term and a term that has been qualified or specified. When I describe a character as "a melancholy pianist," am I doing something fundamentally different from describing her as "a pianist"? Does the qualifier "melancholy" merely narrow the extension, or does it — as Gongsun Long seems to suggest — create a new kind of entity, a new concept that is not reducible to the sum of its parts? And what does it mean to say that "that which fixes what is white is not white"? Is this a claim about the difference between a property considered in the abstract and a property considered as instantiated?

Please help me work through this passage. I am less interested in the historical or Sinological controversies than in the conceptual structure of the argument itself and what it might teach a writer about how language constitutes rather than merely labels the things it names.
