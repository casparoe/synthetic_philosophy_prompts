Two members of my philosophy discussion group got into a protracted argument about anthropic reasoning, and I have been asked to adjudicate it. The dispute began when one of them introduced the following scenario:

Imagine two cosmological theories. Theory T₁ says the universe contains 200 billion observers in total. Theory T₂ says it contains 200 trillion observers in total. The two theories are otherwise identical — they make all the same predictions about physics, cosmic history, and everything we can directly observe. They differ only in how many observers exist, most of whom we will never see. Before we apply any anthropic reasoning, we regard both theories as equally plausible.

Alice argued that we should remain indifferent between T₁ and T₂. Our evidence is just what we observe, and we observe the same things under both theories. The bare fact that T₂ says there are more people somewhere else does not give us a reason to prefer it; that would be like saying a theory is more likely because it posits more unobservable planets.

Ben disagreed. He said: T₂ predicts 1,000 times as many observers as T₁. If I reason as if I am randomly selected from the set of all observers who ever exist, then my own existence is 1,000 times more likely under T₂ than under T₁. So I should update my credence toward T₂ by a factor of 1,000.

Alice replied that this is exactly what is wrong with the self-indication assumption. She said: your existence is not evidence for anything, because you could not have found yourself not existing. You can only update on things you could have observed otherwise. You cannot treat your own existence as a random draw from the set of all possible observers.

Ben came back: fine, then consider a different case. A fair coin is flipped. If heads, one person is created in a room. If tails, one hundred people are created in separate identical rooms. You wake up in a room. What should your credence be that the coin landed tails? If you reason as though you are a random sample from the actual observers in the world, you should think tails is 100 times more likely than heads. That is the self-sampling assumption. But now notice: if you accept SSA, you would also have to say, in the original cosmology case, that you should remain indifferent between T₁ and T₂ — because in each theory you are equally likely to be any particular observer, and the fraction of observers who see what you see is the same either way. So SSA gives Alice's answer, and SISA gives mine. Which one is correct?

Alice said: I accept SSA, and I am happy to remain indifferent between T₁ and T₂. But your own argument shows why SISA is absurd. Under SISA, you would be forced to prefer T₂ by a factor of 1,000 based on nothing but the fact that it postulates more people you will never meet. A philosopher who confidently announces that the universe probably contains 200 trillion rather than 200 billion observers, on no evidence except that this would make his own existence less surprising, has earned the label "presumptuous." That is a reductio of SISA.

Ben replied: I do not think "presumptuous" is an argument. SISA gives a clear answer in the cosmology case, and SSA gives a clear answer in the coin case. But now consider a third case. A fair coin is flipped. If heads, one person is created. If tails, two people are created, but one has green eyes and the other has blue eyes. You wake up with blue eyes. Under SSA, what is your credence in tails? If you reason as a random sample from the actual observers, you are equally likely to be any of them. There are three possible observers across both branches: the heads-person, the tails-green person, and the tails-blue person. Given that you have blue eyes, you must be the tails-blue person. So under SSA, you should be certain the coin landed tails. But that seems wrong — before you opened your eyes, you did not know your eye color; once you see it, you have learned something, but why should it make you certain? The coin was fair.

Alice said: That is not a problem for SSA; it is a problem for the reference class. If you treat all observers as belonging to the same reference class, you get that result. But you can choose a narrower reference class — say, observers with your exact evidence. Then the blue-eyes observation tells you only that you are in the blue-eyed subset, and the probabilities shift accordingly. The reference class problem is hard, but it is everyone's problem, not just SSA's.

Ben said: Exactly — the reference class problem is hard. But let me press another point. In the coin case with one versus one hundred, SSA tells you tails is 100 times more likely. But now imagine we run the same experiment, except that if tails comes up, we also create 100 observers on a distant planet who will never interact with you. SSA now says your credence in tails should drop back to 50-50, because the extra observers dilute the fraction. That means your credence depends on people who are causally disconnected from you and whose existence you can never confirm. Does that not bother you?

Alice said: Yes, it does bother me. But I am not sure SISA does better. Under SISA, my credence in tails in the original one-versus-one-hundred case is already close to 100 percent, regardless of the distant observers. And that is also troubling — it means I should be nearly certain the coin landed tails, even though the coin was fair, just because tails would create more people. That seems to license a kind of a priori reasoning about contingent facts that epistemology should not allow.

At this point they turned to me. Please go through their exchange and adjudicate it. For each major move, say who has the better of it, and identify what, if anything, both are missing. Do not simply declare a winner; assess the arguments as they were actually made.
