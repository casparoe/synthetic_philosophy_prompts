I've been going round in circles on the "do artefacts have politics" question—not the strong determinism version, just the narrower one about whether a particular thing can have politics built into it—so I wrote down my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases. I'd like someone to tell me what principle, if any, these add up to.

The cases:

- The low overpasses on the Long Island parkways, in the famous Robert Moses story. If the story's even roughly right, I count that as political: the exclusion is designed into the concrete itself.
- A public bench with an armrest in the middle, or those little spikes that stop people sleeping. Political, and I don't need to know what the designer intended to think so.
- A handgun. Not political in itself. Obviously it's tangled up in politics everywhere, but the thing alone doesn't have politics the way the bench does.
- The mechanical tomato harvester developed in California in the 1960s. It wasn't built to hurt farm workers, but it shifted power towards large growers and helped drive small ones out. My gut says that counts as political, even though it's about effects rather than design intent.
- A nuclear power plant. There's a well-known claim that the technology requires centralised, authoritarian control, but that feels like a claim about how we run it, not about the plant itself. I say not political.
- A social media feed that optimises for engagement. Political, even if nobody sat down intending to polarise anyone. The politics is in the ranking function.
- A hammer. Not political.
- A cul-de-sac suburb versus a gridded street plan. I want to say the layout is mildly political because it makes some kinds of encounter easier and some kinds of policing easier, but I cant tell whether that's the artefact or just the history around it.

The pair that bothers me is the tomato harvester and the handgun: both have big political effects, but I count one and not the other. Is there a single principle that gets all eight, or close? If not, which verdicts should I overturn? And what would your principle say about the case I keep going back and forth on: a large language model whose training data is filtered and whose outputs are steered by a safety policy, where the whole point is to shape what the system can say?

Please answer in American English, and take a definite position: give me one principle (or a specific small set) stated precisely, show how it handles each case, and be honest about which of my verdicts it can't accommodate rather than bending it to fit everything.
