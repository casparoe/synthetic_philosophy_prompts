I've been going round in circles on this argument from Galen Strawson and I need someone to tell me where it breaks.

Here's the thing. Strawson says moral responsibility is impossible. Not just that we sometimes have excuses, or that circumstances mitigate things, but that the whole idea of being truly responsible for what you do is incoherent. His argument goes roughly like this:

(1) To be truly morally responsible for what you do, you must be truly responsible for the way you are — your character, your dispositions, your motivational structure, the works. Because your actions flow from the way you are; you do what you do because of the kind of person you are.

(2) To be truly responsible for the way you are, you must have chosen to be that way. You can't be responsible for something that was just handed to you.

(3) But to choose to be a certain way, you'd already need to have a nature — principles of choice, preferences, a way you are — in light of which you made that choice. You can't choose your choosing-principles from nowhere.

(4) And then, by (1), you'd need to be responsible for that prior nature. Which, by (2), would require you to have chosen it. Which, by (3), would require a still prior nature. And so on, infinitely.

(5) An infinite regress of choices of your own nature is impossible. You can't have completed an infinite series, and there's no point where you could have got it started.

(6) So you are not, and cannot be, truly responsible for the way you are.

(7) So, by (1), you are not and cannot be truly responsible for anything you do.

That's the argument. The conclusion is mad — it says nobody has ever deserved praise or blame for anything, ever. Not even a little. But I can't find which step to reject without paying a price I really don't want to pay.

If I reject (2), I'm saying you can be responsible for your nature without having chosen it. But how? That sounds like I'm responsible for something that just happened to me — something I was saddled with.

If I reject (1), I'm saying you can be responsible for an action without being responsible for whatever produced it. But that seems to treat actions as little uncaused events that just pop into existence, which is no less weird.

I've read some compatibilists who say the whole thing turns on an ambiguity in "truly responsible" or that (1) smuggles in an impossible standard, but I can't make that stick without feeling like I'm just lowering the bar and calling it a solution.

So: where does this argument actually go wrong? And whatever move you recommend, what does it cost you — what intuitively plausible thing do you have to give up to make it?
