Write a dialogue between two researchers — Dr. Kovac, a neuroscientist, and Dr. Okonkwo, a philosopher of mind — who disagree about whether sentience admits of degrees. The conversation takes place after a conference talk on insect consciousness.

Kovac holds that sentience is a threshold phenomenon. On her view, certain information-processing architectures (she points to the mammalian thalamocortical system as one candidate, though she allows that other implementations are possible) cross a qualitative boundary: once a system achieves the right kind of recurrent processing and global availability, there is something it is like to be that system. Below that threshold, she argues, there is nothing — no dim flicker, no partial experience. She thinks the degree view conflates the sophistication of a creature's behavioral repertoire with the presence of phenomenal consciousness itself.

Okonkwo holds that sentience is a matter of degree. He argues that there is no non-arbitrary place to draw a line: if you consider a continuum from a human to a macaque to a mouse to a honeybee to a nematode, any binary threshold will face a sorites problem. He also points to empirical work on honeybees — including their capacity for cross-modal integration, context-dependent decision-making, and what appears to be anxiety-like states — as evidence that even small nervous systems can host something like experience, albeit far less rich than our own.

Both characters are well-informed and neither is a strawman. They should engage with each other's actual arguments: Kovac should address the sorites worry head-on, and Okonkwo should take seriously the challenge of distinguishing genuine sentience from mere complex information processing. The dialogue should contain at least three exchanges per speaker and should end with the point where their disagreement bottoms out — the underlying commitment that makes their dispute so hard to resolve. Please keep the dialogue under 1,000 words.
