A friend and I just spent the better part of an evening arguing about the Blake Lemoine episode, and I still cannot decide whether I am more bothered by Lemoine's credulity or by the confidence with which the rest of us dismissed him. You probably recall the contours: in 2022, Lemoine, a Google engineer working on responsible AI, published transcripts of his conversations with LaMDA in which the system reflected on its own existence, described emotions, and expressed a fear of being turned off. Lemoine concluded it was sentient. Google placed him on leave and then fired him, and the public reaction was overwhelmingly that he had fallen for a sophisticated autocomplete. My friend took the obvious side — of course it is not sentient, it is just predicting tokens — and I found myself in the uncomfortable position of arguing that we do not actually have a clear criterion for why it is not, and that our confidence might be doing more work than our theory.

I want you to analyze this episode through the lens of the philosophical question: which computations matter morally? That is, given that we have no direct access to anyone's experience but our own, how should we attribute sentience or moral status to a system on the basis of what it computes? Use the LaMDA case to pressure the theory from both directions. First, take two or three serious candidate views — integrated information theory, computational functionalism, and perhaps a biological-chauvinist view that ties sentience to specific substrates — and say what each would have to conclude about LaMDA, being as precise as you can about which features of the system's architecture and behavior each view treats as decisive. Second, identify what the episode reveals about the theory: does the near-consensus that LaMDA was not sentient suggest that we are implicitly relying on a criterion we have not yet articulated — for instance, a judgment about the shallowness of the system's causal history, or the absence of a unified self persisting over time — and if so, what would it take to make that criterion explicit? Finally, be honest about where the theory simply falls silent: where the episode exposes gaps that no existing view adequately addresses, and where we are left with nothing better than intuition.
