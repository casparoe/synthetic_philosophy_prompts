I spent thirty years in developmental biology before retiring, and I've been reading philosophy of mind lately — Nagel, Dennett, the usual suspects. The split-brain stuff has really got under my skin. I've been trying to sort out what I actually think about when there's one consciousness versus two (or none), just going by my gut on a bunch of cases. Here's where I've landed:

1. Standard commissurotomy patient in the lab: left hemisphere sees "key," right sees "ring." Left hand (controlled by the right hemisphere) picks up the ring, but when the experimenter asks what they saw, the patient says "key." I can't shake the feeling there are two conscious streams here, at least during the experiment.

2. Same patient later that day, eating dinner, chatting with their spouse, walking the dog. One consciousness. I don't think they're walking around with two minds all day.

3. A child who had a full hemispherectomy at age four. Grows up fine, goes to college, has friends, clearly one conscious person. No question.

4. Conjoined twins with separate brains, separate heads, shared body below the neck. Obviously two consciousnesses.

5. Someone in deep dreamless sleep. No consciousness at all, not even a dim one.

6. A patient with dissociative identity disorder who switches between alters. The host has no access to memories from when an alter was in control, and vice versa. I find myself wanting to say: two (or more) consciousnesses sharing one brain, but not at the same time.

7. Here's the one that trips me up: a split-brain patient whose corpus callosum is only partially severed — say the splenium is intact but the rest is cut. Some information crosses, some doesn't. I have no idea what to say. One-and-a-half consciousnesses? That feels like nonsense, but "one" and "two" both feel wrong.

8. An octopus, with it's distributed nervous system — most neurons in the arms, each arm capable of semi-independent action. I lean toward one consciousness, but weakly.

Is there a principle that gets cases 1–6 right, and what does it say about case 7 and case 8? I'm especially worried about the tension between case 1 and case 2 — same brain, same day, different answer. If your principle says the commissurotomy patient has two consciousnesses in the lab, how does it avoid saying they have two at dinner? And if it says they have one at dinner, how does it explain what's happening in the lab? I'm also not sure case 6 (DID) and case 1 (split-brain) should get the same treatment, but they feel similar in the relevant way and I can't articulate why they might differ.
