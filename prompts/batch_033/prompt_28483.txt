I'm a novelist, and I've been trying to write about pain in a way that doesn't turn it into either a medical chart or a sentimental blur, so I keep circling back to Le Guin's The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas. I run a small speculative-fiction reading group — eight or nine of us, mostly writers, nobody with formal philosophy — and I want to spend an evening on the story. Lately I've been reading about pain asymbolia, the condition where people can locate and rate a painful stimulus, they'll tell you it's sharp, it's in the left hand, it's a seven, but they dont mind it and they don't pull away. That's made me wonder whether the child in the cellar is suffering because of the raw pain, or because of everything around the pain — the locked door, the filth, the fact that the whole city knows and nobody comes. Read the story through that lens for me. Does Le Guin treat the badness of the child's pain as built into the sensation itself, or as something the city adds or withholds? Which moments would you point the group to, and where does the story resist the idea that the badness of pain is a detachable extra that could in principle be switched off? I'm also curious whether the ones who walk away are responding to the pain itself or to the fact that the city has set things up so the child's pain doesn't call on anyone. And be straight with me about where the pain-asymbolia lens distorts the story — it might be that Omelas is aimed at something the philosophy of pain can't reach. What does the story get right that the papers don't?
