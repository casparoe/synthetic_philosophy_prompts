I am writing a paper on whether Frege’s posthumously published 1924 political diary should change how we understand his philosophy of logic, language, and mathematics. Before I read more of the existing interpretations, I want the specification: what must an adequate account of the diary’s bearing on Frege’s philosophical work get right? The diary is ideologically anti-democratic and anti-Semitic, and it takes a radically reactionary political stance, though its precise expressions of sympathy for particular radical right-wing figures are disputed; it was written near the end of Frege’s life, was not published by him, and contains little in the way of philosophical argument. So any adequate account must do more than point at affinities or distance between the politics and the philosophy. It must explain what would count as a bearing.

Construct the desiderata for such an account. At minimum, a defensible account must:

- state what kind of relation it is claiming between the diary and the philosophy—genetic, justificatory, expressive, contextual, or something else—and what evidence would confirm or disconfirm that relation;
- handle the asymmetry between private writing and published doctrine, including the fact that Frege did not authorize publication of the diary;
- respect the textual burden: an interpretation that says the diary illuminates the philosophy must identify which published commitments are illuminated, while one that quarantines it must explain why those commitments are immune to this kind of evidence;
- explain how the diary’s specific content, and not just its general ideological tendency, could make a difference to reading particular philosophical moves in Frege;
- distinguish three questions that often run together: whether the diary is relevant to understanding Frege’s texts, whether it is evidence about Frege’s intellectual character, and whether it undermines the arguments themselves.

For each requirement, say what case or consideration motivates it, and flag which requirements are non-negotiable—such that a view that fails them is not an account of bearing at all—and which are preferences internal to different interpretive schools. Then identify which single requirement does the most work in excluding currently popular positions. Finally, briefly score the three most common positions against the list: the diary is irrelevant to the technical philosophy; the diary reveals the reactionary political substance latent in Frege’s Platonism about thoughts and his anti-psychologism; and the diary matters mainly as context for Frege’s conservative rationalism, without touching the logic. Keep the weight on the desiderata, not on the scorecard.
