"W.K. Clifford argues that it is wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone to believe anything on insufficient evidence. William James, in 'The Will to Believe,' argues that there are circumstances in which it is permissible to believe without sufficient evidence. Does James succeed in refuting Clifford, or are the two addressing different questions? Identify the core claims of each and assess whether James's argument engages Clifford's on its own terms."

And here is the student's answer (lightly edited for clarity):

"Clifford's central claim is an epistemic norm: one ought to proportion one's beliefs to the evidence. His shipowner example is meant to show that even when a belief turns out true, holding it without adequate evidence is epistemically blameworthy. James responds by arguing that Clifford's norm is too strict because there are cases where we must believe something before the evidence is in — specifically, when the option is a genuine one, meaning it is living, forced, and momentous. James's key move is to point out that some beliefs are self-fulfilling: for instance, whether I believe you like me may partly determine whether you actually come to like me. In such cases, waiting for sufficient evidence before believing would be self-defeating, because the evidence only comes into existence once I already believe. James also argues that we are doxastic voluntarists — we can choose what to believe — and therefore we bear responsibility for our beliefs in a way that makes Clifford's purely epistemic norm incomplete. Finally, James's broader pragmatism about truth, according to which truth is what is useful to believe, undermines Clifford's assumption that belief aims at truth in a correspondence sense. I think James succeeds in showing that Clifford's principle cannot be applied universally, because there are domains — religious faith, personal relationships, moral commitments — where the costs of suspending judgment are higher than the costs of being wrong, and where the evidence is necessarily incomplete."
