I've been working in climate policy for a few years now, and something about the way we talk to the public has been gnawing at me. Nearly every environmental NGO, government sustainability campaign, and corporate responsibility program converges on the same move: they hand people a carbon footprint calculator and a list of personal lifestyle changes — fly less, eat less red meat, line-dry your laundry, switch to a green energy tariff — and they frame these as moral obligations. "Do your part," "every action counts," "be the change." But if you corner the people who design these campaigns, they'll usually concede that any single individual's lifetime emissions are a rounding error in the global total, and that structural interventions like carbon pricing, grid decarbonization, and industrial regulation do the overwhelming share of the work. So the practice seems to treat individual consumption choices as morally weighty while simultaneously acknowledging that, taken one by one, they are causally negligible. That looks like a tension, and I suspect it hides some substantive philosophical commitments that nobody has fully spelled out.

I'd like you to approach this as a professional philosopher working in ethics and collective action. Think carefully, and don't just reach for the first theoretical label that fits — work through the problem step by step. What I want you to do is reconstruct the implicit theory of moral responsibility that this widespread practice of carbon-footprint exhortation presupposes. Specifically: does the practice commit itself to a particular answer to the inefficacy objection — the worry that if my individual act makes no detectable difference to the harm, I have no reason to refrain? Does it implicitly rely on a Kantian universalizability test (what if everyone did that?), a virtue-ethical picture where consumption expresses character regardless of outcomes, a rule-consequentialist view about the good of widespread compliance with a norm, or something else? And here is the sharper question: is the practice internally consistent? That is, if you took the implicit theory seriously and applied it across the board, would it also require things the practice conspicuously omits — treating voting, career choice, or political organizing as morally on a par with adjusting your thermostat? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and identify the points where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that several rival theories could license it equally well.
