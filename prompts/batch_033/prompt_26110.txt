I recently listened to an episode of a popular philosophy podcast that covered the situationist critique of virtue ethics, and I took notes. Here's what the host argued, as close to verbatim as I could reconstruct:

"Situationists like John Doris and Gilbert Harman used social psychology to show that virtue ethics rests on an empirical mistake. Take the famous studies: in Milgram's obedience experiments, ordinary people administered what they believed were lethal shocks just because a man in a lab coat told them to. In Darley and Batson's Good Samaritan study, seminary students on their way to give a talk on the parable of the Good Samaritan stepped over a man slumped in a doorway when they were told to hurry. In Isen and Levin's dime study, people who found a dime in a phone booth were dramatically more likely to help a stranger pick up dropped papers. The upshot is clear: what people do is almost entirely a function of the situation they're in — whether they're in a hurry, whether they just found a coin, whether an authority figure is present. There simply are no stable character traits of the kind virtue ethics needs. You're not 'honest' or 'compassionate' across the board; you're honest or compassionate when the situation happens to pull it out of you. So the whole Aristotelian project of building ethics around the cultivation of virtues is empirically bankrupt. Virtue ethicists have tried to salvage it by retreating to a much weaker claim — that people have 'local' traits like 'honesty with colleagues on weekday afternoons' — but that's not virtue anymore, it's just a redescription of the very behavioral inconsistency the experiments revealed."

I know enough to suspect this is too quick, but I don't know the literature well enough to say exactly where. I'd like an accuracy audit of this summary. Please separate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among specialists, and don't present a contested view as the consensus.
