In my course on Marx and political economy we have been working through his treatment of machinery and automation: that under capitalism new technology is introduced less to lighten labor than to cheapen, discipline, and replace it; that it deskills and displaces workers; and that automation carries a contradictory potential, since it could in principle free people from necessary labor while under existing property relations it tends to immiserate them. I want to use the 2023 Hollywood strikes, when the WGA and SAG-AFTRA both struck partly over artificial intelligence and streaming residuals, as a test case for this framework. I already know the broad facts: both unions went out in 2023; AI was a central issue, with writers seeking limits on AI-generated scripts and actors seeking control over digital replication of their performances; the studios control the platforms and, in practice, much of the data on which the models are trained. What I want is a disciplined mapping rather than a loose analogy. Who or what plays the role of capital and labor in this account, and what exactly is the machinery here? What does the framework say the conflict is really about, and what would it have predicted about how the dispute would be resolved? Which features of the episode does it explain well, such as the fear that AI devalues skilled labor, and which features strain it, such as the role of intellectual property, the fact that the models are trained on workers own prior output, and the fact that many strikers framed AI as a tool they could live with under the right contract? Finally, say what the episode shows about Marxs view of technology: does it confirm it, expose a gap in it, or reveal that the theory was aimed at a different kind of technology and labor than this?
