I'm taking a graduate seminar in formal epistemology, and we've been working through Aumann's agreement theorem and the common prior assumption. In last week's session, a fellow student — whose background is in economics rather than philosophy — defended the common prior assumption with an analogy that has been nagging at me ever since. He said: imagine two calculators that have both been programmed with the same arithmetic and are given the same sum to compute. If they display different answers, at least one of them is malfunctioning, or they were not actually given the same inputs. Rational agents, he argued, are just like that: if two people both reason correctly from the same prior probabilities and the same evidence, they cannot end up with different posterior probabilities. Persistent disagreement therefore always traces back to irrationality, different priors, or different evidence — there is no such thing as reasonable disagreement among fully rational people. He then used this to argue that most real-world political and philosophical disagreements are evidence that at least one side is being irrational, since the evidence is largely shared and there is no good reason for priors to diverge so dramatically.

I find the analogy intuitively compelling, but I also suspect it is doing more work than it can bear. I would like you to audit it properly. Lay out what maps onto what — the correspondence between the calculator case and rational agents — and then identify where the mapping breaks down. In particular, I want to know whether the features of calculators that guarantee agreement (deterministic operation, identical programming, perfectly specified inputs) are features we have any reason to expect in human reasoners, even idealised ones. And most importantly: once the disanalogies are on the table, does the conclusion that there is no such thing as reasonable disagreement among rational agents survive, or was the analogy carrying that conclusion the whole time? If the analogy is flawed, is there a better one that captures what is right in the common prior assumption without overclaiming?
