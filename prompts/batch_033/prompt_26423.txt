I've been going to this philosophy reading group at the local bookshop—it's mostly retired folks like me, a few grad students. I spent forty years in molecular biology, so I'm new to this stuff, but I find it scratches a similar itch. Last week we were reading something about Bradley's regress (from F.H. Bradley, late 19th century, about the unity of propositions), and two of the regulars got into a proper argument that I'm still chewing on.

The issue, as I understand it, is this: take a simple proposition like "the apple is red" or "a is larger than b." It seems to be made of parts—a subject, a predicate or relation, another term—but somehow those parts are combined into a single unified thought. The question is what does the combining. Bradley's worry (as our group facilitator put it) was that if you say a relation R relates a and b, you now have R, a, and b sitting there, and you need something further to relate R to a and to b. A new relation, call it R′, that relates R to a and R to b. But then you need R′′ to relate R′ to R and a, and so on forever. So either the unity is never actually achieved, or you've got an infinite regress.

Here's where the argument started. Martin (he taught linguistics before retiring) said this regress is a genuine problem. His line was: the regress shows that relations can't be the kinds of things that just sit alongside their relata as further constituents—they can't be "items" in the same sense. So we need a different metaphysics of relations, maybe one where relations are ontologically dependent on their relata in a way that makes the regress not get started. He said this is actually quite deep and most philosophers since Bradley have been trying to answer it, not just dismiss it.

Susan (she was in mathematics) pushed back hard. She said the whole thing is generated by a bad picture. We imagine a and b and R as three separate beads on a string, and then we notice they need some string between them. But that's just a category mistake. A relation isn't another bead—it's what's already doing the relating. If you ask "what relates the relation to its terms?" you've already misunderstood what a relation is. The regress is an artefact of treating relations as objects. She said it's on a par with asking "what moves the motion?" or "what causes causation?"—the question dissolves when you stop reifying.

Martin came back with: but that's exactly the move that makes the problem interesting. You can't just say relations are special and leave it at that, because the original question was "in virtue of what is a proposition a unity rather than a mere heap of its constituents?" If your answer is "in virtue of the relation, which is special and doesn't need further relating," you haven't explained the unity—you've just given it a name and declared it primitive. The regress puts pressure on anyone who thinks the unity needs explaining, and saying "it's primitive" is an admission that you can't explain it, not a solution.

Susan's final volley: fair enough, but then the regress isn't the argument—it's just a dramatic way of saying "explanations have to stop somewhere." And stopping at the relation itself, by saying it's of the nature of a relation to relate, is no worse than stopping anywhere else. In fact, it's the natural place to stop. The regress only looks vicious if you've already accepted that relations are the wrong kind of thing to stop at—so the regress isn't doing any independent work.

They went back and forth a bit more but I think that was the core of it. I'm honestly not sure who's got the better of this. Could you walk through their exchange and adjudicate it move by move? I'd especially like to know whether the regress has any force once you grant that explanations have to bottom out somewhere, and whether Martin is right that calling something primitive is a concession rather than a solution.
