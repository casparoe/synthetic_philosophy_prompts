I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about whether it is morally defensible to have children given the state of the world, and the discussion kept returning to a principle he called the procreation asymmetry. When I asked him to state it as precisely as he could, he gave me this summary:

"The procreation asymmetry is the claim that there is a basic moral difference between bringing happy people into existence and bringing miserable people into existence. If you bring a miserable person into the world, you harm that person, so doing it is wrong. But if you fail to bring a happy person into the world, you do not harm anyone — there is no one there to be deprived of anything — so it is not wrong. That means we have strong duties not to create miserable lives, but no duty whatsoever to create happy ones. So the asymmetry says it is always wrong to have a child you know will suffer, but never wrong to refrain from having a child who would have been happy. And once you accept this, you are basically committed to the view that coming into existence is always a harm: the asymmetry tells us that the absence of suffering is good in a way that the absence of happiness is not bad, which means nonexistence is better than existence for anyone whose life contains any suffering at all."

I am not asking you to tell me whether the asymmetry itself is true. What I want is an accuracy audit of this summary. Go through it sentence by sentence and tell me what a philosopher who actually defends the asymmetry — a Benatar or a McMahan — would accept, what they would qualify or hedge, and what they would reject as a misrepresentation or a conflation with a separate thesis. I especially want to know whether the asymmetry itself entails that coming into existence is always a harm, or whether my friend has slid from the asymmetry into a further anti-natalist argument without noticing the gap. At the very end, give me a one-sentence overall verdict on whether the summary is fair, formatted as \answer{your verdict here}.
