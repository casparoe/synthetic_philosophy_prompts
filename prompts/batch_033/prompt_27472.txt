I've been thinking a lot about Darwin lately — not in any formal way, just reading through The Descent of Man on my own and getting drawn into the sections where he tries to account for religious belief in naturalistic terms. He speculates that religion might have grown out of simpler mental tendencies: the impulse to attribute agency to natural events when we don't understand them, the way dreams and visions suggest a world of spirits to the "primitive" mind, and the gradual refinement of animistic intuitions into monotheism. What fascinates me is that he's clearly trying to bring religion under the same evolutionary framework he applies to everything else, but he's doing it with the psychological and anthropological tools available in the 1870s, and he's also personally cautious — he was an agnostic, not a polemical atheist, and he seems genuinely concerned not to wound the religious sensibilities of people like his wife Emma.

What I keep coming back to is this: what would Darwin have made of the cognitive science of religion that emerged in the last few decades? I'm thinking of the research program that treats religious belief not as a direct adaptation but as a byproduct of ordinary cognitive machinery — the idea that we have a hyperactive tendency to detect agents and intentions in our environment, an intuitive mind–body dualism that makes the idea of a disembodied spirit feel natural, and a developmental bias toward seeing purpose and design in the world. On this view, religious concepts spread because they fit the contours of mental systems that evolved for entirely different reasons. In some ways this looks like exactly the kind of account Darwin was groping toward. But in other ways it seems more radical than anything he committed to print — because if you can explain why belief in God feels compelling without invoking God's actual existence, you've arguably done something more destabilizing than simply pointing out that religions have evolved over time. Darwin himself seemed to leave room for a divine first cause and for the possibility that evolution was the means by which a creator worked; he was not a reductionist in the aggressive modern sense. So I genuinely don't know whether he would have welcomed this research program as the fulfillment of his own naturalistic instincts, or whether he would have pulled back from the debunking conclusions some people draw from it, or whether he would have found a third position altogether. I'd like to hear a careful extrapolation — grounded in what Darwin actually wrote about religion, about the limits of human knowledge, and about the moral importance of sympathy for believers — of what he would likely have thought about the cognitive science of religion and the claim that it explains religious belief away.
