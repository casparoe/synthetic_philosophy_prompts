I've been working through the paradox of deontology — the charge that deontological constraints seem to forbid precisely the actions that would minimize violations of those same constraints: you may not kill one innocent person even to prevent five other innocents from being killed, you may not torture one even to prevent five tortures, and so on. In a reading group last week, a colleague offered what he presented as a dissolving analogy. Deontological constraints, he argued, are structured like promises. If I've promised Alice that I'll attend her dissertation defense, the fact that skipping it would let me attend the defenses of five other people I've also promised to attend doesn't make it permissible to skip Alice's. My promissory duty to Alice isn't aggregated away by the greater number of promissory duties I'd fulfill by breaking it. Duties not to harm work the same way, he said: they're agent-relative, owed to specific individuals, and not summable across persons. The paradox, on his view, only looks like a paradox if you're secretly assuming a consequentialist framework in which all reasons are agent-neutral and aggregable.

I find the analogy intuitively gripping but I'm suspicious of how much weight it can bear. For one thing, promissory duties are voluntarily undertaken, while duties not to harm are not — I never signed up for them. For another, the analogy seems to presuppose the very thing at issue, namely that harm-based duties are non-aggregable in the way promissory duties are. And the paradox of deontology draws its force specifically from the victim-focused rationale of the constraints: the reason you mustn't kill is that killing is terrible for the victim, which is exactly why it seems perverse to refuse to kill one when that refusal predictably results in more victims undergoing that same terrible thing. The promise analogy, by contrast, doesn't have this structure — the reason to keep a promise isn't that breaking it would be bad for the promisee in the same way that the reason not to kill is that killing is bad for the victim.

I'd like you to audit this analogy systematically. Start by laying out the correspondence table explicitly: what plays the role of the promise, the promisee, the promisor, the conflicting promises, and the act of breaking versus keeping, in both the promise case and the harm case. Then identify where the mapping breaks down, and separate the disanalogies that genuinely matter for the paradox-of-deontology debate from those that are incidental or reparable. Finally, give me your verdict: does the analogy dissolve the paradox, does it merely relocate it into a different vocabulary, or does it quietly beg the question by building the non-aggregability of harm-based duties into the mapping itself? If the analogy fails to do the work my colleague claims for it, is there a better analogy that would succeed, or is the disanalogy here telling us something deeper about why the paradox resists easy dissolution?
