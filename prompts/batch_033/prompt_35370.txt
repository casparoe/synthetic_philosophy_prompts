I've been going down a rabbit hole on information cascades lately — you know, the Bikhchandani-Hirshleifer-Welch stuff where people rationally ignore their own private signals because they see what everyone before them did. I was explaining it to a friend over coffee and she asked: "Does this only work for binary choices? What if there are three options?" And I realized I couldn't give her a clean answer off the top of my head, which is embarassing because I'm supposed to be the one who knows this stuff.

So here's what I'd like: construct a thought experiment where a sequence of rational agents choose among three options (let's call them A, B, and C) based on private signals about which one is best. Set it up so that an information cascade forms — i.e., at some point, agents start picking the same option regardless of their private signal — but the cascade dynamics are qualitatively different from the binary case in an interesting way. For instance, maybe the cascade can form in the "wrong" direction but then self-correct, or maybe two different cascades can coexist, something like that. The setting should be concrete: people choosing between three restaurants in a new food hall, or investors picking between three startups in the same space, etc.

Walk through the first six or seven agents: what signal each draws, what they've observed from predecessors, and what they choose. Then step back and explain what feature of the ternary setup makes the dynamics different from the binary case, and whether that feature is robust or just an artifact of the particular signal structure you chose. If you changed the signal structure (e.g., from symmetric to asymmetric precision), would the interesting cascade behavior survive? Finally, if a skeptic said "this isn't really a cascade, it's just people updating on public information and sometimes coincidentally aligning," how would you defend the claim that it's a genuine cascade in the technical sense?
