Two friends of mine got into it at a bar about whether you can still call a work of art "good" once you know the artist did something awful. The specific case was a film director who turned out to have assaulted several people — the films are widely regarded as brilliant, formally inventive, and deeply influential.

(1) Marco said: of course the films are still good. The aesthetic properties of a work are in the work itself — the framing, the performances, the pacing. The director's crimes don't reach backward in time and change the celluloid. You can deplore the man and admire the art.

(2) Lian said: that's way too clean. When you watch the films now, knowing what he did, you can't just un-know it. It colors the experience — the way he films women, the power dynamics in the scenes — and that coloring is part of your aesthetic response now. You're not watching the same film anymore.

(3) Marco said: sure, your experience changes, but that's a psychological fact about you, not an aesthetic fact about the work. We don't say a painting stops being beautiful because someone told you a sad story while you were looking at it. The work is what it is.

(4) Lian said: but the sad story isn't connected to the painting. Here the moral facts are about the person who made the thing, and the thing bears his fingerprints. It's not random noise — it's relevant information about the work's origins and what it expresses. If a work expresses a vision of human relationships, and the artist's own relationships were predatory, that's not irrelevant to whether the vision is hollow or insightful.

(5) Marco said: so now we're doing biography instead of criticism? You're telling me you can't judge whether a film is well-made unless you've run a background check on the director? That collapses art criticism into moral investigation.

(6) Lian said: no, I'm saying the moral and the aesthetic aren't sealed off from each other in the way you're assuming. A work can be technically accomplished and still fail as art because of what it's doing with its subject matter, and part of what it's doing can become visible only when you know things about its maker. That's not collapsing criticism into biography — it's saying biography can sometimes illuminate what's already there in the work.

They went back and forth a while longer but that's the core of it. I found myself nodding along with both of them at different points.
