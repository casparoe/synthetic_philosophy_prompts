The question the student was set:

Explain what Gödel's first and second incompleteness theorems establish, and then discuss what they do and do not show about whether human reasoning could in principle be captured by a formal system or by a machine. Be precise about the conditions under which the theorems apply.

The student's answer (lightly cleaned up):

"Gödel's theorems are about formal systems: recursively axiomatised sets of axioms and inference rules. For any consistent formal system F that contains enough arithmetic (for example, enough to represent the primitive recursive functions), there is a sentence G_F in the language of F, built via Gödel numbering, which says of itself that it is not provable in F. If F is consistent, then G_F is neither provable nor disprovable in F: G_F is true but unprovable in F. Strictly, Gödel's original proof needed ω-consistency to rule out the negation; Rosser later showed that plain consistency suffices. The second theorem says that, if F is consistent and strong enough, F cannot prove its own consistency, because the first theorem can be formalised inside F as 'if F is consistent, then G_F is unprovable.'

For the philosophy: suppose the human mind were a formal system F of the relevant kind. Then F would have a Gödel sentence G_F that F cannot prove. But a human, standing outside F and understanding what G_F says, can see that G_F is true. Therefore the mind cannot be F. Since F was arbitrary, the mind cannot be any formal system, so human reasoning cannot be captured by any machine. This is the anti-mechanist conclusion Gödel himself drew from his theorems. The same reasoning also shows that any sufficiently complex system of rules — a legal code, a physical theory, a brain — has Gödel-like blind spots, and hence that there are truths about any such system that are unknowable in principle."
