I'm a PhD student in ancient philosophy, and I've been working through the relation between the treatment of drunkenness in the Republic and the much longer discussion of wine-drinking in the first two books of the Laws. On a first reading the two texts seem to point in opposite directions. In the Republic, Socrates appears to rule out drunkenness for the guardians as incompatible with the self-rule and wakefulness appropriate to their role, treating intoxication as a lapse into an unguarded and unseemly condition. In the Laws, by contrast, the Athenian visitor develops an elaborate defence of regulated drinking parties, including the claim that wine can function as a kind of test or gymnasium for the soul's self-control, especially when experienced under the supervision of sober leaders and within a civic institution. What I would like is an explanation of the conceptual issue here, not primarily a historical or biographical story about Plato's development. Is there a single underlying theory of temperance, habituation, and self-knowledge that can accommodate both the Republic's prohibition and the Laws' institution of the symposium, or does the Laws require a revision in the moral psychology of the earlier dialogue? I am especially interested in whether the Laws thinks wine reveals dispositions that are already there rather than creating them, and how that compares with the Republic's account of how the spirited and appetitive parts are trained. Please also address whether the Laws is best read as recommending actual intoxication for citizens or as recommending a controlled exposure to the danger of intoxication, and whether that distinction dissolves some of the apparent tension with the Republic. I would like the response to be substantial and in-the-weeds, engaging the specific argumentative moves in the texts rather than summarizing the secondary literature.
