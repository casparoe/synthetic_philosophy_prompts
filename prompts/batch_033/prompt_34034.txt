I'm reading a chapter on consent in a book my teacher assigned for our ethics unit, and there's a passage that summarizes what makes consent valid. The summary seems clear on the surface, but something about it feels too neat, and I'm not sure whether I'm being unfair to the author. Here's the passage:

"For consent to be valid, three conditions must be met. First, the person must be competent — they must have the cognitive capacity to understand the situation and the consequences of their decision. This is why children and people with severe cognitive impairments cannot give valid consent. Second, the consent must be informed — the person must be told all the facts that would be relevant to a reasonable person's decision. If any relevant information is withheld, the consent is invalid, because the person didn't really know what they were agreeing to. Third, the consent must be given freely — it cannot be the product of coercion, which is any situation in which someone faces a threat for refusing. The classic example is 'your money or your life': the victim 'consents' to hand over their wallet, but this isn't real consent because the alternative is a gun. These three conditions are individually necessary and jointly sufficient. If all three are met, the consent is valid — full stop."

I can see that each condition is doing real work, but I'm suspicious of a couple of moves here. For one thing, the author treats "withholding any relevant information" as automatically invalidating consent, which seems like it would make an enormous amount of everyday consent invalid — most of us don't disclose every fact that might matter to someone else. For another, the coercion condition seems to rule out any choice made under a threat, but what about threats that are morally legitimate, like "if you don't show up to work you'll be fired"? Is that really coercion in the same way a gun is? And the "jointly sufficient" claim at the end strikes me as too strong, but I can't articulate why. Could you go through this passage and tell me which parts a philosopher working on consent would accept, which they'd push back on, and whether the overall picture survives the pushback?
