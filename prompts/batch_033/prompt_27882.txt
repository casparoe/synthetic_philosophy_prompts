I've been covering science and ideas for a while now, and I've realized I have no idea what my actual view is about unrealistic assumptions in models. Like, I have strong feelings about specific cases but I can't extract a principle from them, and it's starting to bug me. Here are my verdicts — maybe you can tell me what rule I'm implicitly running, or what rule I should be running, and which of my verdicts it forces me to give up.

1. The ideal gas law — point particles with zero volume and no intermolecular forces. Completely fine. It's a first approximation, everyone knows the deal, it's useful across a huge range of conditions.

2. Frictionless planes in intro physics. Also totally fine. No one walks away thinking surfaces are actually frictionless.

3. The Modigliani-Miller theorem — firm value is independent of capital structure, but only under no taxes, no bankruptcy costs, no information asymmetries, and perfect markets. As a theoretical benchmark I think it's fine. But I get annoyed when people cite it loosely in policy debates without acknowledging the assumptions, which happens constantly.

4. Rational expectations in macroeconomics — the assumption that people form expectations as if they know the true model of the economy. I basically think this is not okay. It's not a simplification, it's a falsification, and the falsification drives the results. I don't care that it's "as-if."

5. Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium — infinite population, no mutation, no selection, no migration, random mating. Completely fine. It's a null model. You use it to detect departures, not to describe the world.

6. Black-Scholes option pricing — continuous trading, no transaction costs, constant volatility, geometric Brownian motion. As a starting point for thinking about options, fine. Traders who plugged numbers in without thinking about the assumptions were being reckless, but I don't blame the model for that.

7. Friedman's billiard player — you predict an expert's shots by assuming they compute optimal angles and velocities, which they obviously don't do consciously. I'm weirdly fine with this. It's an as-if model and it predicts well. But wait — how is this different from rational expectations? I'm suddenly aware that I'm being inconsistent and I don't know how to fix it.

8. Climate models that parameterize cloud formation crudely because we can't resolve clouds at grid scale. I'm fine with this, even though the parameterizations are known to be rough and they affect the projections. What else are you supposed to do? But I feel like I'm giving climate models a pass I don't give rational expectations models, and that bothers me.

9. The strong-form efficient market hypothesis — prices reflect all information, public and private. I think this is not fine as anything other than a toy, and the fact that it was taken seriously for so long did real damage. But I'm fine with Modigliani-Miller which shares a lot of its DNA. What gives?

So what's my principle? It's not "assumptions are fine if the model predicts well," because Hardy-Weinberg doesn't predict anything by itself and I'm fine with it. It's not "assumptions are fine if they're flagged," because rational expectations models flag their assumptions and I still don't like them. It's not "assumptions are fine if they're false but harmless," because I can't define harmless without just pointing at my verdicts. Is there a coherent position here, and if so, which of my verdicts does it force me to revise? Also, what would it say about a case I didn't list — agent-based models in epidemiology that assume every individual follows identical simple interaction rules?
