Longtime reader of the rationalist/EA blogosphere here, and I keep noticing that arguments about earning-to-give are almost always empirical — does the marginal impact actually exist, does the job do net harm — while the philosophical committments of the practice get left unexamined. So here's what I want. Take the practice, common in effective-altruism circles, of steering a talented young person into a high-paying job they would not otherwise take (quant finance, big tech, management consulting, whatever) on the grounds that they can donate a large chunk of the income to top cost-effectiveness-rated charities and thereby do more good than they would in direct work. Reconstruct what this practice presupposes, philosophically. I'm not after the empirical bets. I mean the deeper commitments: about whether harms and benefits are fungible across persons, whether the right way to evaluate a career is as a marginal contribution conditional on someone else taking the job, whether the good you can buy through a ranked charity is the right unit of moral accounting, what it says about moral demandingness or about the moral relevance of the job's own content. Does the practice strictly presuppose a consequentialist picture, or is it compatible with several? And does it presupose inconsistent things — for instance, trusting cost-effectiveness estimates where they exist while waving off the harms of the job itself as too hard to measure? What would have to change about the practice if you replaced its implicit view of value with one where some harms can't be offset, or some values don't trade off? Act as a professional philosopher working on ethics and the philosophy of social practices; think carefully, maybe start by brainstorming candidate presuppositions, and don't just give me the sympathetic version — I want the version that actually licenses the practice. At the end, say how confident you are in the overall reconstruction and single out the one point you're least sure about.
