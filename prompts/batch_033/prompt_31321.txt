I've come around to the view that the simulation argument, taken seriously, really does take the wind out of fanaticism about the far future. You know the position I mean: the one where a tiny probability of influencing the long-run future should dominate our priorities because the stakes are astronomical, so almost any chance of helping the far future swamps near-term concerns. My thought is that the simulation argument gives fanatics a specific new problem: if we're probably simulated, then the far future we'd be trying to steer is either the base world's future (which we may not be in a position to affect at all) or a simulated future that could be switched off, and our grip on the sign and size of our impact is even worse than the fanatic already admits. Tell me what would count against this. I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I especially want the strongest version of the obvious objection: that fanaticism only needs a nonzero probablity of a huge payoff, and the simulation argument doesn't drive that probability to zero. The nonzero-probability point is the one I suspect would actually move me, so steelman it rather than gesturing at it. Which things counting against my view would a defender of it have to concede, and which would we dismiss as not really engaging it? Is the view in danger of being insulated into unfalsifiability, of just collapsing into the thought that simulation considerations make far-future reasoning even more uncertain? And if it is that insulated, is that a problem for a view of this kind, or is it the right amount of caution?
