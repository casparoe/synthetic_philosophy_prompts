For a unit on causation by omission in an upper-level metaphysics seminar, I want a thought experiment built to order, one in which two standard accounts of omission-causation come apart in the direction that is harder to construct. Please design a realistic case — a hospital or a workplace, no trolleys and no exotic machinery — in which a counterfactual/dependence account of causation by omission denies that a specified agent's omission caused a specified harm, while a common-sense, selection-based account in the spirit of Hart and Honoré affirms that the very same omission caused it. By the counterfactual/dependence account I mean the view that an omission causes an outcome just in case the outcome would not have occurred if the agent had performed the omitted act, with the usual ceteris paribus hold-fixed qualifications. By the selection account I mean the view that causes are singled out as departures from the normal or expected course of events, so that a failure to perform an established routine can count as a cause of the ensuing harm in virtue of being a departure from the normal course, even when the harm would still have occurred through a further departure. The selection verdict must not rest on confusing blame with causation: the omitted act should be a genuine breach of a clear, established expectation, so that the selection account singles it out on principled grounds rather than moralized ones. The divergence should be produced by the causal structure of the case, not by leaving it vague what the agent would have done had they acted. Give each account its strongest, most sympathetic statement, and do not let either verdict rest on a crude version of the view. Keep the scenario itself to a single paragraph.

The case is for a seminar exercise on whether causal judgments about omissions track but-for dependence or departure from an expected routine, so the verdicts must be generated by the two accounts as characterized, not stipulated by the scenario.

After the scenario, verify the case against each specification. State the candidate omission and the harm, then do the checks in full: run the counterfactual test and explain why it denies causation; run the selection test and explain why it affirms causation; identify the structural feature of the case doing the work; say what each account says once that feature is removed; and finish with the best reply a counterfactual theorist could give, together with your assessment of whether it succeeds.
