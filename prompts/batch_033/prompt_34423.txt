I'm in a jurisprudence seminar right now and we've spent three weeks on statutory interpretation, with the textualists and purposivists basically yelling past each other. My final project is to write the document a committed textualist and a committed purposivist would produce after a good-faith adverserial collaboration — a joint statement both could actually sign, not a debate summary. Can you draft that for me?

I need it to have four seperate parts:
(1) a numbered section of agreed claims, and I want them substantive, not just the empty claim that statutes should be interpreted. I'm thinking things like: what both sides think the judge's job is, the difference between semantic meaning and what a speaker manages to communicate, which canons of construction both are fine with, and the fact that unprovided-for cases (the drafters didn't anticipate the fact pattern) exist and a court has to do something with them.
(2) a disagreements table where each side's view is stated in wording that side would accept, not the other side's caricature. So the textualist row can't say "we don't care about purpose" and the purposivist row can't say "we let judges rewrite the statute."
(3) the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one of them. I suspect one crux is whether "legislative intent" picks out a real thing or is just a metaphor for something constructed after the fact, and another is whether legislative history is too easy to game to be probative. But don't just take my guesses; figure out which cruxes are real and which are fake.
(4) for each crux, a concrete case or kind of evidence both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. The no-vehicles-in-the-park example is fine, but push past the stock version — what does each side say about a motorized wheelchair, a drone, an ambulance, a war memorial that's technically a "vehicle"? Also address whether "ordinary meaning" is fixed at enactment or can drift, and whether the relevant intention is the specific provision's or the whole statute's.

And one more thing: say which parts of the dispute you think are just verbal (people using "text" and "purpose" to mean different things) and which parts are genuine. My professor keeps saying a lot of the fight is that.

No bibliography or citations needed — just the document. But please don't make the agreed-claims section empty-ish or the disagreement rows slanted, because the whole point is that each side would actually sign the wording of their own position.
