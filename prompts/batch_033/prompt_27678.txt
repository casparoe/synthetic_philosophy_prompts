Last week in my philosophy of language reading group, two members got into a long argument about the Frege–Geach problem and I've been turning it over ever since. We had just read a chapter on expressivism, and the discussion turned to whether the embedding problem is fatal or merely a technical challenge that expressivists have more or less met.

Clara opened by saying the problem is straightforwardly devastating: if moral utterances express non-cognitive attitudes rather than beliefs with truth conditions, then there's no account of what "wrong" contributes to the meaning of "If lying is wrong, then getting your little brother to lie is wrong." In the antecedent, the speaker isn't expressing disapproval of lying — she's merely entertaining a condition — so the word must mean something different there than it does when asserted. But then the argument "Lying is wrong; if lying is wrong, getting your brother to lie is wrong; therefore getting your brother to lie is wrong" commits the fallacy of equivocation, and expressivism can't explain why it looks like a valid piece of reasoning. David pushed back: he said this demands from expressivism something we don't demand from anyone else. A realist says the inference is valid because the sentences express propositions that stand in entailment relations, but when you ask what "entailment" amounts to, the realist ultimately appeals to the impossibility of accepting the premises while rejecting the conclusion — which is a normative claim about what combinations of mental states are rationally permitted. So the expressivist can say exactly the same thing: the inference is valid because you can't coherently hold the attitudes expressed by the premises while withholding the attitude expressed by the conclusion. Clara replied that this conflates logical validity with psychological consistency. The inference from "Lying is wrong" and the conditional to the conclusion isn't just a fact about which combinations of attitudes we find uncomfortable; it's a fact about the logical structure of the claims themselves, which holds whether or not anyone has any attitudes at all. David answered that "logical structure of the claims themselves" is exactly the kind of mysterious realist posit the expressivist was trying to avoid, and that Blackburn's account of logical validity in terms of clashes of commitments gives us everything we need for moral reasoning — the rest is metaphysical decoration. Clara came back with what she called the real problem: even if Blackburn can handle simple conditionals, the embedding problem recurses. "If it's wrong to get your brother to lie if lying is wrong, then you should tell him so" embeds a conditional inside another conditional, and now the expressivist needs higher-order attitudes about higher-order attitudes, with no uniform account of what "wrong" contributes across all these contexts. David said this is just the compositionality problem that every semantic theory faces, and that realists haven't solved it either — they just help themselves to propositions and possible worlds without explaining how they compose. At that point we broke for coffee, and neither had budged.

I've been trying to figure out who had the better of the exchange, but the moves are tangled enough that I can't sort them. Please go through their argument step by step and adjudicate: at each turn, who scores the better point, and why? I'm looking for a committed verdict — not a survey of the literature or a "both sides have something to say," but a definite assessment of which moves land and which don't, and who emerges with the stronger overall position.
