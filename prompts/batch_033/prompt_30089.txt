I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am writing a piece on the paradox of tragedy — the question of why audiences deliberately seek out art that makes them sad, afraid, or distressed and nonetheless count the experience as good. I would like to close the piece with a sonnet, and I want the poem to do real conceptual work rather than merely gesture at the topic. Please use iambic pentameter and the Italian division of octave and sestet. The octave should state the puzzle in its sharpest form: the grief is foreseen, voluntarily sought, and yet the evening is judged a success partly because of it, not in spite of it. The volta at line nine should turn on the split in the literature between conversion-style views, on which the form or fictional distance transforms painful material into something pleasurable, and compensation-style views, on which the distress remains distress and is accepted as the price of some other value the work supplies. Let the sestet give each family its strongest articulation, and let the final two lines hold the two apart rather than decide between them. A philosopher of art should be able to identify which lines belong to which view, and a reader who has never heard of the debate should still find it a good poem.
