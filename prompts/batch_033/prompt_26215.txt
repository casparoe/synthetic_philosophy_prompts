Two researchers I know — call them Mira and Cal — have been arguing for months about whether "low-impact AI" is a coherent idea, and at a workshop dinner last week the debate spilled out in front of the rest of us.

Mira opened by saying that one of the more elegant results in AI alignment is the intuition that you can penalize a system for having a large impact on the world relative to some baseline, without needing a full specification of human values. The idea, as she put it, is that you compare the state the world would be in had the AI done nothing — or had it taken some minimally invasive action — to the state it actually produces, and you penalize divergence across a broad set of variables. That gives you a notion of "avoiding side effects" that doesn't require solving the whole value-specification problem. You don't need to know exactly what we want; you just need to know that we generally don't want the world radically rearranged as a byproduct of whatever narrow task we assigned.

Cal pushed back immediately. His first objection was that any choice of which variables to measure divergence along is already a value judgment. If you track the positions of every atom, you penalize the AI for doing anything at all, since even speaking a sentence moves air molecules. If you track only certain high-level features — say, economic indicators or ecosystem variables — you've already smuggled in a theory of what matters. So calling the measure "value-agnostic" is misleading: you've simply relocated the value specification into the choice of metric, and that choice is no less contested than the original problem.

Mira replied that this objection proves too much. Of course you need to pick some set of variables, she said, but the standard proposal is to use a very broad, multi-scale set — something like "all the features a human could notice or reasonably care about" — which is still far less demanding than specifying a utility function over outcomes. It's the difference between saying "don't change things too much along any dimension we might turn out to care about" and saying "make the world exactly this way." The first is a loose constraint that rules out extreme disruptions while leaving most of the future open; the second is a full blueprint. That's a real difference in how much normative work you're doing upfront.

Cal granted the distinction but said it doesn't rescue the proposal. "Don't change things too much" only functions as a constraint if you already know what counts as "too much" and along which dimensions — which just circles back to needing values, now under the guise of setting sensitivity thresholds. He then added a second objection: even if you could define a value-agnostic impact penalty, it would be useless for many of the tasks we actually want AI to perform. If you ask a system to cure a disease or redesign the energy grid, the whole point is to have a massive impact on the world. A low-impact penalty would either prevent the task from being accomplished or would need to be precisely calibrated to permit exactly the kinds of impact we endorse and forbid the rest — which, again, requires knowing what we endorse.

Mira conceded the point about high-impact tasks but said that was a problem for a different conversation. The immediate use case for low-impact measures, she argued, is AI systems doing bounded, narrow tasks — fetching objects, scheduling, data processing — where we genuinely don't want the system to reshape the world beyond the task. For those, a low-impact penalty is a useful safety tool that doesn't presuppose a complete theory of value, and the fact that it doesn't scale to every imaginable task is not an argument against using it where it does apply.

Cal came back: even in narrow-task settings, you have to decide which causal consequences count as part of the task and which count as side effects. If you ask a robot to retrieve your slippers and it knocks over a lamp while doing so, is knocking over the lamp an unacceptable side effect or just the cost of doing business in a cluttered room? If it moves a chair out of the way first, is that a side effect or part of the retrieval? Drawing that line requires knowing whether lamp-knocking and chair-moving matter to you, and how much — so the value problem reappears at the very first step of defining the baseline. The low-impact framework presupposes a principled distinction between intended task effects and side effects, and that distinction is itself value-laden.

The argument ended there because someone changed the subject, but I've been turning it over since. If you think the underlying issue is deeper than either of them articulated — for instance, if the debate trades on an ambiguity in what "avoid side effects" is supposed to mean — say what it is and whether it breaks one way or the other.
