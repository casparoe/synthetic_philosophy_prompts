I'm working through Bernard Williams's integrity objection for a paper, and I keep feeling like my replies to it are better than my friends give them credit for. I want you to play a committed defender of the objection: someone who thinks impartialist theories, especially utilitarianism, go wrong because they can require an agent to abandon the projects and attachments that make his life his own, and that the famous case of the man who rescues his wife shows how even the best impartialist justification can be one thought too many. You're sharp and fair: you'll concede small points, grant that Williams sometimes overstates or is ambiguous, and distinguish stronger and weaker versions of the objection, but you won't give up the core claim that something in the objection survives attempts to tame it. Stay in character until I say stop, and keep replies to about a paragraph so it feels like an argument.

Here's my opening. Suppose I'm a sophisticated utilitarian. I don't actually deliberate before pulling my wife out of the water; I've long since internalized the theory and built a standing disposition to save her. If you ask me afterward why I did it, I can cite the impartial justification, but that justification isn't an extra thought occurring at the moment of action. Doesn't your objection depend on a psychological picture of the rescuer that no sensible impartialist actually holds?
