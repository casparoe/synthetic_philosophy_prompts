I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, but I've fallen down a rabbit hole reading about dispositions — fragility, courage, solubility — and the weird cases philosophers have cooked up (finks, masks, reverse finks, all that). I'm trying to work out whether I have a coherent view, because its bearing on a character I'm writing. Here are my verdicts on the standard cases, going by gut.

A wine glass packed in styrofoam is still fragile. The packing doesn't change the glass. So masks don't bother me.

A live wire that would kill you if touched, but which has a safety cutoff built into it that disconnects the current the instant you touch it — I want to say it's not live. The wire-plus-cutoff, taken as a system, lacks the disposition. I'm fine with the fink removing the disposition.

But here's where I get tangled. Suppose the cutoff is a seperate device further down the circuit, not part of the wire. Now I want to say the wire is live and the seperate device is just a mask. That seems to make the metaphysics depend on how I draw the boundaries of the object, which feels wrong.

Then there's the sorcerer case: a sorcerer will turn the glass to steel if anything strikes it. I think the glass is fragile right up to the moment of the strike, even though the strike will never result in shattering. I can't tell if that's consistent with what I said about the wire with the built-in cutoff.

And the reverse fink: the person who lacks courage now but would become courageous if a crisis arose. I say they lack courage, full stop. But I also say the person whose courage is systematically masked by circumstance is courageous. Why should masks preserve the disposition but finks remove it? The assymetry bothers me.

Is there a single principle that gets all of these, or close? And if some of my verdicts have to go, which ones should I reconsider? I'm also wondering what this implies for my character — a woman who is genuinely kind but who never gets to manifest it because the situations that would call for kindness are blocked by the way others treat her. Is she kind, or is her kindness finked?

If I'm making a category mistake by even asking about dispositions this way, tell me. I'd rather hear that I've set the whole thing up wrong than get a tidy answer to a bad question. And please write in British English, and just in normal paragraphs — no bullet points or tables or headings, like a letter not a memo.
