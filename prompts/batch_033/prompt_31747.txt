I've been reading The Prince lately — not for a class or anything, just on my own — and I got really stuck on chapters 25 and 26, where Machiavelli talks about fortune and virtù. The way I understand it, fortune is like the river that floods, it's the stuff you can't control — luck, circumstance, the times you live in. And virtù is your ability to read the situation, adapt, act decisively, build the right defenses before the flood comes. What's fascinating to me is that Machiavelli says fortune controls maybe half of what happens, but the other half is up to us — if we have virtù. And then he says some people succeed with one style and fail with another because the times changed and they didn't change with them. That last bit really got me thinking.

Anyway, I've also been rewatching Breaking Bad, and I can't stop thinking about Walter White through this lens. On the one hand, Walt seems to have a ton of what Machiavelli would call virtù — he's adaptable, he's decisive, he reads situations brilliantly, he does whatever it takes. He builds an empire from nothing. But on the other hand, he also makes these huge unforced errors that come from his pride, his ego, his need to be recognized as the man. And by the end, fortune has definitely turned against him.

So here's what I want to know: does Walt actually have Machiavellian virtù, or does he just think he does? Is his downfall a failure of virtù (he couldn't adapt when the times changed) or is it just fortune reasserting its half of the deal? And more broadly, does Breaking Bad endorse Machiavelli's view of how the world works, or does it complicate it in some way that Machiavelli didn't see? I'd love a reading that takes both the show and the philosophy seriously — not just "Walt is Machiavellian" in the pop-culture sense. Aim for about 800 words max.
