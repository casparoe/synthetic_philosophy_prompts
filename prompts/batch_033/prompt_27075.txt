I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background. Over the past few months I've been reading, in an amateur way, about HCH and factored cognition, and I want to write a short essay that brings them into contact with how I think about software architecture. Before I draft it, I'd like comments on the idea, with special attention to whether I'm representing the underlying ideas accurately and whether the analogy is doing real work.

Here is the working idea.

The essay would start from the observation that both HCH and factored cognition depend on a decompositional picture of reasoning. In HCH, a human facing a hard question can recursively delegate subquestions to copies of themselves; in factored cognition, sophisticated reasoning is treated as something that can be broken into many small tasks that are mostly independent of one another, each of which can be produced and evaluated by someone who does not understand the whole. To an engineer this looks a lot like the claim that a monolith can be refactored into components with clean interfaces and local testability. I want to argue two things. First, that HCH is best read as an idealization of deliberation rather than as a procedure anyone expects to run: its value is that it specifies what would have to be true, at each interface between reasoning steps, for a complex piece of thinking to be trustworthy by construction. Second, that the idealization quietly imports assumptions that software engineering has learned to distrust: that context can be passed across interfaces without loss, that specifications capture everything a downstream step needs, that local checks compose into global correctness, and that there is no hidden coupling between the pieces. My intended conclusion is that the decompositional picture is valuable as a normative target, but that the distance between that target and how humans actually reason is where most of the philosophical difficulty lives, and that this distance is usually left unstated.

I'd like comments on the following. Have I characterized HCH and factored cognition correctly, and have I kept them properly distinct, or am I running them together in a way that would annoy someone who works on this? Is the reading of HCH as an idealization of deliberation fair, or does it miss something central to why the scheme was introduced? Is the software-engineering analogy interesting enough to carry an essay, or is it the first obvious thing an engineer would say and therefore not worth writing? Which specific steps in the argument would need the most defense, and are there places where the engineering analogy actively misleads rather than illuminates? Finally, does the conclusion follow from the setup, or is it stronger than the argument can support?
