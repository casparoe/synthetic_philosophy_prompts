I'm writing an essay that brings Rousseau's critique of theater to bear on a specific play, and I'd like your help developing the reading. In the Letter to d'Alembert on the Theatre, Rousseau argues that the pity we feel for tragic characters is morally counterfeit: we weep in the dark, experience a pleasant discharge of emotion, and leave congratulating ourselves on our sensitivity, having done nothing for any actual sufferer. The theater, on his account, does not correct the passions it depicts — it reinforces them under cover of purging them, and it substitutes a spectators' virtue for the real thing. He also argues that theater is corrosive to republican community because it gathers citizens together only to isolate them in silence before a fiction, teaching them to be passive where civic life demands activity.

I want to read Shakespeare's King Lear through this lens. The play is saturated with the spectacle of suffering — Lear on the heath, Gloucester's blinding, Cordelia's death — and it seems at times almost to anticipate the Rousseauian worry. Edgar's line "the worst is not / So long as we can say 'This is the worst'" reads like a diagnosis of the very self-distancing Rousseau condemns. Yet the play has been defended for centuries as morally deepening rather than narcotizing, and its relentless refusal of consolation — Lear dies holding Cordelia's body, convinced she might still breathe — seems designed to deny the audience the easy catharsis Rousseau describes.

Please give me a reading that takes both Rousseau's arguments and the play seriously, without reducing either to a straw man. Where does King Lear seem to confirm Rousseau's worries about theatrical emotion? Where does the play resist his critique, or offer a model of tragic experience that Rousseau's account cannot capture? Does the play itself — in its own dramatic terms — have an implicit theory about what it is doing to its audience, and does that theory withstand Rousseau's challenge? I am less interested in declaring a winner than in mapping the places where the encounter is genuinely difficult and productive.
