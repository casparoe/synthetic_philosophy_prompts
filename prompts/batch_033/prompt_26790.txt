I bought a few acres of scrubby woods last fall and I'm about to clear maybe half of it for a house and a lawn, and I've gotten myself into a weird headspace about it. The more I read about wild animal welfare, the more it seems like the mice, voles, rabbits, and songbirds on that land mostly live short, hungry, parasite-ridden lives and die badly as juveniles. If their lives are net negative, then me bulldozing their habitat might not just be less bad than it looks — it might actually be a welfare improvement, because fewer of them get born into those lives. That conclusion feels monstrous and I don't trust myself to think strait about it.

So: what's your actual credence in this proposition: over the next, say, 50 years, the summed welfare of the wild animals living on and around my land will be higher (less negative) if I clear half of it for a house and lawn than if I leave it alone? I want a number, and I want the decomposition. How much rides on whether wild animal lives are net negative on average, how much on whether the displaced animals just suffer the same or more on adjacent land, how much on whether the replacement land use actually has lower total wild-animal suffering, and whatever other terms you think belong. Give each sub-question a rough probability and a one-line justification, show how you combine them, and then tell me which term you're least confident in and what evidence would move it most.
