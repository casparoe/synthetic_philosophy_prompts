I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've been trying to write something about mechanistic anomaly detection — the idea that you can tell a model has gone off the rails because it's internal mechanism changed. But the whole debate seems to turn on what counts as the same mechanism, and I've ended up with a set of commitments that I'm not sure cohere. Here they are: (1) The right way to cash out the idea of the same mechanism is causal abstraction: two networks implement the same mechanism when you can line up their components so that interventions on one map onto interventions on the other and the downstream effects match. (2) So sameness isn't about weights or activations themselves; two networks with totally different weights, widths, or even layer counts can be running the same mechanism. (3) Still, I think representational similarity is real evidence of sameness — if you can train a linear map between the two networks' internal states, or their feature geometries look similar, that should push you toward saying they share a mechanism, not just that they happen to have the same outputs. (4) I also think there's a fact of the matter about whether two networks implement the same mechanism; it isn't just a modeling choice or a matter of how coarsly you look, even if we can't always recover the right alignment. (5) And I want to say that a network that classifies by shape and one that classifies by texture are different mechanisms, even if they agree on every input we've tested. What I can't tell is whether this is a stable package, or whether (1) quietly undermines (4) and (5), or whether (3) is in tension with (2) after all. Go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look that way, and rank the tensions by severity. Then tell me what the cheapest revision is and what it costs.
