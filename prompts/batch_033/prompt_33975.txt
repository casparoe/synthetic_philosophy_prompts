I recently found myself having to choose between two job offers: a lectureship that would leave time for my own writing, and a policy-research post that paid substantially more but offered little room for independent work. The choice unsettled me, and I have been turning over what, if anything, could have made one option rationally better than the other. Shortly afterwards, at a dinner, I listened to two friends, Eleanor and Ravi, argue about a mutual acquaintance who was facing a decision of much the same shape. I did not take part, and I found that I could not referee their exchange. I would be grateful for a careful, point-by-point adjudication of it.

Eleanor began with the claim that the options were incommensurable. Neither was better than the other, she said, and they were not equally good either: if they had been equally good, the person choosing would be indifferent between them, and she plainly was not. It followed that no rational comparison was available to her. Whichever option she chose, she would be choosing on non-rational grounds, and that, Eleanor added, was nothing to be ashamed of.

Ravi replied that this assumed that comparison comes in only three kinds — better, worse, and equally good. There was, he said, a fourth relation, parity. Two options could be on a par: in the same neighbourhood of value without one being better than the other and without their being exactly equal. He added that the small-improvement pattern showed why the threefold picture failed: if the options had been merely equal, then a small improvement in one would have made it better than the other, yet a slightly better-paid version of the policy post would still not have been better than the lectureship. What the acquaintance faced, he concluded, was a hard choice, and hardness of that kind was the mark of parity.

Eleanor answered that the diagnosis did no practical work. To say that the options were on a par gave the agent no reason to take one rather than the other, so the situation was exactly as she had described it: the reasons ran out before a verdict was reached. Ravi said that this missed the point. In a hard choice the given reasons did run out, but the choice was not therefore arbitrary. When options were on a par, the agent could settle the matter by committing to one of them and thereby create reasons for it that she had not possessed before. That was an exercise of normative agency, not arbitrariness.

Eleanor countered that this could not be right. Either there was a reason to commit to one option rather than the other, or there was not. If there was, then the options were not on a par after all; if there was not, then the commitment was unguided, and an unguided choice simply is an arbitrary one, whatever one calls it. Ravi responded that she was assuming that all reasons must pre-exist the choice and be discovered by the agent. In a parity choice, he said, the agent's commitment is itself the source of new reasons. Rationality includes this authorial capacity — the capacity to become the author of one's own life rather than a mere responder to reasons already given.

Eleanor said that this seemed to license wishful thinking. One cannot make an option right by committing to it; if commitment could create reasons regardless of how the options compared beforehand, the agent could equally commit to a clearly worse option and thereby make it the right choice. She added that the small-improvement pattern showed at most that the predicate 'better than' is vague: a small pay rise does not flip a non-better option into a better one because there is a borderline region, and borderline cases do not establish a fourth value relation.

Ravi replied that commitment was not unconstrained: it operated only among options already on a par, each good enough that either could be chosen without error, so a clearly worse option was never eligible. As for vagueness, he said, vagueness is a defect in our predicates, whereas parity is a positive relation between the options themselves; and a merely vague predicate would not explain the distinctive practical difficulty of a hard choice, which does not disappear when one sharpens one's language.

I have gone back and forth over which of them was right, and I cannot settle it. Please adjudicate the exchange turn by turn: say, for each move, what it establishes and where it goes wrong, and then give an overall verdict on which position is the more defensible, noting anything important that both of them have missed.
