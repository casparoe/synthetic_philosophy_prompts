I retired from a career in physical chemistry a few years ago and started reading philosophy of science seriously for the first time, mostly the stuff on models and idealization, and I've ended up with a set of views that I can't tell wether they hang together. I was hoping you could audit them rather than lecture me on the topic, because I'd like to know which of these are actually in tension, which only look like they are, and what the least costly fix is. Here's where I've landed:

(1) Predictive success is what ultimately tests a model. If a model keeps getting its predictions right, I dont much care whether its assumptions are literally true. I'm sympathetic to Friedman's F-twist, the idea that unrealistic assumptions don't matter as long as the predictions are good, and I think the as-if move in economics is basically fine.

(2) But I also think models like the ideal gas law or treating a solid as a bunch of independent harmonic oscillators genuinely explain the behavior they describe. They don't just forecast it. Explanation and prediction seem like different jobs to me.

(3) I think the point of most idealizations is to isolate a real causal factor by setting the other factors to zero, the way you set friction to zero to see what inertia does by itself. The model is false about the full situation but tracks something real that's doing part of the work.

(4) When two models predict equally well, I think we should prefer the one with less distorted assumptions. If one model needs a bunch of ad hoc fudge factors and another doesn't, I'll take the cleaner one even if the numbers come out the same.

(5) I'm inclined to say that treating a firm as if it solves an optimization problem is fine even though real managers don't do that, and that this is the same sort of move as in physics, not a special dodge for economics.

(6) I also think some idealizations are just computational conveniences and don't isolate anything real, a continuum approximation for a granular material, say. Those are fine for getting numbers but I wouldn't say they explain anything by themselves.

What I'd like is for you to go through this list and tell me which commitments actually pull against each other and which pairs only seem to. Rank the genuine tensions by severity, and for each one say what would have to give and what the cheapest revision is, and then tell me which single change would do the most to make the whole set hang together. Don't just say they're all fine or all in tension, I want to know where the real fault lines are. And please write it as continuous prose, the way you'd write an email or a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables.
