I'm a PhD student in philosophy of history, and my two supervisors have been arguing for years about what a historical explanation actually is. One of them holds a pretty strict covering-law line: an event is explained only when it's shown to follow from initial conditions plus general laws (or at least law-like statistical regularities), and the fact that historians never spell the laws out just shows they're being sloppy about their own practice. The other thinks narrative form itself does explanatory work — that fitting events into a story with a plot, a trajectory, and an outcome tells you something no list of laws and conditions could, and that trying to reduce 'the fall of the Roman Republic' to covering laws misses what the explanation is doing. They've mostly talked past each other, and I want to write the document they should have produced: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between a committed covering-law theorist and a committed narrativist about historical explanation.

Draft that statement for me. It should open with a numbered section of claims both of them can sign — about what any explanation of something like the July Crisis or the fall of the Roman Republic has to accomplish, about the role of counterfactuals, about why historians cite causes and not laws, about when an explanation can count as complete. Then a disagreements table, where each position is stated in wording its own side would except (not the other side's caricature), followed by the cruxes: the sub-questions where settling them would actually move at least one of them. For each crux, give a case or argument both sides agree bears on it, and record in advance what each predicts the case will show. I'm especially curious whether the narrativist can concede that narrative is a heuristic for picking out covering-law-ish regularities without giving up the claim that narrative does explanatory work of its own, and whether the covering-law theorist can concede that the same sequence can be narrated in incompatible ways without giving up the idea that at most one explanatory account is true. And if part of the dispute turns out to be verbal — over what counts as a 'law' or what counts as 'explanation' — say which part, and what's left over once that's set aside.
