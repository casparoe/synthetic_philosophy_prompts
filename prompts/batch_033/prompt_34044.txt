I am a retired natural scientist—my working life was spent in the experimental sciences—and I have only recently begun reading philosophy in earnest. In the interpretability literature I keep meeting a dispute that no one has yet put in the form I was trained to demand: a specification of what a theory must deliver before we can assess it. I want that specification for the claim that trained neural networks have representations.

Please write the desiderata: what any adequate theory of representation in trained neural networks must deliver. I want a theory good enough to settle, or at least sharpen, the dispute between the view that interpretability recovers features the network really uses and the view that interpretability only projects our concepts onto the network. Give me the requirements, with the case or consideration behind each, and tell me which are non-negotiable and which are merely the preferences of one camp.

At minimum the list should speak to the following, but do not confine yourself to them:

- Polysemanticity. Single units in real models respond to many unrelated inputs, so a theory cannot simply identify representations with individual neurons. What does this force a theory to say about the vehicle of representation?
- Superposition. Models appear to represent more features than they have dimensions, with features encoded as directions in activation space, often non-orthogonal. A theory must accommodate distributed, overlapping encodings rather than requiring one unit per feature.
- Recoverability versus use. A linear probe can be trained to recover a feature from a model's activations even when there is no evidence the model uses that feature in producing its behavior. A theory must distinguish being decodable from being represented in any causally relevant sense.
- Causal role. Interventions such as activation patching and ablations are the standard evidence that a direction or circuit matters to a computation, yet they push the model off its native distribution and can mislead. What standard of causal evidence must a representation claim meet?
- Content. The model was not trained with our concepts and has no access to our vocabulary. A theory must say what makes a direction a representation of a feature rather than merely a direction we find convenient to label that way.

State which distinctions a theory may not collapse: representation versus mere information-theoretic encodability; the vehicle of a representation versus its content; what a model can distinguish versus what it actually uses in a task; and features versus the circuits that compute with them.

Then give the results the theory must not contradict. I take the following to be established enough that an adequate theory must be consistent with them, and you should say if you disagree: superposition occurs in both toy models and large trained models; linear probes recover interpretable features from many layers; sparse autoencoders and similar methods find features that, when patched, have causal effects; and models also compute with features that are spurious or alien to us. A theory that rules any of these out by fiat is not adequate.

Now the part I most want. Mark each requirement as non-negotiable or as a camp-specific preference, and say which subsets, if any, cannot be jointly satisfied. I suspect there is a real conflict between demanding that representations be causally validated by interventions and demanding that representations be what the model actually computes in its own terms, since our only access to the latter is through interventions that distort. I also suspect a conflict between demanding a single canonical decomposition into features and the fact that features live in overlapping, non-orthogonal directions. Tell me whether these conflicts are real or only apparent, and whether the right response is to weaken a desideratum or to give up on a single canonical picture.

Finally, tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding the popular positions—unit-level realism, realism about distributed features, instrumentalism, and deflationism—and then give a brief scorecard of how each fares against the full list.
