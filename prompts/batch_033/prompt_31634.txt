I spent most of my working life as a geologist, and since retiring I have been reading my way through the philosophy of probability, largely to make sense of the hazard assessments I used to review. I keep noticing a pattern for which I cannot find a satisfactory name, and I would be grateful for a careful account of it.

Consider a particular volcano—say, a specific stratovolcano in a subduction zone—and the question of how probable it is that it will produce a significant eruption within the next thirty years. The answer seems to depend entirely on which class I place the volcano in: all volcanoes, all stratovolcanoes, all stratovolcanoes in subduction zones, all volcanoes of comparable magma chemistry, all volcanoes with a similar repose interval. Each class yields a different historical frequency, and the question itself does not force any one of them on me. Worse, I can in principle keep narrowing the class until it contains only this single volcano, at which point there is no frequency left to appeal to. The same difficulty appears in a medical prognosis: a patient is simultaneously a member of the general population, of an age cohort, of a group with a particular family history, and of a group with a particular biomarker, and the numbers differ from class to class.

I understand that some of this may be related to things I have already read about, such as Simpson's paradox or base-rate neglect, but neither seems to capture the specific worry that the choice of class is doing the work and no principle settles which class is the right one. Is there an established name for this problem, and how exactly does it differ from those neighbouring puzzles? I should say plainly that I am not after a bare label; I want to know how the problem is standardly formulated, where its edges are, and what the main positions are on whether it can be solved at all.
