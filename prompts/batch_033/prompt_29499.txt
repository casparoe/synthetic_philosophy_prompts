Two people in my reading group got into an argument about John Stuart Mill that I couldn't settle. The first said that Mill's late self-description as a socialist is flatly inconsistent with the liberalism of On Liberty: the harm principle and the defense of individuality presuppose a protected sphere of private choice, and collective ownership of the means of production would destroy that sphere, so either the early Mill or the late Mill must be wrong. The second replied that this reads socialism anachronistically, as state central planning; Mill's socialism was a program of worker-owned cooperatives and reformed property and inheritance, meant to spread independence and individuality to the working class, and he treated it as something to be reached gradually and voluntarily as people became fit for it, not imposed by the state. The first answered that this only relocates the problem: Mill's socialism is conditional on a moral and intellectual transformation of the working class, and On Liberty is precisely a warning against designing institutions around people becoming a certain kind of person, since that invites coercive paternalism. The second answered that On Liberty itself values voluntary experiments in ways of living and freedom of association, so a cooperative economy is just a large-scale experiment in living, and the conditionality is no more objectionable than Mill's own view that representative institutions suit only peoples who have reached a certain stage of civilization. Please adjudicate move by move, taking a definite position at each step: identify what each side gets right and what it conflates, rate each move, and say who is more right overall.
