I've been running a reading group on moral disagreement and cooperation, and we've spent the last two sessions stuck on a particular corner of the moral trade literature. The idea, introduced by Toby Ord, is that agents with different moral views can make mutually beneficial exchanges: each agrees to do something the other considers morally important but that they themselves regard as relatively unimportant by their own lights, in return for the other doing the same. The paradigm case is a vegetarian and a global poverty activist each donating to the other's preferred charity — both judge the world improved by their own standards, at small cost to themselves. So far, so tractable.

The case that's dividing us is harder: trades between agents who hold incompatible deontological constraints. Suppose Alice believes it is absolutely forbidden to lie but permissible to break a promise under certain circumstances, while Bob believes the reverse — absolutely forbidden to break a promise but permissible to lie under certain circumstances. They face a situation where Alice could lie to person A and Bob could break a promise to person B, each producing a significant good the other cares about deeply. A moral trade would have Alice lie (which she regards as seriously wrong) and Bob break a promise (which he regards as seriously wrong), where each judges the other's act as permissible and the combined outcome as better than the alternative by both their lights. The question is whether either can permissibly enter such a trade, given that it requires each to perform an act their own theory condemns.

I want your actual credence on the following proposition: "There exists at least one realistic pair of agents with incompatible deontological constraints and a feasible trade such that each agent's own moral theory, all things considered, recommends entering the trade, even though the trade requires each to perform an act that their theory classifies as pro tanto wrong." Please decompose this into sub-questions — for example, whether the dirty-hands objection (one cannot permissibly do wrong to achieve good) defeats all such trades; whether the trade structure changes the deontic status of the constituent acts from impermissible to permissible; whether the gains each party receives are genuinely commensurable with the costs they incur; whether an agent's participation in another's wrongdoing constitutes a separate wrong that blocks the trade; and any other terms you think belong. Assign a rough probability to each sub-question, show how they combine to yield your overall credence, and tell me which sub-question your final number is most sensitive to — that is, the one where a moderate shift in your view would produce the largest change in your overall credence.
