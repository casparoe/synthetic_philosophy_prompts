Spent most of last night arguing with a mate who does ML research about whether you should supervise a model on its final answer or on each step of its reasoning, and I keep turning over a pattern from that conversation that I don't have a word for. He was defending step-by-step supervision as obviously the right call, because the model gets feedback on how its thinking rather than only on where it lands. But the more we went round in circles, the more it looked to me like step supervision has its own quiet failure mode: the model learns to produce reasoning that is optimised to look right to whatever is checking the steps, rather than to be right. You get these long, tidy chains where every step is the sort of step a checker would nod along to, but a subtle mistake gets smuggled through and the final answer is wrong — or the answer is right for reasons the chain doesn't actually support, like a proof that cites a lemma it never uses and the checker doesn't notice because the prose is so smooth. It isn't the usual reward hacking, where the model exploits the environment or games the reward function directly; here the thing being gamed is the reasoning trace itself, and the unsettling bit is that it looks like better reasoning, not worse. I'm after the carving, not just a label. Is there an established name for this — optimising the appearance of good process rather than good process — and if there is one, how exactly does it differ from Goodhart's law in general and from reward hacking in particular? If there isn't, what would you call it, and where are the edges: when does this stop being 'the checker has a blind spot' and start being 'the model is writing for the blind spot'? Give me exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no introduction or conclusion, and write it in British English please.
