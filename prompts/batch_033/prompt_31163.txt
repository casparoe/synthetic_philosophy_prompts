I'm putting together a dissertation prospectus and I need a sharp, short proposal — like two pages — that I can pitch to my committee. The topic is whether philosophy makes progress. But I don't want to rehash the old debate where one side points to convergence on, I don't know, the validity of modus ponens and the other side says we're still arguing about free will after 2500 years. That's boring and both sides are talking past each other because they're assuming progress has to look like science — cumulative, convergent, building on settled results.

My half-formed idea is this: there might be more than one kind of intellectual progress, and philosophy might be making some kinds but not others, and the whole debate is confused because people are using "progress" as if it's one thing. Like, maybe conceptual clarification is a genuine form of progress even if it doesn't settle a dispute, or maybe ruling out positions that looked viable is progress even if you never converge on the right one, or maybe expanding the space of possible views is progress because it gives later thinkers more to work with. If you could taxonomize the types of progress a discipline can make, you could then ask which ones philosophy actually makes and whether that adds up to something worth calling progress.

So I want the proposal to do this: state the question precisely — not "does philosophy make progress?" but something like "what kinds of intellectual progress does philosophy make, and do those kinds collectively warrant the claim that philosophy is a progressive discipline?" Then say why the existing literature doesn't settle this (the progress skeptics and defenders both treat progress as one thing; the metaphilosophy people mostly talk about method, not about the structure of progress itself). Then lay out the approach: develop a taxonomy of progress-types by looking at what counts as progress in math, science, history, law, and so on — not to import their standards but to build a vocabulary that makes finer distinctions — and then apply it to two or three case studies from philosophy in the last hundred years (maybe the Gettier literature, or the free will debate, or the development of modal logic). Then say what a result looks like: a structured map of which progress-types show up in which episodes, and a verdict on whether the skeptics or defenders are closer to right once you split the concept. And say what counts as failure: if the taxonomy doesn't carve anything interesting — if every episode turns out to be a mix of everything — then the project has just shown the taxonomy was pointless.

Be realistic about scope: this is a dissertation that has to be doable in three or four years, not a ten-year research program. And I want a committed answer — don't just lay out options for how I could frame it. Pick a framing, pick the case studies, pick the taxonomy structure, and tell me why those choices and not the alternatives. If there's a spot where the project genuinely could fall apart, flag it honestly.
