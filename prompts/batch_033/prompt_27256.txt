I have been trying to work out what I actually think about legal moralism, the question at the center of the Hart–Devlin debate: when, if ever, may the criminal law prohibit conduct simply because it is widely regarded as immoral, even when no non-consenting person is harmed or wronged? As I understand the two sides, Devlin held that a society may enforce the morality it shares, because that shared morality is partly constitutive of the society's cohesion; Hart held that the criminal law's proper business is the prevention of harm to others, not the enforcement of morals as such. My own case-by-case judgments refuse to line up neatly with either side.

Here are my verdicts, reported as I find them before theorizing:

1. Two adult siblings have consensual sex in private, using contraception, and no one else ever learns of it: this should be a crime.
2. Two adults of the same sex have consensual sex in private, and no one else is affected: this should not be a crime.
3. An adult pays another adult for sex, with no trafficking, force, or fraud: this should not be a crime.
4. Two competent adults fight a duel to the death in private, both having freely consented: this should be a crime.
5. Two adults box professionally under standard rules, each consenting to a contest in which serious injury is a foreseeable risk and blows are thrown with intent to disable the opponent: this should not be a crime.
6. A crematorium employee uses a corpse in a way that would deeply distress the family if they knew, in circumstances where the family will never find out: this should be a crime.
7. A person burns their own country's flag in public: this should not be a crime.
8. A competent adult with a terminal illness asks a doctor for a lethal prescription and takes the drug themselves: this should not be a crime, provided safeguards are met.
9. A person has sexual contact with an animal: this should be a crime.
10. A person privately uses a drug whose main risk is serious harm to the user alone: this should not be a crime.

I keep reaching for a Hart-style harm principle, something like: the criminal law may prohibit conduct only when doing so prevents harm or serious wrong to non-consenting others. That principle seems to deliver (2), (3), (5), (7), (8), and (10). But it strains to explain (1) if the siblings are both consenting adults, (6) if the dead cannot be harmed, and it cannot explain the split I feel between (4) and (5), or between (4) and (8), since each involves a consenting adult and serious harm. A Devlin-style principle, by contrast — enforce the community's shared morality when doing so protects the social fabric — would reach (1), (4), and (6), but at the cost of criminalizing (2), (3), (7), (8), and (10), which I am not willing to do.

What I would like is a reconstruction, not a survey. Is there a single principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures these verdicts, or most of them with the remainder explained away? State the principle precisely, indicating which clause is motivated by which of my cases. Apply it to at least one case I have not listed, such as a person who contracts to become the permanent slave of another, or a person who consents to being killed and eaten. Then tell me honestly which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and whether I should overturn those verdicts rather than complicate the principle further. If the best principle forces me to abandon one of my judgments, say which one and why.

Please also say how confident you are in the principle you defend, and single out the point about which you are least sure.
