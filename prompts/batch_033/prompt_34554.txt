So I've been kicking around some ideas about the absent-minded driver problem and I'd love some feedback on these notes. I'm trying to figure out if there's an essay here or if I'm just confused.

Quick recap of the problem: A driver is on a highway with two identical-looking exits. Exit 1 goes to a bad area (payoff 0), Exit 2 goes home (payoff 4), and missing both means ending up far away (payoff 1). The driver is absent-minded: whenever he reaches an exit, he doesn't remember whether he's already passed one. So from inside the car, each exit feels exactly the same. At the planning stage (before driving), the driver can decide to exit with probability p at whichever exit he finds himself at. The planning-stage calculation gives an optimum of p = 1/3, for an expected payoff of about 1.33.

Here's the thing I keep getting stuck on. When you're actually at an exit, you have to update your beliefs about which exit you're at. But your belief depends on p itself — if p is small, the fact that you're at an exit at all is strong evidence that it's the first exit (since if it were the second, you'd probably have already exited at the first). This means the action-stage expected payoff, conditional on being at an exit, is different from the planning-stage expected payoff. And for p = 1/3, the action-stage calculation says you should deviate. So the plan isn't self-ratifying.

What I want to argue is that this reveals something about the difference between "having a plan" and "being able to follow a plan when you can't verify your location within it." Specifically, I think the only strategies that are stable under action-stage reflection are the pure strategies (p = 0 or p = 1). But both of those are worse than the mixed-strategy planning optimum. So there's a genuine tension: rational planning recommends a strategy that rational execution undermimes.

This seems like it should connect to broader things: (1) the literature on dynamic inconsistency and sophisticated vs. naive choice, (2) maybe something about evidential decision theory, since the action-stage reasoning looks like "what does my exiting tell me about which exit this probably is?", and (3) the question of whether precommitment is even coherent if you know your future self will have different information and will reason differently.

Anyway, what I'd love: do you buy the claim that only pure strategies are self-ratifying here, or is there a way to rescue mixed strategies that I'm not seeing? And does the EDT angle seem promising or is that a dead end? Keep it to maybe 400 words max, I'm mostly trying to figure out if this line of thought is worth developing into a proper essay.
