I'm in a grad seminar on Bayesian epistemology and next week I have to give the main presentation defending the claim that the diachronic Dutch book argument doesn't establish conditionalization as a norm of rationality. My line is roughly this: the synchronic arguement already only shows that credences violating the probability axioms correspond to a set of fair betting prices that no one would accept as a package — that's a consistency condition on prices, not an epistemic requirement. For the diachronic version it's worse: the bookie's strategy assumes that my current self is already committed to the credence function I'll have after I learn E, as if I'd endorse my future self's fair prices now. But that's exactly the thing in dispute, so the argument feels circular. I'll also end by saying that a genuinely diachronic norm needs a bridge principle connecting the fact that a policy avoids sure loss over time to the claim that it's rationally required now, and the standard argument just helps itself to that bridge.

The seminar is taught by a professor who's a committed subjectivist Bayesian and thinks the Dutch book arguments are the best reason to require probabilistic coherence, so she's going to defend the home turf. There are two grad students who lean objective Bayesian and will worry that if I give up conditionalization there's no update rule left, and a stats PhD student who thinks this whole thing is navel-gazing and will probably say that real Bayesian practice just is conditionalization. There's also a quiet guy who reads a lot of decision theory and might ask something about the reflection principle.

Please act as a senior formal epistemologist who has run these seminars for years and knows how each of these people thinks. Before you draft anything, brainstorm the two or three commitments each faction in the room has that will drive their objections, then turn those into the questions I'll actually get. I want the prep sheet in these sections, in this order:

1. "The room" (~120 words): who will push back, and from what commitment.
2. "The objections, in the order they'll actually come" (~800 words): each objection phrased the way that person would actually say it, a damage rating out of 5, a two-minute reply, and where that reply is soft. Order by how likely and how damaging, not by logical order.
3. "Concede this up front" (~100 words): what I should give away in the talk or in the first answer so it doesn't get used against me later.
