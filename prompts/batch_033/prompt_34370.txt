I'm taking a graduate seminar on the philosophy of interpretation, and we've been working through the debate between intentionalists and anti-intentionalists about what fixes the meaning of a literary text. One of my classmates has been defending a version of the anti-intentionalist position by leaning heavily on what she calls the "parental analogy." She says: a text is like a child. The author conceives it, carries it through a long gestation, and eventually delivers it into the world. At that point, the text — like the child — becomes an independent entity. The author's intentions for it are no more determinative of its meaning than a parent's hopes and plans are determinative of who their adult child turns out to be. The meaning of a text is constituted by the interpretive community that receives it, just as a person's identity is constituted by their social interactions, not by their parents' designs. From this, she concludes that appealing to authorial intention to settle interpretive disputes isn't just a practical shortcut we sometimes need — it's a conceptual mistake. It treats a text as though it were still tethered to its origin when in fact it has been cut loose.

I think the analogy is doing real philosophical work in her argument, and I'd like to press on it. I can see some obvious points of contact: there's a creator, a created thing, a moment of release, and a subsequent history in which the creator's authority wanes. But I'm worried the analogy is importing features of personhood — autonomy, the capacity for self-revision, the possession of interests — that don't apply to texts, and that these imports are doing the normative heavy lifting. Could you help me audit the analogy systematically? Specifically: (1) provide a table of correspondences between the source domain (parent–child) and the target domain (author–text); (2) identify the significant disanalogies and say which ones matter for the conclusion she wants to draw; (3) assess whether the anti-intentionalist conclusion survives once those disanalogies are acknowledged; and (4) offer a better analogy, if one exists, that captures whatever insight hers contains without the problematic baggage.
