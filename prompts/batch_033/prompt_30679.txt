The paradox of fiction has been debated in something like its modern form since Colin Radford's 1975 paper on Anna Karenina, with roots stretching back to Augustine and Hume. The core puzzle is familiar: we weep for characters we know don't exist, feel dread at monsters we know aren't real, and yet these responses seem to violate the commonsense principle that emotions require belief in the existence of their objects. After fifty years of proposals — pretense theories, thought theories, irrationalism, illusionism — the debate feels stuck. Every solution seems to handle some cases cleanly and stumble on others (horror films vs. tragic novels, involuntary startle vs. lingering grief), and no proposal has attracted anything like a consensus.

I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it a verbal dispute about the word "emotion"? Are the two sides — roughly, those who think the paradox reveals something deep about emotion and those who think it reveals something about fiction — answering different questions under one heading? Is there a hidden empirical premise about the nature of emotion that, if settled by psychology, would dissolve the philosophical problem? Or are we looking at bedrock intuitions about what counts as a genuine emotional state, where neither side can argue the other into submission?

Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people working on this, and don't present a contested view as the consensus. And give me an honest answer about what, if anything, would count as progress — a distinction, an empirical finding, a change of question — or whether we should expect the impasse to persist indefinitely.
