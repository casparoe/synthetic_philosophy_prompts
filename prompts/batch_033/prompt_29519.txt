I'm taking a course on ancient philosophy, and we've just finished the sea battle passage in chapter 9 of Aristotle's De Interpretatione. The instructor stressed how easily the argument gets misremembered, and I now suspect that a short article I read in a general-audience philosophy magazine has done exactly that. The article summarizes the passage as follows: 'Aristotle saw that if every statement is already either true or false, then it was already true the day before that there would be a sea battle, and if it was already true, the battle was inevitable. Since he wanted to leave room for things that could still go either way, he concluded that statements about future contingents — for instance, the claim that there will be a sea battle tomorrow — are neither true nor false until the event actually occurs. In other words, Aristotle rejected the law of excluded middle for these statements: for a future contingent, neither it nor its negation is true, so the alternatives are not exhaustive. Because modern logic restored bivalence as a basic principle, logicians today mostly regard the sea battle argument as an intriguing but confused piece of ancient fatalism.' The piece is confident and clearly written, but I have a nagging feeling that several of these moves are too quick — especially the slide from 'true' to 'inevitable' and the claim about excluded middle — though I can't quite articulate why. Could you give me an accuracy audit of this summary? I'd like to know which parts a careful scholar of Aristotle would accept and which are contested or subtly misrepresented; how bivalence and the law of excluded middle should be distinguished in an accurate account of the passage; whether the fatalist inference at the heart of the argument is one Aristotle actually endorses as presented; and whether the closing claim about how modern logicians treat the problem follows from anything true in the summary or is itself another misrepresentation.
