I have become convinced that epistemicism about vagueness is correct: for any vague predicate, there is a sharp but unknowable boundary between the cases to which it applies and those to which it does not. Vagueness, on this view, is a matter of irremediable ignorance, not semantic indeterminacy. I want to know what would count against this position — not merely what its critics have said, but a systematic map of its exposure. What does epistemicism predict or commit itself to that its rivals (supervaluationism, contextualism, degree theories) do not, and where could those commitments turn out to be liabilities? In particular: under what conditions would higher-order vagueness become a fatal problem rather than a difficulty the view can absorb? Are there conceivable empirical results — from psycholinguistics, from developmental studies of category acquisition, from the neuroscience of perceptual categorization — that would tell against epistemicism specifically, as opposed to merely being awkward for everyone? What would it mean for the view if a semantic theory of vagueness succeeded in explaining the phenomena (penumbral connections, the forced march sorites) without positing sharp boundaries, and how would we recognize such a success? I also want to know whether epistemicism, as it has been developed, has been qualified in ways that insulate it from counterevidence — and if it has, whether that is a defect in a theory of this kind or simply the appropriate response to the subject matter. Rank the defeaters from decisive to merely awkward, and distinguish those an epistemicist would concede as genuine points against the view from those they would dismiss as question-begging.
