Two of my colleagues got into it at a department happy hour last month about the university's new retirement plan. The administration switched new hires from opt-in to opt-out: you're automatically enrolled at a 5% contribution rate unless you fill out a form to change or cancel it. I wasn't part of the argument but I was sitting right there, so I remember the back-and-forth pretty well. Here's roughly how it went.

(1) Mark said it was paternalistic and wrong. The university is treating faculty like children who can't manage their own finances. Respect for autonomy means letting people choose for themselves without the institution stacking the deck.

(2) Sarah replied that calling it coercive is absurd — anyone can opt out with one form. The evidence on opt-out enrollment is overwhelming: it dramatically raises savings rates and prevents real harm. If you can keep people from being broke at seventy at the cost of five minutes of paperwork for the few who genuinely want out, that's a good trade.

(3) Mark said that's exactly the logic of the nanny state: you substitute your judgment for mine because you're convinced you know what's good for me better than I do. The paperwork isn't the point. The point is the default exploits inertia and psychological biases to steer people toward what the designers want. That's manipulative even if it's not coercive.

(4) Sarah said that's too quick. Every choice architecture has a default — there's no neutral way to present options. The old opt-in system also had a default (not enrolled), and that default also exploited inertia, just in the direction of undersaving. So the question isn't whether to have a default but which default to have. Picking the one that leads to better outcomes isn't manipulation; it's responsible design.

(5) Mark came back and said there's a difference between an accidental default and one deliberately chosen to expoit known biases. The old system wasn't designed by anyone to manipulate — it was just the absence of a program. The new one was crafted by people who read the behavioral economics literature and explicitly set out to use cognitive biases against their own employees.

(6) Sarah said intention doesn't matter to whether people are better off. And his distinction between accidental and designed defaults collapses anyway: once you know that any default will shape behavior, keeping the old default is itself a choice — and one that predictably leads to worse outcomes. Refusing to change it because you didn't personally design it is just negligence dressed up as neutrality.

At that point they noticed everyone else had left and called it a night. I've been turning it over since and honestly can't decide who gets the better of each exchange. Could you adjudicate this move by move? For each of the six steps, say who has the stronger argument and why, and flag anything important that both of them are missing.
