I'm teaching an upper-level undergrad seminar on philosophy of mind — about 14 students, one 90-minute session. We've just read Dennett's "Quining Qualia," and most of them hate it. They're convinced qualia are obviously real and Dennett is just being difficult. I want an activity that makes them feel the instability of the concept from the inside, before we discuss the paper.

Here's the rough idea I have: a game built around the Chase and Sanborn cases from the paper. Participants get assigned a "private taste profile" for coffee — say, a little card that tells them what they supposedly experience when they sip it (bitter-forward, bright and acidic, smooth and chocolatey, etc.). Over several rounds, their profile might change in one of two ways: either their *judgment* changes (they used to like bright coffee, now they prefer chocolatey) or their *qualia* invert (what they used to call "bright" now tastes like what they used to call "chocolatey," but they keep using the same words because that's how they learned them). The catch is: from the inside, these two kinds of change feel identical. Participants have to make decisions each round — which coffee to order, how to describe it to a friend, whether to adjust the espresso machine — and then we compare notes.

I want them to discover, by playing, that there's no fact of the matter they can point to that distinguishes "my qualia inverted" from "my preferences shifted" or "my memory is faulty" or "I'm just using words differently now." The debrief would walk through Dennett's four properties (ineffable, intrinsic, private, directly apprehensible) and show how each one failed them during the game.

But I'm not sure about the mechanics. Should the changes happen publicly (everyone sees a card swap) or privately (only the participant knows)? How many rounds? Should there be money or points involved to force them to actually care about getting it right? And I'm worried the whole thing might just feel like a setup — like I've rigged a game to make a philosophical point and they'll resist it on those grounds. Can you flesh this out into something runnable: rules, materials, timeline, debrief structure, and an honest note on what the activity can't capture and where the facilitator has to fill in?
