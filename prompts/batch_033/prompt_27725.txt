i run a small philosophy reading group at my job — im a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, so this is all self-taught and probabaly a bit off in places. we've been reading about panpsychism and everyone keeps getting stuck on the combination problem. the version that really bugs us is the subject-summing problem: if a bunch of tiny subjects each have thier own point of view, how do you get one big unified point of view out of them? points of view dont seem like the kind of thing that sums the way mass or charge sums. the move i keep seeing is to posit some phenomenal bonding relation that glues micro-subjects into a macro-subject, but to me that just relabels the problem unless you can say what the relation actually does.

i want to run a 90-minute session for about 12 people, mostly engineers, that makes them feel this problem in their bones before we name it. no lecture up front, no just-describing-a-thought-experiment: i want a real activity or game where the participants themselves try to build a macro-subject out of micro-subjects and run into the wall on their own. we think about composition all day in code, and composing objects is easy because an interface just calls another interface; but a point of view isnt an interface, and i want the activity to force them to feel exactly where that analogy breaks.

what i want them to come away seeing is: (1) aggregating physical stuff feels fine, but aggregating subjects or points of view seems to go wrong, and why; (2) the bonding-relation move as something they'd be tempted to invent themselves once they're stuck, so it stops looking like a cheat and starts looking like a real theoretical option with real costs; and (3) the difference between the constitutive view (macro-subjects are literally made of micro-subjects) and the emergentist view (macro-subjects are something over and above).

constraints: no real money, conference room, whiteboards and index cards are fine. include the rules, roles, a timeline, and a debrief that maps what happened in the activity onto those positions and concepts (constitutive panpsychism, emergentism, subject-summing, phenomenal bonding) without me lecturing for more than about ten minutes. also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time i run it, and be honest about which part of the actual philosophical problem the activity cant capture and ill have to just supply myself.
