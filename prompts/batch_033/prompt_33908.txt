Okay so in my philosophy of logic reading group we got into this huge argument about Lewis Carroll's tortoise argument — you know, the one from "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles." Quick summary of the setup (everyone in the group agrees on this): Achilles is trying to get the Tortoise to accept proposition Z, given that the Tortoise accepts (A) and (B) — A and B logically imply Z by modus ponens. The Tortoise accepts A and B but won't accept Z. Achilles says: well if you accept A and B, you must accept Z. The Tortoise says: fine, write that down as (C): "If A and B are true, Z must be true." The Tortoise now accepts A, B, and C — but still won't accept Z. So Achilles writes (D): "If A, B, and C are true, Z must be true." The Tortoise accepts D but still won't accept Z. And this regress goes on forever.

So here's how the argument went:

(1) Mira kicked things off by saying the Tortoise is making a genuine and deep point: deduction can't be justified without circularity. The Tortoise is showing that you can't get from premises to a conclusion without a rule of inference, and if you try to add the rule *as another premise*, you're right back in the same position. So deduction depends on something that can't itself be justified by deduction — at some point you just have to accept the rule, which is a real epistemological problem. There's a gap between accepting premises and accepting what follows from them, and no amount of adding more premises closes the gap.

(2) Derek said nah, the Tortoise is just confused. Accepting a rule of inference isn't the same kind of thing as accepting a proposition. The Tortoise treats modus ponens as if it were a premise you could write down and add to the list — but it's not a premise, it's a rule for how to move *between* premises. The infinite regress only happens because the Tortoise is making a category mistake. Once you see that rules and propositions are fundamentally different kinds of things, the whole problem dissolves.

(3) Mira came back hard: "but that's just declaring the problem solved by fiat!" She said: you're saying rules are different from propositions — fine, but *why should the Tortoise, or anyone, accept a rule?* If you say "because it's rational to do so," you're using the very notion of rationality that's in question. If you say "because it works," you're giving a pragmatic justification, which is fine, but that's not the same as *justifying deduction*. So the Tortoise still has a point: at the bottom, we can't give a non-circular justification for our most basic rules of inference. Saying "they're rules, not propositions" doesn't answer the question — it just relabels the thing you're refusing to justify.

(4) Derek replied: look, the Tortoise literally can't even state the problem without using deduction. When the Tortoise reasons "I accept A, B, and C, but I still don't see why I must accept Z," the Tortoise is *itself* engaged in a piece of reasoning. The whole skeptical challenge presupposes the very thing being questioned. So the Tortoise's position is self-undermining — you can't coherently ask for a justification of deduction because the very act of asking already employs it. That's not a pragmatic punt; it's a transcendental point about the conditions of intelligible thought.

(5) Mira shot back: "exactly! That's my point!" The fact that you can't step outside deduction to justify it is *precisely* the problem. You're saying the Tortoise can't coherently ask the question — but that doesn't answer the question, it just shows we're stuck inside a circle. The Tortoise identified a genuine limit on what we can justify. Saying "well you can't even ask without begging the question" doesn't dissolve the problem — it IS the problem. The circularity isn't a refutation of the Tortoise; it's what the Tortoise was trying to get us to see all along.
