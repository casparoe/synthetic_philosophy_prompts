so i just rewatched Arrival (the Villeneuve one) and i can't stop thinking about the whole Louise/Hannah thing. quick recap in case it's been a while: Louise learns the heptapod language, which lets her experience time non-linearly, so she knows before she even conceives her daughter Hannah that Hannah will get a rare disease and die as a teenager. and she goes ahead and has her anyway, knowing exactly what's coming.

i recently fell into a wikipedia hole and ended up reading about something called the procreation asymmetry. the basic idea (as i understand it) is: there's a strong moral reason not to bring a miserable person into existence, because the suffering that person would experience is bad and we should prevent it. but there's no symmetrical reason to bring a happy person into existence — the absence of that happiness isn't bad if there's no one around to be deprived of it. preventing suffering has a kind of moral weight that creating happiness doesn't. (i think this is associated with Benatar and some others but i'm fuzzy on the details.)

and i feel like Arrival is basically a head-on collision with this idea. Louise knows her daughter will suffer horribly and die young, and she chooses to create her anyway. and the film doesn't frame this as selfish or wrong — it frames it as this profound, almost transcendent affirmation of life, even a life cut short. the joy Hannah experiences and the love Louise feels for her are treated as worth the grief.

so i want someone to read Arrival through the lens of the procreation asymmetry and tell me what's actually going on here. a few specific things i'm wondering about:

- does the film successfully challenge the asymmetry, or does it just emotionally overwhelm it? like, is there an argument buried in the film or is it just making you feel the love and hoping you won't notice the philosophical problem?
- does Louise's foreknowledge actually change the moral calculus? in ordinary procreation you're rolling dice — you don't know if your kid will suffer. but Louise knows with certainty. does that make her choice worse (she can't plead ignorance) or does the certainty somehow change the nature of the act?
- the film's whole non-linear-time metaphysics: if the future is already fixed and Louise is just living out a timeline that already exists, is she even making a choice? does that let her off the hook, or is that a cop-out?
- and here's what i really can't shake: if the asymmetry is right, then Louise's choice looks like a moral mistake dressed up as courage. but if the film is right and her choice is genuinely admirable, what does that tell us about the asymmetry? is there a way to reject the asymmetry without saying we have a duty to pop out as many happy babies as possible?

lead with your bottom line — what's your overall verdict on whether the film lands a real blow against the asymmetry or not — and then walk me through the reasoning. no preamble, no restating the question.
