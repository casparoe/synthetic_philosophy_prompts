I've been circling epistemology for a few years, and the one dispute I can't get a handle on is the stalemate between contextualists, subject-sensitive invariantists, and purist invariantists about knowledge attributions. Everyone has seen the same bank case and airport case a thousand times: in the low-stakes version people happily say 'I know the bank is open,' and in the high-stakes version they pull back and say they don't. The contextualist says the word 'knows' shifts with the speaker's context, like 'tall' or 'flat,' so both reports can be true because they express different propositions. The subject-sensitive invariantist says no: 'knows' means the same thing in both contexts, but whether you actually know can depend on your practical interests, so high stakes really can cost you knowledge. The purist says the word and the knowledge are both invariant, and the high-stakes hesitation is explained by something other than a change in truth conditions — salience, the norms of assertion, what it's reasonable to act on. What bothers me is that all three camps accept the same surface data, the experimental philosophy crowd has gone and checked what ordinary speakers say, and still nobody moves. It's been picked over ad nauseum, and each side has a story about why the other side's data doesn't cut it.

My working theory is that the contextualists and the subject-sensitive invariantists are closer than they look: they agree that what you can appropriately say and do with 'I know' shifts with stakes, and they're mostly fighting about where to locate that shift — in the semantics of the word or in the knowledge relation itself. If that's right, the real standoff is between them and the purists, and it might come down to whether the bank-case intuitions are evidence about knowledge or just evidence about what people will say and do under pressure. But I've also worried it's more tangled than that: maybe it's a verbal dispute about what the word 'knows' is for, or a substantive dispute about how much your practical situation can affect your epistemic standing, or two seperate questions — one about language, one about the mind — being run together under one heading.

I want a diagnosis, not a verdict and not a survey of the three positions. What kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it verbal or substantive — about the facts, about which cases count as data, or about starting intuitions neither side can argue for? Is there a hidden empirical or formal question doing the work? Is the impasse a sign that the question is ill-posed, or just that it's hard? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a distinction, an experiment, a formal result, a change of question — or should I expect none? Keep it under 900 words, and no citations or reading lists.
