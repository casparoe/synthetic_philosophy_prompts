A recurring topic in value theory is whether the relation "better than" is transitive. Larry Temkin and others have offered spectrum arguments suggesting that it may not be: one can construct a chain of states of affairs A₁, A₂, …, Aₙ such that each seems better than its predecessor, yet Aₙ does not seem better than A₁. A stock example involves gradually trading off between two values—say, mild headaches across a large population against intense suffering in a single individual—where each step in the chain seems like an improvement but the endpoints reverse the intuitive ranking.

Write a dialogue between two philosophers who meet at a conference. One defends the transitivity of "better than" and argues that spectrum arguments reveal inconsistent pairwise judgments rather than genuine intransitivity. The other defends the view that "better than" is not always transitive, drawing on the spectrum argument literature. Both characters should be well-informed and argue in good faith, responding directly to each other's points. The dialogue should touch on the analogy to the Sorites paradox, the distinction between intransitivity and incommensurability or parity, and what the practical upshot would be for moral decision-making if "better than" turned out to be intransitive. Write the dialogue in American English.
