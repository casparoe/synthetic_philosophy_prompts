The standard asymmetry line says that in the lottery case your belief that ticket 1 will lose is based on purely statistical evidence — you've got no individual reason to pick out that ticket — whereas in the preface case your belief that there's an error somewhere in the book is based on inductive evidence about your own track record of fallibility, which is evidence of a different epistemic kind. But I don't buy that this distinction does the work people claim it does. In both cases you're reasoning from base rates: the base rate of winning tickets in a fair lottery, and the base rate of errors in books written by careful people. If the preface belief is rationally permissible and the lottery belief isn't, that's not because the evidence differs in kind — it's because we've smuggled in an unprincipled intuition that statistical evidence is epistemically second-class. So tell me: what exactly does the asymmetry consist in, and why should I think it's genuinely epistemic rather than just a psychological quirk about how we happen to process different kinds of uncertainty?
