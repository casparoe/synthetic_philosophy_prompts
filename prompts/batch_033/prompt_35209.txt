I run a philosophy-for-scientists reading group — computational neuroscientists and a few machine-learning researchers, no formal philosophy — and next month I'm leading a two-hour session on illusionism about phenomenal consciousness. The group will arrive convinced that the felt character of experience is the one thing a person cannot be wrong about, and that any view denying it must be either a joke or a refusal to introspect. Don't explain illusionism to me; give me a plan for making the position intelligible and worth taking seriously in two hours. I need the order in which the components must go and what depends on what: in particular, how early the phenomenal/access distinction has to be drawn, and whether the claim that phenomenal consciousness is an illusion can even be stated before that distinction is in place. I want one carrying example that does most of the work — presumably a case in which introspection systematically misreports what is actually in experience, not a merely perceptual illusion — and I want you to say what that example must have and must lack, and why the obvious candidates (change blindness, the dress, ordinary optical illusions) would not carry the whole session even if each deserves a minute.

Then give me the misconceptions to expect, in the order they will actually surface — "illusionism says we are zombies," "illusions are conscious states so the view is self-defeating," "without phenomenal consciousness there is no access consciousness either," "illusionism is just eliminativism with better branding" — and for each, the exact point in the sequence where I should head it off and how. End with a check for understanding that would show someone has grasped the position rather than merely stopped objecting, and say explicitly what I should leave out at this level and announce that I am leaving it out. Please do not give me a reading list; I want the architecture of the session, not citations.

Finally, tell me where this plan is most likely to fail: which substantive consideration about the view — for example, if the phenomenal/access distinction cannot bear the weight the plan places on it, or if illusionism really does collapse into eliminativism about experience — would lead you to reorder the sequence or replace the central example, and why.
