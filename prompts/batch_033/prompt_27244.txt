Two friends of mine got into it at a party — Maya (philosophy PhD) and Dan (computational neuroscience). They were arguing about whether the hard problem of consciousness is a real problem or a confusion, and I ended up taking mental notes cause neither would budge.

Dan started by saying the hard problem rests on a bait-and-switch: we imagine we can conceive of a creature functionally identical to us but without consciousness, but that's just an intuition pump, not an argument. Maya said it's more than an intuition — the fact that we can even ask "why is there something it's like to be me?" shows there's an explanatory gap that doesn't arise for, say, digestion or circulation. Dan said that's question-begging: you're assuming the question is well-formed, but maybe it's like asking "why is water wet?" — it sounds deep until you realize wetness just is the macroproperty of certain molecular interactions, and the question dissolves. Maya said no, wetness is a functional property — it's about how water interacts with other things. Consciousness isn't like that. You can give a complete functional account of pain — tissue damage, nociception, avoidance behavior — and still not have explained why it hurts. Dan said that's because "why it hurts" smuggles in the very thing you're trying to explain; you're demanding an explanation of the quale in non-qualitative terms, which is like demanding an explanation of why triangles are three-sided in non-geometric terms. Maya said that's exactly her point: the hard problem is that we don't have non-qualitative terms that even gesture at an explanation, and that's a genuine fact about our current conceptual situation, not a confusion. Dan said okay, but there's a difference between "we don't currently have the concepts" and "no possible concepts could do it" — and the hard problem as usually stated assumes the latter. Maya said she's not assuming the latter; she's saying the former is already a serious problem worth taking seriously, and dismissing it as a confusion is premature.

Then Dan pivoted and said look, even if there's an explanatory gap, that doesn't mean there's an ontological gap — it could just be a gap in our understanding, like the gap between "life" and "chemistry" before we had biochemistry. Maya said the life/chemistry analogy is exactly wrong: life was a functional concept all along — reproduction, metabolism, etc. — so it was always the kind of thing that could be reduced. Consciousness isn't functional in that way; that's the whole point. Dan said she's just asserting that consciousness isn't functional, but that's what's at issue. Maya said no, she's pointing to a difference in the kind of concept it is — you can't define consciousness in terms of what it does without leaving out what it is.

At that point they asked me to weigh in and I was completely stuck. Can you adjudicate this move by move? Who's right about which claims, where does each argument go wrong, and is there a way to settle whether the hard problem is genuine or not, or does the disagreement bottom out in something neither of them can prove?
