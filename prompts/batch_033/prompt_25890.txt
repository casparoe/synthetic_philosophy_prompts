I'm designing a handout for an upper-level epistemology seminar on the epistemology of disagreement, and I want a decision tree that takes students from a few core diagnostic questions to a named position in the peer disagreement debate. The tree should be built from the logical space rather than from a list of names: first the questions and the combinations of answers they allow, then the actual positions located in that space, with unoccupied combinations noted and briefly diagnosed (is nobody there because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored?).

The tree must include the following as leaves: the Equal Weight View, at least one form of moderate conciliationism, the Total Evidence View, the Right Reasons View, and at least one position that turns on whether higher-order evidence can be defeated by first-order evidence. If any pair of these cannot be cleanly separated by the questions in your tree, flag that explicitly and explain why they collapse or overlap at the level of the tree's diagnostic power.

The diagnostic questions should be concrete enough that a student can answer them without already knowing which position they point toward. For example, rather than asking "Do you accept the independence principle?", give them a case: you've just worked through a complex proof and are highly confident in your conclusion; you then learn that a colleague you respect, with the same training and access to the same axioms, has reached the opposite conclusion. Can you legitimately rely on your memory of your own reasoning to dismiss their dissent, or must you set that reasoning aside and treat the disagreement as neutral evidence that you might have made a mistake? Questions of this kind — built around specific scenarios — should populate the branch points.

The audience is advanced undergraduates or beginning graduate students who have done the reading for two weeks on this topic. The tree should have roughly five to seven branch points and roughly six to nine leaves. For each leaf, give the name of the position, a one-sentence statement of its core commitment, and the name of at least one philosopher associated with it (surname and year of a relevant paper is fine). For any region of the logical space that is unoccupied, give a one-sentence diagnosis of why: is the combination of answers incoherent, merely unattractive given other commitments most participants share, or simply unexplored in the literature?

Finally, add a brief note at the end about which distinctions your tree is structurally unable to capture — for instance, if two positions differ only in their account of what makes someone an epistemic peer, and your tree treats peerhood as a fixed input, say so. The point is to give students an honest map, not a Procrustean one.
