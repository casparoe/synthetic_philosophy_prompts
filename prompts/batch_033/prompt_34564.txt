I'm TAing an upper-level philosophy of mind course where we just did the Lucas–Penrose argument, and I want to make a handout that sorts students into positions on whether Gödel's theorems show a mind can't be a machine. The thing I keep running into is that people will say they're a mechanist or that they're with Penrose and then give totally different reasons for it, so I want the sort to be driven by what they actually accept in the argument rather than by the label they walked in with.

Could you write a decision tree, maybe seven or eight branch points and eight to ten leaves, where each node is a claim or a tiny scenario they have to accept or reject in plain English. Some nodes I definitely want in there: whether for any consistent formal system a human can in principle see that the system's Gödel sentence is true, whether the mind (if it's a machine at all) has to be one particular formal system whose axioms we can in principle know, whether you have to know your own mind is consistent before you're entitled to say a Gödel sentence is true, and whether the anti-mechanist conclusion is about Turing machines specifically or about any physical process at all. The leaves should include at least a Lucas-style anti-mechanist, a Penrose-style anti-mechanist, a Gödelian disjunctivist who refuses to pick a side, a mechanist who denies we can know our own consistency, a mechanist who says the mind might be an unsound or inconsistent machine, and a Benacerraf-flavored mechanist who says we can't identify our own program. Also flag any leaves the tree can't cleanly seperate, and mark combinations of answers that nobody seems to occupy because the view is incoherent or unmotivated rather than because you forgot it. The students have seen the incompleteness theorems but won't remember technical details, so each node should be answerable without knowing which position it leads to.
