ok so I've been going down this rabbit hole about climate economics and I keep getting stuck on the same thing. when governments do cost-benefit analysis on climate policy they use something called a discount rate, which basically says future costs and benefits are worth less than present ones. and I get why you'd discount money — a dollar today is worth more than a dollar in 50 years cause you can invest it and get interest. but here's what I don't get: they apply the same math to everything. future deaths, future suffering, future extinctions. like the US government uses 3% and 7% for federal benefit-cost stuff, and at 3% a death in 2100 counts as about a tenth of a death today. and the Stern review used 1.4%, which sounds tiny, but over a century even that makes a big difference. and the really wild thing is that this one parameter basically decides the whole policy recommendation — a low rate says we should spend big on climate now, a high rate says it's not worth it.

what I'm trying to wrap my head around is: what does this practice actually presuppose about the value of future people? like if you set the discount rate above zero for harms that aren't money, are you implicitly saying that a future person's suffering matters less than a present person's suffering just because of when they're born? is that an actual philosophical position you're taking, or is it just a technical convenience that nobody means literally? and if someone thought that future people count equally — a death is a death no matter what century it happens in — what would have to change about how we do these cost-benefit numbers? would the whole framework just fall apart?

I'm not an economist or anything I've just been reading about this and it seems like this one parameter is doing a huge amount of philosophical work that nobody really talks about outside of a few academics.
