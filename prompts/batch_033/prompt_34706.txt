I've been reading Adam Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments and I'm trying to pin down what I actually believe about this stuff. Here's my current list:

(1) I think Smith is basically right that moral judgment is about what an impartial spectator would feel — when I judge something as wrong, what I'm really doing is judging that a suitably impartial observer would react with something like resentment or disapproval.

(2) This impartial spectator isn't some spooky objective standard floating out there. It's built from sympathy, which is just our ordinary psychological tendency to put ourselves in other people's shoes.

(3) But sympathy is obviously biased — I sympathize way more with my friends, with people who share my background, and so on. That's just a fact about how human psychology works.

(4) Still, the impartial spectator manages to correct for these biases and deliver genuinely impartial verdicts. It's not just a dressed-up version of whatever I happen to feel after thinking about it for five minutes.

(5) And I think we can be genuinely wrong about what the impartial spectator would say. If I think the impartial spectator would approve of something but actually a properly informed and impartial spectator would condemn it, then my moral judgment is just mistaken.

I've got a nagging feeling these don't all fit together. Like, if the impartial spectator is built from biased materials (2 and 3), how does it reliably produce unbiased results (4)? And if it's ultimately a psychological construct (2), in what sense can I be wrong about it (5)? But maybe I'm conflating things that are actually fine once you draw the right distinctions. Can you audit this set and tell me where the real problems are, which pairs are in tension and which only look like it, and what the cheapest fix would be?
