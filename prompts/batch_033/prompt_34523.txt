"Nāgārjuna's Mūlamadhyamakakārikā makes extensive use of the tetralemma (catuṣkoṭi). Explain the structure of the tetralemma, illustrate how Nāgārjuna deploys it in at least one specific argument from the text, and discuss how this method relates to the Madhyamaka doctrine of emptiness (śūnyatā)."

Here is the student's answer:

"The tetralemma is a fourfold logical structure that Nāgārjuna uses to exhaust all possible positions one could hold about a given topic. The four alternatives are: (1) P, (2) not-P, (3) both P and not-P, and (4) neither P nor not-P. Nāgārjuna's strategy is to show that all four are untenable, which pushes us to abandon conceptual thinking altogether.

A clear example appears in the opening chapter of the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā, where Nāgārjuna examines the causal relation. He asks whether an effect is produced from itself, from something other than itself, from both, or from neither. He argues that self-causation is incoherent because it would make production pointless; other-causation fails because it severs any intelligible connection between cause and effect; the conjunction of both inherits the problems of each; and production from neither makes the arising of anything inexplicable. By rejecting all four, Nāgārjuna undermines the very idea of causation as conventionally understood.

This method connects directly to emptiness. To say that all phenomena are empty (śūnya) is to say that they lack svabhāva, or inherent existence. The tetralemma demonstrates that no phenomenon can be pinned down by any of the four positions, which means nothing possesses a fixed, independent nature. Nāgārjuna is not a nihilist, however. He distinguishes between conventional truth (saṃvṛti-satya) and ultimate truth (paramārtha-satya): conventionally, things arise dependently in accordance with pratītyasamutpāda, and we can speak and reason about them; ultimately, they are empty.

What is crucial to see is that by rejecting all four lemmas, Nāgārjuna is pointing us toward an ineffable ultimate reality — emptiness itself — which transcends the limits of propositional thought. The tetralemma is a ladder we climb and then kick away: once we see that no conceptual position can be maintained, we are left with a direct, non-conceptual apprehension of the way things really are. Emptiness is therefore not merely the absence of svabhāva but the positive, though inexpressible, truth about the nature of all things."
