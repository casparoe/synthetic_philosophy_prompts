As a novelist, I have spent the better part of three decades acquiring a kind of knowledge that resists formulation. I can tell you that a scene needs cutting, that a character has not yet earned her anger, that the rhythm of a paragraph is wrong by half a beat — but I cannot give you the rule that generated these judgements, nor can I reduce them to a set of propositions I once learned and now apply. Like many practitioners of an art, I have come to the view that knowing how to write a novel is not reducible to knowing that certain facts about writing obtain. This is, in philosophical terms, the anti-intellectualist position about know-how: the view associated with Gilbert Ryle's attack on what he called the intellectualist legend, and defended more recently by philosophers who argue that abilities, skills, and practical mastery constitute a distinct kind of knowledge, not merely the covert application of propositional knowledge.

I want to know what would count against this view.

I am not asking for a survey of the literature, which I could not assess without reading it. What I want is a map of the view's exposure: the observations, arguments, possible developments in cognitive science or artificial intelligence, or even changes in my own experience as a writer that should, if they occurred, cause me to revise or abandon the position. I want the defeaters ranked by how decisive they would be — from fatal to merely awkward — and I want to know which of them a committed anti-intellectualist would concede as genuine counterevidence and which they would dismiss as resting on a misunderstanding of the view. I am particularly interested in what would happen if it turned out that a system which had no practical experience of writing, but which had been trained on an enormous corpus of propositional knowledge about fiction, began producing novels that skilled readers could not distinguish from human work. Would that show that the know-how was in the propositions all along, or would it show something else? And I want to know whether the intellectualist, in turn, risks anything of her own: what would have to be true for the intellectualist position to be wrong, and whether the two positions are genuinely in contact or merely passing each other by.

Finally, I want an honest assessment of whether the anti-intellectualist position, as it is typically defended, has been insulated against falsification in a way that ought to trouble someone who holds it — and if it has, whether that kind of insulation is a defect in a claim about the nature of a skill, or whether it is simply the right shape for a philosophical thesis of this sort, which is not in the business of making empirical predictions at all.
