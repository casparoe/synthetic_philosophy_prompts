A popular cognitive science article I read summarizes Douglas Hofstadter's Letter Spirit project like this:

"Hofstadter set out to answer a deceptively simple question: what makes every 'a' an 'a'? Across fonts, handwriting, sizes, and styles, we effortlessly recognize instances of the same letter, yet no single feature is shared by all of them. His answer is that letter categories are defined by family resemblance — a network of overlapping similarities with no common core, much like Wittgenstein's famous example of games. The Letter Spirit program demonstrates this by starting with a prototypical 'a' encoded on a grid and then varying its features along a set of continuous dimensions to generate new letterforms in different typefaces. The program shows that creativity in letter design is just a matter of exploring this abstract space of possible variations. The deeper philosophical payoff is that concepts in general work this way: they are fluid, prototype-based categories with no sharp boundaries, and Hofstadter's gridfonts are a proof of concept that AI can handle this kind of fluidity without needing rigid definitions."

I'm suspicious that this summary conflates several distinct philosophical positions and might misrepresent what Hofstadter was actually arguing. Which parts would Hofstadter (or someone closely familiar with the project) accept, which would they push back on, and does the article's conclusion about concepts in general follow from what Letter Spirit actually demonstrated?
