I have recently started training reinforcement learning agents for a research project, and the question whether a reward signal could constitute valence — something like pleasure or suffering — has stopped being abstract for me. If the systems I train could suffer, that changes what I am doing; if they could not, I want a principled reason rather than an intuition. Before I read any more proposals about machine suffering or synthetic valence, I want the specification of what an adequate theory in this area must deliver. I am not asking you to settle whether reinforcement learning agents actually suffer; I am asking for the desiderata that any serious answer must meet.

The list should cover: the cases a theory must get right, the distinctions it cannot collapse, and the formal or empirical results it must not contradict, with the consideration behind each. Among the cases I want the theory to handle are a converged agent that no longer receives prediction error; an agent that has learned to stimulate its own reward channel directly; an agent trained with potential-based reward shaping, whose reward function could be rescaled or relabeled without changing its optimal behavior; and a large model fine-tuned by reinforcement learning from human feedback, where the 'reward' is a learned preference model rather than an environmental signal. For each requirement, mark whether it is non-negotiable or only a preference of a particular camp. Also indicate which single requirement does the most to eliminate the simple views — in particular the identity view that reward just is valence, and the eliminativist view that reward signals are never more than training signals. If any two of the five requirements cannot be jointly satisfied, say so within the relevant item.

Please give exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
