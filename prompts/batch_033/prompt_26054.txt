So here's a thing I've been chewing on. In development economics and a lot of social policy evaluation these days, the gold standard is the randomized controlled trial — you take a bunch of villages or schools or households, randomly assign some to get an intervention and some not, and you measure individual-level outcomes (income, test scores, health metrics). The whole enterprise is built around estimating an average treatment effect on individuals. If you can't randomize at the individual level you randomize at the cluster level, but the thing you care about is still the effect on individual people.

Now here's what puzzles me. A lot of the interventions being tested are things like "change the governance structure of the village council" or "introduce a new norm about girls' education" — stuff that sure seems to operate at the level of groups, institutions, social structure. But the RCT framework forces you to treat all that as just a treatment applied to individuals, and the thing you measure is individual outcomes. It's like the method itself is committed to the idea that social reality bottoms out in facts about individuals — that if you just had a complete enough picture of individual-level causes and effects, you'd have the whole story.

So my question is: what philosophical view of social reality does the RCT-as-gold-standard practice actually presuppose? Does it strictly presuppose methodological individualism (the view that social facts are nothing over and above facts about individuals), or is it compatible with holism (the view that social structures have autonomous causal powers that can't be reduced to individual-level facts)? And if it's compatible with both, what would a genuinely holist-friendly evaluation method look like — and would it have to give up on randomization?

Also, there's a weird tension I can't resolve: the same development economists who swear by RCTs will also talk about "institutions" and "culture" as deep causes of poverty, but their method can only test interventions, not institutions. Is that just a practical limitation, or does it reveal that the practice is philosophically confused — that it's quietly commited to a picture of social causation that it can't openly avow?
