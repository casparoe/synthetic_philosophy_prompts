I am a high school student, and I help run my school's ethics bowl team, which has ten members and meets for one hour after school. I have been asked to lead a training session on the moral difference between lying and misleading, and I want the team to run into the problem themselves rather than hear a presentation about it.

Please design a runnable activity — a game or structured role-play — for ten students in a sixty-minute session. The activity should make participants feel the force of the question whether there is a moral difference between telling an outright falsehood and saying something literally true while knowing that the listener will draw a false conclusion. By the end, the team should have a felt sense of why the distinction seems to matter so much in ordinary life, and also of where that feeling becomes unstable or self-serving. The rules, roles, and incentives should be arranged so that participants face the choice before it is named. Include a payoff or scoring scheme that does not require real money, and list the materials I would need.

I would like the activity to include at least two conditions or rounds: one in which a participant may assert falsehoods, and one in which they may make only true statements but may choose, time, or phrase them so as to mislead. The scoring should be set up so that misleading and lying can sometimes achieve the same practical result, so the group can compare what they choose, how they justify it, and how the person on the receiving end reacts.

Then give me a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the philosophical positions, without lecturing up front. The debrief should cover at least: the view that lying is morally worse because the liar directly asserts the false proposition and thereby takes a different kind of responsibility for it; the view that, once the intent to deceive and the expected harm are held fixed, there is no morally important difference; and the view that misleading is sometimes worse because it is more deniable and harder for the target to detect. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be explicit about which feature of the philosophical debate the activity cannot reproduce and that I will need to supply afterward.

One further instruction: in the debrief, clearly separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who have worked on this topic, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. If you rely on a particular reading of the distinction between lying and misleading, say that it is a reading and not universally shared.
