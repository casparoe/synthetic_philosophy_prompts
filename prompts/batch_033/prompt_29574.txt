I'm a CS grad student sitting in on a philosophy of mind seminar, and last week the discussion turned to whole-brain emulation. One of the philosophy students kept trying to settle things with "the brain is just a computer": since brains are computers, and computers can in principle be simulated on other computers, an emulated brain would automatically have whatever conscious mental life the original has. When someone pushed on whether the simulation would actually feel anything, he just repeated the slogan louder, like the argument was over. I don't even know that I disagree with his conclusion, but I'm pretty sure the slogan is doing most of the work for him.

My problem is that "computer" seems to slide around depending on who's talking: sometimes it means a digital symbol-manipulator running a stored program, sometimes it means anything that processes information, sometimes it's just a loose metaphor for a system with inputs and outputs. Those readings seem to license very different conclusions, and I can't tell which one his emulation argument actually needs. Could you seperate the distinct precise claims someone might be making when they say "the brain is a computer" — from the obviously true to the obviously false — and say for each whether it's plausible and whether it would actually get him to his conclusion about emulated consciousness? A menu-style breakdown would be ideal, since I want to come to next week's class with something concrete.
