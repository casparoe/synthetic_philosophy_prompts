I hold the view that when a lump of clay is molded into a statue, the statue and the lump are two distinct material objects that exactly coincide — they occupy the same region of space, share all their microphysical parts, yet differ in their persistence conditions (the lump survives squashing; the statue does not) and in their sortal properties. This is roughly the constitution view, defended in different forms by Lynne Rudder Baker and others, as against both eliminativism (there are no statues, only lumps arranged statuewise) and the identity view (the statue just is the lump).

What would count against this view? I want a map of its exposure: which arguments, thought experiments, or theoretical costs should move me, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. In particular, I want to know whether the grounding problem — roughly, why the statue and the lump differ modally and sortally given that they share all their non-modal parts and properties — is a decisive objection or one the view can absorb, and whether some versions of the constitution view handle it better than others. I would also like to know whether the view, under sustained pressure, collapses into a rival account (a four-dimensionalist perdurantism, a hylomorphic view, or something else), and whether its defenders would themselves concede that as a problem rather than a feature. Separate what is settled in the literature from what remains genuinely contested among those working on material constitution, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
