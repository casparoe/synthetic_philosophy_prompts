In an introductory philosophy of mind course, the instructor gave the following summary of the knowing-how versus knowing-that debate:

"Gilbert Ryle argued that knowing how to do something, like swimming or playing chess, is fundamentally different from knowing that some proposition is true. His central argument was an infinite regress: if knowing-how were just a kind of knowing-that, then applying any piece of knowledge would require a further piece of knowledge about how to apply it, and so on forever. Ryle's positive view was that knowing-how amounts to having the right behavioral dispositions — you know how to swim if you reliably produce the correct movements when you're in the water. This anti-intellectualist picture was standard until the early 2000s, when Jason Stanley and Timothy Williamson demonstrated that knowing-how is in fact a species of knowing-that. Their key move was linguistic: sentences like 'Hannah knows how to swim' embed an interrogative, just like 'Hannah knows who to invite,' and since knowing-who is uncontroversially a kind of knowing-that, knowing-how must be too. The regress problem dissolves once you see that the relevant propositional knowledge is tacit or automatic. On this intellectualist view, knowing how to swim is simply knowing, of some way w, that w is a way for you to swim, under a practical mode of presentation. Intellectualism is now the dominant view in the field."

I suspect this summary is not entirely fair to Ryle and perhaps overstates what the intellectualists established.
