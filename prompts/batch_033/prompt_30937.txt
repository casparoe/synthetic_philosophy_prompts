I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, but a question has been nagging at me for some time, and I would be grateful for help in thinking it through. I notice that I speak about "things" — tables, cats, heaps, swarms — as though some collections of parts add up to a single object and others do not, yet I cannot state the rule I am actually using. A friend challenged me to write down my verdicts on particular cases before trying to extract a principle, and I have done so below.

A heap of sand is not a single thing; it is just some sand. A single grain of sand is one thing. If I shape that same heap into a sandcastle, I think there is now one thing there — the castle — at least whilst it lasts. A table made from a top and four legs screwed together is one thing, and it remains one thing if I unscrew one leg and rest the table on a crate. A pile of unconnected Lego bricks is not one thing, but the same bricks clicked together into a model are one thing. A ship made of hundreds of planks nailed together is one thing. A swarm of bees is not one thing, however coordinated it looks; it is many bees. Yet I watched a documentary about the Portuguese man o' war, which is a colony of zooids, and I found myself wanting to count it as a single animal. A living cat is definitely one thing, over and above its cells. A pair of shoes is two things, not one.

I am trying to find the principle behind these verdicts, if there is one. Is it arrangement, function, integration, the whole's having powers that the parts lack, something about living things, or something else entirely? Which of my verdicts would each candidate principle force me to give up, and what would the principle say about cases I have not listed — for example, a human body after death, a forest, a rock, a wave, or a committee? I would rather accept a simple principle that overturns one or two of my verdicts than a baroque one that saves them all, but I would like to know exactly what I am buying at either price.
