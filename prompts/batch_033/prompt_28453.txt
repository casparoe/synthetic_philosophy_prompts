I want you to act as a professional philosopher of mind who works on personal identity and who is familiar with how the rationalist and LessWrong communities have engaged with Douglas Hofstadter's ideas. Think carefully and step through your reasoning explicitly. Begin by brainstorming three or four candidate scenarios and briefly say why you reject the weaker ones before developing the strongest one.

Construct a thought experiment in which Hofstadter's pattern view of the self, as developed in *I Am a Strange Loop* (Basic Books, 2007), delivers a verdict that diverges from *both* of the two standard secular theories of personal identity: psychological continuity theory (survival is continuity of overlapping psychological states, whatever the substrate) and biological/animalist continuity theory (survival is continued functioning of the same organism). That is, the psychological continuity theorist and the biological continuity theorist should agree with each other about the central survival question, while the Hofstadterian gives a different answer.

Constraints:

- No science fiction. Ordinary human lives and relationships only — no uploading, teleportation, split-brain surgery, or gradual neuron replacement.
- The divergence must come from a single structural feature of the case. Remove that one feature, and the Hofstadterian verdict should collapse back into agreement with one of the standard views.
- The scenario must be emotionally plausible, not merely logically possible. A normal person should be able to imagine it happening and feel the force of the question.
- Do not make the Hofstadterian verdict come out as obviously right or obviously wrong; the case should put genuine pressure on at least one of the standard views.

After presenting the scenario, do the following: (a) walk through the verdict each of the three views gives and the reasoning behind it; (b) identify which structural feature is doing the work to make the pattern view peel off, and explain what happens to the verdicts if that feature is removed; (c) explain how a committed defender of psychological continuity theory would resist the Hofstadterian verdict — is their objection ad hoc, or does it point to a principled limitation of the pattern view; and (d) give your honest assessment of whether the case does real philosophical work or merely restates the pattern view in scenario form.
