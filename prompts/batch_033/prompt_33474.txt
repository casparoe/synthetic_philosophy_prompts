I'm a journalist working on a piece about the identity of indiscernibles — the principle, due to Leibniz, that objects sharing all their properties are identical. The canonical challenge to this principle is Max Black's thought experiment: a universe containing nothing but two qualitatively identical iron spheres. They share every qualitative property and stand in perfectly symmetric relations to each other, yet we want to say there are two spheres, not one.

I'd like you to construct a thought experiment for my article that goes beyond Black's in the following specific way. Build a case in which two objects are indiscernible with respect to all their qualitative properties — monadic and relational alike — yet the scenario is set up so that a reader would naturally judge them to be distinct. Unlike Black's universe, however, the scenario must contain other objects as well; the two objects must be symmetrically situated with respect to those other objects, so that no relational property distinguishes them. The challenge is to make the case richer than Black's without introducing a qualitative asymmetry that would let the identity of indiscernibles off the hook.

Constraints: ordinary objects, no longer than one paragraph, no appeal to possible worlds, no exotic physics, and no haecceities or primitive thisness built into the setup. The distinctness of the two objects should emerge from the scenario, not be stipulated.

After presenting the scenario, do the following:
- Walk through the reasoning of a defender of the strong identity of indiscernibles, who must either conclude that the two objects are identical (counterintuitively) or find a qualitative property that distinguishes them.
- Walk through the reasoning of an opponent of the principle, who accepts that distinctness does not require qualitative difference.
- Identify which specific feature of the case does the work in forcing the disagreement between these two positions, and explain what happens if that feature is removed.
- Give the identity-of-indiscernibles defender's best response to the case, and say whether it requires them to enrich their ontology.

I'm looking for a committed response — one that takes a definite position on how the case should be constructed and what it shows, rather than surveying options. Please write in American English.
