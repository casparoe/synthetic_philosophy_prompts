I'm putting together a syllabus for a grad seminar on social norms and I want to pair Lewis's Convention with Golding's Lord of the Flies. The idea is to treat the island as a kind of uncontrolled experiment: you've got a group of children with no shared history (well, some from school) who have to build social order from scratch, and we get to watch both the construction and the collapse.

Here's the specific thing I'm stuck on. Lewis's account says a convention is a regularity in behavior that solves a recurrent coordination problem, where everyone prefers to conform conditional on others conforming, and there's an alternative regularity that would also work. The conch rule is a textbook case — it's arbitrary (you could use a stick, a raised hand), it solves a coordination problem (who speaks when), and everyone does better following it than talking over each other.

But the novel also shows something that doesnt fit neatly: the conventions become normative really fast. When Jack smashes the conch, it's not just that coordination fails — it's that something sacred has been violated. The boys treat the rules as having a kind of weight that goes beyond "this is the equilibrium we happen to be in." And the breakdown isn't a shift to a different equilibrium; it's a descent into something that isn't governed by convention at all — fear, hunting, the lord of the flies.

So here's what I want to know: can a Lewisian framework handle the normative dimension of the boys' conventions — the way the rules feel binding, not just useful? Lewis does talk about conventions becoming norms when supported by sanctions, but that seems to miss the phenomenology: the boys don't follow the conch rule because they're afraid of punishment, at least not at first. They follow it because it feels like the right thing to do, and when it breaks, something breaks in them too. Is Lewis just leaving out the moral psychology, or is there a deeper problem — that his account of convention can't explain why we ever come to treat arbitrary coordination solutions as anything more than that?

And the second thing: the collapse. In Lewis's picture, conventions are self-enforcing as long as the underlying preferences hold. But in the novel, the preferences themselves seem to shift — not because the coordination problem changes, but because fear and hunger and the sheer absence of adult authority change what the boys care about. Does Lewis's theory have the resources to explain how conventions can be undermined by changes in the broader motivational landscape, or does it assume a kind of stability in preferences that the novel shows we can't count on?

I guess I'm asking for a reading of the novel that takes Lewis seriously but also lets the novel talk back — where does it confirm the theory, where does it complicate it, and what would Lewis have to add to his account to make sense of what happens on the island?
