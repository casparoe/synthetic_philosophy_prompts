Okay so I just spent like two hours arguing with a friend about whether the guy who saves a drowning kid but only because he wants to look good did something with moral worth, and we kept going in circles because we clearly meant different things by "worth." I run a small philosophy blog and I want to settle this with a quiz: "what do you actually think about moral worth?" The point is to sort readers by what they say about cases, not by which label sounds nice.

Here's what I want. Eight to ten questions, each a short concrete case, with two or three answer options about whether the act has moral worth and why or why not. Then a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto positions. The leaves I want at minimum:

- the duty view (worth only when you act from the thought that it's right or your duty)
- the good-motive view (worth when you act from the right kind of concern, like compassion, even if you never think about duty)
- the right-reasons view (worth when you act for the features that actually make the act right, whether or not you describe them as "duty" — the Huck Finn thing)
- the virtue/character view (worth when the act expresses stable good character, so unthinking habits count)
- the deflationary/act-only view (either "moral worth" isn't a real extra property, or all that matters is that the act is right and the worth-talk adds nothing)

Use at least these cases: the grudging helper who hates every second but does it because it's right; the person who helps out of spontaneous sympathy and never once thinks about whether it's required; the person who returns a wallet because they misread the name and think it belongs to someone they owe money to; the person who does the right thing from a rule they memorized but don't understand; the habitually generous person who doesn't deliberate; and a Huck-Finn-type case where someone helps a friend while sincerely believing that helping is wrong. Add two or three more of your own that force the positions apart on things like overdetermination (she would have helped anyway, duty or not), whether reluctance adds or subtracts, and whether acting on a cold cost-benefit calculation can carry worth.

A few constraints. Don't ask any question like "do motives matter to you?" — every question has to be a case where someone can answer from there gut without knowing the labels. Build the scoring key from the logical space the questions open up, not from a pre-chosen list of names: say which combinations of answers are possible and which are empty, and for the empty ones, say whether nobody's there because the view is incoherent, just unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any two leaves the quiz can't seperate with these cases (I suspect the good-motive and virtue views will blur, and maybe right-reasons and good-motive too). Keep it readable for a smart but non-specialist blog audience, and don't just serve up the standard encyclopedia version — the diagnostic work should be in the cases, not the labels.
