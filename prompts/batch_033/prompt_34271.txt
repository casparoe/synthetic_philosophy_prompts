In my biology class we have spent the last few weeks on genetics, and last Friday we looked at twin and adoption studies reporting that the heritability of IQ within wealthy countries is fairly high. One of my classmates argued that if differences in IQ are substantially heritable, then spending money on tutoring and school-improvement programs is probably futile, since the differences those programs target are mostly genetic in origin. My teacher stopped the discussion and said, more than once, "Heritability is not destiny," and then moved on as though that single sentence had disposed of the argument.

I am fairly sure she was right that my classmate's conclusion does not follow, but I cannot tell exactly which claim her slogan was meant to assert, because I can think of several: that a highly heritable trait can still be changed by a sufficiently different environment; that a heritability estimate describes variation in a particular population and says nothing about any individual; that knowing genes explain much of the existing variation does not by itself tell us how much an intervention would improve outcomes; and probably others I have not managed to separate. Some of these readings sound almost trivial, while others sound like substantive claims about the power of environments, and I doubt they all support her reply equally.

Could you lay out the distinct precise theses that "Heritability is not destiny" could be used to express, say which are trivial, which are substantive and defensible, and which are false or overstated, and then say for each whether it actually blocks my classmate's inference? I would like a menu of readings I can bring to class rather than a single interpretation.
