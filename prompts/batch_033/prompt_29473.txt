I'm a software engineer, and I've been reading around the frame problem — the one from old AI debates about how an agent is supposed to figure out which facts are relevant to an action without explicitly reasoning through everything it knows. In discussions with colleagues, I've repeatedly come across the claim that the frame problem is fundamentally the problem of relevance filtering, and that modern attention mechanisms in transformer architectures are effectively dissolving it. The analogy goes: just as a transformer learns which tokens to attend to and which to ignore when producing an output, an agent facing the frame problem needs to learn which features of its world model matter for a given decision and which can be safely overlooked. The practical conclusion people draw is that the frame problem was never a deep philosophical puzzle — it was a pre-deep-learning way of describing an engineering bottleneck, and we are steadily machining our way through it.

But I have also seen hints that philosophers meant something more fundamental — not just "how do we filter efficiently" but something about the structure of reasoning itself, or about how we represent change, or about a limit that no amount of statistical pattern-matching can circumvent.

I would like a careful audit of this analogy. Please lay out the mapping explicitly — what in the transformer story corresponds to what in the frame-problem story — then identify where the analogy breaks, separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from the ones that do not, and tell me whether the conclusion ("the frame problem is just an engineering challenge, and attention mechanisms are dissolving it") survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If there is a better analogy that captures what the philosophers were worried about, I would like to hear that too. Please write your answer in British English.
