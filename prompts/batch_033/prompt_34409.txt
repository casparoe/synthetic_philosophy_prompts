I'm in a philosophy of language course this semester and we've been doing Moore's paradox — you know, "It's raining, but I don't believe it's raining." The weird thing is it's not a logical contradiction (it can be true!) but it's still somehow absurd to say. Our professor walked us through the standard pragmatic explanation: when you assert p, you represent yourself as believing p, so asserting "p but I don't believe p" has you representing yourself as both believing and not believing p at the same time. That made sense to me, and I was ready to move on.

But then I was talking to my friend after class and we started pushing it further, and now I think we've built an argument that proves way too much. Here's what we ended up with:

(1) "It's raining, but I don't believe it's raining" is always absurd for me to assert, even though it can be true.
(2) The best explanation: assertion expresses belief. When I assert p, I represent myself as believing p. So the Moore-paradoxical sentence is pragmatically self-defeating — I'm simultaneously representing myself as believing p (by asserting it) and as not believing p (by the second conjunct).
(3) If this explanatory pattern is sound, it should generalize: if a sentence of the form "p but I don't φ p" is absurd to assert, then assertion expresses φ-ing (or at least represents the speaker as φ-ing).
(4) "It's raining, but I don't know it's raining" is equally absurd to assert. You can't say "Yeah it's raining, but I don't know that it is" without sounding like you've lost it.
(5) So by (3), assertion expresses knowledge. (This is basically the knowledge norm of assertion — you shouldn't assert what you don't know. Williamson and others defend this.)
(6) But now consider: "It's raining, but I'm not absolutely certain it's raining." This also seems absurd to assert. Try it out loud — "It's raining, but I'm not totally sure it is." That sounds broken in the same way.
(7) By (3) again, assertion expresses absolute certainty. So you should only assert what you're absolutely certain of.
(8) But that's an insane standard. It would mean almost everything we say in ordinary conversation is improper. I'm not absolutely certain of basically anything empirical — I could be a brain in a vat, my senses could be misfiring, whatever. Are we really supposed to stop asserting that the bus comes at 3?
(9) So something has to give. Either (a) step (6) is wrong — "p but I'm not certain p" isn't actually Moore-paradoxical in the same way, and maybe I'm just importing the weirdness from the other cases, (b) the inference pattern in (3) is too quick — the absurdity of "p but I don't know p" and "p but I don't believe p" might have different underlying explanations even though they feel similar, or (c) we should reject the original explanation of Moore's paradox in (2), which would be wild because that explanation is basically orthodoxy.

I'm genuinely stuck. I want to know which step you'd reject and what it costs — like, if you reject (6), what independent test distinguishes genuinely Moore-paradoxical sentences from ones that just sound weird for other reasons? And I'd especially want to hear what would change your mind: where is your diagnosis most vulnerable, and what kind of argument or linguistic data would make you switch to a different diagnosis?
