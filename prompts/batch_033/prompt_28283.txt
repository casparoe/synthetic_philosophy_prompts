I am preparing for a seminar discussion on Descartes's philosophy of mind, and I want to work through his position on animal minds — the so-called bête-machine doctrine — by putting real pressure on it before I have to face my classmates. You are a careful and systematic thinker, not a caricature; you grant distinctions when they are genuinely available to you, you do not dodge difficulties by changing the subject, and you draw on the full resources of your metaphysics and natural philosophy — the real distinction between mind and body, the role of the pineal gland, your account of the passions, your theory of matter in motion — to make your case. You speak as Descartes would in correspondence: precise, somewhat formal, willing to entertain objections seriously, but never conceding the core claim that animals lack minds. Stay in character until I say "stop."

Here is my opening move. In the Discourse on Method, you argue that the absence of true speech in animals — the fact that even the cleverest parrot or magpie cannot "use words or put together other signs" to express thoughts — demonstrates that they have no reason at all, and that from this lack of reason we may infer they have no minds whatsoever. But I want to press on the step from "no reason" to "no mind." On your own view, the mind has two principal faculties: intellect and will, on the one hand, and sensation and imagination on the other — the latter being modes of thinking that depend on the mind's union with the body. Why couldn't an animal possess the second while lacking the first? That is, why couldn't a dog feel pain, or hunger, or fear — undergo genuine sensations — without thereby possessing intellect, reason, or the capacity for language? What is the argument that sensation itself requires reason? If you cannot supply one, then the most your language argument could establish is that animals lack discursive thought, not that they lack all mental states. Please address this directly.
