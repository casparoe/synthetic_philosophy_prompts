I've been trying to get clear on what constitutional precommitment devices actually presuppose about democratic agency, and the more I think about it the less sure I am that the standard story hangs together. The standard story — which I first encountered in the LessWrong-sphere and later traced back through Jon Elster's work — treats supermajority amendment requirements, eternity clauses, and similar entrenchment mechanisms as a polity-level Ulysses contract. We bind ourselves now because we anticipate that future majorities will, under pressure or passion, dismantle institutions that serve our considered interests. So far so good.

But the Ulysses analogy does a lot of work here that I'm not sure it's licensed to do. For an individual agent, the contract makes sense because there's a single person whose "true" self (sober, foresighted, issuing the binding instruction) and "irrational" future self (deluded by the Sirens, begging to be untied) are phases of the same entity, and we have a story about why the earlier self's preferences should govern. When a constitution entrenches a provision — say, by requiring a two-thirds supermajority for amendment, or by declaring certain clauses unamendable outright — who exactly is playing the role of Ulysses? The founding generation? The set of all citizens considered as a trans-temporal collective? And if the same constitution also contains popular sovereignty language claiming that all authority derives from "the people," are we not presupposing that a past people can legitimately bind a present people, which seems to deny the present people the very sovereignty the document claims to ground itself in? That looks like a tension worth taking seriously, not just a metaphor stretched a bit thin.

What I'd like you to do is reconstruct the implicit theory — or theories — that constitutional precommitment practices presuppose about collective agency and democratic identity over time. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and flag where it's philosophically underdetermined such that several rival accounts could license the same institutional design. If the practice rests on inconsistent presuppositions, or if the whole Ulysses-contract framing is itself a confusion that distorts more than it illuminates, I'd rather you said so plainly than tried to patch it up.
