I chair the monthly risk committee of a mid-sized insurer. For familiar lines — car collisions, house fires — our actuaries give us clean numbers and we price accordingly. But we are increasingly asked to underwrite exposures with no stable frequency behind them: a new class of ransomware attack, a first-of-its-kind industrial process, a liability line for a technology that barely exists. The analysts still want a single probability for each, and they get frustrated when I say the number is doing no real work.

Keynes's account of probability and uncertainty holds that probability is often not a single number at all: some comparisons are only partial, some matters admit no calculable probability, and the amount of evidence behind a probability — what he called the weight of argument — matters as much as the probability itself. Turn that into a procedure my committee can actually run in a two-hour meeting. It should be a fixed sequence of questions with rules for what counts as a sufficient answer, a point at which we must refuse to assign a number and classify the case as uncertainty rather than measurable risk, and a point at which the decision is handed to human judgment because it turns on values or risk appetite rather than calculation. Do not offer me a menu of considerations; commit to one procedure and defend each choice.

Then run the procedure on two cases: (1) pricing coverage for a new strain of ransomware attack where we have only a handful of incidents worldwide, and (2) deciding whether to enter a market for insuring autonomous-vehicle fleets before any major loss data exists. Mark the steps at which reasonable people could follow your procedure and still disagree.
