I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy. For the past several months I have been designing the governance layer for a small DAO: a set of rules, partly encoded in smart contracts and partly held in off-chain documents, that specifies how proposals are made, how votes are weighted, and above all how the rules themselves may be changed. The work has forced me to think about self-amendment in a concrete way, and a friend recently pointed me to Peter Suber's game Nomic and to the philosophical discussion of self-amending rules and legal self-reference. I understand my own technical material well — runtime modification of code, reflection and eval, self-hosting compilers, quines, upgradeable smart contracts and proxy patterns, configuration-as-code, version control, merge conflicts, forks, consensus mechanisms — but I do not have the philosophical vocabulary, and I worry that when I try to think about Nomic I simply redescribe it as a programming problem and miss whatever is distinctive about it.

Please restate the central philosophical problem or problems about self-amending rules in the vocabulary I actually use, as faithfully as you can. In particular, I want to understand: the apparent paradox that an amendment procedure seems to require an unamendable fixed point — the rule that authorizes amendment, or the criterion that identifies what counts as a rule — while also seeming to permit amendments that could alter the amendment procedure itself, perhaps even abolishing it; the relation between a rule and the later rule produced under it; and whether a self-amending rule is best understood as one rule that changes over time or as a sequence of distinct rules.

Then give me the residue report. Tell me which parts of the philosophical problem translate cleanly into my vocabulary — which parts really are like a self-hosting compiler, a quine, or a contract behind an upgradeable proxy — and which parts you had to distort in order to fit. Most importantly, tell me what does not survive the translation at all. I suspect the untranslatable residue includes the authority a rule has over its own framers, the difference between a rule's being in force and its actually being followed, and the role of interpretation in constituting what a rule says, but I want your judgment rather than my guesses. Conclude by saying whether the residue indicates that my software vocabulary is missing something the philosophers are right to insist on, or whether some of the philosophical problem is less substantive than it looks once it is stripped of a certain way of talking.
