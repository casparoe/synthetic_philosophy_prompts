You are a professional philosopher of biology who works on theories of function. Think through the argument below carefully before answering, and do not settle for the textbook contrast between the two accounts. I want a diagnosis, not a summary.

I have been trying to adjudicate between the etiological account of biological function and the causal-role account, and a colleague has convinced me that the following argument shows something deeply strange. I cannot find the flaw, but I cannot accept the conclusion either.

(1) Function ascriptions are normative. To say that the function of the heart is to pump blood is to say what hearts are supposed to do; that is what allows us to call a heart that fails to pump blood malfunctioning rather than merely unusual.

(2) On the standard causal-role account, a trait's function is its causal contribution to some capacity of a containing system, where the system and the capacity are fixed by an explanatory interest.

(3) A causal-role ascription therefore records what a trait does, or is disposed to do, within a system. It cannot record what a trait is supposed to do, and it cannot distinguish a trait that is malfunctioning from one that simply lacks that causal role.

(4) So the causal-role account cannot capture the normative character of function ascriptions.

(5) The etiological account appears to do better. On that account, the heart's function is to pump blood because pumping blood is the effect of ancestral hearts that explains why hearts were selected for and persisted.

(6) But "hearts were selected for pumping blood" is itself only a causal-historical claim: hearts whose pumping of blood contributed to their bearers' reproductive success were the hearts that proliferated.

(7) A purely causal-historical claim cannot ground a standard of correctness. From the fact that pumping blood happened to be associated with reproductive success in the past, nothing follows about what this particular heart, here and now, is supposed to do.

(8) So the etiological account's "proper function" is normative only by courtesy. It describes a causal history and then imports the word "proper," but the history by itself supplies no norm.

(9) If neither the causal-role account nor the etiological account can ground the normativity of function ascriptions, then function talk in biology is either not normative after all, or it is systematically misleading and should be replaced by purely causal or statistical description.

(10) But that conclusion is unacceptable. The distinction between malfunctioning and merely being different is load-bearing in physiology and medicine, and ordinary biological practice treats "the heart is supposed to pump blood" as true.

(11) Therefore at least one of (1)–(8) is false, although each step looked forced when I went through it.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would most like to keep (1) and (10); beyond that I have no settled loyalties. Walk me through the strongest versions of both accounts before you diagnose the argument, and in your answer please separate clearly what is settled among philosophers of biology from what is genuinely contested. Do not present a contested view as the consensus. If the argument contains an equivocation or other fallacy, name the step and the move; if it is valid and the real question is which premise to give up, say which premise is least costly to abandon and what I pay for abandoning it.
