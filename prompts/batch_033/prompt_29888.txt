i got called for jury duty last month and they kept asking whether we could convict on purely statistical evidence, which got me thinking about when high probability is enough for belief. i'm putting together my views for a reading group and want them stress-tested before i present. heres where i've landed:

(1) rational belief is closed under conjunction: if you're rational to believe p and rational to believe q, then you're rational to believe p-and-q.

(2) rational belief doesn't require certainty. a sufficiently high probability is enough, even though there's no sharp cutoff for how high counts as sufficient.

(3) if i hold one ticket in a fair 10,000-ticket lottery, i'm rational to believe my ticket will lose. the fact that some ticket must win doesn't seem to touch my belief about my own ticket.

(4) the preface case also seems fine to me: an author can be rational to believe each individual sentence in her book, and rational to believe that at least one sentence in the book is false. that's just intellectual humility about your own fallibility.

(5) you can't rationally believe an outright contradiction.

(6) courts are right not to convict on bare statistical evidence alone, even when the probability is very high, like 99%. i'm not sure whether that's in tension with (2) or (3), but it feels like it might be.

can you go through these and tell me which pairs are actually in tension and which only look like they are? rank the tensions by severity, say what the cheapest fix would be for each, and tell me which single revision would clean up the most. dont just say every pair is equally bad. at the very end, put \answer{...} with the number of the commitment you'd revise first (for example, \answer{3}).
