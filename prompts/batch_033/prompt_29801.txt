Two people in my political theory reading group have been arguing for months about whether citizens in a liberal democracy may base their political positions on religious reasons. One is a Rawlsian about public reason: he thinks that on constitutional essentials and matters of basic justice, citizens owe one another reasons they can reasonably be expected to share, and while he has no problem with people also voicing religious reasons, he denies that those can serve as the decisive justification for laws that coerce people who don't share the faith. The other thinks this gets it exactly backward. She says requiring religious citizens to translate their deepest commitments into secular or public vocabulary treats those commitments as second-class, and that a law can be fully legitimate even if the main case for it, made honestly in public, is a religious argument. I finally got them to agree to an adversarial collaboration instead of another year of talking past each other.

Write the document they should produce. Include: (1) a numbered list of claims about pluralism, coercion, legitimacy, and the role of reasons in politics that both of them can sign, in wording that is not vague enough to paper over anything; (2) a table of the exact remaining disagreements, with each side's position phrased so that side would accept it as accurate rather than a caricature; (3) the cruxes, meaning the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them; and (4) for each crux, a concrete case or kind of evidence both would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. I suspect one crux is whether the supposedly neutral justifications for the contested cases they actually care about (school vouchers, abortion, immigration) really are freestanding, or whether they always smuggle in a comprehensive doctrine; another is whether public reason demands something asymmetric from religious citizens that it never demands of secular ones. But dont just take my guesses—make the cruxes as sharp as you can. Format it as numbered agreed claims, then a disagreements table, then a cruxes section.
