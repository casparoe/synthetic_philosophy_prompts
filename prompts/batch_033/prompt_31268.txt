Two people in my reading group got into it last week, and I'm still not sure how to score it, so I'd like a referee's breakdown. Dana has been defending something she calls lexical threshold negative utilitarianism. Her version: below some intensity of suffering, pleasures and pains trade off roughly the way ordinary utilitarians assume; but past a threshold — the kind of suffering that fully takes over a person's experience — comensurability just breaks down, and no amount of mild pleasure anywhere can outweigh or compensate for it, so preventing that suffering always wins. Marcus thinks this is either incoherent or deontology in a consequentialist costume.

(1) Marcus opened by saying that if no amount of pleasure can outweigh torture-level pain, she's effectively assigned infinite disvalue to it — or at least made the view non-Archimedean, which is the same thing for practical purposes. A finite ethics can't run that way: a 0.001% reduction in the risk of one such pain would be worth literally any amount of mild pleasure, which would mean stripping the world bare for a rounding error.

(2) Dana said that's a misreading. Lexical priority isn't infinite value. It's a lexicographic ordering, like dictionary order: if the first letters differ, the rest of the word never matters, but that doesn't make 'b' infinitely greater than 'a.' She's not saying torture has infinite magnitude; she's saying the scale that works below the threshold doesn't extend above it.

(3) Marcus said that's worse, not better. Consequentialism only gets its bite from having a common currency. If you make pleasure and pain incommensurable exactly where trade-offs are hardest, you've stopped being able to give guidance precisely when it's needed, and you've smuggled in a deontological constraint — "never allow extreme suffering for pleasure" — under utilitarian-sounding language.

(4) Dana replied that it's still a maximizing view with a lexicographic ordering, so it gives guidance; it just gives guidance he doesn't like. And we already act as if some pains are non-compensable: no society lets a rich person buy the right to break someone's arm no matter how much they pay, and that tracks something real about moral phenomenology, not just law.

(5) Marcus answered that the rich-person example is about consent and rights, not about the commensurability of pleasures and pains. Her view still implies one person's agony should be prevented at the cost of any amount of mild pleasure to everyone else — a billion people each losing an hour of mild contentment, say — and she hasn't earned that bullet.

(6) Dana said she'll bite it. Mild contentment and agony are not the same currency, and the whole point of the view is that refusing to bite that bullet is what makes ordinary utilitarianism too coarse.

They both think they won. Can you go through this move by move and say who's right about what — where each move is solid, where it's off, and what they're both missing? I'm less interested in a winner than in a precise scorecard.
