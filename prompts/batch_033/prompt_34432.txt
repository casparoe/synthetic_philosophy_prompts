I've been trying to get clear on when someone has the standing to blame, and I keep going in circles. Here are my gut reactions on a bunch of cases -- I'd like to know what principle (if any) captures them, and which of my verdicts I might have to give up.

1. The hypocrite politician. A senator who's currently evading taxes gives a floor speech denouncing tax evasion as an outrage against the social contract. I think he has no standing to blame tax evaders. Not even a little.

2. The recovering alcoholic. A man who ruined his marriage through drinking, has been sober for six years, and now volunteers at a recovery center tells a friend he needs to stop drinking or he'll lose his family. I think he absolutely has standing, and maybe even more than someone who's never struggled with it.

3. The meddling stranger. Someone in another country, with no connection to me or my situation, posts a long public condemnation of how I'm raising my kids, based on a single article they read. I think they lack standing. It's none of their business.

4. The complicit planner. Three people plan a robbery. One of them gets cold feet the night before and doesn't show up, but also doesn't warn anyone. The other two go through with it and someone gets hurt. The one who backed out now blames the other two for the violence. I think he has no standing -- he helped set the whole thing in motion.

5. The "I would have done it too." A friend tells me that what I did was wrong, but then adds, honestly, "I would have done the exact same thing in your shoes." I can't shake the feeling that this undercuts their standing to blame me, even though they're not a hypocrite in the usual sense -- they haven't actually done it.

6. The racist whistleblower. Someone reports their company's accounting fraud, but their motivation is clearly racist: they want to bring down the CEO, who is a racial minority. The fraud is real and serious. I'm torn. Part of me thinks the racism poisons their standing to blame. Another part thinks the fraud is still fraud and calling it out is still the right thing.

7. The former victim. An adult who was severely bullied as a child now calls out a colleague for bullying behavior at work. I think they have standing -- maybe unique standing, actually. Their history doesn't disqualify them; it gives them a kind of authority.

8. The close friend vs. the stranger. My best friend tells me I was unfair to my partner. A stranger who overheard the conversation tells me the same thing. Both are right -- I was unfair. But the stranger's blame feels like an intrusion in a way the friend's doesn't. I think the friend has standing and the stranger doesn't, or at least much less.

9. The reformed boss. Someone who was a terrible, exploitative manager for a decade, genuinely came to see the harm they caused, apologized, and changed their behavior, now calls out another manager for the same exploitative practices. I think they have standing, and it doesn't feel like hypocrisy.

10. The dirty hands accomplice. A whistleblower who participated in the wrongdoing for years before coming forward. They were fully complicit, not just a planner who backed out. They now publicly blame their former colleagues. Unlike case 4, I think this person might have standing -- or at least, I'm not sure they don't. And I can't figure out what the difference is between case 4 and case 10.

My tentative guess is that standing has something to do with whether your blame expresses a genuine commitment to the value you're invoking, rather than just using it as a weapon. But I can't make that work for all of these. Cases 1, 2, and 9 seem to hang together -- the politician hasn't changed, the alcoholic and the reformed boss have. But then case 4 and case 10: the complicit planner hasn't changed either, yet I treat them differently from the politician. And case 6 -- the racist whistleblower -- seems to be genuinely committed to the value of honesty in accounting, just for the wrong reasons. Does that matter?

What I want from you: tell me what principle (or small set of principles) best captures my verdicts, where it forces me to revise one, and what it predicts for a case I haven't listed. And if my tentative principle is on the right track, fix it.
