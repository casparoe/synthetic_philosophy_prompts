I'm in a metaphysics reading group and we've been going through the dispositions literature—finks, masks, antidotes, mimics, the whole menagerie. I tried writing down my verdicts on the standard cases, and now I'm stuck: I can't tell whether there's a single principle behind them or whether I'm just pattern-matching without a theory. Here are my verdicts, and I'd like you to help me find the principle—or tell me which verdicts I need to give up.

(1) A wine glass is fragile. A sorcerer watches over it; if anything were to strike it, he would instantly change its molecular structure to steel, and it wouldn't break. I still say the glass is fragile. (2) The same wine glass, no sorcerer, but wrapped in four inches of packing foam. It's struck with a hammer and doesn't break. Still fragile. (3) A solid steel paperweight is not fragile. But a different sorcerer watches over it; if the paperweight were struck, he would instantly turn it to glass with the same shape, and it would shatter. I say the paperweight is not fragile—it's steel, and the sorcerer's intervention doesn't make it fragile. (4) A vial of cyanide is poisonous. But every potential victim in the room carries an antidote that completely neutralizes cyanide, which they would take immediately upon ingestion. Still poisonous. (5) A sugar cube is not poisonous. But one person in the room has a rare metabolic disorder that makes even a gram of sugar lethal. I still say the sugar cube is not poisonous—the disorder is in the victim, not the cube. (6) A man is irascible—he has a hair-trigger temper. But he also takes a beta-blocker every morning that suppresses his anger response; when provoked, he stays calm. I say he's still irascible; the drug masks the manifestation. (7) A match is flammable. It's been soaked in water for an hour. I say it's still flammable, though it won't light right now—the water is a mask, and if you dried it out, it would light fine. (8) Here's the one that bothers me. A sample of uranium-238 has a half-life of about 4.5 billion years. In any given minute, it's overwhelmingly likely not to decay. When I say it's "disposed to decay," I mean something true. But the conditional "if it were to undergo the right stimulus, it would decay" doesn't seem to apply—radioactive decay is spontaneous, not triggered. So whatever principle I come up with has to handle dispositions that don't have stimulus conditions in any straightforward sense.

I can feel a few tensions in my verdicts. Cases (1) and (3) are mirror images—in (1) the conditional "if struck, would break" is false but fragility is present; in (3) the conditional is true but fragility is absent—and yet I'm confident in both. That suggests I'm implicitly relying on something about the object's intrinsic nature, not just the counterfactual. But then (2) and (4) and (6) and (7) all involve cases where the manifestation doesn't occur, and in none of them do I want to say the disposition is absent, even though in (2) the intrinsic nature of the glass isn't being changed by the foam. So "intrinsic nature unchanged" isn't doing all the work either. And (5) and (3) both involve a factor external to the object that would produce the manifestation, yet I call the object non-fragile and non-poisonous respectively—but in (3) the factor is a sorcerer who changes the object, while in (5) the factor is a feature of the patient. Does that distinction matter? I think it does, but I can't articulate why.

What I'm after: a precise statement of the conditions under which an object has a disposition D (to manifest M in response to stimulus S, or spontaneously as in the decay case), which gets my verdicts right on cases (1) through (7) and says something sensible about (8). If no single principle gets all of them, tell me which ones I should revise and why. Also, apply your principle to a case I haven't considered: a person who has the photic sneeze reflex—they're disposed to sneeze in bright sunlight—but who has been given a drug that temporarily blocks the reflex. Does the principle say they still have the disposition while the drug is active? And what does it say about the fragility of a glass that is currently being heated in a furnace to the point of melting—is it fragile during the process, or is the disposition temporarily absent?
