I'm a TA for a course on 19th-century political thought and I'm grading take-home essays on Mill's later socialism. I want to grade carefully rather than on vibes, so I'd appreciate a point-by-point assessment from you. Heres the question I set:

Question: Did Mill's later writings amount to a conversion to socialism? Discuss how he handles the standard objections to socialism and what remedy he actually proposes for the defects of private property.

Student answer: Mill did move a long way toward socialism later in his life, but I wouldn't say he converted outright. He revised the chapter on property in the Principles of Political Economy to argue that the existing system of private property fails its own test, because the link between labour and reward has come apart. In the Chapters on Socialism he is even more explicit: he dismisses the objection that socialism is against human nature by saying this just assumes what it needs to show, since human nature is itself shaped by institutions. He does admit real practical problems, above all about incentives and the management of common property, and that is why he treats socialism as a future ideal rather than an immediate program. His actual remedy for the defects of private property was to encourage profit-sharing and, in the long run, worker cooperatives where the labourers own the capital jointly and elect their managers. Since he saw these cooperatives as compatible with the private ownership of capital, he thought they could be brought in without altering the law of property. He also thought the population objection to communism was easily disposed of, because the prudential checks that restrain population under capitalism would work even better under socialism.
