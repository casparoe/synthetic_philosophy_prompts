You are a professional philosopher who works on decision theory and the foundations of game theory; I am a philosophy professor commissioning a document for an advanced seminar on rationality and the foundations of game theory. Think carefully, and begin by sketching the core commitments of each position below before drafting anything; do not move straight to the final text. The deliverable should be self-contained, with no citations or bibliography, and should not fabricate sources.

Draft the joint statement that a committed proponent of Hofstadter-style superrationality and a committed standard game theorist could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration about the one-shot, two-player, symmetric Prisoner's Dilemma between equally rational players.

The two sides accept the same setup: each player chooses exactly once, without communication, commitment, or enforcement; the payoffs have the usual ordering (temptation > reward > punishment > sucker), so defection strictly dominates cooperation for each player. The superrationalist maintains that cooperation is nevertheless the rational play: equally rational players in identical situations will choose identically, so only the symmetric outcomes—both cooperate or both defect—are live, and each does better under mutual cooperation than under mutual defection. The standard game theorist maintains that defection is the unique rational play: a strictly dominated act cannot be rational, and neither symmetry nor common knowledge of rationality justifies treating the asymmetric outcomes, in which one player cooperates and the other defects, as not live, absent a causal or institutional mechanism linking the two choices.

Produce the document such a collaboration would produce, as follows.

(1) Agreed claims. A numbered section of claims both parties can sign. Cover at least: the payoff structure and what dominance does and does not establish; what symmetry and common knowledge of rationality each do and do not entail; the conditions under which cooperation would be rational, such as against a known deterministic copy of oneself, under enforceable agreement, or in indefinitely repeated play; and the distinction between recommending an act and predicting an outcome. Make these claims precise rather than vacuous; if a formulation commands agreement only because it is ambiguous, rephrase it until it is not.

(2) Disagreements table. A table with one row per residual disagreement. Columns: (i) the question at issue; (ii) the superrationalist's position, worded as the superrationalist would accept it; (iii) the standard game theorist's position, worded as the standard game theorist would accept it. Neither column should read as a strawman of the other side.

(3) Cruxes. Identify the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. Include among the candidate cruxes: whether identical reasoning processes in identical situations can ground a non-causal dependence between the two players' acts; what the symmetry condition must include—payoffs, information, computational capacities, decision procedures—and how much of it is doing the work; whether the superrationalist recommendation is robust to small asymmetries or to a small probability that the other player is irrational, and what it would mean if it is not; and whether the reasoning that yields cooperation in the symmetric Prisoner's Dilemma commits the superrationalist to one-boxing in Newcomb's problem, to cooperating in an asymmetric Prisoner's Dilemma, or to team reasoning in Hi-Lo.

(4) Tests and predictions. For each crux, specify a concrete case or argument that both sides would accept as bearing on it—for example, a game against a known deterministic copy of oneself, the same game with a payoff-irrelevant or payoff-relevant asymmetry introduced, Newcomb's problem, and a variant in which one player is known to be irrational with some small probability. For each case, record in advance what each side predicts the case would show, and what result would count as a revision of that side's view.

Finally, if you judge that any part of the dispute is merely verbal—a disagreement that dissolves once terms such as "dependence," "rational," or "identical situation" are fixed—say which part and why, and adjust the agreed-claims section accordingly. The value of the deliverable is in the drafting: formulations that are neither slanted nor vacuous, and a residual disagreement made exact rather than rhetorical.
