A colleague of mine, Dana, has spent years teaching the separateness-of-persons objection to utilitarianism, and I've been keeping a mental file of how she rules on the standard cases. It's a surprisingly stable list:

- Trolley switch: permissible.
- Footbridge push: impermissible.
- Transplant (one healthy patient carved up to save five): impermissible.
- Drowning rescue, five on one side and one on the other, can only reach one side: she says you're required to save the five.
- Scarce drug, one patient needs the entire dose to survive, five others each need a fifth of it: she says give it to the five.
- Torture of a suspect to find a bomb: impermissible, even to save a lot of people.

So she isnt a straight consequentialist, and she isnt an absolutist about harming either. The case I'm stuck on is new, and it's not about lives at all: a public-health program would predictably cause one person a serious, permanent injury (say, blindness) as a side effect of preventing several hundred thousand people from getting a mild, week-long rash. Nobody is used as a means, and the injury isn't intended; it's just a statistical certainty of the intervention.

What would Dana say about the public-health case, if she's trying to stay consistent with her six verdicts? Lay out the candidate rules that fit her track record, say what each one predicts about the new case, and then give me your best guess about which rule she's actually running. And tell me which further case would cleanly separate the leading candidates. Ive got my own suspicion, but I'd rather see it worked through.
