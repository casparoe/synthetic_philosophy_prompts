I'm in a graduate seminar on Hume, and we've spent the last three sessions on Section X of the Enquiry. I've constructed an argument that worries me, because it seems to show that Hume's maxim for evaluating miracle testimony either collapses into a much broader skepticism about testimony than Hume himself would accept, or relies on a distinction that his own framework can't draw without begging the question. I can't tell whether the argument is sound, and if it isn't, I can't locate the mistake.

Here's the argument:

(1) Hume's maxim: "no testimony is sufficient to establish a miracle, unless the testimony be of such a kind, that its falsehood would be more miraculous, than the fact, which it endeavours to establish."

(2) A miracle, for Hume, is a violation of a law of nature. And a law of nature is established by "a firm and unalterable experience" — that is, by uniform, exceptionless experience of the relevant regularity.

(3) So the maxim instructs us to weigh the probability that the testimony is false against the probability of the event reported, where the latter probability is fixed by our uniform past experience.

(4) But now consider how we come to know the laws of nature in the first place. We do not personally observe most of the uniformities that constitute natural law. We rely on testimony: the reports of scientists, the readings of instruments, the contents of textbooks, the consensus of experts. Each piece of this testimony is fallible; each has some probability of being false.

(5) By the same maxim, when we receive testimony for a surprising scientific finding — say, that particles can be in superposition, or that time dilates at relativistic speeds — we must weigh the probability that the testimony is false against the probability of the event reported. But the event reported conflicts with our prior uniform experience (or at least with the experience of ordinary observers), so its probability is extremely low.

(6) Therefore, by parity of reasoning, Hume's maxim seems to require us to reject testimony for surprising scientific discoveries in just the same way it requires us to reject testimony for miracles. But we do not, and should not, reject such testimony. Scientists rationally revise their understanding of natural law on the basis of testimony.

(7) So either Hume's maxim is not the correct principle for evaluating testimony (it proves too much), or it is being applied to miracles in a way that does not generalize — and if it does not generalize, we need an account of what special feature of miracle reports justifies a stricter standard.

I'm most suspicious of step (5). Perhaps there's a difference between "improbable relative to ordinary experience" and "violation of a law of nature," and that difference does the work. But here's why I can't make that stick: Hume defines a law of nature in terms of uniform experience. If new testimony can lead us to revise what we took to be a law of nature — as it does in the scientific cases — then whether some piece of testimony counts as a report of a miracle or as a report that prompts revision of a law depends on whether we are antecedently willing to revise the law. But that is exactly what is at issue when we evaluate the testimony. So the distinction between a miracle and a merely surprising natural event seems to presuppose the very judgment the maxim is supposed to guide. If that's right, the maxim either begs the question against the miracle reporter or must be applied so broadly that it rules out rational belief revision across the board.

I'd like to keep Hume's basic insight — that testimony should be evaluated against background experience — but I want to know exactly which step to reject, and what I lose by rejecting it. Where does this argument go wrong?
