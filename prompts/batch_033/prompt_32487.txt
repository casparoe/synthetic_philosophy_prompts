In most democracies, a person who immigrates, lives in the country for twenty years, pays taxes, and raises children there cannot vote in national elections unless they naturalize. Meanwhile, a citizen who moves abroad and never returns can often vote for life in the country they left. Between these two people, the one with the thinner connection to the country's laws and policies has the franchise, and the one with the thicker connection does not.

I'm trying to understand what theory of democratic inclusion — of who properly belongs to the demos — this pair of practices presupposes. It seems to rule out the view that the franchise should track who is subject to the laws (since the non-citizen resident is, and the expatriate often isn't) and the view that it should track who is affected by the government's decisions (for the same reason). What remains? A theory based on national membership or citizenship as a kind of identity that persists independently of where you live? But then what is the philosophical justification for defining the demos that way rather than by residence, subjection to law, or affected interests? And is the practice internally consistent — can it justify also denying the vote to sixteen-year-olds who work and pay taxes, or to the residents of a capital city who are governed by a federal district without full representation? Reconstruct the implicit theory of the demos, identify where it is philosophically underdetermined, and say what institutional changes would follow from replacing it with an all-affected-interests principle or an all-subjected principle.
