I spent my career in the lab — physiology, mostly — and I've only recently started reading philosophy. I'm trying to get clear on what I actually think about the tradeoff between pleasure and pain, and I'd like help extracting the principle behind my reactions to the following cases.

1. A friend tells me she would accept a year of moderate back pain in exchange for a single afternoon of the most intense aesthetic pleasure she has ever experienced — say, hearing a perfect performance of the Bach cello suites in an ideal setting. I find this trade bewildering; the pain seems vastly weightier than the pleasure, and I can't imagine making it myself.

2. But if you asked me whether I'd accept a single brief pinprick in exchange for a year of mild contentment — the quiet satisfaction of a well-lived ordinary day — I'd take the pinprick without hesitation. The pleasure seems to win here, even though it's diffuse and unremarkable.

3. Now consider a different scale. Would I authorize the infliction of a minute of genuinely excruciating torture on one person, if doing so would guarantee a thousand people a lifetime of moderate happiness each? My answer is no — the torture is simply not for sale at that price, or at any price paid in happiness.

4. Yet when I flip the numbers, I grow uncertain. What if the torture is inflicted on one person to prevent a billion people from each experiencing a single pinprick? Here I feel the aggregate matters, and I'm not sure the torture is worse than a billion pinpricks. But if I accepted that, wouldn't I have to revisit case 3? A thousand lifetimes of happiness might well aggregate to more than a billion pinpricks.

5. One more. A medical researcher proposes a trial that will cause fifty volunteers to experience moderate nausea for a week, in order to develop a drug that will spare millions from a slightly worse nausea for a day. I find this acceptable. But if the trial required inflicting severe, traumatic pain on a single volunteer — the kind that leaves psychological scars — no number of beneficiaries spared a day of moderate nausea would make it acceptable to me.

I seem to have a threshold somewhere: sufficiently intense suffering cannot be outweighed by any amount of mere pleasure or mild discomfort, no matter how many people enjoy the benefit. But I also seem to think that mild pains can be aggregated to outweigh more intense ones, at least in some cases (as in case 4, where a billion pinpricks might outweigh a minute of torture). And I'm not sure these two thoughts are consistent.

Is there a principle that captures all or most of these verdicts? Please state it precisely, test it against cases I haven't listed, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. I'd also like to know what argument or consideration would most likely change my mind about the core intuition — that extreme suffering is lexically prior to pleasure — and where my reasoning on this is most vulnerable.
