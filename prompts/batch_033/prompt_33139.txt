I teach a course on weakness of will and time preference, and this term I've started collecting my own snap judgments on cases, and they refuse to line up. Let me give them to you as honestly as I can.

1. If you offer me $100 now or $120 in a month, I take the $100 now. But if you offer me $100 in eleven months or $120 in twelve, I take the $120. So I'm not just impatient; my impatience only kicks in when the sooner option is right in front of me.

2. Every January I buy a gym membership, go twice, and quit. I know in advance I'll quit — I'm not fooling myself. But I still buy it, and I don't think this is akratic. It feels like I'm paying for the chance of being someone who works out.

3. I set my alarm for 6 a.m. to run. At 6, half-asleep, I switch it off. At 8, showered and awake, I think I should have gotten up. But at 6 I didn't feel torn; I just felt like sleep was obviously the thing to do.

4. I make a dentist appointment for a procedure I genuinely dread, and I go. I don't cancel. No drama. That feels like the resolution working, not weakness of will.

5. In September I agree to give a colloquim talk in April. Two days before the talk, I want to cancel — not from nerves, but because the work genuinely seems pointless to me now. I think canceling would be akratic, but I also think the September me had no business signing up the April me for that much work. I can't get these two thoughts to sit together.

6. I quit smoking with a quit date of June 1. On June 1, I have a last cigarette and move the quit date to June 7. That feels like weakness of will. But on Sunday night I decide to cut out sugar, and on Tuesday I have dessert, and that just feels like revising a diet. Both cases: an earlier self sets a rule, a later self breaks it for a small sooner pleasure. I cant say what the difference is.

My working theory is that all of this is hyperbolic discounting — the near-term reward gets over-weighted, and that's all there is to it. But I don't think it handles the cigarette-versus-dessert difference, and it says nothing about why the dentist visit doesn't feel like a struggle. Is there one principle that captures my verdicts, or close? What does it say about cases I haven't listed — say, someone who pre-commits by putting money in an account that charges a fee for early withdrawal, or someone who takes a pill that makes the later temptation impossible? And which of my verdicts does the best principle force me to drop?
