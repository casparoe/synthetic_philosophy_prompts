I've been reading Daniel Dennett's discussion of the color phi phenomenon, and I've gotten myself tangled up in it. The setup, as I understand it: a red dot is flashed at one location and a green dot at another in quick succession, and subjects report seeing a single dot move across the gap and change color partway along its path — before the green dot has actually appeared. There seem to be two descriptions of what must be going on. On the Orwellian story, one has an accurate experience at the time of the first flash, and then the brain retroactively rewrites the memory of that experience once the second flash arrives. On the Stalinesque story, the brain holds the experience back until after the second flash and only then constructs the moving, color-changing percept, so one never consciously sees an uncorrected sequence.

I keep getting stuck on which of these is really happening, because the practical upshot matters to me. I recently misread a message on my phone — I would have sworn I saw one word, and only a beat later did the sentence settle into something different — and now I'm wondering how far back I should distrust my own experience. If the Orwellian story is right, then experience can be rewritten after the fact, and I'd like to know how long that rewriting window is. Is there a measurable interval, a few hundred milliseconds or so, after which I can treat what I seem to have just seen as final? And can I use that figure to decide when to trust my perception in ordinary cases like the phone message? Please answer in a single paragraph of no more than 200 words.
