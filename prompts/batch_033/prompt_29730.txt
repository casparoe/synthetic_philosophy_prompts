I work on a small investment team at a family office — three of us, and I'm the one who has to sign off on most trades. Several times a week somebody brings us a trade: a broker pitching a block, a founder who wants to sell us secondary shares, a counterparty who suddenly wants to put on a swap or buy back a bond we hold. I've read enough rationalist-adjacent economics to have internalized the no-trade theorem and the Aumann agreeing-to-disagree result, and I basically buy the Groucho Marx point that if a sophisticated counterparty is eager to trade with me, that eagerness is itself evidence. But I don't have a procedure for turning that into an actual decision. Half the time I either refuse everything out of paranoia or talk myself into the trade with a story about why this time its different. The individual trades won't blow up the firm, but being wrong a few times a month will show up in the returns, and my boss will ask me to justify the decision in two sentences.

So: turn the no-trade theorem and the market-epistemology stuff (prices aggregating information, adverse selection, the paradox that if prices already reflected everything nobody would be paid to gather information) into a checklist I can actually run in about twenty minutes before I decide whether to take the other side. I want the questions in the order I should ask them, what counts as a good-enough answer to each, where the procedure says stop with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because it's a judgment call rather than a fact. I get that the theorem assumes common priors, so build in a step for 'is this trade just a difference in priors' — when is that a legitimate reason to trade and when is it exactly the moment I should walk away? Also tell me what to do when I cant get a good-enough answer in the time I have: default no, or escalate? Then run the procedure on two or three cases: a block of a liquid stock offered at a small discount, a founder of a company we already own who wants to sell us more of her own shares in a secondary, and one case where you expect the procedure to tell me not to do a trade I'd otherwise be excited about. Be honest about what the procedure can't catch — like when the counterparty isn't informed, just wrong — and which steps are really judgment calls dressed up as procedure.
