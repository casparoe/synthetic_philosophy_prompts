I am a consultant in a busy accident and emergency (A&E) department. Several times each shift I make high-stakes diagnostic and disposition decisions under time pressure, and I routinely receive information that bears not on the patient's condition directly but on my own reliability in making the decision: I have been awake for a long stretch, departmental audit data show that error rates for this class of decision rise late in a shift, a junior doctor who reviewed the same notes and investigations disagrees with my diagnosis, or I recognise that my reaction to a particular patient may be driven by bias rather than by the clinical findings. I have no formal background in epistemology, but I am told there is a body of work in formal epistemology on higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia, and I want it turned into something I can use, not summarised.

Please write me a reusable decision procedure for deciding when and how far to revise, suspend, or override my own first-order clinical judgement in the light of evidence about that judgement itself. The procedure must be runnable in under ten minutes, at the bedside or at a workstation, by someone with no philosophical training. It should take the form of questions asked in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and it should say at each point whether I may decide alone, whether I must seek a second opinion, or whether I must hand the decision over entirely. In particular, the procedure must distinguish evidence that identifies a specific step of my reasoning as faulty from evidence that merely lowers my confidence in my reasoning without identifying where it went wrong, and it must say how to avoid double-counting considerations I have already taken into account. Mark clearly the steps that are genuine judgement calls rather than procedure, and mark the points where the underlying epistemology is itself unsettled and the procedure has to stipulate an answer.

Then apply the procedure to these three cases, showing each step: (a) a colleague who has reviewed the same notes and investigations reaches a different diagnosis; (b) I learn that I have been awake for twenty hours and that our own department's audit shows a substantially higher error rate for decisions of this kind in the final two hours of a shift; (c) a laboratory result is borderline and the laboratory reports that the assay was operating outside its calibration range when it produced the result. At least one of these should be a case in which the procedure tells me something I will not want to hear, and I want that said plainly.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: where the distinction between evidence about the patient and evidence about my own reasoning breaks down in practice, and where a step that looks procedural is really a value choice about acceptable risk.
