so my mom had early-onset alzheimers and about five years in, when she was still mostly lucid, she signed an advance directive saying no feeding tubes, no antibiotics for pneumonia — basically nothing beyond comfort care. then she declined way past that point and spent about three years in a nursing home where — and this is the thing i honestly cant get past — she genuinely seemed happy. not her-old-self happy, but she'd smile when someone held her hand, she loved the music therapy sessions, she had clear preferences about which aides she liked and which ones she didnt. she wasnt suffering. when she got pneumonia the directive said no antibiotics. the nursing home called me and i followed it. she died.

im supposed to give a short talk at a medical ethics conference — maybe eight minutes — about advance directives and the authority of the earlier self. the audience is mostly clinicians and clinical ethicists, some of whom have been doing this for decades and have strong views. i want to take a real position, not just "its complicated." ive come to think that when the later self is happy and content, even if its a radically different self from the one who signed the directive, the earlier selfs directive shouldnt automatically win. i want the talk to use my mothers case as the spine of it, but the argument has to be philosophical not just emotional — i need to actually engage with the reasons someone would give for overriding the advance directive or for insisting it holds. i also want to acknowledge the strongest objection to my view — the "thats exactly what advance directives are FOR" objection — and answer it squarely. committed position, definite reasons, not a survey of the landscape.
