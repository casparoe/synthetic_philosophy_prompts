I'm taking a seminar on moral responsibility this term, and we've spent the last three weeks on the moral luck literature. I've been trying to figure out what I actually think by working through cases, and I'd like to know what principle — if any — captures the pattern of verdicts I've settled on. Here they are.

1. Two drivers, equally drunk, run a red light. Driver A's car strikes and kills a child crossing the street. Driver B's car passes through the same intersection moments later and hits no one. I think Driver A deserves harsher legal punishment than Driver B, and I also think Driver A is more blameworthy — not just that we treat him differently for practical reasons, but that there is genuinely more for which he is to blame. But I think the gap in blameworthiness is much smaller than the gap in appropriate punishment.

2. Two people each attempt to murder their spouse by putting what they believe is arsenic into the spouse's tea. The first would-be murderer's supplier gave her real arsenic; the spouse dies. The second's supplier cheated her and gave her sugar; the spouse lives. I think the two are equally blameworthy. The outcome makes no difference here.

3. Anna grew up in a deeply racist community with racist parents and, as an adult, holds racist beliefs. Beth grew up in nearly identical circumstances — same town, similar family — but in high school happened to be seated next to a Black student in one class, became close friends with her, and as a result shed those beliefs. I think Anna is less blameworthy for her racism than someone who holds racist beliefs despite every advantage, but I don't think she is off the hook. I also don't think Beth deserves extra moral credit for not being racist — she just got lucky in who she sat next to.

4. Two soldiers in the same unit face the same terrifying battle. Soldier X freezes and abandons his post. Soldier Y also wants to flee but manages to hold his ground. I think Y deserves praise and X deserves blame, even though I accept that the difference between them may come down to neurochemistry neither chose. I don't think the fact that their temperaments are unchosen lets X off the hook.

5. A business executive makes a risky decision that, against the odds, pays off and saves the company. A second executive makes the same decision, with the same information and the same reasoning; in her case, the risk materializes and the company collapses. I don't think the first executive deserves more moral credit than the second. But I do think the first deserves more of something — call it esteem or admiration — and I'm not sure whether that something is moral or whether distinguishing it from moral credit just relabels the problem.

6. Here is the case that bothers me. Compare two people who each fail to donate to an effective charity. One was raised in a family that emphasized charitable giving; the other was raised in a family that never mentioned it. I think the first is more blameworthy than the second. But I can't see how this differs from case 4, where I said that unchosen temperament doesn't excuse. In case 4, the difference between the soldiers is unchosen; in case 6, the difference in upbringing is also unchosen. So why do I want to mitigate blame in one case but not the other?

My question: is there a coherent account of when and how factors beyond our control affect blameworthiness and credit that captures my verdicts in cases 1 through 5 and then helps me resolve case 6? If no single principle gets all of them, which verdict should I reconsider, and why? And if there is a principle, what does it predict about a case I haven't listed: someone who votes for a political candidate who, unexpectedly and against the voter's own expectations, goes on to cause great harm?
