I recently read a magazine essay on antinatalism, and the author compressed David Benatar's asymmetry argument into the following five sentences:

1. David Benatar argues that coming into existence is always a net harm, because every human life contains at least some pain.
2. The argument rests on an asymmetry: for an existing person, pain is bad and pleasure is good, but for a person who never exists, the absence of pain is good, while the absence of pleasure is not bad, since there is no one to be deprived of it.
3. So nonexistence would have been better for every actual person: the pains they avoided would be a positive gain, and the pleasures they missed would cost them nothing.
4. It follows that even a life that is happy on the whole was a harm to the person living it, and not merely worse than some alternative.
5. If the asymmetry is accepted, the only consistent conclusion is that procreation is always wrong and we should stop having children.

I find the argument gripping, but sentence 4 sounds stronger than anything I remember from the philosophical literature, and I cannot tell whether sentence 2's talk of what is good for a person who never exists even makes sense. Take the summary sentence by sentence: for each sentence, say whether it is accurate, subtly wrong, or overstated, and how a careful defender of the asymmetry would correct or qualify it.
