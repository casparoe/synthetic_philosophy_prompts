I'm preparing for my comprehensive exam in philosophy of science, and I want to get clear on what exactly a realist about scientific theories is committed to delivering before I start evaluating the main positions. The pessimistic meta-induction is the obvious problem: Laudan's list of once-successful, now-abandoned theories suggests that empirical success is not a reliable indicator of approximate truth, and any realist who wants to keep the no-miracles argument has to explain why the historical record doesn't simply refute it. But there are other constraints too — underdetermination, the nature of theory change, the role of idealization, the continuity question — and I want the full specification of what an adequate realist account must do, not just the one constraint that happens to be fashionable in the latest exchange.

Write the desiderata that any realist position in the scientific realism debate must satisfy to count as adequate. For each desideratum, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and flag whether it is non-negotiable (any realist must meet it) or merely a preference of a particular camp (e.g., structural realists, entity realists, selective realists, semirealists). Pay special attention to desiderata that pull in opposite directions: the realist wants to explain the success of science, which pushes toward robust truth-commitments, but also wants to survive the pessimistic induction, which pushes toward weakening those commitments. If some pair of desiderata cannot be jointly satisfied — or can only be satisfied by paying a cost most realists would reject — identify that tension explicitly.

Please give your answer as a numbered list of exactly seven desiderata, each described in two to three sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. One of the seven items should identify the single desideratum that does the most to eliminate currently popular realist positions, and another should flag the most serious tension between any two desiderata on the list.
