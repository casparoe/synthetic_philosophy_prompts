So in my math club two kids got into this whole argument about the continuum hypothesis and it kinda went in circles, so I want someone to actually referee it. I remember the back and forth pretty well.

Leo said he thinks CH is probably true. His reason was that nobody has ever managed to point to an actual set that's strictly bigger than the naturals and strictly smaller than the reals. Every set of reals anyone actually runs into, he said, is either countable or has the size of the continuum. So why believe in some invisible in-between size?

Sana said that's backwards. She said Gödel and Cohen already showed that—assuming the standard axioms (ZFC) are consistent—you can't prove or disprove CH from them, so there's no fact of the matter about it. She compared it to the parallel postulate: you can do Euclidean geometry and you can do non-Euclidean geometry, and asking which one is "really true" is a confused question. She said CH is the same kind of thing.

Leo came back and said independence just means our axioms are too weak, not that there's no truth. Sets are supposed to be one definite thing, like the natural numbers—we don't say arithmetic is "just a matter of which axioms you pick" just because there are nonstandard models of arithmetic. And he said the parallel postulate comparison fails because "point" and "line" were never pinned down to one intended meaning, whereas "set" is. Plus, he said, even for geometry the question of whether actual space is Euclidean turned out to have a real answer—it isn't—so the analogy cuts the other way.

Sana replied that set theory has the same problem as geometry: there's Gödel's universe L, where CH is true, and there are forcing universes where CH is false, and they both satisfy all the axioms. She said if two different universes both satisfy everything we've written down, what could possibly make one of them "the real" one? She said Leo is just assuming the exact thing he's trying to prove—that there's a real universe of sets out there.

Leo said that's giving up too early. He said people have proposed new axioms—forcing axioms, large cardinal stuff, I don't remember all the names—that could settle CH, and in fact some of them force CH to be false, which he said is a hint about what the truth is. The fact that ZFC alone can't decide it just shows ZFC is incomplete, not that the question is meaningless.

Sana said the fact that nobody can agree on which new axioms to add is itself evidence that there's no single right answer. She said the honest conclusion is that "set theory" is a whole family of equally legitimate universes, the way "geometry" is a whole family of spaces.

I couldnt follow all of it but it felt like both of them were making good points and also maybe both of them were sneaking something past.
