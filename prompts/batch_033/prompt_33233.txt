i'm a high school junior and something in my physics class has been bugging me. on free-response questions my teacher gives partial credit: if you set up the right equation but mess up the arithmetic you get most of the points, if you use a related-but-wrong formula you get a couple points, and if you write something completely off you get zero. he writes comments like 'closer' or 'right idea, wrong execution.' but on multiple-choice quizzes any wrong answer is just zero, and he never distinguishes between picking the answer that was one step off from the right one and picking something random. my friend says all false statements are equally false so partial credit is just made up, but that doesn't seem right either. so the same class treats wrongness two different ways: on free response, wrong answers can be more or less close to the truth, but on multiple choice all wrong answers are equally wrong. what view of truth is that grading practice presupposing? it seems to assume there's a real sense in which one false answer can be closer to the true answer than another false answer. is that assumption actually defensible, or is it just a convenient fiction for grading? and if someone rejected the whole idea of 'closeness to the truth' for false answers, what would have to change about how the class is graded? i'm not really asking for a grading policy; i want to know what the implicit theory is, whether its coherent, and where it falls apart.
