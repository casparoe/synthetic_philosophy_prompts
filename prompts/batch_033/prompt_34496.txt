I help run a philosophy discussion group at a community center, made up of thoughtful adults with no formal training in philosophy; I have none either, so everything I know on this topic comes from my own reading and thinking. Next month I want to lead a session on the cosmological argument, specifically the version that begins from the observation that some things exist but might not have existed, and reasons to something that exists of its own nature and could not have failed to exist. I know this is a different argument from the one that begins from the claim that the universe had a beginning in time, and part of my goal for the session is to keep the two from collapsing into each other, because the group tends to run them together.

What I am asking for is not a summary of the argument but a teaching plan for one ninety-minute meeting. I want the order in which the ideas have to be introduced, with the reason each idea cannot land until the previous one is in place; for example, I suspect the word necessary will cause trouble unless some prior distinction has already been made. Then I want the single concrete example or image that should carry the whole session: what it must have, what it must not suggest, and why the familiar domino-chain or everything-has-a-cause picture is probably the wrong carrier for this version, if it is. Then I want a map of the misconceptions the group will arrive with or develop, my own guesses being that God would then need a cause too, that this is just the beginning-of-the-universe argument dressed up, and that necessary just means unimaginable not to exist; for each misconception, tell me the exact point in the sequence where I should head it off before it hardens. I would also like to know which standard objections I should openly set aside for the night and say so, and which ones I cannot set aside because they would leave a hole in the middle of the explanation. Finally, give me one question to ask at the end whose answer would show whether someone has really grasped the difference between a part of the universe having an explanation and the whole collection having one, rather than merely repeating the conclusion.

Please answer in American English. End with a single line beginning "Summary:" that gives a one-sentence summary of the teaching plan.
