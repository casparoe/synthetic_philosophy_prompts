I've been trying to understand Nozick's tracking theory of knowledge on my own, and I've worked through an argument that seems to show the theory collapses into a dilemma — either it leads to skepticism about almost everything, or it makes knowledge so cheap that it stops being worth wanting. I can't find the mistake, but the conclusion can't be right, or if it is right I want to understand why Nozick himself didn't draw it.

Here is the argument:

(1) Nozick says knowledge requires sensitivity: if P were false, you wouldn't believe P via the same method you actually used.

(2) This is meant to explain why you know ordinary things — that you have hands, that you ate toast for breakfast — but don't know the negations of skeptical hypotheses. If you were a brain in a vat, you'd still believe you weren't one, using the same method (how things seem to you). So "I am not a brain in a vat" fails sensitivity and you don't know it.

(3) Now take any ordinary belief based on memory. You believe you ate toast this morning via the method of recalling it. If you hadn't eaten toast — if you'd eaten cereal — you'd recall cereal, not toast. So the belief is sensitive. You know it.

(4) But now consider a different proposition: "My memory of eating toast this morning is not a false memory implanted five minutes ago." Is this belief sensitive? If it were a false memory, you'd still believe it wasn't — you'd consult the very memory in question, using the very same method. So it fails sensitivity. By Nozick's criterion, you don't know your memory isn't fabricated.

(5) The same structure repeats everywhere. For any perceptual belief, there is a corresponding "my experience is not a hallucination" proposition that fails sensitivity. For any testimonial belief, there is a "this person is not lying to me" proposition that fails sensitivity. For any belief at all, there is some error possibility — not a far-fetched skeptical scenario, just an ordinary way the belief could be false while leaving your evidence untouched — whose negation you cannot know.

(6) Nozick accepts this much. He denies that knowledge is closed under known entailment: you can know P, know P entails Q, and yet not know Q. So far, so good.

(7) But now notice what happens when we combine this with the fact that these unknowable propositions are not idle — they are presuppositions of your method. You believe you ate toast on the basis of memory. Whether your memory is reliable in this instance is not some distant skeptical worry; it is a precondition of your method working.

(8) So we reach the following picture: you know P via method M, but you do not know that M is trustworthy in this instance — you don't know that your memory isn't fabricating, that your eyes aren't hallucinating, that your informant isn't lying. And you cannot come to know these things, because any method you'd use to check would face the same problem at one remove.

(9) This means knowledge has become compatible with being unable to rule out that your evidence is completely misleading. You can know your spouse is upset (you see her expression) while admitting that, for all you know, you're misreading her. You can know the meeting is at three (she told you) while admitting that, for all you know, she's lying.

(10) But if knowledge is compatible with your evidence being worthless, then knowledge loses its connection to being a good basis for action. Why should I rely on something I know, if I simultaneously admit I can't tell whether the grounds for it are any good? Knowledge starts to look like a label we stamp on true beliefs that happen to be formed the right way, with no further significance.

(11) So Nozick's theory faces a dilemma. Horn one: bite the bullet and say you really don't know your spouse is upset, your meeting is at three, or anything else whose method-presuppositions you can't rule out — which is nearly everything. That's skepticism. Horn two: insist you do know these things, but accept that knowledge requires no ability to discriminate good evidence from bad. That makes knowledge trivial.

(12) Either way, the theory fails. But since Nozick was clearly trying to avoid both skepticism and triviality, I must be misreading him somewhere.

I can feel that something shifts between steps (7) and (9) — some slide from "you don't know one specific entailment of P" to "you can't trust your method at all." But I can't say exactly what the slide is or whether it's legitimate. I also suspect step (10) is smuggling in a requirement — that knowledge must be a good basis for action, or that you must be able to tell whether your evidence is good — that Nozick would simply reject. But if he rejects it, I want to know what knowledge is supposed to be for, on his picture, and whether the resulting account is still recognizably about knowledge.

Which step in this argument should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
