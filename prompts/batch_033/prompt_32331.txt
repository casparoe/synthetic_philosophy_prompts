Take the March 2018 Tempe crash — the Uber self-driving Volvo XC90 that struck and killed Elaine Herzberg while she was walking her bicycle across Mill Avenue — and run it through the responsibility-gap literature on autonomous systems. I dont want the cheap version where you conclude "nobody was responsible, gap confirmed." The NTSB blamed the safety driver for being visually distracted by her phone (she looked up about a second before impact), but it also cited Uber for disabling the Volvo's automatic emergency braking, for an inadequate safety culture, and for having no real mechanism for handling automation complacency, plus Arizona DOT for weak oversight, plus the pedestrian herself for crossing outside a crosswalk while impaired. That isnt obviously a gap; it looks more like an excess of responsibility, spread across a bunch of hands. So do the careful mapping: who are the candidate agents, what could each of them actually know and control, and which of them satisfies the conditions — causal, epistemic, control — that the gap argument says a responsible agent needs? Keep the no-hands/many-hands distinction in view: the gap literature says autonomy leaves you with no hands, but Tempe looks like it leaves you with too many. Which is it? Then use the case to push back on the theory. For instance, the ADS detected Herzberg 5.6 seconds before impact and tracked her until the crash but never classified her as a pedestrian or predicted her path, and the system was deliberately set up so it could not brake on its own — it had to rely on the human. Does that show the "gap" is less a fact about machine autonomy than something an organization produces by design, by splitting knowledge and control between a system and a bored human? And does corporate liability fill the hole that the individualist framework cant see, or just paper over it? I want the analysis to run both directions, theory to case and case back to theory, with an honest statement of where the framework goes silent.
