I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've been going back and forth on the old Gödelian anti-mechanist argument because the debate seems permanently stuck in a wierd way. The formal facts aren't in dispute: for any consistent formal system rich enough to do arithmetic, Gödel gives us a sentence the system can't prove but whose truth a human can see, at least if the human is allowed to see that the system is consistent. Lucas, and then Penrose in a stronger form, took this to show that no fixed machine program can capture human mathematical insight—we can always out-Gödel the machine. Mechanists came back with the obvious-looking moves: the argument only works if I can know that the candidate program is consistent, and Gödel's second theorem blocks that for any system I might actually be; the mind might not be a single consistent formal system; and the whole thing may only show that I can't identify my own program, not that I'm not one. Both sides seem to accept the mathematics and just pick different horns of the disjunction Gödel himself left us with. That's what puzzles me.

Put on your philosopher-of-mathematics-and-mind hat and think carefully. I want a diagnosis rather than a survey: is this a substantive disagreement about what a human mind can do, a verbal one about what it means to see a sentence is true or what consistency amounts to, two camps answering different questions under one heading, or an argument resting on an idealization nobody has cashed out? Start by brainstorming the two or three strongest mechanist replies and the strongest Lucas/Penrose rejoinder, then use them to isolate the move actually doing the work. Be explicit about what is settled—the theorems, the logical structure—versus what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and don't present a contested reading as the consensus. Then say what would actually count as progress: a sharper formal result, a new distinction, an empirical claim about human mathematicians, or a reason to expect none. And be honest if the right conclusion is that the question was badly posed from the start.
