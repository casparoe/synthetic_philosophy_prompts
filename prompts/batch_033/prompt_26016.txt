I've been running a multidisciplinary pain clinic for about eight years, and for most of that time I operated on a straightforward model: pain is bad, more pain is worse, and our job is to reduce pain scores. But two things have shaken that. First, we've had a handful of patients whose numerical pain ratings stayed high even as they reported that the pain had stopped ruining their lives — they'd say things like "it's still a seven, but I can live with it now" — which made our outcome metrics look like failures when clinically things had clearly improved. Second, our staff psychologist recently gave a journal-club presentation on pain asymbolia, the phenomenon where patients with lesions to certain cortical areas report that they continue to feel pain — they can locate it, rate its intensity, describe its quality — but insist it no longer bothers them. The unpleasantness has genuinely detached from the sensation. This isn't just an exotic neurological curiosity. It suggests that the badness of pain might not be a single thing, and that our standard treatment armamentarium — opioids, nerve blocks, gabapentinoids — mostly targets the sensory component while leaving the suffering component to luck or bedside manner.

I'd like you to help me turn this conceptual distinction into a practical decision procedure my team can use. The procedure should take a clinician through a structured assessment that teases apart the sensory intensity of a patient's pain from the suffering, distress, or life-disruption it causes, and then maps the results onto different treatment pathways — pharmacological, psychological, or combined. It should be usable by physicians, nurse practitioners, and physical therapists without background in philosophy or neuroscience, and it should take no more than fifteen minutes during an initial intake. It needs to handle the common case where the two dimensions are tangled together, and it should flag the points where the procedure's categories break down and a human has to exercise judgment. Please provide two or three worked examples — at least one where the procedure recommends deprescribing opioids in favor of psychological approaches (which the patient may resist), and at least one where the procedure can't give a clear answer. Finally, tell me what kinds of pain or patients this framework is likely to systematically mishandle.
