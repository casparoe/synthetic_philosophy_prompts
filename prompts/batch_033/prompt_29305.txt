I'm teaching a graduate seminar on practical rationality this term, and we're spending a few weeks on money pump arguments. The students can all reproduce the basic mechanics — cyclic preferences, a trader offers a series of trades each of which you prefer at the time, and you end up back where you started with less money. But I'd like to push them passed the mechanics, and I'm realizing I don't have a crisp way of laying out the conceptual geography myself. So here's what I want to get clearer on. First, what is the money pump argument actually an argument for? Is the irrationality of cyclic preferences supposed to consist in the fact that they leave you open to exploitation, or is the pump just a vivid way of dramatizing a deeper coherence problem — for instance, that such preferences can't be represented by a real-valued utility function? Second, does the argument carry over to an agent whose preferences merely change over time? Say someone prefers A to B on Monday and B to A on Tuesday; a trader can then buy and sell the same pair back to them and pocket a fee each time. In the synchronic case we can say the very same agent holds all three preferences at once, but in the diachronic case there's no single time at which the agent's preferences are intransitive. Does the diachronic pump only go through if we presuppose that the agent across time is a unified subject whose preferences ought to be stable across that span? I'd like an explanation of the conceptual structure of money pump arguments and how far they extend to preference change over time — focus on what the argument is really doing, not on the formal details.
