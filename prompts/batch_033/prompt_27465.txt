Two friends of mine got into it after a seminar on social choice theory, and neither would budge, so they asked me too referee. I told them I'd get back to them. The argument was about what Sen's liberal paradox actually shows.

To make sure we're all working from the same example: one copy of Lady Chatterley's Lover, two people, Prude and Lewd. Prude's ranking is: nobody reads it, then Prude reads it, then Lewd reads it. Lewd's ranking is: Prude reads it, then Lewd reads it, then nobody reads it. Sen's minimal liberalism condition gives each person a say over whether they themselves read it: so society ranks nobody-reads above Prude-reads (following Prude), and ranks Lewd-reads above nobody-reads (following Lewd). Transitivity then forces society to rank Lewd-reads above Prude-reads. But both of them prefer Prude-reads to Lewd-reads, so the Pareto principle says society should rank it the other way. Contradiction.

Their argument went roughly like this.

(1) Mira said the paradox shows individual rights and the Pareto principle are incompatible: you can't consistently respect both, so a liberal has to choose, and should choose rights. (2) Dev said that's way too fast. Sen models a right as nothing more than a person's preference over two outcomes being allowed to set the social ranking. Real rights are side-constraints on what others may do to you, not a veto over how the group ranks a state of affairs, so the theorem doesn't touch real liberalism. (3) Mira came back: even if rights are side-constraints, people still have to exercise them somehow, and then we still have to rank the resulting states; as soon as you do, the same conflict reappears, unless you give up the idea that unanimous preference should always be decisive — which was her point. (4) Dev said no, the real culprit is the Pareto principle counting meddlesome preferences. In the very example, Prude cares about whether Lewd reads, and Lewd cares about whether Prude reads. If you only let Pareto count preferences people have over their own lives, the paradox dissolves. (5) Mira said that's just rigging the welfare standard to save the theorem, and a liberal can't start deciding which preferences are respectable enough to count. (6) Dev said that's exactly backwards: the lesson is that we shouldn't be aggregating preferences over the private sphere in the first place, so a liberal can accept the theorem and walk away from preference aggregation altogether.
