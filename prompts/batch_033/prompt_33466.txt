I'm a novelist. I've been working on an essay that tries to connect the philosophical literature on money pumps and diachronic rationality to the craft of characterization in fiction, and I'd like critical comments on the argument — especially on whether I'm handling the philosophy correctly and whether the central analogy holds up.

The core idea is this. When we say a fictional character is badly written because their desires shift without motivation across a narrative, we tend to treat this as an aesthetic failure. But I suspect we are responding to the same structural feature that the money pump argument formalizes. An agent with cyclical or unstable preferences over time can be offered a series of trades, each advantageous at the moment of choice, that together leave them worse off. The pump works because the agent endorses each trade by their own lights, yet the sequence is destructive. A fictional character who wants something different in every chapter, without any through-line, produces a similar effect on the reader: the reader tracks the character's preferences across time and finds them incoherent. The reader's withdrawal of engagement, I want to suggest, is the aesthetic analogue of the money pump's extraction of resources — a penalty for diachronic irrationality.

But I also want to argue in the other direction. Fiction sometimes deliberately violates consistency norms, and the result can be revelatory rather than sloppy. A character who acts against their established grain in a way that deepens rather than undermines them seems to escape the pump. If this is right, then the money pump argument may be too demanding as a normative constraint on real agents: it rules out forms of diachronic flexibility that we recognize as valuable. The novelist's practical judgment about when inconsistency works and when it doesn't might be a useful data point for philosophers interested in the scope of diachronic rationality requirements.

A more specific claim I'm considering: the money pump scenario assumes an adversary who offers trades. In fiction, the adversary is the reader, who tracks the character's preferences across time and punishes inconsistency with disengagement. But the reader, unlike the money pumper, doesn't need to extract material resources — the mere perception of incoherence is enough to break the aesthetic contract. This might mean the aesthetic norm is actually stricter than the rational norm, because the reader is a more patient tracker of preference than any real-world exploiter. I'm not confident this point holds, and I'd especially welcome scrutiny here.

I'd be grateful for comments on: (1) whether I'm accurately representing what the money pump argument shows and what it's meant to establish, (2) whether the analogy between the reader's response to inconsistent characters and the money pump's exploitation is defensible or merely suggestive, (3) where the essay's most promising move is and whether it's worth developing, and (4) what I'm missing — objections I should anticipate, literature I should be engaging with, or ways the argument overreaches.
