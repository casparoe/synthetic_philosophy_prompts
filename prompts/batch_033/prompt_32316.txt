I've been wrestling with an argument that I first sketched for a paper and now find unsettling because I can't locate the mistake.

(1) The no-free-lunch theorem for supervised learning shows that for any two learning algorithms, their expected off-training-set error is identical when averaged uniformly over all possible target functions.
(2) If (1) holds, then no inductive generalization strategy is better than any other unless we assume a non-uniform prior over the space of possible target functions—that is, unless we already privilege some regularities over others before seeing any data.
(3) Any such prior must itself be justified, either by further induction or a priori.
(4) If justified by further induction, we face a regress: the further induction itself requires a prior, and so on.
(5) If justified a priori, we face an arbitrariness problem: why this particular prior rather than another, given that a uniform prior over target functions treats all strategies as equal?
(6) Therefore induction cannot be given a non-circular, non-arbitrary rational foundation.
(7) But if induction has no rational foundation, then all empirical science, everyday learning, and the practical success of machine learning systems are rationally unmotivated—which is absurd.
(8) So at least one of the premises must be false.

I am most reluctant to give up (2), since it seems to follow directly from the mathematics, and (7), since the success of induction does not feel like an illusion. Tell me which premise you would reject, explain why it looked plausible, and lay out the costs of the alternative—including what happens to the epistemic status of machine learning if you take the route you recommend.
