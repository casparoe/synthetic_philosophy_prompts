In my graduate seminar on the ethics of immigration, the same argumentative move keeps reappearing whenever we discuss the right to exclude. Its proponents press an analogy between a political community and a club, and sometimes between a political community and a private household. The analogy is made to do real work: just as a club may refuse applicants and a household may decline to take in strangers, so a state may refuse would-be immigrants; to deny the state this power, the argument continues, is to deny the community's self-determination, no less absurd than compelling a family to house people it never chose to admit. Several students find this decisive; others suspect the conclusion is being smuggled in by the comparison itself. I want an audit of the analogy, not a verdict on open borders.

Please provide: (1) an explicit correspondence table, mapping the elements of the source — members, admission rules, shared purposes, the application, the excluded non-member — onto the political case as precisely as the analogy allows; (2) the points where the mapping genuinely holds and does argumentative work; (3) the points where it breaks down, separating the disanalogies that matter for the intended use from those that are merely cosmetic; and (4) an assessment of how much of the conclusion — that a right to exclude is a core incident of collective self-determination — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, rather than being carried by the analogy. In particular, attend to features the source quietly imports: voluntary entry and exit, joint ownership of the premises, small-scale face-to-face relations, shared purposes that precede and justify the membership decision, and the difference between being refused admission to a club or house and being excluded from a territory on which one's life chances substantially depend. If there is a better analogy available to either side of the immigration debate, say what it is, what it maps cleanly, and what it would and would not license.
