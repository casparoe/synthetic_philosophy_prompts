Take the language the Bush administration used during the War on Terror — "enhanced interrogation techniques," "extraordinary rendition," "enemy combatants," "collateral damage" — and analyze it through Orwell's ideas about political language from "Politics and the English Language." I know the basic Orwell line: that sloppy language and euphemism are used to defend the indefensible, make murder respectable, that kind of thing. But I want a more careful mapping than that. Which of his specific categories (dying metaphors, operators or verbal false limbs, pretentious diction, meaningless words) do these phrases actually fall into, and do any of them resist his taxonomy? Then go the other direction: does the War on Terror language confirm his thesis, or does it reveal limits? For instance, a lot of these phrases became so notorious they turned into punchlines — "enhanced interrogation" basically became a euphemism for a euphemism. Did Orwell anticipate that euphemisms could fail in that particular way, by becoming too visible and collapsing under their own weight? And here's the thing that nags at me: Orwell's essay mostly treats bad political language as something that degrades the thinking of the people who use it — they reach for the ready-made phrase and the thought gets worse. But the War on Terror language seems to have been deliberatly chosen by smart people who knew exactly what they were doing. Does that distinction matter for his argument, or does it just make his point in a different register?
