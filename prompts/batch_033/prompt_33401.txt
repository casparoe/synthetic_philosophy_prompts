I am trying to reconstruct the rule my philosophy of mind professor is implicitly applying when she decides whether some external device or resource counts as part of a person's cognitive system, in the sense of the extended mind thesis. Here are the cases she has judged so far, with her verdict on each.

1. Otto's notebook. Otto has mild Alzheimer's and always carries a notebook in which he writes down information he will need. He consults it automatically and trusts its contents just as a cognitively typical person trusts biological memory. Verdict: part of his cognitive system.

2. Inga's biological memory. Inga recalls the location of a museum through ordinary biological memory. Verdict: part of her cognitive system.

3. The home diary. A person keeps a detailed diary at home and rereads it roughly once a month to refresh their memory of past events. They trust the entries, but the diary stays at home. Verdict: not part of their cognitive system.

4. The smartphone calendar. A person enters every appointment and commitment into a smartphone calendar, carries the phone at all times, and automatically trusts what the calendar says without checking. Verdict: part of their cognitive system.

5. The Wikipedia checker. A person frequently looks up factual questions on Wikipedia from a phone they always carry, but reads the entries critically and often rejects or cross-checks what they find. Verdict: not part of their cognitive system.

6. The cochlear implant. A deaf person uses a cochlear implant that processes incoming sound and stimulates the auditory nerve; the resulting perceptual experience is trusted in the ordinary way. Verdict: part of their cognitive system.

7. The voice assistant. A person uses a phone-based voice assistant for factual questions. They always carry the phone and usually accept the answers, but they sometimes ask follow-up questions to verify, because they know the assistant can be wrong. Verdict: not part of their cognitive system.

8. The slide rule. An engineer always carries a slide rule and uses it for all calculations, automatically trusting the results without rechecking them by hand. Verdict: part of their cognitive system.

Next week's case is this. A person wears an always-on earpiece connected to a human research assistant who monitors their conversations and surroundings from a remote office. Whenever the person pauses to recall something, the assistant instantly supplies the relevant fact or detail through the earpiece. The person automatically trusts everything the assistant says, wears the earpiece during all waking hours, and the response is as fast and seamless as biological recall. The person did not enter, vet, or generate the information; it comes entirely from the assistant's own knowledge and files.

Predict her verdict on the earpiece case. Formulate the candidate rules that fit her earlier verdicts, say what each rule predicts for the earpiece case, and give your best guess with the reasoning that supports it. Then describe a further case, not among the ones above, whose verdict would most decisively tell apart any candidate rules that still make the same prediction about the earpiece case, and say what each rule would predict for that further case.
