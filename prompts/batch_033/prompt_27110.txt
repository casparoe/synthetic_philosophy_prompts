I'm a retired physical chemist who started reading philosophy of mind a couple years ago, so I'm still getting my bearings and I'd like a second opinion. I just read a popular book about consciousness — written for a general audience, though the author clearly knows the field — and one chapter gives a summary of epiphenomenalism and something it calls the paradox of phenomenal judgment. As best I can reconstruct it, the chapter says this.

Epiphenomenalism is the view that conscious experience is a kind of illusion, or at best a harmless shadow cast by the brain. Your pain, the taste of coffee, the redness of red — they seem real, but their just byproducts of neural firing, and they do nothing. They never cause a behavior, never influence a decision, never even cause a thought. The brain would run exactly the same if they weren't there. Then the book introduces the paradox of phenomenal judgment: when you say that you are in pain, that sentence is produced entirely by your neurons, and the pain itself plays no causal role in producing it. So your report about your own experience is cut off from the very experience it reports, and your belief that you're in pain is no more justified then a lucky guess. The chapter then says epiphenomenalism refutes itself: if the view were true, you could never know you were conscious, and so you could never have good reason to believe epiphenomenalism. It ends by saying this is why almost nobody takes the view seriously anymore.

I'd like to know how fair this is. Which parts would a careful epiphenomenalist accept, and which are misrepresentations or overstatements? Does the self-refutation conclusion actually follow from the accurate parts, or does it depend on the shaky bits? I'm especially unsure about the claim that the brain would run exactly the same without the experiences — that sounds like a stronger claim than experiences don't do anything, but maybe they amount to the same thing. And is it true that almost no one defends the view today? I'm less interested in who holds what than in whether the chapter's argument is sound.
