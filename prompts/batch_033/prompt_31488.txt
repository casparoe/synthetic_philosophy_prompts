I'm teaching a graduate seminar on paradoxes and want to give students a diagnostic tool that sorts the main resolutions of the two-envelope paradox by their structural commitments rather than by the labels they have heard. Please construct a decision tree that takes someone from accepting the standard setup — two envelopes, one contains twice the money of the other, you pick one at random and upon opening it find $X, and you reason that the other envelope has expected value (0.5)(X/2) + (0.5)(2X) = 1.25X, so you should switch — to a named position on whether the switching argument goes through. Each internal node should be a yes/no question about a specific step in the reasoning, phrased so that someone can answer it without already knowing which resolution the answer points toward. Leaves should identify the position by its core theoretical move (e.g., denying that the expected-value calculation is well-defined, rejecting an implicit uniform prior over possible dollar amounts, accepting switching but blocking the paradox by a symmetry argument, and so on), with one sentence stating its core commitment. Flag any pairs of leaves your tree cannot fully distinguish from each other, and note any empty leaves — combinations of answers that are logically possible but that no one, to your knowledge, defends — with a brief remark on why they remain unoccupied. The tree should have at least six leaves.
