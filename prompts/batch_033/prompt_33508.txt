Here's the pattern. You're in a situation with a group of other people, all facing the same choice with the same information. The payoff to each person depends on what everyone does. The standard approach says: figure out what the others will do, then pick your best response to that. But here's the thing: everyone in the group is reasoning identically. Same knowledge, same goals, same style of thinking. So whatever line of reasoning you follow, you can expect everyone else to follow the same line. And you know this, they know this, they know you know it, and so on.

Depending on the payoffs, this creates a strange loop.

Case one: the best outcome is for exactly one person to take some action. If you take it, and everyone reasons the same way, then everyone takes it, and you all get the worst outcome. If you don't take it, and everyone reasons the same way, nobody takes it, and you all get a mediocre outcome. Symmetric reasoning seems to rule out the best outcome entirely — unless you can somehow break the symmetry without communicating.

Case two: if enough people cooperate, everyone does well, but any individual does even better by defecting while others cooperate. The textbook analysis says: whatever others do, you're better off defecting, so defect. But if everyone is reasoning identically, then "everyone defects" is the only symmetric outcome of that reasoning — and everyone can see that this is worse for everyone than "everyone cooperates." And everyone can see that everyone can see it. So maybe the reasoning should go: "I'll cooperate, because I know that everyone else is going through this same reasoning and will also conclude to cooperate." But that feels like a different kind of reasoning from what the textbooks teach.

My question is: is there an established term for this specific kind of situation — where the symmetry of the players' reasoning itself becomes a premise in the reasoning, and the standard "best-respond to what others will do" framework runs into trouble because your action and theirs are outputs of the same process? And if there isn't one settled term, what are the competing terms, and how do they carve up the territory differently?
