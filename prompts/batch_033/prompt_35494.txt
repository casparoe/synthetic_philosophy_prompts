I'm a first-year associate at a midsize law firm, and I've been put on a case that's been bothering me — like, keeping-me-up-at-night bothering me. Here's the situation: our client is a small manufacturing company that's been operating for thirty years, family-run, the kind of place where everyone knows everyone. They've been disposing of a certain chemical byproduct in a way that was perfectly legal under the regulations as they stood when the company started. Five years ago, the state environmental agency issued new regulations that made the disposal method illegal. Our client genuinely didn't know about the change — the regulations were published in the state register, but they were buried in a thousand-page omnibus document and the agency didn't do any outreach to small businesses. Our client continued the old disposal method for five years after the regulation changed.

Now the state is suing for massive fines — potentially enough to shut the company down. Our defense is basically that the regulation, while technically valid under state administrative law, is so obscure and so poorly promulgated that it shouldn't count as law for the purposes of punishing someone who had no realistic way of knowing about it. The senior partner on the case keeps saying things like "there's law on the books, and then there's law that a reasonable person can actually follow." He's asked me to research the Hart–Fuller debate to see if Fuller's idea of the "inner morality of law" can help us argue that a regulation that's not properly promulgated isn't really law.

But I took jurisprudence in law school, and I remember that Hart's positivism says that a law can be valid even if it's procedurally flawed or morally objectionable — validity is a matter of the rule of recognition, not of moral merit. I'm worried our argument is going to get laughed out of court, and I'll look like the idiot associate who tried to argue legal philosophy to a judge. On the other hand, there's something intuitively right about what my boss is saying: if a regulation is basically impossible to know about, how can it be law in any meaningful sense?

I'm supposed to write a memo on this. Can you help me think through whether Fuller's framework actually helps us here, or whether we're better off sticking to a more straightforward procedural due process argument? And more broadly, does this case expose a genuine weakness in legal positivism, or does positivism have the resources to handle it? I'm not asking for legal advice, obviously — I want to understand the philosophical structure of what we're arguing.
