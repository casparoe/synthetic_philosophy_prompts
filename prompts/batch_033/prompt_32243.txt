I'm a journalist working on a piece about Bernard Williams's critique of impartial morality, and I keep coming back to his "one thought too many" argument — you know, the famous example where a man saves his wife rather than a stranger from drowning, and Williams says that if the guy then justifies it by appealing to some impartial principle (like "well, it was permissible because in situations like this, saving your spouse generally produces the most utility"), he's had one thought too many. The standard utilitarian reply is that this conflates the criterion of rightness with a decision procedure: utilitarianism is a theory about what makes actions right, not a manual for how to deliberate in the heat of the moment. I've never found this reply partiuclarly satisfying, and I want to nail down why. Please argue that Williams's objection survives the criterion/decision-procedure distinction, and that the "one thought too many" problem cuts deeper than that distinction can capture. Make the strongest case you can, and engage seriously with why someone might think the distinction does defuse the objection.
