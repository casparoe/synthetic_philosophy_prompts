I'm a philosophy professor who teaches ethics, and I've noticed that the phrase "the separateness of persons" does a lot of heavy lifting in seminar rooms when utilitarianism comes up. Someone invokes it, and the conversation moves on like the objection has landed. The problem is, I'm not sure my colleagues (or, honestly, I) can say exactly what the objection is. When I've asked, I've gotten answers ranging from "it's the point that there's no super-person whose pleasure sums across individuals" to "it's the claim that tradeoffs between persons need a different justification than tradeoffs within a life" to "it's a point about the intrinsic badness of inequality." Those seem like very different claims. I'd like you to disambiguate "the separateness of persons" into the distinct precise theses it might be advancing — call them Thesis A, Thesis B, etc. — and for each, tell me (i) whether it's plausible or at least defensible, (ii) whether it's actually an objection to utilitarianism specifically or just a general moral truth that a utilitarian can happily grant, and (iii) which version a utilitarian should be most worried about. I want to hand this out to my grad students before we read Parfit on the topic, so be thorough — the ones who've read Nozick will have follow-ups.
