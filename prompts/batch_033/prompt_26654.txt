I would like you to write a sonnet about the Boltzmann brain problem. The octave should present the physical argument: in a universe that persists for an enormous stretch of time — perhaps eternally — with matter in thermal equilibrium, random fluctuations will occasionally assemble configurations that look like brains having experiences. A disembodied brain that fluctuates briefly into existence, complete with false memories of a past, is staggeringly more probable than a brain embedded in a long evolutionary and cosmological history of the kind we take ourselves to inhabit. The volta should mark the turn from this thermodynamic claim to its epistemological consequence: that if the argument is sound, the vast majority of observer-moments in the universe belong to such freak fluctuations, and I therefore have no reason to trust that my own present experience is not one of them. The closing couplet should state, with precision, what kind of skepticism this commits us to — and, just as importantly, what kind it does not. The poem should be accurate enough that a philosopher of cosmology would recognize the structure of the argument rather than merely catch a namedrop, and good enough that someone unfamiliar with the problem would still find it a compelling piece of verse.
