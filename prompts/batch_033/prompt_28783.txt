You are a political philosopher who works at the intersection of social choice theory, liberal political thought, and AI alignment. I want you to think carefully and systematically — begin by mapping the positions and their core commitments before you diagnose anything — and then give me a rigorous diagnosis of why a particular debate has stalled.

The debate concerns whose values an aligned AI should serve. Nearly everyone in AI alignment agrees that advanced AI systems should be "aligned with human values," but as soon as you ask whose values and how disagreements among people should be handled, the consensus fractures. Roughly four positions have emerged, and none has gained decisive ground over the others:

(1) Aggregate preference satisfaction: the AI should act to satisfy the preferences of all affected humans, weighted somehow, in a broadly utilitarian framework. This runs into Arrow-style impossibility results, the problem of interpersonal utility comparisons, and the charge that it licenses tyrannies of the majority.

(2) Liberal neutrality / public reason: the AI should be neutral between different conceptions of the good and should enforce only principles that all reasonable people could accept. This runs into the problem that "reasonable" does a great deal of work, that neutrality may be impossible in practice because every design choice embeds values, and that it seems to leave the AI paralyzed whenever reasonable people disagree — which is most of the time.

(3) Individual value alignment / moral pluralism: the AI should be aligned to each user's individual values rather than to any collective aggregation. This runs into obvious problems with coordination, externalities, and the fact that many AI systems act on populations, not on isolated individuals.

(4) The question is ill-posed: "values" are not the kind of thing that can be extracted, aggregated, or optimized over in the way the alignment framing assumes. This camp tends to argue that the whole project of "value alignment" smuggles in a mistaken metaethics — roughly, that it treats values as if they were preferences with a well-defined structure amenable to optimization, when in fact they are not.

Each of these positions has been articulated, criticized, and defended for years. The same moves recur: someone points out an impossibility result, someone else says the impossibility result only bites under unrealistic idealizations, someone else says the idealizations are precisely what make the problem tractable, and around we go. My working hypothesis is that this debate has stalled because it is not really one debate but two: a technical debate about what aggregation procedures are mathematically possible, and a normative debate about what political morality demands — and the two sides keep talking past each other because each treats the other's question as subsidiary to its own. The technical camp thinks the normative camp is hand-waving about constraints that have no formal specification; the normative camp thinks the technical camp is solving toy problems that miss what is normatively at stake.

I want you to diagnose this impasse. Is my two-debates hypothesis correct, or is there a better way to characterize what kind of disagreement this is? Is it substantive — the camps disagree about facts or about what would count as evidence? Is it verbal — they mean different things by "values" or "alignment" or "serve"? Is it a clash of bedrock normative intuitions that no amount of formal work will resolve? Or is the question itself ill-posed in a way that none of the four positions fully acknowledges? Be specific about what each camp would have to accept for progress to happen, and be honest if you think the impasse is permanent. The deliverable I want is a structural diagnosis of the disagreement, not a verdict on which position is right.
