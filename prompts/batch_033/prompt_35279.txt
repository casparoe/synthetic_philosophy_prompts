I'm a software engineer and I spend all day thinking about tradeoffs and optimization, so when a friend told me about the old "torture vs. dust specks" thought experiment it stuck in my head: the idea that you have to choose between one person being tortured and an astronomically huge number of people each getting a single dust speck in their eye, and people's intuitions split on whether the specks ever add up. Then I read Ursula Le Guin's "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" and it felt like the same problem but embodied. For context: Omelas is a city where everyone's happiness depends on one child kept in a locked room, suffering; everyone knows, most people make peace with it, a few walk away. I'd like a reading of the story through the lens of whether and how tiny or distributed harms can be traded against one concentrated harm. Where does the story actually map onto the dust specks setup, and where does it break the mapping — e.g., the city's joy isn't a pile of dust specks, it's real flourishing, and the child's suffering is continuous rather than a one-off, so do those differences matter to the argument or just to the emotional effect? Does Le Guin commit to a position on whether the aggregate can justify the child, or does the story structure the question so that any answer feels wrong? What would a hardcore aggregator say the citizens of Omelas should do, and what would someone who refuses the tradeoff say about the walkers, who reject the deal but don't free the child? And finally: is the story a fair version of the thought experiment, or does it cheat by making you look at the child's face — and if it cheats, is that the actual point, something the abstract tradeoff can't capture? I'm trying to figure out wether the story is doing real philosophical work or just emotionally rigging the game.
