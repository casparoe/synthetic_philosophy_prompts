I'm taking this seminar on the history of analytic philosophy and we're up to the 1950s — Carnap, Quine, Grice & Strawson, that whole debate. The prof assigned us to write a dialogue between two characters arguing about whether the analytic-synthetic distinction holds up after Quine's "Two Dogmas." Not a summary, but an actual back-and-forth where both characters are philosophically competent and neither is obviously winning.

I've been trying to think about what a good version of the pro-distinction character would say. Like, they can't just keep pointing at "all bachelors are unmarried" — Quine's whole point is that the obvious examples don't settle the matter because the circularity problem is deeper. But there are serious responses: Grice and Strawson's point about the "family circle" of intensional notions, or the idea that Quine's own holism presupposes something like meaning identity to even state what counts as "adjusting" the web. And then the Quinean character would need to push back on that intelligently.

I guess what I'm stuck on is making the dialogue feel alive rather than like two position papers alternating. The prof said good dialogues have characters who change their mind a little, or at least refine their view in response to the other person. Also she said we should avoid the cliché where one character is the "naive" defender of analyticity — both should be sophisticated. Could you write this for me? Maybe set it in a library or a coffeeshop, 15-20 turns. I'd espescially love it if they end up pinpointing the exact disagreement — like, is it about the nature of synonymy? About whether circularity is always vicious? About what counts as an explanation? And maybe one of them concedes a point they hadn't thought of before.
