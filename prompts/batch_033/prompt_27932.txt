I've long been persuaded by the position Wigner staked out in his 1960 essay: that the effectiveness of mathematics in the natural sciences is unreasonable. The core of the view, as I understand it, is that purely formal mathematical structures — developed for their own sake, often with no eye toward empirical application — turn out, again and again, to provide the exact language in which physical laws are written. Complex numbers for quantum mechanics, non-Euclidean geometry for general relativity, group theory for particle physics: in each case the mathematics was there first, waiting. This is not just the claim that mathematics is useful; it is that the fit between abstract formalism and physical reality is so precise and so pre-emptive that it seems to demand an explanation that naturalism — the view that our cognitive apparatus evolved to track middlesized objects at middlesized speeds — cannot readily supply.

I want to know what would count against this view. I am not asking for the standard objections I have already encountered (that we only remember the successes, that mathematics was shaped by physical problems more than Wigner acknowledged, that the anthropic principle dissolves the surprise). I want a systematic map of the view's exposure. Specifically:

1. What evidence, arguments, or developments — whether already in the literature or merely conceivable — would show that the effectiveness of mathematics is, after all, reasonable? What would a world look like in which mathematics was effective but not unreasonably so, and how would we tell whether we live in that world?

2. Sort the defeaters from those that would decisively undermine the view to those that would merely qualify or soften it. Which of these would a committed defender of the Wigner position concede as counting against them, and which would they dismiss as missing the point?

3. Has the view been qualified over time into unfalsifiability? If the claim has been refined from "this is surprising and calls for explanation" to something like "this is surprising given a certain set of naturalistic assumptions, though one could always revise those assumptions," is that a problem for the view — or is it the appropriate shape for a claim of this kind?

4. Are there rival positions that make different predictions — for example, about whether we should expect future physics to continue to be describable by antecedently developed mathematics, or about whether we should expect pure mathematics developed for its own sake to keep finding physical application — and what would it take to bring those predictions into contact with evidence?

I would personally find most persuasive an argument that identifies something the view is committed to that turns out to be false, rather than one that merely offers an alternative explanation for the same data. But I want the full map, not just the arguments I would find congenial.
