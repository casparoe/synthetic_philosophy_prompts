I'm taking this course on the cognitive science of religion and I've been going back and forth with my roommate about whether religion is basically a by-product of how our brains work or whether it's something that evolved because it helped groups survive. The frustrating thing is that whenever we try to find a real test case, the two views seems to predict the same thing — like, both can explain why a community would develop moralizing gods, or why rituals get more intense during crises. So I'm trying to construct a case where they actually come apart.

Here are the two views as I understand them (and I'm happy to be corrected if I'm getting them wrong):

The by-product view (Boyer, Atran, Barrett, etc.): Religious ideas aren't adaptations. They're parasites on cognitive systems that evolved for other reasons. We have hyperactive agency detection — we see faces in the trees and assume someone's there. We have promiscuous teleology — we think everything exists for a reason. We have theory of mind and we apply it to invisible agents. Religion is what happens when you combine these biases. The content of religious beliefs is constrained mainly by what's cognitively catchy — minimally counterintuitive concepts, emotionally salient narratives, and so on.

The cultural-group selection view (Wilson, Sosis, Norenzayan, etc.): Religious beliefs and practices are cultural adaptations that help groups solve cooperation problems. Groups with costly rituals, moralizing high gods, and sacred values outcompete groups without them, because those traits reduce free-riding and increase within-group trust. So the content of religion should be responsive to the specific cooperation challenges a group faces.
