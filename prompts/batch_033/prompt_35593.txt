I help run a rationality meetup in a mid-sized city — we get about 20 people on a good night, mix of software engineers, grad students, the usual crowd, most have read the Sequences but not all. We've got a two-hour slot. I want to run a session on Dutch book arguments that actually makes people *feel* why incoherent credences are a problem, rather than just nodding along at the abstract argument. I've seen too many meetups where someone says "yeah but maybe I'm fine being Dutch-booked if I'm not actually encountering a perfect bookie" and the conversation stalls.

Design a betting game that I can run. Here's the goal: participants should state their own fair odds for a set of propositions, and then the facilitator (acting as bookie) constructs a Dutch book against anyone whose odds are incoherent. The game should surface the intuition that being Dutch-bookable isn't just about losing money to a clever adversary — it's about having an internal inconsistency that the world can exploit regardless of how things turn out.

Constraints: no real money. Use a points system or something with social stakes. We can do small groups (tables of 4–5) with one person rotating as bookie, or whole-group with the facilitator as bookie — whichever you think works better.

For the debrief: map what happened during the game to the formal argument. Cover why the probability axioms (non-negativity, normalization, finite additivity) fall out of "don't be exploitable." Be honest about what the Dutch book argument doesn't give you — e.g., countable additivity needs a separate argument, and it doesn't directly address the problem of priors. Also, connect it to things rationalists actually care about: how does this relate to making bets on prediction markets, or to the idea that your beliefs should "pay rent"?

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong. I'm especially worried about the person who insists on setting P(A) = P(~A) = 0.3 because they're "uncertain about their uncertainty" — how do I handle that without turning the whole session into a one-on-one debate? And what about the person who just refuses to give any number at all?
