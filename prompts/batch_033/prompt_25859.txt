I was reading this bioethics piece online about advance directives and dementia, and it gave a summary of the pro-advance-directive position that goes something like this:

"The argument for advance directives is pretty straightforward. You have a right to determine what happens to your body, and that right doesn't evaporate just because you lose capacity — it's like a contract that binds your future self, the same way Ulysses had himself tied to the mast. If you signed a directive refusing life-sustaining treatment if you ever develop severe dementia, then honoring that directive respects your autonomy. The person with dementia who says they want to live is not really 'you' in the relevant sense — they lack the psychological continuity and critical interests that made you the person you were. What matters is what the real you decided when you were competent. Most bioethicists agree on this, and the main pushback comes from people who havent thought through the implications of letting the demented self override the earlier self's settled wishes."

Something about this feels too tidy to me — I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on personal identity, and I know there's a huge literature pushing back on exactly this line (Dresser, Jaworska, etc.), but I'm not a bioethics person and I don't trust my sense of where the misrepresentations are. Can you go through this summary and tell me which parts a careful defender of advance directives would accept, which are oversimplifications or conflations, and whether the conclusion — that honoring the directive is clearly the right call — survives the corrections?
