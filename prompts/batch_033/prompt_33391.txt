I spent the better part of yesterday evening arguing with a friend about whether any of our everyday choices actually matter from a consequentialist point of view. He had just read something about 'consequentialist cluelessness' and was practically glowing with the pleasure of having found a new objection. His summary of the position, as he put it to me near the end of the night, went roughly like this:

'Consequentialism tells you to evaluate actions by their consequences, but here's the problem: you can never know what the consequences will be. If you give money to a charity, maybe that charity turns out to be ineffective — or worse, maybe the child you help grows up to be the next Stalin. The ripple effects of any action go on forever and branch out in ways you cannot possibly trace. So consequentialism demands that you do the impossible, which means it fails as a guide to action and collapses into a kind of practical nihilism where no choice can be shown to be better than any other. That's why most consequentialists have quietly abandoned the view and become deontologists.'

Something about this felt off to me, but I struggled to articulate exactly what during the argument. He was so confident, and I could tell there was a genuine problem in the vicinity. Now I want to understand: which parts of his summary would a philosopher working on this issue recognise as fair, and which parts are misrepresentations or overstatements? In particular, does the final claim — about consequentialists abandoning the view — actually follow from anything true in the rest of what he said? I'd appreciate an answer in British English.
