I've been working through Samuel Scheffler's afterlife conjecture and I'd like to stress-test it. The position, roughly, is that the continued existence of humanity after our own deaths is a non-instrumental condition of many of our present activities and projects having the value and significance we take them to have. The central thought experiment is the doomsday scenario: suppose you learned that thirty days after your death, an asteroid would wipe out the entire human species. No additional people would die who wouldn't have died anyway — everyone currently alive lives out their natural lifespan. Yet, Scheffler argues, this knowledge would drain the point from a great deal of what we do: cancer research, building a business, writing a novel, preserving a tradition, even certain forms of love and friendship. The conclusion is that we are far less individualistic about value than we suppose, and that purely person-affecting accounts of our concerns cannot capture what we actually care about.

I find the core intuition gripping, but I want to know what would genuinely count against this view — not objections from someone who simply lacks the intuition, but the considerations that a defender of the afterlife conjecture should themselves regard as threatening. What are the alternative explanations of the doomsday-scenario reaction that don't require positing a deep dependence on humanity's survival? Does the view make predictions about other cases — say, about how we would react to learning that humanity will survive but in a radically diminished or unrecognizable form — and could those predictions fail? Are there empirical findings from psychology or anthropology that would undermine it, for instance about how people in different cultures or historical periods relate to collective survival? Could the whole intuition be an artifact of how the thought experiment is framed, and if so, what experimental or philosophical work would expose that? I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I'd also like an honest assessment of whether the view has been or could be qualified into unfalsifiability — and if so, whether that is a problem for a thesis of this kind.
