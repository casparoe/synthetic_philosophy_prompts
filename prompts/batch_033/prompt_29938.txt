Lately I've been reading philosophy — decision theory, specifically — and I keep running into these two thought experiments that people use to pull apart different decision theories. They're clearly about causation and counterfactuals, but every time I try to think about them in the potential-outcomes vocabulary I know, something feels off. Here are the two cases, as I understand them:

1. Counterfactual mugging. A perfect predictor (Omega) flips a fair coin. If tails, it asks you for $100. If heads, it gives you $10,000 — but only if it predicted that you would have paid in the tails branch. The coin comes up tails; Omega asks you for the hundred. Causal decision theory says don't pay (the coin is already tails, so paying can't cause the heads reward). Evidential decision theory also says don't pay (you already know it's tails, so paying doesn't give you evidence of heads). But updateless decision theory says you should pay, because your decision procedure determines what happens in the counterfactual heads branch even though that branch isn't actual.

2. XOR blackmail. A perfect predictor sends you a message that says, in effect: I sent this message if and only if exactly one of these is true — your house has termites, or you will pay me $1,000. Termite damage would cost you a million. Causal decision theory says don't pay: the termites are either there or not, and paying can't change that. Evidential decision theory says pay, because paying is evidence that the message was sent due to your payment rather than due to termites, so you infer your house is fine — worth the thousand bucks. (And then people argue about whether this makes EDT vulnerable to being money-pumped.)
