I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so forgive me if I get the terms wrong. I'm working on a novel where the climax turns on an accident: the protagonist glances down at her phone for maybe two seconds, the same way she has a hundred times before, and this time a kid runs into the street and she hits him. I want the moral texture of the book to be more precise than "she feels bad but it wasn't really her fault," so I've been reading around, and I kept hitting the idea of moral luck. A friend who did a bit of philosophy in undergrad explained it to me like this: Nagel and Williams showed that moral judgment has a luck problem, because the control principle says you can only be praised or blamed for what is under your control, but nearly everything about who you are and what you do is shaped by luck — genes, upbringing, which situations you happen to face. So strictly speaking nobody ever deserves praise or blame. The stock example is two drunk drivers, equally reckless, where one hits a kid and the other makes it home; we blame the one who hit the kid far more, which shows ordinary moral judgment is irrational. Nagel apparently lists four kinds of luck — resultant, circumstantial, constitutive, and causal. And the upshot, according to my friend, is that since all moral assessment rests on luck, we should drop blame altogether, or at least stop treating outcomes as morally significant.

This feels too neat to me, and I suspect my friend is filing the sharp edges off, but I don't know enough to say where. Could you go through that summary and tell me which parts are fair to Nagel and Williams, where a careful defender of them would wince, and whether the "drop blame" conclusion actually follows from the accurate bits? I'd also like to understand the difference between resultant luck and the other three, because thats the distinction my novel actually lives or dies on. And if Nagel and Williams are up to different things, I'd want to know that too, since my friend lumped them together.
