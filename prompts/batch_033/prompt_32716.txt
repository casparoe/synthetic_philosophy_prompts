I'm an actuary who prices extreme-tail insurance — catastrophe bonds, terrorism reinsurance, pandemic stop-loss — and I keep hearing philosophers and decision theorists mention something called Pascal's mugging. As I understand it, the scenario goes like this: a mugger stops you on the street and says that unless you hand over your wallet, he will inflict an astronomically large harm on an astronomically large number of people — or, in some versions, he'll withhold an astronomically large reward. When you ask why you should believe him, he concedes that the probability he can actually do this is vanishingly small, but points out that the product of that tiny probability and the enormous payoff still dwarfs the certain loss of your wallet. So expected-utility maximization seems to demand that you pay up. The puzzle is meant to expose a pathology: if you allow utility to be unbounded, or if you allow agents to assign non-zero probabilities to arbitrarily wild claims, then expected-utility reasoning can be hijacked by anyone who can describe a sufficiently extreme outcome.

I want you to restate this problem — its structure, the standard responses, and what is really at stake — in the vocabulary I use every day. Translate it into the language of premium principles, risk loading, process risk versus parameter risk, ruin theory, reinsurance layers, tail dependence, regulatory capital, and the practical impossibility of pricing a risk when you cannot estimate a loss distribution from data. Assume I am fluent in this vocabulary but have no formal training in philosophy or decision theory.

Then give me the residue report: which parts of Pascal's mugging map cleanly onto actuarial concepts, which parts you had to distort or stretch to make them fit, and which parts have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. I especially want to know whether actuarial practice already has principled ways of refusing to price risks whose probabilities cannot be meaningfully quantified — loading factors that go to infinity, premium principles that are undefined below some confidence threshold, regulatory rules that simply exclude unmodelable perils — and if so, whether that means the mugging is not a problem for us, or whether our refusal amounts to an implicit rejection of expected-utility theory that we have not owned up to.
