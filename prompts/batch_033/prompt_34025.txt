ok so im in a reading group going through Leibniz's Theodicy (the Huggard translation) and we got stuck on a passage in Part I, section 8, where he's arguing that God had to choose the best of all possible worlds. The passage is:

Now this supreme wisdom, united to a goodness that is no less infinite, cannot but have chosen the best. For as a lesser evil is a kind of good, even so a lesser good is a kind of evil if it stands in the way of a greater good; and there would be something to correct in the actions of God if it were possible to do better. And as in mathematics, when there is no maximum nor minimum, in short nothing distinguished, everything is done equally, or when that is not possible nothing at all is done: so it may be said likewise in respect of perfect wisdom, which is no less orderly than mathematics, that if there were not the best (optimum) among all possible worlds, God would not have produced any.

I get the first sentence, but the group got into a long fight about what the "as in mathematics" part is actually doing. Is he just saying that God's creation is like a mathematician choosing the unique optimum — so if there were a tie between two equally good worlds, God would have made neither? Or is the mathematics analogy doing something stronger, like saying that without a unique best there is no sufficient reason to pick one world over another? And if the latter, does that mean the principle of sufficient reason is doing the real work here, and the claim about God's goodness is just along for the ride? Also, would Leibniz have accepted that if there were two equally best worlds, God could have actualized either, or is he committed to saying there would have been no creation at all? We kept going in circles, so could you unpack the passage sentence by sentence and then settle what the mathematics analogy is supposed to get us to see?
