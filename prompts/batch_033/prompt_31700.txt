In a faculty working group on open-access publishing, a colleague of mine keeps invoking the slogan "information wants to be free" to settle disputes — most recently to argue that journal paywalls are not merely imprudent but inherently unjust, because they frustrate something built into the nature of information itself. I find the phrase maddeningly slippery, and I suspect it collapses several distinct claims that need to be pulled apart before any of them can do the normative work she is asking them to do. What are the different precise theses that the slogan "information wants to be free" could be expressing — about the nature of information, about human interests, about moral rights, about economic or technological forces, about something else — and which of those readings are trivial, which are substantive and defensible, which are false, and which, if any, would actually support the conclusion that paywalling academic research is inherently unjust? Please present the readings as a menu with a verdict for each, and indicate which reading or readings my colleague would need to be relying on for her argument to go through. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. Please write the answer in American English.
