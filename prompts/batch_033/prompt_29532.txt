so after way too many years reading rationalist blogs I've ended up an expressivist about normative language: "lying is wrong" doesn't report a moral fact, it expresses a con-attitude — or a plan, or a state of being-for-not-lying, depending which flavor you like. I know the big problem is Frege–Geach: how do the embedded occurrences in "if lying is wrong, then getting your brother to lie is wrong" mean anything, when the speaker plainly isn't expressing disapproval of lying in saying the conditional? Same issue for negation, questions, and mixed normative-descriptive sentences. I've read the standard moves (quasi-realism, higher-order attitudes, Gibbard-style plans, being-for semantics, the negation-problem stuff) and I currently think they handle it well enough that the problem is dissolved, not just patched over.

I want you to be the professional metaethicist who tries to talk me out of this. Dont just rehearse Frege–Geach; I already know the shape of it. I want the map of what would actually count against my position, with Frege–Geach as the central stress test. What would the world — or the semantics — have to look like for expressivism to be false, or for the standard solution to be fake? Which formal results, linguistic data, or internal-tension arguments should actually move me? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say which ones a committed expressivist would themselves concede count against the view rather than dismiss. Then give me an honest verdict on whether the view has been qualified (say, into "expressivism is just a metasemantic research program" or "the descriptive semantics falls out for free") to the point where nothing could refute it — and whether that's a real problem for a view like this or just what a mature philosophical position looks like. For what it's worth, the thing most likely to move me is a worked case where the attitude-based semantics gets an embedded normative sentence wrong, or can't distinguish two sentences that obviously differ in meaning; I'm not much moved by the generic worry that moral talk feels fact-like. Start by brainstorming the strongest counterarguments before you commit to an answer, and think carefully about which cut deepest.
