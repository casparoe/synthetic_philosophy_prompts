You are a senior clinical psychologist and philosopher of psychiatry serving on your national psychiatric association's working group for the next revision of its diagnostic manual. Please think carefully and reason step by step where helpful.

Our working group is considering whether to include Complex Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (C-PTSD) as a freestanding diagnosis, distinct from standard PTSD. C-PTSD is already recognized in the ICD-11 with a cluster structure that adds disturbances in self-organization — affect dysregulation, negative self-concept, and interpersonal difficulties — to the core PTSD symptoms. Our current manual treats these features only as an optional specifier under PTSD. The clinical evidence is mixed: some studies find distinct latent classes and treatment responses, others find near-total overlap with PTSD comorbid with borderline personality disorder, raising the worry that C-PTSD merely re-describes a familiar patient population under a new name.

But the real sticking point in our deliberations has been a philosophical argument. One of our members, drawing on Ian Hacking's work on looping effects and interactive kinds, argues that creating a freestanding C-PTSD diagnosis would change the very phenomenon it aims to describe. On her view, psychiatric classifications don't just label pre-existing natural kinds; they interact with the people classified. Patients learn the diagnostic criteria, reshape their self-understanding and symptom presentation around the category, clinicians begin to see and elicit the symptoms the criteria prime them to find, advocacy groups form around the diagnosis, and the whole feedback loop can make the construct appear validated when in fact the classification has partly manufactured the evidence for itself. She argues this isn't merely a caution about overdiagnosis or medicalization — it's a claim that formalizing a diagnosis can destabilize the evidence base that supposedly supports it, because the act of classification alters the target.

Others on the working group find this argument either too abstract to guide a concrete nosological decision or question-begging: not classifying also has looping effects, they point out, since it leaves severely distressed patients without a recognized framework to make sense of their suffering and without a diagnostic code that unlocks access to trauma-focused treatment. Some also argue that Hacking's point, even if correct, applies to all psychiatric diagnoses and therefore cannot discriminate among them — it proves too much.

I need to draft a memo for circulation before our next meeting that recommends a concrete decision: include C-PTSD as a freestanding diagnosis, retain it only as a specifier, or some third option (e.g., include it in an appendix for further study with explicit monitoring for looping effects). The memo should engage Hacking's looping-effects argument seriously — not dismiss it as mere sociology of science, and not accept it as an automatic trump card — and should give an honest statement of the strongest consideration on the losing side. The recommendation needs to be something the working group can actually vote on, with reasons that clinicians and researchers with no background in philosophy of psychiatry can assess.
