I've been rereading Gödel, Escher, Bach for a reading group — first time since undergrad, and it's hitting differently now — and there's one bit I keep tripping over no matter how many times I reread the chapter. It's the stuff about tangled hierarchies and what Hofstadter calls the "inviolate level." The central example is Escher's Drawing Hands: two hands, each drawing the other into existance, a loop with no ground floor. Hofstadter says we percieve this from an inviolate level, a level where the meaning is stable and coherent even though the levels underneath are all tangled up in a strange loop. And I think I understand the basic move: at the level of ink on paper there's no paradox, it's just marks; at the level of "hands drawing" there's a tangle; and at some higher level we see the whole thing as a meaningful image and the tangle doesn't bother us.

But here's the thing that's been nagging at me. If the inviolate level is where meaning lives, and the meaning we perceive when we look at Drawing Hands is precisely "two hands drawing each other into existence" — well, that meaning is the tangle. The content of the inviolate-level perception just is the strange loop. So what work is the word "inviolate" doing? It can't be that the inviolate level is free of self-reference, because the self-reference is exactly what we're perceiving as meaningful. It seems like Hofstadter wants the inviolate level to be a kind of escape hatch — we step up a level and suddenly the paradox dissolves — but the paradox hasn't dissolved at all; we've just decided to call it "meaning" and stop worrying about it. My friend in the group says I'm missing something obvious but couldn't quite put his finger on what. Is there a genuine tension here, or am I misunderstanding what "inviolate" is supposed to mean in the first place?
