I've been thinking about consent and deception, and I've landed on a view that I'm not entirely comfortable with but can't see my way around. Here it is: whether deception invalidates consent depends entirely on what the deception was about. If someone lies about something that's internal to the act they're asking consent for — like lying about wearing a condom, or lying about what medical procedure they're about to perform — then consent is void. But if they lie about something external to the act — like lying about being married, or about their job, or about their feelings — then the consent stands, even if the person wouldn't have consented had they known the truth. The distinction is between deceptions that change what the act *is* and deceptions that only change what the act *means* or what its broader context is.

I want to know what would count against this view. What cases does it get wrong? Are there deceptions about context that are so central to someone's reasons for consenting that they should count as invalidating? And on the flip side, are there deceptions about the act itself that shouldn't invalidate consent? I'd also like to know whether this view collapses into one of the standard positions under pressure — like the view that consent is just about what's subjectively material to the consenter, or the view that consent is never invalidated by deception at all. Rank the problems, and tell me honestly whether the view is salvageable or whether I should abandon it. Oh, and please write in British English.
