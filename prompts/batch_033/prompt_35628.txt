I am a novelist at work on a book set among the Victorian intellectual milieu, and George Eliot figures as a character in several scenes. I am trying to get her religious voice right—not merely the vocabulary but the underlying structure of her commitments. She was a translator of Strauss and Feuerbach, a secular humanist who rejected Christian supernaturalism while taking religion with the utmost seriousness as a moral and emotional phenomenon, and someone for whom the religion of humanity was not a rhetorical flourish but a genuine ethical project.

Here is my question: what would she have made of the contemporary phenomenon of people identifying as "spiritual but not religious"? I have in mind the widespread rejection of institutional religion in the West over the past half-century, coupled with the persistence of a language of transcendence, meaning, and connection that resists reduction to a purely secular humanist framework. Would Eliot have recognized this as a natural extension of her own project—extracting the ethical and emotional core of religion from its supernatural husk—or would she have found something evasive or ethically anaemic in it, a retention of religious feeling without the rigor of either doctrinal commitment or the kind of moral seriousness she demanded of her own secular alternative? I am asking for a reasoned extrapolation from her actual stated views as they appear in her essays, letters, and the religious dimensions of her novels, not from the general spirit of Victorian secularism.
