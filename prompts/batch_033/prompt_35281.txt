I'm a philosophy professor working on a paper about the temporal structure of conscious experience, and I've been in an ongoing conversation with a colleague in computer science who designs distributed event-processing systems. The conversation has been genuinely productive, and I'd like to push the translation exercise further than we've managed over coffee.

Here is the debate I want restated. In the color phi phenomenon (Kolers and von Grünau, 1976), subjects are shown a red spot followed in quick succession by a green spot at a different location; they report seeing a single moving spot that changes from red to green roughly midway along the trajectory. The puzzle is that the brain apparently needs information about the green spot before it can construct the conscious experience of the color change — the experience of the change precedes, in experienced time, the stimulus that makes that change intelligible. Daniel Dennett framed two ways the brain might handle this. On the "Stalinesque" account, the brain delays conscious experience itself, waiting until it has all relevant information before constructing a coherent narrative — rather like a show trial where the verdict is written before the evidence is heard. On the "Orwellian" account, the brain produces experience in something closer to real time but revises memory after the fact — rather like the Ministry of Truth rewriting the historical record. Dennett's provocative claim is that these two accounts are not empirically distinguishable, and moreover that there is no fact of the matter about which is correct, because the question presupposes a "Cartesian Theater" where experiences happen at definite moments for a definite audience. The debate has continued in the philosophy of mind and time-consciousness literature ever since.

My colleague's framework involves concepts like event sourcing and log-structured storage, stream processing with windowing and watermarks, buffering and backpressure, exactly-once versus at-least-once semantics, and the distinction between event time and processing time. I would like you to restate the Orwellian/Stalinesque distinction — and Dennett's argument that they collapse into each other — in the vocabulary of that framework, as faithfully and systematically as you can. Then give me a residue report: which parts of the philosophical debate translate cleanly into distributed-systems concepts, which parts translate only with distortion, and which parts have no counterpart at all. I especially want to know whether the Orwellian/Stalinesque distinction maps onto a real architectural choice in event-processing systems, and if so, whether Dennett's claim that the distinction is empirically empty would, in that translated form, be obviously true, obviously false, or still a live dispute. I am less interested in whether the analogy is clever than in whether the translation exercise reveals something about the original debate — perhaps that it rests on assumptions that look parochial once you try to express them in a framework built without them.
