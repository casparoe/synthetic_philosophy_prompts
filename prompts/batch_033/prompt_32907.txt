I'm teaching an upper-level undergrad seminar on rational choice and the foundations of game theory — about 18 students, mostly philosophy and econ majors, one 75-minute session. We've done Nash equilibrium and dominance reasoning, and now I want to spend a session on the idea that standard game theory assumes each player treats the others' strategies as fixed and independent of their own reasoning. I want to introduce superrationality — the idea that when you know all the players are copies of each other in terms of reasoning power and information, you should ask "what strategy would be best if we all independently converge on the same reasoning?" — and I want them to actually feel the force of the problem, not just hear about it.

The source material is Douglas Hofstadter's Platonia dilemma and the Luring Lottery. (In the Platonia dilemma, 20 people are told that if exactly one sends a telegram by noon next day, that person gets a billion dollars; if zero or more than one send, no one gets anything. No communication. The superrational prescription is to send with probability 1/20. In the Luring Lottery, readers of Scientific American in the 1980s were actually invited to send in postcards with a number of entries; the prize was $1M divided by total entries, awarded to a random entry, so you could game it by sending a huge number. The superrational prescription was to roll a die with sides equal to the number of expected responders and send "1" only on a roll of 1. In practice, people sent googolplexes and the whole thing became a joke.)

So here's what I want: design a classroom activity (or pair of linked activities) that makes the students wrestle with the core tension before they get the vocabulary. I want them to discover for themselves that standard individual rationality gives terrible advice when everyone is reasoning symmetrically, and then I want them to try to invent something like superrationality on their own. Ideally the activity would have a payoff structure that doesn't require real money — maybe points toward a grade or some kind of token that matters only within the class. The debrief should map what they did onto the formal difference between strategic reasoning (I treat you as an independent agent) and superrational reasoning (we are instances of the same decision procedure), and should be honest about what the activity can't capture — e.g., the real Luring Lottery involved thousands of strangers, not a seminar room of people who know each other. Also tell me what predictably goes wrong. I've never run anything like this before and I'm a little nervous about it devolving into chaos.
