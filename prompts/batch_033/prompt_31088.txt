I have been reading Grice's work on speaker meaning and the logic of conversation, and I keep noticing a pattern in everyday talk that I do not know how to classify within his framework. I am hoping someone can tell me whether this phenomenon has an established name, and if not, how it should be carved up.

The pattern is this. A speaker says something that, in the conversational context, licenses a hearer to derive a particular implicature by perfectly standard Gricean reasoning. The hearer does derive it, and the derivation is reasonable: it relies on the Cooperative Principle and the ordinary maxims, and most competent speakers in that context would draw the same inference. But the speaker, if challenged, can sincerely and plausibly deny having intended that implicature — not because they are lying, and not because they are opting out of the Cooperative Principle, but because they were genuinely unaware that their utterance would carry that implicature in that context. The implicature is generated by the utterance-in-context, and the hearer's recovery of it is licensed, yet the speaker's mind contains no corresponding communicative intention.

Here is a concrete instance. A colleague at a conference dinner tells a visitor, "Professor Chen's lab is in the old building on the west side of campus." The visitor has been asking around about which labs are doing good work, and she reasonably infers that Chen's lab is worth visiting. The colleague, however, was simply answering the literal question about location and had no thought of recommending the lab; he was just being helpful about directions. The visitor's inference is Gricean — the colleague would not have supplied the location unless it was relevant to the visitor's larger project of finding good labs — but the colleague can honestly say, "I was not recommending anything; I was only telling you where it is."

A second instance. A supervisor writes in a quarterly review, "This employee has been very productive this quarter and has completed all assigned tasks on time." The reader infers that the employee is satisfactory but not exceptional — the supervisor conspicuously avoided stronger praise. But the supervisor, who is new to the role and unsure of the norms, intended only to report facts and had no intention of conveying anything about the employee's relative standing. The damning-with-faint-praise effect is real, but the intention behind it is absent.

I am trying to understand what to call this. It is not a standard conversational implicature in Grice's sense, because the speaker lacks the relevant reflexive intention. It is not simply a case of the hearer making a mistake, because the inference is licensed by the maxims and by the assumption that the speaker is cooperative. It seems to be an implicature that is generated by the utterance-in-context rather than by the speaker's communicative intentions, and the speaker can be held conversationally responsible for it even though they did not mean it. Is there a term for this in the Gricean or post-Gricean literature — something like "objective implicature," "hearer-implicature," or "implicature without speaker meaning"? How exactly does it relate to the distinction between speaker meaning and utterance meaning, and to the notion of commitment that some neo-Griceans have developed? Does this phenomenon create a genuine difficulty for intention-based accounts of communication, or is it simply a case of a speaker being ignorant of the pragmatic norms that apply to their utterance in a given context, and therefore not a counterexample to anything? If no established term exists, I would welcome a proposal for how to define it and distinguish it from nearby phenomena such as accidental implicature, indirect speech acts that the speaker performs unawares, and cases where the hearer simply reads too much into an utterance.
