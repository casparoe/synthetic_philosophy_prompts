You are drafting the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two legal philosophers who have debated self-amending rules for years. One holds the formal-determinacy view: for any self-referential amendment to a finite system of rules, the rules together with correct principles of legal validity determine whether the amendment succeeds; apparent paradoxes either mark the amendment as void or resolve determinately from within the system. The other holds the practical-indeterminacy view: some self-referential amendments create genuine gaps that no prior rule can resolve from within; resolution requires a fresh act of norm-making authority — a decision not itself dictated by the prior rules — so there is no fact of the matter about validity until someone decides.

They work in the domain of Nomic-style games of self-amendment (the kind of game introduced by Peter Suber in 1982, in which changing the rules is a move governed by the rules, and the rules governing rule-change are themselves subject to amendment). They have agreed to treat such games as a tractable model of the problem and to focus on three canonical puzzle cases:

(i) the self-voiding rule: a rule whose entire content is that this very rule is void;
(ii) the self-entrenching rule: a rule that raises the threshold for amending itself, enacted under the lower threshold it seeks to replace;
(iii) the omnibus repeal: a rule that repeals every rule in the system, including itself.

Draft the document they would produce. It should contain:

1. A numbered list of claims both can sign. These should be substantive, not merely terminological — they should capture genuine common ground about self-reference, validity, what formal models can and cannot show, and what happens in actual play. Avoid vacuous formulations.

2. A disagreements table. For each point of disagreement, give the precise formulation each side would accept as representing its own view. Neither side's formulation should be a caricature of what the other believes. Include at least four rows.

3. A section identifying the cruxes: the sub-questions such that, if they were settled, at least one side would change its view. For each crux, describe a concrete case or argument that both sides agree bears on it, and state what each side predicts about that case or argument. The cases may be drawn from the three puzzle cases above or from variants of them.

4. If any part of their dispute turns out to be merely verbal — a difference in how they use terms like valid, determine, or rule rather than a substantive disagreement — identify it and explain why.

The document should read as something both could actually sign. It should be precise enough that a third party could see exactly where the disagreement lies and what would need to happen to resolve it.
