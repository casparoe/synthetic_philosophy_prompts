I'm a novelist. My current protagonist is a philosopher of mind, and in the course of researching her intellectual world I've gotten tangled in a problem I can't resolve.

The conceivability argument against physicalism, as I reconstruct it:

(1) I can conceive of a being physically identical to me, molecule for molecule, that entirely lacks phenomenal consciousness — a philosophical zombie.
(2) Ideal conceivability entails metaphysical possibility.
(3) Therefore, a physical duplicate of me without consciousness is metaphysically possible.
(4) If such a duplicate is possible, phenomenal properties do not metaphysically supervene on physical properties.
(5) Therefore, physicalism is false.

Now here is what worries me. The same pattern of reasoning seems to prove far too much:

(6) I can conceive of a substance physically identical to H₂O — same molecular structure, same behavior — that is not water. (I imagine a world where the clear drinkable stuff is XYZ; "water" rigidly designates whatever actually fills the lakes, so the H₂O isn't water.)
(7) By (2), such a substance is metaphysically possible.
(8) Therefore, water is not identical to H₂O.
(9) The identical reasoning works for every successful reduction: heat is not mean molecular kinetic energy; genes are not DNA; life is not a set of biochemical processes.
(10) (9) is false. So something has gone wrong.

I know the standard physicalist reply: water zombies are conceivable only under a different "mode of presentation" — I can conceive of H₂O not being water only by misdescribing the scenario — whereas phenomenal zombies are conceivable in a thicker sense that survives full ideal reflection. But stating the distinction is not the same as justifying it without begging the question. What independent criterion tells me that my conceiving in (6) is the wrong kind to trigger premise (2), while my conceiving in (1) is the right kind — without already presupposing that consciousness is special in precisely the way a physicalist would deny?

Where does the parallel argument fail? Give me a committed diagnosis: reject a specific step, explain why you reject it rather than the others, and say what your rejection implies about premise (1) of the original.
