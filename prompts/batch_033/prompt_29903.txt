"The question of whether DNA is literally a code has been a central debate in philosophy of biology. On one side, theorists like John Maynard Smith argue that DNA genuinely contains semantic information — that is, information with meaning or 'aboutness.' Maynard Smith's key argument is that the genetic code is arbitrary in the same way human languages are: the mapping from nucleotide triplets to amino acids is not determined by chemistry (there's no chemical reason why CUG codes for leucine rather than serine), and this arbitrariness is a hallmark of genuine codes. Just as the word 'dog' has no intrinsic connection to dogs, the codon CUG has no intrinsic connection to leucine — the connection is established by the 'conventions' of the translation machinery. This, Maynard Smith claims, makes DNA a literal code in the semiotic sense.

On the other side, critics like Paul Griffiths argue that the 'code' talk is merely a heuristic metaphor. They point out that DNA doesn't have representational content in the way that, say, a map or a sentence does. DNA causes protein synthesis through brute causal mechanisms — there's no 'reading' or 'interpreting' going on in any intentional sense. Griffiths argues that when we say a gene 'codes for' a trait, we're really just describing a causal chain, and dressing it up in semantic language invites confusion — particularly the idea that genes contain a 'blueprint' or 'program' for the organism, which fuels genetic determinism.

I think Maynard Smith has the stronger case. The arbitrariness of the genetic code really does seem to parallel the arbitrariness of linguistic signs, and it's hard to see why we'd call one a code but not the other. Moreover, the fact that the same codon table is shared across nearly all life (with minor variations) suggests it's a genuine system of representation, not just a causal regularity. What hangs on this is significant: if DNA is literally a code, then talk of 'genetic information' is scientifically respectable and we can legitimately speak of genes 'programming' development. If it's just a metaphor, then a lot of molecular biology's language is misleading and we should be more careful about how we describe gene action — especially in public-facing science communication where 'blueprint' talk feeds into genetic determinism."
