I'm in a metaethics course and we've been doing expressivism. I tried to reconstruct the Frege-Geach problem as a tight argument and now I'm worried I've made it look like expressivism is just obvioulsy impossible, which can't be right because smart people still defend it. Here's the argument:

(1) According to expressivism, the meaning of a moral sentence like "Lying is wrong" is the non-cognitive attitude it expresses — say, disapproval of lying.
(2) The meaning of a complex sentence is a function of the meanings of its parts (compositionality).
(3) So in a conditional like "If lying is wrong, then getting your little brother to lie is wrong," the antecedent "lying is wrong" must have the same meaning it has when it stands alone.
(4) Therefore the antecedent must express disapproval of lying, by (1) and (3).
(5) But a speaker who utters the whole conditional does not thereby express disapproval of lying — they might be reasoning hypothetically, or exploring the consequences of a moral view they reject.
(6) So expressivism predicts that the antecedent both does and does not express disapproval of lying, which is a contradiction.
(7) Therefore expressivism is false.

I know this can't be the end of the story because Blackburn, Gibbard, and others have been working on this for decades. But I can't see which premise the expressivist should actually reject. Premise (2) seems non-negotiable — giving up compositionality is giving up on language working at all. Premise (1) is just what expressivism is. And premise (5) seems like a plain fact about how conditionals work. So where does this argument go wrong? If the expressivist has to reject one of these premises, tell me which one and what they have to give up to do it — and if the problem is something subtler (like an equivocation or a scope error I've baked into the way I've set it up), point that out instead.
