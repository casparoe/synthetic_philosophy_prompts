I'm teaching a philosophy of mind unit in a course on emergence, and I want to use the Ant Fugue dialogue from Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach — the one where the ant colony Aunt Hillary carries on a conversation as a single mind and Dr. Anteater keeps insisting there's nobody home, just ants. Read it through the actual debate about whether a colony (or a brain) could have a mind. I want a reading that's fair in both directions: don't reduce the dialogue to an illustration of functionalism, and don't pretend the philosophy is settled just because the dialogue is charming. Is Aunt Hillary best read as a functionalist, an emergentist, or something closer to a dualist when she talks about the colony's soul? Does the dialogue run together multiple realizability — the same mind could be implemented in ants or neurons — with the much stronger claim that the colony is conscious, and does it notice the difference? Does the Anteater actually make a decent eliminativist argument, or does Hofstadter stack the deck by making him the one character who wants to eat the thing he says doesn't exist? Where does the dialogue anticipate something like the hard problem of consciousness, and where does it quietly change the subject from what the ants do to what the colony-level description does? I'd also like to know how the dialogue would read if Searle or Dennett had written it — would the Chinese Room or the intentional stance undercut Aunt Hillary, or would she survive both? I want to be able to show my students where the ant colony is doing real philosophical work and where its just a vivid picture, so they don't leave thinking a pile of ants literally feels things because it can talk in a dialogue. And say what the dialogue gives us that a straight functionalist paper doesn't.
