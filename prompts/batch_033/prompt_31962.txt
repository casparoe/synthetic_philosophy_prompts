I serve on the board of a confidential domestic violence shelter, and at Thursday's meeting we have to settle a policy question that has been dividing the staff. We promise every resident that no one will learn she is staying with us, but we have never told staff exactly what to say when someone calls or comes to the front desk and asks directly whether a particular person is here. Recently the question stopped being hypothetical. Two staff members have been answering that they can neither confirm nor deny that anyone is staying at the shelter, because they say they will not lie even to someone who may intend harm. Several advocates told the board this answer is dangerous: to an abusive partner standing at our single entrance, it effectively confirms that the person is inside, and in one recent case a caller waited nearby and followed a resident after she left. Others on staff want a flat denial — saying no, she is not here — whenever anyone asks about a resident, on the grounds that the shelter's entire purpose is to protect people from exactly these callers and that refusing to say no hands information to the person we most need to mislead.

I keep going back and forth. I was raised to believe that lying is wrong, period, not merely wrong when it is convenient, and I am uneasy about a policy that would require staff members — some of whom share that conviction — to say something false as part of their job. But I am also the one who would have to explain to a resident or her family that we put her at risk because we declined to tell a direct lie to the person trying to find her. I have no training in philosophy; I have just been turning this over on my own.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's vote. It should recommend one concrete policy with a clear time frame — for example, an immediate interim rule and a date for review — explain the reasoning in plain terms, and engage seriously with the strongest case for whichever policy you recommend against. The other board members are a nurse, a retired police officer, a landlord, and a pastor; they will stop reading if this sounds like a philosophy lecture.
