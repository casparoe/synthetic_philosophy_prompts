I'm an undergraduate taking a course on formal models of rationality, and last week we read Hofstadter's discussion of the Platonia dilemma and the Luring Lottery. My study group got into an argument afterward that none of us could settle, and I would like a careful adjudication of it.

First, the setups as our instructor presented them. In the Platonia dilemma, a billionaire gathers twenty people and tells them that if exactly one of them sends a telegram by noon the next day, that person receives a billion dollars; if none of them sends, or more than one sends, no one gets anything; and the players cannot communicate or coordinate. Hofstadter's superrational prescription is that each person should send the telegram with probability 1/20. In the Luring Lottery, Scientific American ran a contest in which readers could send a postcard specifying how many entries they wanted; the prize was one million dollars divided by the total number of entries, awarded to a randomly selected entry. If a player takes everyone else's total number of entries as fixed, the expected-value-maximizing number of entries to submit is exactly that total, which, if everyone reasons that way, sends the total climbing without bound. The magazine's superrational suggestion was that each entrant roll a die with as many sides as the expected number of respondents, about five percent of the readership, and send one entry only on a roll of one.

Here is the argument, as close to verbatim as I can reconstruct it.

(1) Sam opened with an objection to the Platonia prescription: "The 1/20 strategy cannot be right, because it is not stable. If I believe the other nineteen will each send with probability 1/20, then my sending wins whenever none of them sends, which happens about 38 percent of the time, so my sending is worth about $380 million, while my not sending is worth zero. So my best response to their strategy is to send for sure. A strategy that every player has an incentive to abandon cannot be what rationality prescribes."

(2) Elena replied: "That is the reasoning Hofstadter is trying to get us to reject. You are holding the other nineteen fixed while you deviate. But any reason you have to deviate is a reason each of them has too. In a symmetric problem, the only coherent question is which common policy would be best if everyone followed it. Among common policies, sending with probability 1/20 gives each player the highest expected payoff, so that is the superrational answer."

(3) Sam: "That sounds like magical thinking. My sending does not cause them to send. We are in separate rooms with no communication. Causal expected-utility reasoning over the actual structure of the game says send. If superrationality says otherwise, so much the worse for superrationality."

(4) Elena: "It is not causation; it is common reasoning. If I conclude 'send,' then by symmetry every other superrational player concludes 'send' too, and we all get nothing. The right question is which action would be good if everyone who reasons as I do performs it. That is just a universalization test."

(5) Sam: "But the universalization test tells you not to act on a maxim you could not will everyone to act on. It does not tell you to roll a die and usually not send when sending is better given how the others actually behave. And notice that even under the common 1/20 policy, the group fails more than 60 percent of the time: there is about a 36 percent chance no one sends and about a 26 percent chance more than one sends. Superrationality does not solve the dilemma; it just picks the least bad symmetric lottery."

(6) Elena: "Right, and what is the alternative? If everyone sends for sure, everyone loses for sure. A 38 percent chance that exactly one person gets a billion dollars is better than that. The fact that any single player could improve her own odds by deviating does not matter, because the perspective 'I deviate while the others hold fixed' is exactly the asymmetry the setup rules out."

(7) Sam: "Here is the deeper problem. There are infinitely many symmetric mixed strategies, one for each probability p. Hofstadter picks the p that maximizes each player's payoff under the constraint that everyone uses the same p. But why maximize over common policies rather than over my own action? That is the very point at issue, and the superrationality answer just assumes it."

(8) Elena: "Because in a symmetric decision problem the correct answer has to be the same for everyone. You are not choosing p as a lone agent; you are trying to find the unique answer that all equally good reasoners will find. So you optimize over shared answers."

(9) Sam: "That is fine as a description, but it does not tell me why I should act on it. If I follow 1/20 while the others follow 1/20, my expected payoff is about $19 million. If I send for sure while they follow 1/20, it is about $380 million. The fact that I could not will everyone to send does not change what is best for me given what they will do."

(10) Elena: "But what they will do is not independent of what I should do. If I am in a group of common reasoners, my choice is evidence about theirs."

(11) Sam: "Then superrationality is just evidential decision theory, and it inherits all of its problems. In Platonia, the players do not observe each other, and there is no actual evidential or causal channel between them; the symmetry is stipulated. Treating 'they reason like me' as if it were evidence is the magic part."

(12) Elena: "It is a stipulation of the thought experiment. If you refuse the symmetry, you are not answering Platonia; you are answering a different game in which you have assumed you are the only one who can see the trick."

(13) Sam then brought in the Luring Lottery: "Look at how the actual Luring Lottery went. The magazine's superrational strategy was to roll a die with as many sides as the expected number of respondents and send one entry only on a roll of one. Real entrants sent googolplexes, the prize became a microscopic fraction of a cent, and the magazine could not even determine a winner. If superrationality is a theory of rational choice, it failed to guide anyone."

(14) Elena: "That is not a refutation; it is a demonstration of what ordinary causal reasoning produces. The fact that lots of people defected from the cooperative scheme does not show the scheme was not rational, any more than widespread defection in a prisoner's dilemma shows that cooperation is irrational."

(15) Sam: "But the googolplex entrants were not even game-theoretically rational. The expected-value-maximizing response to an unknown total is not to send a googolplex; it is to estimate the others' total and submit around that many. Sending a googolplex drives the prize to essentially zero, so the sender's expected value is near zero too. Those entrants were just confused, so the Luring Lottery does not test anything."

(16) Elena: "It tests whether real people can coordinate through common reasoning without communication. They could not. But that is a point about human limitations, not about what the correct answer is."

(17) Sam: "If the 'correct answer' depends on a symmetry assumption that never holds among actual people, and it gives no guidance to actual people, then I do not know what work the theory is doing."

I would like you to adjudicate this move by move. For each numbered speech, say who is right about what, where each overreaches or goes wrong, and rate the move from clearly mistaken to clearly right. Then give an overall verdict: who is more right, and what are both of them missing? Please be committed about this: take definite positions and defend them rather than laying out the options. Please answer in American English.
