I hold the following view about finite strategic-form games: the strategies that are consistent with common knowledge of rationality are exactly those that survive iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies, where a strategy may be eliminated because it is strictly dominated by a mixed strategy. The load-bearing result, as I see it, is the equivalence between a strategy's being strictly dominated by some mixed strategy and its being never a best response to any belief about the opponents' play; I take the view to be a substantive claim about what rationality and common knowledge commit players to, not a stipulative definition of a solution concept.

I want to know what would count against this view. Give me a map of its exposure rather than a restatement of the standard equivalence. In particular: (1) the formal results, finite examples, and conceptual considerations that should move me, ranked from decisive to merely awkward; (2) what the view predicts about play that its rivals — Nash equilibrium, correlated equilibrium under a common prior, rationalizability with independent beliefs, forward-induction reasoning — do not, and where those rival predictions bite; (3) which of these a defender of the view would themselves concede count against it, as opposed to dismissing as changing the subject; and (4) an honest assessment of whether the view has been insulated by its qualifications — expected-utility players, finite games, the allowance of correlated beliefs, the shift to talking about consistency with rationality rather than prediction of play — and whether that insulation is a defect for a view of this kind.

What would move me most is a finite example in which a strategy that survives the elimination is ruled out by a rationality assumption I already accept, or a formal result showing that under conditions I regard as natural the view collapses into or diverges from one of its rivals. If the view survives only by saying almost nothing about what players will actually do, say so plainly.
