I've been arguing with a friend about a politician we both generally respect who authorized a drone strike that killed a high-value target but also killed several civilians. My friend's defense went like this: politics is like surgery. A surgeon cuts through healthy tissue to reach a tumor, and we don't call the surgeon a murderer — we call it medical necessity. The surgeon who refuses to operate because they don't want to cause any harm at all isn't the moral one; they're failing at their job. And a good surgeon should feel the weight of the incision even when it's clearly the right thing to do. My friend says all of this transfers directly to the politician: the civilian deaths were the incision, the terrorist leader was the tumor, the politician's visible anguish afterward is proof of moral seriousness rather than hypocrisy, and anyone who says you should never authorize a strike that might kill civilians is like a surgeon who lets the patient die with their body intact.

I can feel that this analogy is doing a lot of work in the argument, but I don't know how to assess whether it's doing honest work. Can you audit it properly? I want a correspondence table — what in politics maps to the surgeon, the patient, the scalpel, the tumor, the healthy tissue, the operating theater, the consent form, the prognosis, the malpractice suit — and then I want to know where the mapping breaks. In particular, which features of surgery does the analogy quietly import that politics simply doesn't have, and do those missing features matter for the conclusion my friend is drawing? Once the disanalogies are on the table, does the conclusion — that the strike was justified and the anguish is morally creditable — survive, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? If there's a better analogy that captures what's actually going on here, I'd like to hear it. And if I've set this up in a confused way — if the question rests on a mistaken assumption about how analogies work in moral argument, or if I'm asking for something that can't be done well — tell me that plainly instead of humoring the question.
