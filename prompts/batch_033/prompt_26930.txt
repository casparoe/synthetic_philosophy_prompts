I've been going down a rabbit hole on David Lewis's triviality results for indicative conditionals. You know the basic story — he showed that if you try to treat the probability of a conditional as its truth-conditional probability, and you want P(A→C) = P(C|A) to hold generally, the whole thing collapses: either the logic is trivial or probabilities can only take a couple of values. Pretty devestating for the idea that "if...then" works like a regular propositional connective.

Anyway I've been reading around and I've come across a handful of different escape routes people have proposed. I'm trying to figure out which ones are actually worth my time. Could you rank these approaches from most to least promising as responses to the triviality results, and give me a sentence or two for each explaining why it lands where it does?

- The restrictor view: "if" isn't a connective at all, it just restricts the domain of a (sometimes covert) modal operator.
- The non-propositional route (Edgington, etc.): conditionals don't express propositions, so they don't have truth-conditional probabilities in the first place — Adams's thesis is all we need.
- Biting the bullet on iterated conditionals: accept that P(A→C) = P(C|A) only works for simple, non-embedded conditionals, and that's fine.
- Lewis's own imaging proposal: instead of conditionalizing on A, you shift probability mass to the nearest A-worlds.
- Three-valued semantics: give the conditional a third truth-value (like de Finetti tables) so that the probability of a conditional isn't quite the probability of truth.
- The indexicality move: the proposition expressed by A→C depends on the probability function of the speaker, so it's not the same proposition across different credal states.
