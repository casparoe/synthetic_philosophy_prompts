The classical Indian debate over how many *pramāṇas* — irreducible sources of knowledge — there are has been running for well over a millennium. The Cārvāka admits only perception; Buddhist epistemologists admit perception and inference; Nyāya admits four, adding comparison (*upamāna*) and testimony (*śabda*); and the Bhāṭṭa Mīmāṃsākas add postulation (*arthāpatti*) and non-apprehension (*anupalabdhi*), for a total of six. Other schools draw the lines elsewhere. The arguments on all sides are well known, and they turn on whether putatively distinct pramāṇas can be reduced to others — whether, for instance, upamāna (knowing that a wild ox is like a cow and then recognizing one) is really just testimony plus perception plus memory, or whether arthāpatti (postulating that Devadatta must be outside because he is alive and not at home) is just inference in disguise, or whether anupalabdhi (knowing that there is no pot on the table from not perceiving one) is really just perception of an empty surface.

As far as I can tell, nobody moves. The same reduction arguments and counterexamples have been on the table for centuries, and the proponents of each school continue to maintain exactly the number of pramāṇas their tradition settled on. Diagnose this debate: is it a substantive disagreement about the structure of epistemic warrant, a verbal dispute about how to individuate knowledge-sources, a clash of bedrock methodological commitments that cannot themselves be argued for, or something else? I want a diagnosis that engages concretely with what the different schools actually say about why their chosen pramāṇas cannot be reduced, and I want you to tell me what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a formal result, a new case, an agreement on criteria for individuation, or an argument that no progress should be expected and why.
