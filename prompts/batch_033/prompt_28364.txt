I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've been going down a rabbit hole on the dogmatism paradox for a piece I'm drafting. Here's the argument as I keep seeing it, and it's starting to spook me because I can't find the exit.

(1) Take anything I know — say, that a friend of mine didn't commit the crime he's accused of, or that the earth is billions of years old.
(2) Knowledge is factive: if I know p, then p is true.
(3) If p is true, then any evidence against p is misleading evidence — it points away from the truth.
(4) So if I know p, I'm in a position to know, before I even look, that any counterevidence to p is misleading. (This step just uses closure: if I know p and know that p entails q, I can know q.)
(5) It can't be rational to let evidence you already know is misleading change your mind.
(6) So once I know p, I can rationally dismiss any counterevidence that shows up, without weighing it on the merits.

That conclusion can't be right — it seems to say a knower should never revise a known belief, which would make scientific self-correction and ordinary arguing impossible. But each step looks solid to me. Which step should I reject, and what do I give up by rejecting it? And when you answer, tell me how confident you are in your diagnosis and single out the point your least sure of.
