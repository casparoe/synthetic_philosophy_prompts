You are a philosopher of language and aesthetics who has written on the metaphysics of meaning and the epistemology of interpretation. Please think through the following problem methodically. Begin by mapping out the logical space of the disagreement — what each side must claim, what each side can concede, and where the real points of tension lie — before offering your considered diagnosis.

The debate about whether authorial intention determines the meaning of a literary text has been with us in something like its current form since at least the middle of the twentieth century, and I cannot see that it has moved. The anti-intentionalist says: the meaning of a text is a matter of what the words, in their linguistic and literary context, convey to a competent reader — the author's private mental states are neither necessary nor sufficient. The intentionalist says: a text is a communicative act, and like any communicative act its meaning is fixed by what the speaker or writer intended to convey; linguistic conventions are evidence of that intention, not independent determinants of meaning.

The impasse is striking because both sides appear to agree on a great deal. Both agree that readers standardly and legitimately appeal to biographical evidence when interpreting. Both agree that an author can fail to realise their intentions — that a text can say something other than what the author meant it to say. Both agree that there are cases where the author's intention is inaccessible and we must make do with the text alone. And both agree, at least in practice, that a reading which attributes to a text something the author could not possibly have meant — given their historical context, their other works, the language they had available — is a bad reading. Where they differ is on what makes it bad: the intentionalist says it is bad because it gets the meaning wrong; the anti-intentionalist says it is bad because it fails as an interpretation of this text by this author, even though meaning is not intention-determined.

I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is, not a survey of the literature and not a verdict on who wins. Specifically:

(1) Is this a substantive metaphysical dispute about the nature of meaning, or is it a verbal dispute in which 'meaning' picks out two different things — roughly, what was intended and what was conveyed? If the latter, why has that not been obvious to the participants?

(2) Alternatively, is this a disagreement that is not about facts at all but about norms — about how we ought to read, about what makes an interpretation worthwhile, or about which features of a text we should care about?

(3) Could there be a hidden empirical question here — for instance, about how ordinary speakers actually use 'means' when talking about written texts, or about the cognitive processes involved in interpretation — whose resolution would break the impasse?

(4) Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress? A new conceptual distinction, an empirical result, a formal semantic or pragmatic framework, a change of question — or should we expect no progress, and if so, is that a problem for the debate or a sign that we have been asking the wrong question?
