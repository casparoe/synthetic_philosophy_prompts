Please argue that the Luring Lottery, not the Platonia dilemma, is the thought experiment that genuinely separates Hofstadter-style superrationality from standard game-theoretic rationality. In the Platonia dilemma, twenty players independently decide whether to send a telegram; exactly one sender wins a billion dollars, and zero or multiple senders means nobody wins. There the superrational prescription—each player sends with probability 1/20—is already the unique symmetric equilibrium in which players randomize, so standard game theory with symmetry-based equilibrium selection reaches the same practical conclusion. In the Luring Lottery, each player chooses how many entries to submit; a one-million-dollar prize is divided by the total number of entries and awarded to a randomly selected entry. There the standard best-response rule—submit a number of entries equal to the total submitted by all the others—has no stable symmetric fixed point for a large number of players and escalates without bound, while the superrational rule—each of the M expected players submits a single entry with probability 1/M—gives a definite common strategy. That superrational rule, however, is not a best response to itself: conditional on the others following it, a player would prefer to submit one entry with certainty. Explain why this last fact does not refute superrationality but reveals it as a distinct normative standard, and state the strongest reply available to a defender of standard game theory.
