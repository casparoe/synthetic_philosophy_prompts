I am a professor of moral philosophy. One of my former doctoral students — now a lecturer in the same department — is getting married this Saturday, and I have been asked to give a toast at the wedding reception. She wrote her dissertation on Bernard Williams's integrity objection to impartial moral theories, and she has remained a committed Williams-style sceptic about the demand that our deepest personal commitments be justified from a neutral standpoint. I know her well enough to know that she would find it alienating — indeed, a small but real betrayal — if my toast treated her wedding as an occasion for a philosophy lecture, or if it sounded as though I were justifying my affection and good wishes by appeal to some general principle about the value of marriage or the institution of love. Williams's phrase "one thought too many" will be in the air for at least some of the guests, and the toast itself must not earn that description.

The reception will include perhaps a dozen academic philosophers who know Williams's work well, alongside the couple's families and non-academic friends, who do not. The toast must land for both halves of the room. It should be warm, genuinely celebratory, and roughly four to five minutes long when spoken aloud. Above all, it must not itself commit the fault Williams names: it must not be a speech that, in reflecting on why we care about the couple, has one thought too many.

Please write the toast. Before drafting, think carefully about the structural problem this occasion poses: a toast that philosophises about the danger of philosophising about love is straightforwardly self-undermining. How can the speech acknowledge the philosophical idea — for those guests who will recognise it — without performing it? You may find it helpful to begin by brainstorming two or three distinct approaches that avoid this trap, then select and develop the most promising one.

The toast should be written in British English.
