I teach a cross-listed philosophy and economics seminar on the epistemology of markets: fourteen upper-level students, each with at least one prior course in decision theory or microeconomics, and I have a single 80-minute session to spend on no-trade theorems. I want to stage the central result as something the students do to themselves before they ever hear it stated. The result I want them to feel is this: when rational Bayesians with common priors hold only private information about an asset, there should be no purely speculative trade, because each side's willingness to trade at a given price is itself evidence that, once it becomes common knowledge, should erase the perceived gain. A market that produces no such trade is not failing; it is working exactly as their own epistemic standards demand.

Design a runnable classroom game for this group, with rules, materials, and a payoff scheme that does not use real money (course points, tokens, or the like). Students should be given private signals about the value of an asset, be able to post bids and asks or negotiate trades, and then have the true value revealed and their payoffs settled. The game should be arranged so that the mechanism appears in their own behavior: someone's eagerness to buy or sell at a price is information, and after a round or two they should begin to see that a mutually acceptable price is exactly the price at which neither side should want the trade. The activity should also connect, in the debrief, to the agreeing-to-disagree theorem and to the distinction between trade motivated by private information and trade motivated by hedging, liquidity, or differing priors.

Give me the complete package: setup and materials, exact rules for each round, a minute-by-minute timeline for the 80 minutes, a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the formal pieces (common prior, Bayesian updating, common knowledge, the information revealed by an offer, ex ante efficiency), and an honest account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how I should handle each failure. End with a candid note on which features of the theorem the activity cannot reproduce — for instance, that no classroom can enforce a genuine common prior or full Bayesian rationality, and that the face-to-face setting may leave the common-knowledge step underdetermined — and what I will need to supply explicitly as the instructor. No lecture up front; the students should not hear the phrase "no-trade theorem" until the debrief.
