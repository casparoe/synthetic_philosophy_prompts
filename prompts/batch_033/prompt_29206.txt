I teach a philosophy of rationality seminar — twelve graduate students, one three-hour session. The week's topic is superrationality: the idea, most associated with Douglas Hofstadter, that rational agents who know each other to be rational can sometimes coordinate without communication by reasoning about what any rational agent would do in their position. I want the students to encounter the problem before they encounter the theory.

Design a classroom game built around the Platonia Dilemma (the Luring Lottery). The core setup is this: there are N participants, each must independently choose whether to submit a claim or not, and the prize goes to a single claimant if and only if exactly one person claims it — if zero or more than one claim, nobody gets anything. Participants cannot communicate, and they all know the rules and each other's rationality. The puzzle is that standard causal decision theory says "if you think anyone else will claim, don't claim; if you think nobody will, claim" — but since everyone reasons symmetrically, this leads to either zero claimants or too many, and the prize is lost. Superrationality is the proposal that players can break the symmetry by asking "what policy would I want all of us to adopt?" and acting on that.

I need a runnable activity, not a lecture. Please provide:

1. Rules, materials, and a payoff scheme that doesn't require real money (e.g., points toward a small course-credit bonus, or a token system with a tangible but trivial prize like a chocolate bar for the highest cumulative score over several rounds).

2. A structure of multiple rounds with variations, so students can discover the pattern: early rounds where they play naively, later rounds where you introduce a shared arbitrary tiebreaker (e.g., "the person whose birthday is earliest in the calendar year should claim"), and a final round where the tiebreaker is removed and they must decide whether superrationality alone can do the work. Specify what you announce before each round and what you withhold.

3. A debrief that maps what happened in each round onto the theoretical landscape: where standard causal decision theory predicts failure, where a Schelling point or an arbitrary convention succeeds, where superrationality is supposed to succeed on its own, and where the experiment leaves the question open.

4. An honest note on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — e.g., students finding covert ways to signal, the group converging on a convention you didn't intend, or the whole thing feeling like a lottery rather than a reasoning problem — and how to handle each.

5. Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this topic. For instance: it is settled that causal decision theory cannot solve the Platonia Dilemma on its own; it is contested whether superrationality is a genuine normative requirement or merely a relabeling of the problem; it is contested whether the move from "what should I do?" to "what policy would I want everyone to adopt?" is justified without additional premises. Do not present a contested view as the consensus. Flag where the activity itself leans on a particular reading of the dilemma (e.g., Hofstadter's) and where alternative readings would require a different setup.
