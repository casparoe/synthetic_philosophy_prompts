Analyze the Han van Meegeren case through the lens of philosophical theories about the value of originals. Quick recap in case you need it: van Meegeren forged several "Vermeers" in the 1930s and 40s, the most famous being "Christ at Emmaus," which art critics and experts hailed as a masterpiece. He sold one to Göring during the war, and after the war he was arrested as a collaborator for selling off Dutch cultural treasures. To save his neck he revealed they were fakes and painted another one in prison to prove it. The thing is, the paintings didn't change at all physically — same paint, same canvas — but the way people saw them and valued them flipped completely. First they were transcendent Vermeers, then they were worthless frauds, then eventually they became valuable van Meegerens in their own right.

What I want: go through the main philosophical positions on why we value originals over visually indistinguishable copies — the achievement view, the causal-history view, the idea that there's something magical or irrational about it — and say what each of them would make of the Emmaus painting. Where does each theory get the case right, and where does it struggle? In particular, I'm interested in two things: (1) does the fact that the painting's aesthetic reception changed so radically without any physical change count against the rational defensibility of caring about originals, or is it exactly what you'd expect on a good theory? and (2) the post-reveal status of van Meegeren's paintings — they're now collected and displayed as famous forgeries, which gives them a kind of value again — seems to mess with the simple story where exposure makes a forgery worthless. Is that a problem for these theories, or do they handle it fine?
