I am a retired physicist who has recently taken up philosophy, and I have been helping to grade an advanced undergraduate course in epistemic game theory. Question: "What is common knowledge, and what role does it play in epistemic game theory? In your answer, explain (a) how common knowledge differs from mutual knowledge, (b) what common knowledge of rationality licenses as a prediction about play, and (c) what Aumann's agreement theorem states and which assumptions it requires."

Student's answer: "Common knowledge of a proposition p is the infinite hierarchy: everyone knows p, everyone knows that everyone knows p, and so on without bound. It is strictly stronger than mutual knowledge, which stops after the first level. The standard illustration is the coordinated attack problem, in which two generals can exchange messages but can never attain common knowledge, and therefore cannot coordinate an attack with certainty.

In epistemic game theory, one usually assumes that the structure of the game and the rationality of the players are common knowledge. Under common knowledge of rationality, each player can iteratively delete strategies that are never a best response to any surviving conjecture about the other players; the strategies that remain are the rationalizable strategies. If we add a common prior, the play that survives this reasoning corresponds to a correlated equilibrium, which need not be a Nash equilibrium.

Aumann's agreement theorem shows that rational agents cannot agree to disagree. If two Bayesians share a common prior and their posterior probabilities for some event are common knowledge, then those posteriors must be equal. The theorem is often glossed as showing that common knowledge of rationality is what forces people's beliefs to converge, and it is the main reason speculative trade should be impossible: if it is common knowledge that both parties are rational, then one party's willingness to trade would already reveal their private information, and the other would refuse.

The main lesson, then, is that common knowledge of rationality is a very strong assumption: it yields rationalizability, yields correlated equilibrium when combined with a common prior, and drives the agreement and no-trade results."
