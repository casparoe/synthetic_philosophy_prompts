I'm in a reading group that's been working through the algorithmic fairness literature, and we've hit a point of frustration. Every week someone presents a paper proposing a new fairness criterion — demographic parity, equalized odds, predictive parity, calibration, individual fairness, counterfactual fairness — and every week someone else points out that the new criterion either collapses into an old one under mild conditions or conflicts with another criterion that seems just as well-motivated. The impossibility results (the ones showing that calibration and certain balance conditions can't simultaneously hold except in degenerate cases, and their relatives) keep coming up, but we never seem to ask what they're actually telling us about the project of formalizing fairness in the first place.

So before we read another proposal, I want to do the groundwork properly. Please write the desiderata that any adequate theory of algorithmic fairness must meet. I want the full specification: which cases a fairness criterion has to handle (hiring, lending, pretrial detention, medical triage — do these call for different things?), which distinctions it must respect (between process and outcome, between group and individual, between predictive and causal), which formal properties are required and which are merely nice to have, and which results from the adjacent literatures (the impossibility theorems, the causal hierarchy, results about infra-marginality) it cannot contradict. For each desideratum, give the motivating case or consideration — why should a theory of fairness care about this? Then flag which desiderata are non-negotiable and which are modeling choices that reasonable people can disagree about. Most importantly: tell me which subsets of these desiderata are jointly unsatisfiable given what we already know, and whether that means some of them need to be abandoned, weakened, or indexed to context. I'm less interested in a scorecard for existing proposals than in the structure of the requirements themselves — if the list turns out to be internally inconsistent, that's the result I most want to see laid out clearly, because it would explain why the literature feels stuck in a loop.
