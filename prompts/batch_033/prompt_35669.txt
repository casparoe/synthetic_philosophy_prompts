Ok so I've been trying to get my head around Grice's distinction between conventional and conversational implicature and I keep hitting a wall. I understand the textbook definitions — conversational implicatures are generated by the cooperative principle and the maxims (you figure them out by assuming the speaker is being cooperative), while conventional implicatures are just baked into the meaning of certain words regardless of context. But the more I read the less clean the distinction seems.

Here's what's bugging me. Take "but." Grice's classic move is to say that "p but q" and "p and q" have the same truth conditions, and the contrastive flavor of "but" is a conventional implicature — it's attached to the word "but" itself, not derived from any maxim. But then you've got people (I think it's a neo-Gricean thing?) arguing that the contrast reading of "but" could actually be a generalized conversational implicature — it arises by default unless something cancels it, but it's still calculable from the maxim of relevance or manner. And if that's right, then "but" isn't conventional implicature at all, it's just a really entrenched conversational one.

So here's what I actually want to understand: what turns on this? If we reclassify "but" as a generalized conversational implicature, does that change anything important about the overall Gricean picture, or is this just taxonomic housekeeping? And more generally — if the boundary between conventional and conversational implicature is this blurry, does that cause trouble for the broader project of explaining how speakers mean more than they literally say? Like, does the whole framework need a clean cut between the two to work, or can it tolerate some fuzziness at the edges?

I guess I'm asking for an explanation not just of the distinction itself but of why the distinction is theoretically load-bearing (or isn't), and what exactly breaks if you can't draw it sharply.
