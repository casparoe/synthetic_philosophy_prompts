I've been reading the back-and-forth about Aumann's agreement theorem and what it says about real disagreement, and honestly I can't tell whether the debate is still alive or just spinning its wheels. The theorem itself is clean: if two agents have the same prior, update by Bayesian conditionalization on private information, and their posteriors about some event become common knowledge, then those posteriors have to be equal. So rational agents with common priors can't "agree to disagree." That's the 1976 result.

What I'm stuck on is the decades of argument about what this actually *means*. One camp says the theorem shows that persistent real-world disagreement has to come from somewhere — irrationality, different priors, or failures of common knowledge — and that's a substantive, nontrivial claim about the structure of disagreement. The other camp says the conditions are so demanding (common priors! common knowledge of posteriors!) that the theorem is basically a formal curiosity: it tells you what would happen in a world nothing like ours, so drawing conclusions about actual political or scientific disputes is a category mistake. And then there's a third layer where people argue about whether "common priors" is a rationality requirement at all, or just a modeling assumption with no normative bite.

As far as I can tell, these positions were all on the table by the early 90s and nobody has moved since. The same moves get made, the same counterexamples get trotted out, and the literature has mostly just gotten more technical without resolving the underlying standoff.

So here's what I want: a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it a verbal dispute about what "explains" means — one side using it in a formal, conditional sense and the other in a causal, mechanistic one? Is it a disagreement about the norms of modeling: how idealized a model can be before it stops telling you about the world? Are the two sides just answering different questions (one about the epistemology of ideally rational agents, one about the sociology of actual belief)? Or is there a hidden empirical question in here — something about how often real people approximate the conditions, or about what "common knowledge" amounts to in practice — that nobody's bothered to test?

I have a pet hypothesis but I don't trust it: I suspect the impasse comes from the fact that Aumann's theorem is a negative result dressed up as a positive one. It says what *can't* happen under certain conditions, but people keep reading it as a claim about what *does* happen or *should* happen, and the two readings pull in opposite directions. Is that right, or am I missing something?
