I recently returned to the small, devout community where I grew up, after my first year away at university, and found myself unable to make myself intelligible to people I love. A tutor pointed me to a passage from Plato's Republic, and it has stayed with me. I understand the broad outline of the cave allegory: the shadows are the world of becoming, the objects carried along the wall are the perceptible particulars, the world above stands for the intelligible realm of the forms, and the sun is the form of the good. But the passage about the freed prisoner's return seems to me stranger and far less often discussed than the initial escape. Here is the passage:

"Imagine once more, I said, such a one coming suddenly out of the sun to be replaced in his old situation; would he not be certain to have his eyes full of darkness? To be sure, he said. And if there were a contest, and he had to compete in measuring the shadows with the prisoners who had never moved out of the den, while his sight was still weak, and before his eyes had become steady (and the time which would be needed to acquire this new habit of sight might be very considerable), would he not be ridiculous? Men would say of him that up he went and down he came without his eyes; and that it was better not even to think of ascending; and if any one tried to loose another and lead him up to the light, let them only catch the offender, and they would put him to death."

I need help understanding what this passage commits Plato to, beyond the standard "philosopher returns to enlighten the prisoners" reading. Specifically, I have three questions. First, what exactly is meant by the remark that the returned prisoner "went up and came down without his eyes"? Is this merely about temporary visual impairment, or is it making a point about the kind of knowledge the philosopher has acquired and lost in the descent? Second, why does Socrates have the returned man become ridiculous in a contest of measuring shadows with the prisoners? Does this imply that knowledge of the forms actually makes one worse at judging the visible world, and if so, how does that square with the claim that the forms are supposed to explain the visible world? Third, given that the prisoners would kill anyone who tried to lead them out, does this passage treat the philosopher's return as a duty, a hazard, or both—and how should I understand that in light of the theory of forms rather than just as a biographical remark about Socrates?
