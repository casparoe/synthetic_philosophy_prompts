I teach an undergraduate philosophy of mathematics course, about 18 students, one 75-minute session. We are about to cover Russell's paradox and the limits of naive set theory. I want an in-class activity that makes students feel the paradox before they see it formally—they should run into the contradiction themselves through the rules of the activity, not through a lecture.

Design a sorting game. The core idea: students physically sort cards into two piles according to a single rule—whether a set contains itself as a member. The cards should start with harmless, concrete examples so that students get comfortable with the rule and develop some momentum. Then, late in the sequence, they encounter the Russell set and discover that it cannot consistently be placed in either pile. The moment of realization should come from the activity itself; I should not have to announce that something has gone wrong.

Include all of the following in your design:

- The full list of set-cards (at least 12 sets, carefully sequenced to build toward the paradox). Each card should name a set in plain language, and the sets should be genuinely graspable by undergraduates who have not yet seen axiomatic set theory. Use a mix of ordinary sets (sets of physical objects, sets of numbers, sets of sets) so that the containment relation is intuitive.
- The sorting rule exactly as you would state it to students at the start.
- The physical setup: what goes on the table, how students interact with the cards, whether they work individually first and then compare, or sort collectively.
- A timeline for the 75 minutes, including setup, the sorting activity itself, the moment where the paradox surfaces, and the debrief.
- A debrief script that maps specific moments in the activity onto the formal statement of Russell's paradox—the unrestricted comprehension principle, the definition of the Russell set, and the derivation of the contradiction. The debrief should also give students a way to see why the paradox is not merely a verbal trick or a quirk of the example.
- A candid note on what predictably goes wrong when this activity is run: what confusions students bring (e.g., conflating a set with its members, thinking the paradox is about self-reference in general rather than about unrestricted comprehension, or trying to "solve" the paradox by gerrymandering the sorting rule). For each, say how the facilitator should handle it in the moment.
- An honest account of what the activity cannot capture. In particular, the activity works with a finite collection of sets, but the paradox in its full form concerns the unrestricted comprehension axiom and the assumption that every predicate defines a set. How should I address this gap in the debrief without undermining the insight the activity produced?

Finally, I want your response to flag where its own reasoning is most likely to fail. Which design choice would you reconsider first if you ran the activity and it did not work—and what kind of evidence from the classroom would lead you to abandon the sorting-game approach altogether in favor of a different kind of activity?
