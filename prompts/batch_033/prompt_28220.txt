Alright, here's a challenge for you. I'm teaching a grad seminar on epistemic rationality and we're about to spend two weeks on the inside view / outside view distinction — you know the one, from Kahneman and Tetlock: the inside view is your detailed causal model of your particular situation, the outside view is the base rate from a reference class of similar cases, and the standard wisdom is that you should basically always anchor on the outside view because the inside view is a factory for overoptimism. My students are smart but they've mostly just read the greatest hits, and I want to give them something that makes the standard advice look less like a theorem and more like a heuristic that can fail in principled ways.

So here's what I need. Construct me a thought experiment where an agent faces a decision between trusting their inside-view reasoning and deferring to a well-constructed outside-view base rate, and the inside view is actually the epistemically right call. Not just psychologically comforting, not just what the agent wants to hear — epistemically correct. The agent should not defer.

Constraints:

- The reference class has to be genuinely well-chosen. No cheating by making the outside view rely on a bad reference class, noisy data, or an obviously inappropriate comparison. The base rate has to be well-calibrated for the reference class as a whole. If the outside view says "90% of these fail," that has to be the actual, well-measured failure rate for a category the case genuinely belongs to.
- Realistic scenario. Something an ordinary person could encounter — workplace, health decision, financial choice, relationship, whatever. No demons, no perfect predictors, no sci-fi.
- The reason the inside view wins can't be "the agent has a gut feeling" or "they're just special." There has to be a structural, articulable feature of the case that makes inside-view reasoning more reliable here than the outside-view base rate, even though the base rate is accurate for the reference class.
- Keep the scenario to about a paragraph. Then walk me through the reasoning: what the outside view says, what the inside view says, and a clear argument for why the inside view is correct in this case.

After you've given me the case and the walkthrough, I want two more things. First: name the structural feature that's doing the work. What exactly is it about this scenario that makes the usual outside-view deference fail? Second: tell me what happens if you remove or reverse that feature — does the case collapse back into a standard "just trust the base rates" situation, or does it turn into something else?

And one meta-level request: when you're presenting the reasoning, please flag what's settled among people who actually work on this (the Kahneman/Tetlock crowd, the forecasting people, epistemologists who've engaged with this) versus what's genuinely contested. I don't want my students walking out thinking there's a consensus that the outside view is overrated, when the real picture is more like: the outside view is extremely powerful but there are identifiable conditions under which it can mislead, and people disagree about how often those conditions obtain in practice.

Your a professional philosopher who knows this literature cold. Take your time, maybe start by brainstorming a few candidate reference-class problems where the inside view might legitimately beat the outside view, then pick the cleanest one and build it out carefully. Think through whether a defender of the "always defer to the outside view" position would have a good rejoinder, and if they do, mention it — I want the case to be resilient, not a setup.
