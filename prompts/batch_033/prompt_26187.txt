I'm working my way through a popular book on political theory — the kind you'd find in an airport bookstore, written by a journalist rather than an academic — and I came across a paragraph on Arrow's impossibility theorem that stopped me cold. I'd encountered the theorem once in an undergraduate course, but the author's gloss seemed to be claiming something far more sweeping than I remembered, and I don't trust my own recollection enough to say where the exaggeration lies. Here is the passage as I recall it (I don't have the book with me, but I'm confident I've got the substance right):

"Kenneth Arrow proved in 1951 that democracy is, strictly speaking, impossible. His impossibility theorem shows that no voting system can ever reliably reflect the will of the people, because whenever there are three or more options, majority rule can produce cycles — A beats B, B beats C, but C beats A — meaning there is no coherent 'popular will' to begin with. Arrow demonstrated that this problem afflicts every conceivable voting method, from simple plurality to ranked-choice, and that the only way to avoid it is to appoint a dictator. In other words, any fair voting system can be made to produce arbitrary results just by adding an irrelevant third candidate to the ballot, which is why third parties are so destabilizing. The theorem is sometimes called the 'paradox of voting,' and it explains why all real-world democracies are, at bottom, exercises in choosing the least bad option."

I have a nagging sense that this paragraph runs together several distinct ideas — the Condorcet paradox, Arrow's theorem proper, maybe even Downs's paradox of voter turnout — and that the author is treating Arrow's result as a kind of proof that democracy is incoherent, which feels like a leap. But I'm not a social choice theorist, and I want to be fair to the book rather than just dismissing it because I don't like the conclusion. Could you go through this summary and tell me, as precisely as you can, which claims a social choice theorist would accept as accurate, which are misleading or conflate distinct results, and which are simply wrong? I'd also like to know whether the author's practical conclusion — that real-world democracies are just exercises in choosing the least bad option — actually follows from anything Arrow proved, or whether it's an independent claim dressed up in the authority of the theorem.
