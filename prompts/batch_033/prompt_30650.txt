I am a retired natural scientist, and after a career spent thinking about causal mechanisms I have recently begun reading philosophy of mind. You are a professional philosopher of mind. Please think carefully, and start by brainstorming the nearest concepts before you answer, because a bare label will not satisfy me.

I keep noticing a difficulty I do not yet have a word for. A writer will maintain that every piece of human behavior, including speech and the writing of philosophy papers, is fully produced by physical processes in the nervous system. The same writer will also maintain that conscious experience is real: there is something it is like to feel pain or to see red, and this felt character is not itself a physical process. The writer then adds that this felt character makes no causal difference to anything anyone says or does. A representative case: a philosopher argues that verbal reports of pain are wholly caused by neural events and that the felt hurt itself contributes nothing, yet also offers the felt hurt as a reason to reject purely functional accounts of pain. In the very same papers, these writers treat their own felt experiences as evidence and conclude from them that consciousness is real, which seems to require those experiences to have influenced the words on the page.

Does this specific difficulty have an established name? What exactly does it consist in, and how should it be distinguished from a merely self-undermining position in general, from the claim that consciousness is an illusion, and from the bare possibility that a real phenomenon could be causally idle? Which versions of the underlying view, if any, avoid the difficulty, and which cannot?
