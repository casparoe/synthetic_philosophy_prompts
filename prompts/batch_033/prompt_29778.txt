I am a retired physical chemist, recently drawn into philosophy, and I have been asked to lead a ninety-minute session for a philosophy reading group on causation and evidence. The topic is Simpson's paradox, under the heading "what Simpson's paradox shows about the limits of purely statistical evidence for causal claims." The audience will be philosophers — faculty and graduate students — who can read a two-by-two table but have no training in regression or causal modelling. I know the statistical material well; what I need from you is a teaching plan. Please do not explain the paradox to me. Plan how I should explain it to them.

Take the role of a professional philosopher of science who has repeatedly taught this material to audiences without statistical training. Think the problem through before committing: begin by sketching two or three candidate orders for the session and say explicitly why you reject the ones you do not use.

The plan should specify the following. First, the order in which the pieces must be introduced and why — in particular, what has to be in place before the phenomenon can even present itself as a paradox rather than as an error in the arithmetic. Second, one concrete carrying example, described in enough detail that I could put it on the board, with an account of the features it must have and the features it must not have; if you would set aside the familiar admissions-bias example, say why. Third, the misconceptions this audience will predictably bring or form, and the exact point in the sequence at which each should be headed off. I anticipate at least three: that the paradox shows aggregate statistics are unreliable and the disaggregated table is always the honest one; that the whole thing is a weighting artifact with no conceptual interest; and that there must be a fact of the matter about which level of analysis is correct, which the data alone can settle. Do not treat my list as exhaustive. Fourth, what should be left out at this level, and what I should say when someone asks about it. Fifth, one closing question or small exercise that would show me whether they have the concept — the ability to choose the right comparison for a causal question — rather than merely the ability to retell the example.

End by telling me which part of your plan you are least confident in, and what consideration — about how non-statisticians learn this material, or about the logic of the paradox itself — would lead you to revise it.
