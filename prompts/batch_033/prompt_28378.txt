I was at this stats conference last month and got stuck in a conversation that's been rattling around my head ever since. Two researchers—both pretty senior, both with solid publication records—got into it over drinks about whether the replication crisis is fundamentally a *frequentist* problem or just a *people being sloppy* problem. One of them (let's call her the Bayesian) was arguing that the whole NHST framework basically bakes in the replication crisis: dichotomous significance thresholds, the fact that p-values overstate evidence against the null, the way stopping rules mess everything up, and how you can't coherently update your beliefs as data accumulates without basically pretending you didn't peek. The other one (the frequentist) was pushing back hard, saying this is all a red herring—the real culprits are low power, p-hacking, file-drawer effects, and the perverse incentives in publishing, and that a sloppy Bayesian analysis with a conveniently chosen prior is just as capable of producing non-replicable results. At one point the Bayesian said something like "the replication crisis *is* the Bayesian critique made flesh" and the frequentist nearly choked on his drink.

I'd love to see this argument fleshed out properly. Could you write a dialogue between the two of them—say, the next morning, when they've cooled off a bit and are actually trying to engage with each other's points rather than score rhetorical wins? I want the back-and-forth to get into the weeds: specific technical arguments about why p-values behave the way they do under optional stopping, the role of prior distributions in addressing (or not addressing) publication bias, whether preregistration is a patch on a broken paradigm or a genuine fix, and so on. Neither should be a strawman—I want the frequentist to have genuinely sharp comebacks and the Bayesian to concede where the critique overreaches. Maybe set it over coffee in the hotel lobby before the morning sessions start.
