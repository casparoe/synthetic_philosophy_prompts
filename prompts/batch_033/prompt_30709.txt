Two members of my philosophy discussion group have been arguing about Newcomb's problem for weeks, and they've asked me to referee. I've transcribed their latest exchange below. Could you adjudicate it move by move — identify who is right about which specific claims, where each argument goes wrong if it does, and state what your overall verdict is about who has the stronger position? Please also flag anything important that both parties are missing.

Here is the exchange:

**Eva:** The setup is this. There's a Predictor — not omniscient, but frighteningly accurate: 99% of the time it has correctly predicted human choices in this exact scenario. It presents you with two boxes. Box A is transparent and visibly contains $1,000. Box B is opaque. Before you enter the room, the Predictor has placed $1,000,000 in Box B if and only if it predicted you would take only Box B; otherwise Box B is empty. You know all of this. You must choose: take both boxes, or take only Box B. I say you should take only Box B.

**Daniel:** And I say that's irrational. The money is already in the boxes — or it isn't. If the million is in Box B, taking both boxes gets you $1,001,000, which beats $1,000,000. If Box B is empty, taking both gets you $1,000, which beats $0. Either way, two-boxing dominates. Your choice can't retroactively cause the Predictor to have done something different yesterday.

**Eva:** Dominance reasoning is only valid when the state of the world is independent of your choice. Here it isn't independent — the Predictor's track record means the contents of Box B are evidentially correlated with what you do. You can't just bracket that correlation and pretend the states are fixed independently of your decision.

**Daniel:** They are causally independent, and that's what matters. The correlation exists because of a common cause — whatever psychological profile the Predictor scanned to make its prediction. Your choice doesn't cause the money to be there; it merely reveals what the Predictor already did. So the causal structure licenses the dominance argument, and two-boxing is mandatory.

**Eva:** Let me press you on that. If the Predictor had been right 100% of the time for a billion trials, would you still two-box?

**Daniel:** Absolutely. The dominance argument doesn't turn on the accuracy rate. Whatever is in Box B, you get strictly more by taking both. The number of past successes just tells you how well the Predictor reads people; it doesn't change the fact that, at the moment of choice, the contents of Box B are fixed.

**Eva:** Then your decision theory is telling you to perform an action that, by your own lights, will almost certainly leave you with $1,000, while my decision theory has me walking away with $1,000,000. At some point a theory of rational choice that systematically makes its adherents poorer than the alternative is self-undermining. If rationality is supposed to guide action toward better outcomes, and your rule reliably delivers worse outcomes, what work is the concept of rationality doing?

**Daniel:** You're conflating the normative question with a kind of evolutionary audit. Rationality isn't about which disposition tends to make you rich across possible lives; it's about which act you have most reason to perform in the actual situation, given the actual causal facts. The Predictor has already acted. The causal structure of the world at the moment of choice makes two-boxing the act you have most reason to perform, even if the kind of person who two-boxes ends up with less money.

**Eva:** But you're smuggling in a particular conception of "most reason" that's doing all the argumentative work. If I care about outcomes, and one-boxing reliably produces better outcomes for agents in my exact epistemic position, why isn't that itself a reason to one-box? You seem to be stipulating that only causal consequences count as reasons, but that's precisely what's at issue.

**Daniel:** Because the outcome isn't produced by your choice — it's produced by the Predictor's prior prediction. Your choice merely reveals which outcome you were already slated to get. You can't make yourself a millionaire by taking one box any more than a rooster can make the sun rise by crowing.

**Eva:** That analogy is hopeless. The rooster has no evidence that its crowing correlates with sunrise; it's just a brute temporal sequence. I have overwhelming evidence that my choice correlates with the contents of Box B. Acting on that evidence — choosing the act that is the hallmark of millionaires in this scenario — is paradigmatically rational. Your rooster analogy treats all correlations as spurious by definition, which simply begs the question against the evidential approach.
