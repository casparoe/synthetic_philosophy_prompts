I've been turning something over in my head for a while and I can't tell if it's one thing or three seperate things, and I don't know what to call it. It's about when a "yes" actually counts. In ordinary life we all seem to treat some yeses as not counting. If someone agrees to a surgery because the doctor lied about the risks, we say they didn't really consent. If someone agrees because the other person said "do this or I'll make sure you lose your job," same thing. If someone agrees but they're so drunk they won't remember the conversation, or they have advanced dementia and can't follow what's being asked, same thing again. These don't obviously feel like the same failure: one is about being tricked, one is about being pressured, one is about the person not being in a state to understand. But we reach for the same language for all of them.

So my question is: is there an established name or concept for the thing these all share, the thing that turns a bare "yes" into the kind of yes that actually binds? Or is the honest answer that these are just different things we happen to lump together? And if there is a shared thing, I want to know where its edges are. Is not telling someone something the same as lying to them, or does a yes stay valid when nothing false was said? Is a very tempting offer (I'll pay off all your debts if you do this) the same kind of pressure as a threat, or does it leave the yes intact? How drunk, how young, or how confused does someone have to be before their yes stops counting? I'm not going to be satisfied with a one-word label; I want to know how the pieces fit and where the concept starts to break down. If no established concept actually covers all three, I'd rather hear that than have you force a label onto them.

Could you act as a professional philosopher who works on consent and moral psychology? Take your time with this, and maybe brainstorm out loud about the candidate concepts and where each one fails to cover all the cases.
