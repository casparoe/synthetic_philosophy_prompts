Last night a friend and I argued for hours about Dutch book arguments, and he ended by defending a claim I find too strong to accept, though I cannot locate the step where it goes wrong. His argument ran as follows.

(1) The Dutch book argument for probabilism says: if an agent's degrees of belief violate the probability axioms, then there is a set of bets, each fair by the agent's own lights, that the agent accepts and whose combination loses money no matter how the world turns out; this vulnerability is what makes the agent's degrees of belief irrational.

(2) The same construction applies to logical non-omniscience. Take an agent who is less than certain of a complicated tautology, or who assigns higher credence to A than to B while failing to see that A entails B. For such an agent there is likewise a set of bets, each fair by her lights, whose net payoff is negative in every logically possible world: the bookie simply buys the tautology bet at her low odds, or sells A and buys B in the combination that exploits the entailment.

(3) Every finite agent is logically non-omniscient in this sense: there will always be some tautology she is less than certain of, or some entailment she fails to see.

(4) So the Dutch book argument classifies every finite agent as irrational.

(5) But a standard of rationality that no finite agent can meet proves too much to be the right standard for evaluating actual degrees of belief.

(6) Therefore the Dutch book argument does not support probabilism after all.

I would like to keep (1), or something close to it, because I still think Dutch books reveal a genuine defect in non-probabilistic credences. But I cannot say which step to reject. Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? If the argument is valid, say so and tell me what Dutch book arguments are still good for. End with \answer{...} containing the number of the step you reject, "suppressed premise" if the fault lies in an unstated step, or "none" if you accept the argument.
