I'm teaching an upper-level seminar on moral psychology and want to pair Melville's "Bartleby, the Scrivener" with the debate over internal and external reasons. Read the story through that debate. On the internalist view associated with Bernard Williams, an agent has a reason to do something only if the action can be reached by sound deliberation from her existing motivational set — her desires, projects, and commitments; on the externalist view, some reasons apply to an agent regardless of what she happens to want or care about. The lawyer narrator proceeds as though Bartleby plainly has reasons to copy, to answer his questions, to leave the office, and to accept money; Bartleby's repeated polite refusal, framed as a preference rather than a defiance, seems to show that none of the lawyer's reasons engages anything in Bartleby's motivational set. Is Bartleby best read as vindicating internalism — a man with no internal reason to comply, and therefore no reason to comply at all? Or does the story's later course — Bartleby refuses even food, and the narrator comes to feel he has reason to care for him — expose the internalist's limit, since we are reluctant to say a starving man has no reason to eat or that the narrator has no reason to help him? Be faithful in both directions: do not turn the story into a mere illustration of the theory, and do not blur the internal/external distinction to fit the story. In particular, say what the story does to the internalist's picture of a motivational set, given how opaque and almost purely negative Bartleby's preferences are. End by saying what the story adds to the debate that the arguments alone cannot.
