Question: Present the central commitments of moral particularism as defended by Jonathan Dancy, with particular attention to the holism of reasons. Then explain how a generalist might respond, and assess whether the holism of reasons, if true, forces the rejection of moral principles.

Student answer: Moral particularism, associated above all with Jonathan Dancy, is the view that sound moral judgment does not consist in applying a set of general principles to particular cases. Generalism, by contrast, holds that the possibility of moral thought and judgment depends on there being a suitable supply of principles specifying which features count as reasons and with what valence. The particularist's central commitment is the holism of reasons: a consideration that counts in favor of an action in one context may count against it, or count for nothing, in another. For instance, the fact that an action would cause someone pain is normally a reason against performing it, but in some contexts the very same feature could be a reason in favor, or no reason at all. If holism is true, then no moral generalization of the form such-and-such a feature is always a reason with a fixed valence can be true, since every candidate principle will meet with exceptions. Generalism is therefore committed to fixed-valence principles, and holism refutes it. The generalist W. D. Ross tried to preserve principles by treating prima facie duties such as fidelity to promises as exceptionless absolute requirements, but this is exactly the kind of view holism undermines. Particularists can still allow rough rules of thumb that summarize past experience, but these are not principles in the relevant sense, because they do not determine or ground a correct moral judgment. I conclude that if the holism of reasons is true, generalism is untenable.
