I'm taking a seminar on eighteenth-century aesthetics, and we've been working through Hume's 'Of the Standard of Taste'. Last week a fellow student — who had clearly done the reading but was growing impatient with the whole enterprise — finally said something to the effect of: 'Look, isn't Hume just trying to rescue objectivity from a slogan everyone already accepts? Beauty is in the eye of the beholder. That's the end of it. There's no fact of the matter about whether one poem is better than another, and Hume's talk of ideal critics is just snobbery dressed up as philosophy.' The professor gave a pained smile and moved on, but I've been turning it over since.

What bothers me is that 'beauty is in the eye of the beholder' seems to be doing an enormous amount of work without anyone specifying what it actually amounts to. It could be the trivial observation that people disagree about aesthetic matters. It could be the psychological claim that beauty is a response in the perceiver rather than a property of the object. It could be the normative claim that no aesthetic judgement is more correct than any other. It could be a claim about the semantics of 'beautiful' — that it isn't a predicate that ascribes a property at all but merely expresses the speaker's approval. And I suspect some of these readings are compatible with Hume's project and some are not, but the slogan gets wielded as though they all come as a package.

I'd like you to lay out the distinct precise theses that 'beauty is in the eye of the beholder' could be asserting, from the least to the most contentious. For each, tell me whether it is something Hume would have accepted, something he would have rejected, or something his view complicates in an interesting way. And crucially, tell me which reading my classmate actually needed for his dismissal of Hume to go through — and whether that reading is defensible. Please write the answer in British English.
