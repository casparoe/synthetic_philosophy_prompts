For a feature I'm writing on the cultural afterlife of Derek Parfit's thought experiments, I've been revisiting Christopher Nolan's The Prestige and I find myself unable to shake the sense that the film is doing real philosophical work on the question Parfit made famous with his teletransporter: if a machine destroys you here and produces an exact replica elsewhere, did you survive? The film's version of the problem is, if anything, more unsettling than Parfit's, because Angier's machine does not transport him — it duplicates him, and the original drowns in a locked tank beneath the stage each night while the duplicate takes the bow. The film gives us a man who knows, every time he steps into the apparatus, that he will both survive and die, and he does it anyway. That seems to me like a dramatization of something Parfit's cleaner thought experiment only gestures at: the question of what it is like to live as though the ego-theory of survival were false.

I want to use the film to walk readers through Parfit's shift from an ego-theory to what he called Relation R — the idea that what matters in survival is not the persistence of a Cartesian ego but the right kind of psychological connectedness and continuity, and that identity is not always a matter of all-or-nothing. But I also want to be honest about where the film complicates or resists that reading. The film is saturated with the language of selves, souls, and irreducible identity in ways that seem to presuppose exactly the picture Parfit wants us to abandon; Angier's horror at the drowned bodies in the tanks is not just horror at death, it is horror at the thought that those bodies were him, and the film seems to endorse that horror rather than treat it as a confusion. And then there is Borden's secret — twin brothers sharing a single identity across a lifetime — which appears to be a different thought experiment entirely, one about shared identity rather than branching identity, and one that raises the question of whether psychological continuity can be sustained by a pact as much as by a brain.

What I would like is a careful reading of The Prestige through the lens of Parfit's teletransporter and the broader question of what matters in survival. Where does the film dramatize Parfit's insights in ways that philosophical argument alone cannot? Where does it smuggle in assumptions that Parfit would reject, and are those assumptions weaknesses in the film or challenges to the theory? And what does the final image — Angier's dead body among the rows of drowned copies, and the closing voiceover about what the audience sees — say about whether we can actually live with the relation-R view, even if we can argue for it? I am less interested in whether the film gets Parfit right than in what the confrontation between the two reveals about the limits of both.
