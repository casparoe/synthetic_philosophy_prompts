I'm giving a faculty seminar next month at a business school where I'm defending the claim that the dollar auction — Shubik's 1971 game where two players bid for a dollar, the highest bidder wins the dollar minus their bid, and the second-highest bidder pays their bid and gets nothing — provides the best structural model for understanding why competent managers escalate commitment to failing projects. My argument is not that the dollar auction is a perfect analogue, but that it captures the feature that makes escalation rational without invoking cognitive bias: once you have bid, the choice at each step is between losing your sunk cost for certain (by quitting) and taking a chance that one more bid will force the other party to quit, letting you recover your losses. I argue that this structural account outperforms the standard psychological explanations — sunk cost fallacy, ego defense, prospect theory's loss aversion — because it predicts escalation even in fully rational agents, explains why escalation persists in institutional settings where individual psychology is supposedly dampened, and accounts for the stubborn fact that escalation often survives explicit training designed to counteract it.

The audience will be about twenty-five faculty: roughly a third behavioral economists who think the right explanation for escalation involves cognitive bias and prospect theory and who will want to know what the model predicts that theirs doesn't; a third organizational psychologists who study escalation in the field, are skeptical of game-theoretic models, and will object that the model assumes away everything interesting — the social context, the organizational politics, the way failing projects become identified with their sponsors' careers; and a third strategy and management scholars who care less about whether the model is psychologically accurate than about whether it generates actionable advice for managers. There is also one senior faculty member, a former colleague of Staw, who has published on escalation for thirty years and will likely regard the whole project as a regression to the rational-choice imperialism he spent his career arguing against.

For each, give me a two-minute reply and a candid note on which part of the reply is weakest.
