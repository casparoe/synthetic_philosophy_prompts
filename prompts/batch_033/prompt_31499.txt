For an upper-level undergraduate seminar I teach on formal epistemology and decision theory, I'm drafting the final exam. I want one essay question on evidential cooperation in large worlds — the idea, associated especially with the work of Gary Drescher and later writers, that in a sufficiently large universe agents who cannot causally interact may nevertheless have reason to act as if cooperating, because their decisions provide evidence about the behavior of similar agents elsewhere.

The question should be unanswerable by simply reciting the standard argument for acausal trade or summarizing the usual objections. I want it to force students to confront a specific structural question: whether the case for evidential cooperation depends on the large-worlds assumption in the way it is usually presented, or whether the large-worlds assumption is doing a different kind of work — perhaps epistemic rather than metaphysical — than the standard story suggests. In particular, a strong answer should distinguish between two roles the large-worlds assumption might play: (1) guaranteeing that sufficiently similar agents actually exist so that the evidential link is grounded in fact, and (2) providing a setting in which the relevant correlation between one's own decision and the decisions of others is not confounded by common causes, selection effects, or the unilateralist's curse.

Please write the question, a model answer of roughly 800 words, and a marking scheme. The question should be designed to take about 45 minutes of a three-hour exam. The marking scheme should separate an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific conceptual moves each must contain, and should flag at least two common student mistakes that would pull an answer down to a C. The model answer should separate what is settled from what remains genuinely contested among people working on this topic, and should not present a contested view as if it were the consensus position. Use American English throughout.
