So I was at this interdisciplinary workshop last week — mix of cognitive science, ML, and philosophy people — and two of the attendees got into this argument at the poster session that I keep turning over in my head. It was about Bongard problems, those little puzzles where you see six panels on the left and six on the right and you have to figure out the rule that separates them. (Like, all the shapes on the left are convex and all the shapes on the right are concave, or the left ones all contain a triangle somewhere, that kind of thing.)

The argument started when one of them — let me call him Dan, he's in computational neuroscience — said Bongard problems are a beautiful demonstration that humans can induce concepts from radically few examples. You get six positive and six negative instances and boom, you've got the concept. He said this is a problem for the view that concept learning requires massive amounts of data, the way you'd train a deep net on ImageNet.

The other person — call her Mira, she does philosophy of cognitive science — basically said he had it exactly backwards. She said Bongard problems actually show how much heavy lifting your inductive biases are doing. There are, in principle, infinitely many rules consistent with any set of twelve panels. The only reason you land on the "right" one is that your cognitive system is massively pre-constrained to consider some kinds of rules and not others. So if anything, Bongard problems are an argument *for* rich innate constraints, not for the power of few-shot learning.

Dan pushed back and said that's just restating the problem of induction, which everyone already knows about. The interesting thing, he said, is that given the right biases — which we evidently have — you *can* learn from very few examples. That's the positive result, and it's not trivial.

Mira said he was missing her point. She said the "right biases" in a Bongard problem are whatever biases the problem's *designer* happened to build the problem around. The designer picks a rule that humans find natural, and then picks examples that uniquely pick out that rule *given human biases*. So the whole setup is circular as a demonstration of few-shot learning: it only works because the examples were hand-picked to work with our specific biases. In the real world, nature doesn't curate your examples to match your priors.

Dan came back and said that's a point about the ecology of learning, not about the underlying computational problem. The computational problem is: given a set of examples, infer the concept. Bongard problems show this can be done from sparse data. Whether real-world data happens to be as nicely structured as a Bongard problem is an empirical question about the environment, not a strike against the learning mechanism.

Then Mira said she didn't think it was just ecology. She said: if you gave the same Bongard problem to a solver with different biases — an alien mind, or a neural net with a different architecture — they'd find a different rule and think it was equally obvious. So what the problem actually demonstrates isn't few-shot learning per se; it's that what counts as "few-shot" is entirely relative to your inductive biases. That's not a positive result about learning from sparse data; it's a point about the relativity of simplicity.

Dan said that relativity point is exactly what a Bayesian would expect. Learning always depends on the prior. The fact that different priors lead to different generalizations isn't a bug — it's a feature of rational inference. Bongard problems are a clean demonstration of Bayesian concept learning in action, and the fact that we can solve them at all tells you something about the structure of human priors.

At that point they got pulled into another conversation and I never heard the resolution. I've been trying to figure out who's right about what. It feels like they're partly talking past each other but also like there's a genuine disagreement in there — something about whether Bongard problems are evidence for the power of few-shot learning or evidence for the richness of innate constraints, and whether those are even different things.

When you do this, please separate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this stuff, and don't present a contested view as if it's the consensus.
