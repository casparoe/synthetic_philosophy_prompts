I've been reading the literature on non-empirical theory confirmation in high-energy physics for a few years now, and I keep hitting the same impasse. The debate, in its modern form, traces to Richard Dawid's *String Theory and the Scientific Method* (2013), which argued that string theory is confirmed — not merely promising or worth funding — by three non-empirical considerations: the absence of viable alternatives after decades of search; the way string theory's components (gravity, gauge fields, fermions, extra dimensions) hang together in ways no one designed or anticipated; and a meta-induction from the historical record, according to which theories with these features have tended to survive. The same style of argument recurs in debates over the multiverse and the inflationary landscape, where proponents treat the lack of alternatives and the explanatory coherence of the framework as evidence in the absence of direct empirical tests. Critics — Peter Woit, Sabine Hossenfelder, and a number of philosophers trained in the empiricist tradition — reply that none of this is confirmation in the sense that licenses belief. They say it is, at best, a pragmatic reason to keep working on a research program, or a change in subjective credence, and that treating it as confirmation is exactly the kind of move Popper warned about. The defenders respond that the critics are appealing to an unworkably strict notion of empirical confirmation that the actual history of science doesn't instantiate, and that science has always used non-empirical considerations implicitly.

Both sides agree on the mathematics, the experimental data, and the broad history. They even agree that string theory and the multiverse have no novel empirical prediction that has been tested. And yet after more than a decade of exchanges, nobody has moved. My working hypothesis is that the debate isn't really about the physics at all, but about what kind of concept "confirmation" is: whether it has necessary and sufficient conditions that can be settled by analysis, or whether it's a cluster concept whose extension is fixed partly by the sociology and pragmatics of scientific practice. If the former, the critics are likely right that non-empirical arguments don't confirm in the relevant sense. If the latter, the defenders are likely right that we already rely on such arguments and merely lack an explicit account of them. But the literature rarely makes this meta-question explicit, so the two sides keep arguing past each other about the first-order question.

I want a diagnosis of the impasse, not a verdict on string theory. Specifically: Is my hypothesis about the structure of the disagreement right? If not, what kind of disagreement is it — verbal, substantive but about standards of evidence or epistemic values, a hidden empirical disagreement about the history of science, or something else? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a formal result in Bayesian epistemology, a case study from the history of science that both sides would accept as a shared precedent, a conceptual distinction that dissolves the question, or an argument that no progress should be expected?

Please answer as a philosopher of science who knows this literature well. Think carefully before diagnosing, and don't just survey the positions — I want the structure of the disagreement. And please be explicit about your confidence: say how confident you are in each part of your diagnosis, and single out the point you are least sure about.
