I've been taking a course in aesthetics this term, and we spent two weeks on the paradox of fiction. I thought I had settled on a resolution — roughly, that our emotional responses to fiction do not require belief in the reality of their objects, only something like vivid sensory and imaginative engagement with a representation. But then I tried to work out what this implies for ordinary, non-fictional emotions, and the result seems to prove far too much. I would like someone to tell me exactly where the reasoning goes wrong.

Here is the argument I ended up with:

(1) When we watch a horror film, we feel genuine fear, even though we do not believe the monster exists. Therefore, belief in the reality of the emotion's object is not necessary for the occurrence of the emotion.

(2) What triggers and sustains the fear in the fictional case is the vivid sensory and imaginative representation of the threatening object — the image on the screen, the soundtrack, the narrative context our imagination fills in.

(3) If vivid representation is sufficient for emotion in the absence of reality-belief, then the representational features alone are what generate the emotion. The reality-belief, when present, is not doing any of the causal work.

(4) Now take a case of fear about something real: you are hiking and see a bear on the trail ahead. You have a vivid sensory representation of the bear (the visual experience), and you also believe the bear is real.

(5) By (3), the representational features alone are sufficient to generate the fear. The additional belief that the bear is real is causally idle with respect to the emotion itself — it is, at most, a post hoc rationalization of a response that would have occurred either way.

(6) Therefore, none of our emotions are ever genuinely about reality. They are about our representations, and the belief that those representations correspond to something real merely tags them after the fact.

The conclusion is absurd, but I cannot locate the misstep. If I reject (1) and insist that fictional fear is not genuine fear — that it is a quasi-emotion, as Kendall Walton argues — then I have to explain why it feels indistinguishable from genuine fear and why we use the same word for both, and I also have to explain what the functional difference consists in. If I reject (3) and say that vivid representation is not sufficient — that belief is also necessary — then I am back to the original paradox and must deny that we feel genuine emotions toward fiction. If I try to split the difference by saying that belief modulates the intensity or character of the emotion without causing it, I need a mechanism for that modulation, and I do not have one. Which step should I give up, and what does giving it up cost?
