I've been following the consciousness debates for a while and I'm getting frustrated in a way that makes me think the problem isn't the arguments but the structure of the disagreement itself. Here's what I mean.

On one side you've got people like Block who draw a sharp distinction between access consciousness (information is globally available for reasoning, report, and the rational control of action) and phenomenal consciousness (there's something it's like). The claim is that these come apart — there could be access without phenomenality and maybe phenomenality without access. On the other side you've got the global workspace people — Baars, Dehaene, and a bunch of others — who basically say: once you've explained how information gets into the global workspace and becomes available for report and reasoning, you've explained consciousness, full stop. There isn't some extra residue left over.

And the thing is, both sides have been saying this for decades now. The GWT side points to all the empirical progress — masking experiments, the attentional blink, the vegetative state work — and says "look, we're making real headway on consciousness." The Block side says "no, you're making headway on access consciousness, and we already knew that was the easy part." The GWT side comes back with: phenomenal consciousness just IS access consciousness, or at least the alleged distinction collapses under scrutiny. Then the other side says that's question-begging.

My working hypothesis is that this isn't a neuroscientific disagreement at all. It's a disagreement about what counts as an explanation. The GWT side thinks that if you've explained the functional role — global availability — you've explained everything there is to explain. The Block side thinks there's a further fact, the what-it's-like-ness, that isn't captured by any functional account, and that this is a conceptual point, not something that another experiment is going to settle.

But I'm not sure. Maybe it's a verbal dispute about the word "consciousness." Maybe it's a genuine empirical bet that one side is losing. Maybe it's a clash of bedrock intuitions where neither side can argue for their starting point. Maybe it's something else entirely.

What I want: a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is, with reasons, and a concrete answer to whether anything — a new experiment, a conceptual distinction, a formal result — could actually move it. And if nothing can, say so honestly and explain why the debate keeps going anyway.
