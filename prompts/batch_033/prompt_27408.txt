My philosophy of mind professor has been doing a unit on Strawson and the reactive attitudes, and she's been handing down verdicts on cases like she's some kind of moral court. I've been keeping track because I'm trying to figure out the principle before she says it outright. Here's what she's given us so far:

- Resentment is fitting toward a colleague who knowingly spreads a false rumor to sabotage your promotion.
- Resentment is *not* fitting toward someone who screams insults at you during a severe psychotic episode — here the objective stance is what's called for.
- Gratitude is fitting toward a stranger who returns your lost wallet with all the cash still inside.
- Resentment is not fitting toward a four-year-old who deliberately smashes your favorite mug — we take the objective stance toward young children.
- Resentment is fitting toward a normally functional adult who, in a flash of anger, says something cruel they immediately regret.
- Resentment is not fitting toward someone given a post-hypnotic suggestion to humiliate you, who has no memory of the event afterward.

Next week we're discussing a case she hasn't touched yet: an adult who grew up in extreme abuse and near-total social isolation — no stable caregivers, no real exposure to moral norms, basically raised in a basement — and who, as an adult, commits a serious assault. They're not psychotic, not a child, not under anyone's direct control. But by any reasonable measure their capacity to grasp moral reasons was severely compromised by their upbringing.

What's her verdict gonna be? I'm less interested in the answer itself than in the rule she seems to be running — is it about the capacity to choose otherwise, about moral competence, about whether the person is inside or outside "the participant stance," or something else entirely? And if you had to cook up one more case that would decisively tell those candidate rules apart, what would it look like?
