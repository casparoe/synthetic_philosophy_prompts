I keep coming across debates about whether a large language model, an insect, or a simulated brain could be the kind of thing that can be wronged, and each side points at a single feature — pain, self-awareness, goal-directed behavior, having a body — and treats it as decisive. Before I read any more arguments, I want the full specification: what would a theory of which computations matter morally have to get right, if the theory is meant to guide how we actually treat the artificial systems we build? Give me the requirements list — the cases it must classify correctly or at least handle without absurdity, the distinctions it must respect (for example, between being the kind of thing that can be wronged at all and how much weight it gets, and between behaving as if something is in pain and its actually feeling anything), and the empirical facts about brains, animals, and computing systems it must stay consistent with. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement. Then flag which requirements are non-negotiable and which are only the preferences of particular camps, say which single requirement does the most to exclude popular answers, and say whether any pair of requirements looks impossible to satisfy together. Take a definite position: give me your actual list and your actual judgments about it, not a survey of options.
