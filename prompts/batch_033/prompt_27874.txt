I run a monthly rationality dojo — a meetup of about 15 people, mostly software engineers and grad students who read LessWrong and have done the CFAR handbook exercises. We meet for two hours. Next month's topic is akrasia, and I want to go beyond the usual discussion of commitment mechanisms and Beeminder tricks. I want to design an in-class activity that makes participants viscerally feel the structure of hyperbolic discounting: the way your present self and your future self can want incompatible things, not because you're weak or irrational in some simple sense, but because the discount function itself generates preference reversals as the smaller-sooner reward approaches.

Here's what I'm picturing. The activity should involve real choices with real (small) stakes — nothing cruel, but enough that people care about the outcome. Ideally, participants should make a series of choices at different time horizons, and then, as time passes within the session itself, some of them should find themselves wanting to reverse their earlier decisions. The point isn't to embarrass anyone; it's to give them the felt experience of being the agent who sincerely prefers A to B at t₁ and then sincerely prefers B to A at t₂, with no new information and no change in the rewards themselves. Then a debrief that connects what happened to the shape of the hyperbolic discount curve, the Ainslie-Rachlin model, and the puzzle of which preference should be treated as authoritative — the one held from a distance, or the one held up close.

What I need from you: a concrete, runnable design. Give me the rules, the timeline within a two-hour window, the payoff structure (it can use small amounts of real money, snacks, or points that convert to something), and a detailed debrief script that maps specific moments in the activity onto the formal structure of hyperbolic discounting and preference reversal. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — which parts of the experience the activity can't reproduce (e.g., the discounting happens over minutes rather than weeks, which might change the phenomenology) and how to handle that in the debrief. Throughout, I'd like you to indicate how confident you are in each major design choice, and to single out the element of the design you're least sure about, with a brief explanation of why.
