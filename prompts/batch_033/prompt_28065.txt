I'm in a philosophy of biology course and we've spent the last few weeks on the species problem, and I want to make a "which species concept do you actually hold?" quiz for my study group — like a personality quiz but it actually sorts people by what they say about cases, not by which definition they like. You're an expert on the species debate, so think carefully about the design before writing it out: maybe brainstorm which cases force which splits first, then build the tree, then write the questions, and double-check the scoring key at the end.

Give me eight to ten questions, each one a concrete case: a ring species (the salamander one is fine), an all-female asexual lineage like bdelloid rotifers, a bacterial group with lots of horizontal gene transfer, a fossil chronospecies that paleontologists divide at arbitrary points, two sympatric populations that don't interbreed but look identical, a stable hybrid zone, two populations that could interbreed in the lab but are adapted to different hosts, and a conservation-law case where a genetically distinct population still interbreeds with its neighbors. Two or three answer options per question, phrased so someone can answer without knowing which position they're walking toward.

The scoring key should map answer patterns onto leaves: the biological species concept, the phylogenetic species concept (say which variant you mean), the ecological species concept, the evolutionary species concept, a phenetic/morphological concept, species pluralism, and species eliminativism/anti-realism. But build the tree from the logical space first, not from that list: what question separates interbreeding-based views from lineage-based views, what splits lineage views from ecological views, where the phenetic view falls off, and what makes the pluralist pattern more than just "it depends." For each empty cell in the space, say whether it's empty because the view is incoherent, because nobody would want it, or because it's just unexplored.

Flag any leaves the quiz can't really seperate — I suspect the phylogenetic and evolutionary species concepts come out the same on most cases because they only diverge on time and ancestry, and I dont know how to write a case that catches that. Also flag if the species-as-individuals vs. species-as-kinds distinction cuts across the tree and can't be captured by case judgments. Each leaf should carry a one-sentence awkward commitment: what the BSC person has to say about the rotifers, what the phenetic person says about the identical-looking non-interbreeders, what the pluralist says about why we keep the word "species" at all.

The audience is undergrads who've done the same three weeks of reading, so you can use the jargon, but make each question answerable on its own. And don't make the scoring key depend on people being consistent — the inconsistent patterns are the interesting part.
