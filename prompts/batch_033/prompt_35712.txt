I'm a PhD student in moral philosophy, working on the ethics of food production. In a recent seminar, a colleague presented what they called the 'symmetry argument' against ethical veganism. It runs roughly as follows: producing plant-based foods also kills sentient animals — combine harvesters shred field mice, pesticides poison insects, ploughing destroys burrows — so the vegan who believes they have opted out of killing animals for food is simply mistaken. The colleague then extended the point: if what matters is the number of animal deaths per calorie, then eating beef (where one animal provides many meals) might actually be morally preferable to eating bread or tofu from large-scale arable farming (where many small animals die per hectare). The argument treats the two kinds of production — animal agriculture and arable farming — as analogous in the morally relevant respects, and concludes that the usual ethical distinction between them collapses under scrutiny.

I find this frustrating but I am struggling to articulate exactly why. It seems clear to me that the analogy is doing real argumentative work: it imports features of one domain into the other without justification, and much of the conclusion depends on the analogy holding. I would like a systematic treatment rather than a gut reaction. Please audit the analogy: lay out a table of correspondences (what is being mapped to what), identify where the mapping breaks, and distinguish the disanalogies that are morally irrelevant from those that genuinely undermine the conclusion. I am particularly interested in whether the 'beef versus bread' extension of the argument survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether there is a better analogy that captures the moral relationship between the two kinds of harm without begging the question either way.
