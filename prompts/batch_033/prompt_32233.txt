I'm a novelist, and I've been trying to work out what I actually think about moral motivation because it shows up in every character I write. I read Williams's "one thought too many" argument a while back and its been gnawing at me. Here's where I've landed, and I want to know if the set is consistent:

(a) I think Williams is right that if you save your spouse from a fire and your motivating thought is "this is morally permissible" or "this is what a good person would do," you've had one thought too many. The right motive is just that it's them.

(b) But I also think that if a character literally cannot articulate why they acted — if they have no reflective access to their reasons — that's a defect. Not a moral defect necessarily, but a defect in self-understanding. I write characters who are inarticulate about their motives as somehow less than fully human.

(c) I think there's something admirable about the person who acts from principle even when inclination fails them — the person who visits the difficult relative not because they feel like it but because they think it's right. I've written characters like this and meant them to be admirable.

(d) Yet I also think, with Williams, that a life organized entirely around impartial moral principles is a life estranged from itself. My most alive characters are the ones whose ground projects are personal, not moral.

(e) I'm drawn to particularism: I don't think there's a finite set of principles that tells you what to do in every case. Moral judgment is a kind of perception, and I try to write characters who have it rather than characters who apply rules.

So here's my worry: (b) and (c) seem to push toward wanting characters to have principles, but (a) and (d) and (e) seem to push against the idea that principles should be doing the motivational work. Is this a real tension or does it dissolve once you distinguish different roles principles can play? And if it is a real tension, which commitment is doing the most damage and what's the cheapest fix?
