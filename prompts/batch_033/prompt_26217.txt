At a recent consciousness-studies conference, I found myself standing between two researchers who were debating the color phi phenomenon. (Quick background: when a red dot is flashed briefly and then a green dot is flashed nearby, subjects report seeing a single dot that moves across and changes from red to green mid-trajectory. The puzzle is that the color change seems to be experienced before the green dot could have been processed.) Their argument went as follows:

(1) "The Orwellian account is clearly right," the first said. "Your visual system initially represents two stationary dots, but after the second one registers, memory edits the record. You end up with a false memory of a moving, color-changing dot. It's retrospective falsification."

(2) The second shook her head. "That multiplies entities. The Stalinesque account is simpler: consciousness runs on a delay. The brain waits, gathers both stimuli, and only then constructs the single conscious percept. There's no overwriting because there was never an initial experience of two separate dots."

(3) "But a delay that long would be evolutionarily disastrous," the first replied. "If consciousness lags 200 milliseconds behind the world, we'd be swatting at where flies were. The Orwellian account lets consciousness track the world in real time and only revises in unusual cases."

(4) "Fair, but the Orwellian story still needs revision machinery operating after the fact, which takes time too. And here's the deeper problem: on your account, the original experience is gone and only the false memory remains. So we can never, even in principle, detect the original. That makes it an empty posit."

(5) "Not empty — it's the best explanation of the data. And your Stalinesque delay faces the same problem in reverse: if the delay is real, what happens to stimuli arriving during the delay window? You'd need a buffer, and that buffer starts to look a lot like… a brief conscious experience that gets revised."

They were pulled into a panel before I could hear the resolution. Could you adjudicate this exchange move by move? Rate each of the five moves on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and briefly justify each rating. Also, say how confident you are in your overall assessment and single out the point you're least sure about.
