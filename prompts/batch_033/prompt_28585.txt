I'm putting together a handout for an epistemology seminar on peer disagreement, and I want it to work as a decision tree — the kind of thing where a student starts from a concrete case, answers a series of questions about what they think should happen, and ends up at a named position. The starting case: you and a colleague are both competent statisticians who've independently analyzed the same dataset from a clinical trial. You've both been careful. You conclude the drug is effective; she concludes it isn't. You have no independent reason to think either of you made a mistake, was sloppy, or missed something. As far as you can tell, this is a genuine peer disagreement. The tree should branch from there based on what students think you ought to do with your belief. I want the leaves to include at minimum the Equal Weight View, the Right Reasons view, at least one moderate conciliationist position, and at least one view that challenges whether the whole idea of "peer disagreement" as a special epistemic category makes sense. Phrase every branching question in terms of the case or a natural variant of it — never in terms of theoretical labels. Flag any positions the tree can't separate and any logically possible combinations of answers that nobody actually defends, with a sentence on why the spot is empty. And if you think a decision tree is the wrong format for this material — if it forces dichotomies where the real views are matters of degree, or hides distinctions that matter more than the ones the tree captures — just tell me that and suggest what would work better. Write it as continuous prose, no headings or bullet points, like you're emailing me.
