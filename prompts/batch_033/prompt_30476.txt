I'm a retired physicist — solid state, mostly, so I'm not claiming any expertise in foundations — and since I stopped working I've been reading philosophy of science on the side. Lately I've gotten stuck on Wigner's old puzzle about the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics, and a former collegue talked me into an argument I can't find the exit from. It seems to prove way too much, but each step looked fine when he said it. Here it is.

(1) The laws of physics, as we actually write them, are pieces of mathematics — differential equations, symmetry groups, Hilbert spaces.

(2) Wigner's puzzle is that mathematics developed for its own internal reasons, with no thought of application, later turns out to be exactly what physics needs, to ridiculous precision.

(3) If the world were not itself mathematical, this fit would be a standing miracle. The only non-miraculous explanations on offer are: (a) the world just is a mathematical object, or (b) the fit is a selection effect.

(4) Take (a). If the world is a mathematical object, then what makes the actual world actual rather than some other mathematical structure can't be a physical fact — there are no non-mathematical facts left to do the job.

(5) So no mathematical structure is priviledged over any other; there's no reason for this one to be actual and not another.

(6) But if all mathematical structures are on a par, the only stable view is that they're all realized — a world for every consistent structure.

(7) And if all of them are realized, then our finding ourselves in this one is just a selection effect: we couldn't find ourselves in a world that couldn't contain us.

(8) So (a) collapses into (b).

(9) But (b), applied honestly, doesn't stop at Wigner's examples. Any theory's predictive success can always be re-described as our having selected, from all the theories people tried, the ones that happened to fit.

(10) So predictive success can never be evidence that a theory is true, and the whole empirical method is bankrupt.

Now, I lived by (1) and (2) for decades and I'd rather not give them up. But I can't tell whether the trouble starts at (3), or in the slide from (4) to (6), or at (9), and I don't know what I'd have to pay to reject whichever one it is. Where does this argument go wrong? And if it doesn't go wrong, which premise is the least costly to give up?
