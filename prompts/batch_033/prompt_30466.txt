A freind sent me Lewis Carroll's story with Achilles and the tortoise and it's been bugging me for weeks. I'm a software engineer and I use logic all day in a loose sense — type systems, proof assistants, rewriting rules — but this makes me feel like I don't understand what a rule of inference actually is. Let me lay out the argument as I understand it.

(1) Suppose I accept two premises: P, and if P then Q.
(2) To be justified in moving to Q, I need something that licenses the move.
(3) The only thing that can license a move is a further premise, so I add: if P and if-P-then-Q, then Q.
(4) But now I have three premises and I still haven't gotten to Q. The same demand applies again, so I need: if P and if-P-then-Q and (if P and if-P-then-Q, then Q), then Q.
(5) This regress never bottoms out, so Q never gets derived.
(6) Therefore no deductive inference is ever justified.

That conclusion is nuts — I typechecked a proof in Lean this morning, and the function-application rule in a typed language feels like exactly the same move as modus ponens. Surely that inference was justified. So either one of the steps is bad, or I have to accept something wild about deduction. Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it force me to say about what a rule of inference is? If the regress is real, what exactly does it show about whether deduction can be justified without circularity?
