I've been reporting a piece on contemporary metaphysics — specifically the debate over whether ordinary objects like tables and chairs actually exist, or whether we live in a world of simples (particles, or whatever the bottom level turns out to be) that are just arranged in useful patterns. I've spent a lot of time with philosophers who call themselves mereological nihilists, and I've noticed something that keeps bugging me, and I don't have a word for it.

The pattern is this. In the seminar room, they're absolutely clear: there are no tables. There are no chairs. There are simples arranged table-wise and chair-wise, and that's all. They'll defend this against all comers. Then we go to the pub, and they ask me to pass the peanuts, and I say "I thought there were no peanuts, only simples arranged peanut-wise," and they roll their eyes and say it's just a convenient shorthand, like when an astronomer says the sun sets. But the astronomer's shorthand is parasitic on a shared understanding of the real model — everyone knows the earth rotates, and "sunset" is a harmless façon de parler. In the nihilist's case, the "real model" is one in which there are no peanuts, no pint glasses, no bar stools, and no people to sit on them. If you actually took that model seriously, you'd expect it to show up somewhere in how you navigate the world — not that you'd walk into tables, but that you'd at least register some kind of tension, some difference in attitude. But there isn't any. The ordinary talk flows as naturally as anyone else's, and the metaphysics seems to be kept in a hermetically sealed compartment that never touches lived experience.

I've seen versions of this gap in other domains — people who say the self is an illusion but get genuinely angry when slighted, people who say colours aren't real but then spend an hour choosing a paint colour — but the mereological case feels especially clean, because the claim is so stark (literally no composite objects) and the practical engagement so complete.

So my question is: does this specific phenomenon have a name? Not just "fictionalism" — that's a whole position one can adopt, and I'm asking about the observable gap between the position and the practise. I want to know whether philosophers have identified and labelled this precise configuration, and if so, how they distinguish it from ordinary inconsistency, from harmless shorthand, and from the kind of register-shifting we all do between, say, talking about "the sun rising" and talking astronomy. And if there isn't a name, what would you call it, and what are its boundaries — at what point does a gap between professed belief and linguistic practice become a problem rather than just a sensible division of labour?
