I recently read a piece that summarized Julian Savulescu's principle of procreative beneficence like this:

Savulescu argues that when parents are using IVF and have multiple embryos to choose from, they are morally obligated to select the one expected to have the best life, based on whatever genetic information is available. In practice, this means screening for genes associated with higher intelligence, better health, and other traits conducive to well-being, and implanting the embryo that scores best. The reasoning is straightforward: since a choice among embryos is already being made, beneficence requires making the choice that gives the future child the strongest prospects. Savulescu calls this "liberal eugenics" because it operates through individual choice rather than state coercion, but the underlying logic — that we have a duty to improve the human gene pool one reproductive decision at a time — is ultimately the same.

Something about this summary strikes me as off, but I haven't read the original paper and I can't tell whether the problems are in Savulescu's view or in how it's being presented. Could you go through this characterization and tell me what a defender of procreative beneficence would accept, what they would push back on, and what is just wrong? I'm especially interested in whether the slide from "select for expected welfare" to "screen for intelligence and health" is faithful to the principle, and whether the closing claim about improving the gene pool is something Savulescu would endorse or a misreading.
