I direct assessment for a state emergency medical services authority that licenses EMTs. Twice a year, a committee of working paramedics and emergency nurses reviews every exam item and skill station to decide whether the license exam actually tests what it claims to test, and the committee's classifications go to the state board for sign-off. We are under pressure from the legislature to cut the cost of the practical examination, and the obvious cost-saving move is to replace hands-on skill stations with written items that nominally cover the same content. I need a reusable decision procedure, grounded in the philosophical distinction between knowing how and knowing that, for deciding when a written item can legitimately stand in for a performance assessment and when it cannot.

The committee has no background in philosophy, meets for two days, and can spend about fifteen minutes per item. The procedure must fit in a short manual and must produce a record that would hold up if a candidate who passed the written exam but later harmed a patient challenged the exam's validity. Please write the procedure as a set of questions to ask in order, with an account of what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with "this competency requires a performance assessment," where it stops with "a written item is sufficient," and where it hands the decision to a human examiner because the distinction alone does not settle the matter. Do not let the exam format settle the question: some written items come close to testing execution, and some hands-on stations only test whether the candidate can recite the steps aloud. The procedure should handle the hard cases, including a written item that requires the candidate to reason stepwise through a procedure as if performing it, a hands-on station whose scoring rubric rewards saying the right thing rather than doing the right thing, and a competency that is a matter of judgment under conditions too varied to specify in advance. Be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, about where the procedure is likely to misfire, and about where the philosophical literature itself divides, for example over whether knowing how is a distinct kind of knowledge or a species of knowing that, and what difference each side makes to the procedure.

Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) a written item that asks the candidate to put the steps of applying a tourniquet in the correct order; (2) a written scenario item about triaging a mass-casualty incident; (3) a hands-on station in which the candidate must ventilate a patient with a bag-valve mask on a manikin while an examiner scores the seal, rate, and chest rise, and the candidate is not asked to explain anything. Include at least one result the board will not like. Please write the answer in American English.
