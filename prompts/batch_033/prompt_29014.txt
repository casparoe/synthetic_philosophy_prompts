Last month my business partner and I got into a disagreement that has begun to feel less like a practical matter and more like a puzzle about how we talk about objects. We run a small glassblowing studio and ship delicate pieces across the country. Each piece is fragile, but we wrap it in custom-cut foam so that, while packed, you can drop the box onto concrete and the glass will not break. My partner says that once a piece is packed, it is simply no longer fragile: "fragile" describes how the thing behaves, and packed in foam it does not break, so it is not fragile. I say that is wrong. The glass is still fragile; it is just being protected. If you unwrapped it and dropped it, it would shatter. He replies that I am changing the circumstances, and that "fragile" only means something relative to a set of circumstances. A customer recently asked whether our pieces are fragile, and I found I could not answer without telling a long story, which made me realize we have no settled view.

We now have to resolve this, because we are writing product descriptions for our catalog and our insurance carrier takes the position that the "fragile" surcharge should not apply to pieces that are always shipped in protective packaging. I would like you to help me think through who is right in this argument, and more generally what it means to say that a thing has a property like fragility even when that property never actually shows up in the circumstances the thing is in. I am not trained in philosophy, so I would appreciate an explanation I can use with my partner and with an insurance adjuster.
