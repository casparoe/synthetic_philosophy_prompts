I just spent three hours arguing with a friend about the Lucas–Penrose anti-mechanist arguments, and he kept pushing me to write down exactly what I believe so he could find the weak point. I've done that below, but before I send it to him I'd like a neutral expert to go through it first. I'm not asking who is right in the broader debate — I want to know whether my own position hangs together internally, or whether I've papered over a crack I didn't notice in the heat of conversation. The argument got tangled enough that I'm no longer confident I can see the architecture of my own view.

Here is where I stand after the argument, set out as clearly as I can:

(1) I accept Gödel's first incompleteness theorem in its standard form: any consistent, recursively axiomatizable formal system that contains enough arithmetic to represent primitive recursive functions has a Gödel sentence — a true sentence of arithmetic that the system itself cannot prove.

(2) I find Lucas's 1961 argument persuasive at its core: for any such formal system F, a human mathematician can, by reasoning about F from outside, see that F's Gödel sentence is true. Therefore the human mind's mathematical capacities cannot be captured by any formal system — we are not Turing machines, and mechanism about the mind is false.

(3) I hold a straightforward physicalism: mental processes are brain processes, and the brain is a physical system governed by ordinary physics. I don't think there is any non-physical substance or property involved in cognition.

(4) I accept the physical Church–Turing thesis — sometimes called the Church–Turing–Deutsch principle — according to which any physical process can be simulated by a Turing machine. I take this to be a well-confirmed empirical claim about the physical world, not a logical thesis.

(5) I think Penrose's later appeal to quantum gravity and objective reduction in Shadows of the Mind is unnecessary and probably a red herring. Lucas's original point, I believe, stands on its own without needing exotic physics or a revision of quantum mechanics.

(6) I am unimpressed by the standard reply that we don't know the human mind is consistent, and that Lucas's argument therefore fails because the Gödel sentence for an inconsistent system is not a truth we can recognise. Even granting that human mathematicians sometimes make mistakes, our capacity to recognise and correct errors — to reflect on our own reasoning and revise it — is itself a capacity that no formal system possesses in the same self-directed way. So the consistency objection doesn't undermine Lucas's core insight.

(7) I also think the widely discussed reply from Putnam, Bowie, and others — that Lucas only shows that for each formal system there is some sentence a human can prove that the system cannot, and that this is equally true of one Turing machine relative to another — misses the point. That reply, as I understand it, illicitly treats "the human mind" as a different system at each step of the argument, whereas Lucas's argument concerns a single fixed reasoner.

Please approach this as a professional philosopher of mind and logic who is comfortable with the technical details of the incompleteness theorems and the decades of literature they have generated. I want a consistency audit, not a lecture on who is right in the Lucas–Penrose debate. Specifically: go through these seven commitments and identify which pairs are genuinely in tension, which pairs only look like they are in tension but dissolve on closer inspection, and which single revision would do the most to restore overall coherence. Rank the tensions by severity, explain what each would cost to resolve, and flag any commitment that turns out to be ambiguous between two readings in a way that matters for whether the whole set hangs together. If some tension I haven't noticed is more serious than the obvious ones, I especially want to hear about that.
