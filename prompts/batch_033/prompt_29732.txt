I am giving a departmental talk next month in which I defend the view that shared intention is neither a primitive we-intention lodged in any individual's head nor a joint commitment that stands above the individuals, but is fully constituted by interlocking individual intentions, meshing subplans, and common knowledge among the participants, with no sui generis group-level normativity added on top. My positive claim is that once one has specified what each agent intends, what each believes about the others' intentions and plans, and what is common knowledge between them, one has specified everything there is to shared intention; the sense that a partner who abandons a joint project is answerable to the other is, on my view, explained downstream by ordinary moral and promissory considerations rather than by a distinctive class of joint commitments. My argumentative strategy is to walk through the standard examples, walking together, painting a house together, playing a duet, and argue that my configuration of individual attitudes plus common knowledge captures everything the collectivist insists requires a we-intention or a joint commitment, and then to show that the residual normativity can be accounted for without special collective entities.

Last night a friend and I argued about this for two hours. She works on Gilbert and kept pressing the walking-together case: she says my account predicts that when one walker unilaterally peels off, the other is merely surprised or disappointed, whereas in fact the other is entitled to rebuke them, and that entitlement is evidence of a joint commitment I have not acknowledged. I had replies, but by the end I had conceded more than I meant to, and it struck me that the same pressure will come at the talk from people who know this literature better than she does. The audience is a philosophy department with a strong action-theory group: two people who work on planning theories of intention in the Bratmanian tradition and will be sympathetic but will press on whether common knowledge can bear the weight I assign it; one Searlean who holds that collective intentionality is biologically primitive and that we-intentions are irreducible, and who will say my account leaves out the doing-it-together; one social ontologist in the Gilbertian tradition who will insist that the obligation to a co-walker is exactly the kind of thing a joint commitment explains; a Wittgensteinian who is suspicious of the entire project of analyzing shared mental states and will ask what the analysis is for; and a decision theorist who will want to know whether common knowledge as I use it is strong enough, whether I need something like team reasoning, and what my view says when the participants' subplans mesh only by luck.

Please prepare my Q&A prep sheet. I want the objections ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way this particular audience would phrase them, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is softest. Tell me what to concede up front and what to hold. Identify the one reply that has to land for the talk to survive, and include the question I am hoping nobody asks.
