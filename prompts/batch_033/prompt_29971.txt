ok so i've been noticing this thing in everyday moral life and i can't figure out if it has a name. it goes like this. someone starts doing something that's clearly above and beyond — like they're the only person in the friend group who always brings food when someone's sick, or the coworker who quietly fixes other people's mistakes without being asked. at first people say it's generous, saintly, whatever. but give it a year, and the same people start relying on it so completely that if the person stops — or even scales back — they get genuinely resentful, like the person is now failing at something they owe. the weird part is that the expectation doesn't generalize: nobody thinks the new person in the group should be doing it. it's a one-person duty, created entirely by that person's own track record of going above and beyond.

i know there's stuff about how supererogatory acts can become obligatory if they're cheap for you or if someone's life depends on it. and there's stuff about how we resent moral saints because they make us feel bad. but what i'm trying to isolate is narrower: the process by which one person's repeated supererogatory behavior creates a person-specific, asymmetric expectation, and the resentment that follows when they violate it. it's not quite that the community silently changed the norm for everyone — they didn't. and it's not that the person promised or took on a role. it's something about precedent and reliance.

does this have an established name in the supererogation or moral psychology literature? and if it doesn't, can you carve it off from nearby things like role obligations, promissory obligation, baseline shift, and whatever phenomenon explains why saints are annoying? i'm less interested in the label and more in what exactly the phenomenon is and where its boundaries are.
