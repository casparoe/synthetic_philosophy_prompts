Consider two familiar institutional procedures. A university with rolling admissions reads applications in the order they arrive and admits any applicant whose file clears a set of posted thresholds — a grade floor, certain courses completed, a qualifying test score — without waiting to see the rest of the applicant pool. An employer announces that a position will remain open until a candidate meeting the minimum qualifications is found; the first such candidate is offered the job and the search stops. Both institutions also say, in their public-facing materials, that they aim to enroll the strongest possible class or to hire the best available candidate. What theory of practical rationality, if any, does this pair of stopping rules presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit account of good decision-making that would make the practice more than an administrative shortcut. In particular, does the practice presuppose that a rational agent should satisfice — set an aspiration level and accept the first option that meets it — rather than maximize expected value, and does it presuppose a picture of the agent as boundedly rational, limited in time, attention, and computational capacity? Or is the practice fully consistent with a maximizing account once the costs of continuing to search and of delaying a decision are themselves counted as real costs? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and mark the places where it is philosophically underdetermined: where a maximizer, a satisficer, and someone who takes the operative value to be procedural fairness or predictability could each endorse the same written procedure for different reasons. Finally, say what would have to change in how such institutions write and justify their procedures if they replaced this implicit account with a strictly optimizing rule that required them to identify and select the best member of the applicant pool.
