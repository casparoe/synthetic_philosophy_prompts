I keep noticing a thing in the way people talk about artificial intelligence, and I can't tell if it has a name or if I'm just making a category mistake. The clearest examples come from old-style symbolic AI systems, like the ones that do analogy puzzles with letters. Take a system like Copycat, the one Hofstadter and Mitchell worked on. It sees "abc is to abd" and has to answer what "xyz" should be changed into. The interesting thing isn't that it sometimes says "wyz" while other times says "xyd" — it's that the system itself treats the concepts involved as malleable. For one run, "successor" slips toward "predecessor" because the letters at the end of the alphabet activate that idea. In another run, "replace the rightmost letter by its successor" stays rigid, and it goes with "xyd." The concept of "same position in the alphabet" bends, and so does "same direction of change."

What I'm asking about is the underlying idea that a concept can be stretched, partly overwritten, or blended with a nearby concept under pressure from context, and that this isn't a malfunction but part of how the system produces plausible analogies. The system has a concept like "successor," but under the right pressures, it gets used as if it meant "predecessor," or as if "last letter" meant "first letter" when the string is presented as a set rather than a sequence. The concepts have a kind of fluidity: they retain their usual shape in easy cases, and they shift in hard ones.

Now, the thing is, I've also seen people use this same language about human concepts. When someone sees a child's drawing and says "that's a dog," the concept "dog" isn't being applied by checking a list of necessary and sufficient features. The features themselves flex: "four legs" can alter its weight, "furry" can be overlooked, "has a tail" can be inferred and filled in. And when a biologist squints at a fossil and calls it a bird, some original notion of "bird" has had to give way or slip. In all these cases, the concept isn't just being used more or less carefully; it is temporarily reshaped, and the reshaped concept can be the correct thing to think with, not an error.

I want to know if there is an established term for this phenomenon as a general feature of concepts, not just for the specific mechanism in one computer program. I can think of nearby ideas: Lakoff and Johnson's conceptual metaphor says concepts are structured by other concepts, but the examples I'm describing are within a single domain — "successor" slipping to "predecessor" in letter strings, "dog" stretching to include a drawn dog. I've heard the phrase "conceptual slippage" but I can't tell if that's a term of art or just a loose description the Copycat people used. Is there a name for the view that this tendency, for concepts to slip under contextual pressure, is constitutive of concepts rather than a limitation to be corrected? And if it has no name, what would you call it, and what would the boundaries be: when is a slip a legitimate reapplication, when is it a plain mistake, and is there a principled difference between the two, or just a smooth gradient?

I'm not after a bare name. I want the carving. If the nearest established concepts are "conceptual metaphor," "family resemblance," "prototype effects," and "categorisation under pressure," then I want to know exactly how this differs from each — for instance, whether conceptual metaphor is just one species of it, and whether family resemblance describes the result but not the process. And if the term "conceptual slippage" is actually used in the literature, tell me whether it is defined narrowly enough to exclude ordinary category extension, or whether it covers the full range of cases from dog-like drawings to fossils.
