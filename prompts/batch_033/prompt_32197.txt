There is a familiar pattern in the law and in hospital policy that I find puzzling. A competent patient may refuse life-sustaining treatment — a ventilator, say — and the medical team is generally required to honor that refusal, even though death will follow. If a ventilator is already in place, a physician may withdraw it at the patient's or family's request; the death is then attributed to the underlying disease, and the physician's pulling of the tube is not treated as a killing. A physician who writes a do-not-resuscitate order and stands by during a cardiac arrest is practicing within the standard of care. Yet in most jurisdictions, if that same physician were to administer a lethal injection, or in many jurisdictions merely prescribe a lethal dose for the patient to take, the act would be treated as a crime or as professional misconduct. Similarly, in the common-law tradition a bystander who watches a stranger drown is generally under no legal duty to rescue, while a bystander who pushes someone into the water is criminally liable. But withdrawing a ventilator is an act, not a mere omission: a hand moves, a tube comes out, and the patient dies sooner than they otherwise would have. So the practice seems to draw a line, but it is not obvious where the line falls or what it tracks.

What view of the difference between doing and allowing — or between killing and letting die — does this body of practice presuppose? Is it best explained by the idea that omissions are less culpable than actions, by the idea that patients have a right to refuse and that treatment is only permissible while consented to, by the idea that death must be traceable to the underlying disease rather than to a new lethal intervention, or by some other principle? Does the practice strictly require a doing/allowing distinction, or could it be fully explained without granting that distinction any fundamental moral weight? Is the practice coherent, or does it paper over an inconsistency? Finally, what would have to change in law or hospital policy if the doing/allowing distinction were abandoned?
