I host a small philosophy podcast, and I am preparing an episode in which I imagine interviewing Jean-Jacques Rousseau about his Letter to d'Alembert on the Theater. I have read the Letter carefully, and I am struck by how many tensions it seems to contain — tensions that do not feel like oversights but like fault lines running through the whole argument.

I need eight to ten interview questions that push Rousseau on these tensions rather than merely inviting him to restate his position. I want to press on at least the following clusters:

First, Rousseau wrote plays and operas himself — Le Devin du village was a success — yet he argues that theater is morally corrupting and that a virtuous republic should prohibit it. He must have anticipated this objection. I want a question that forces him to explain whether his own dramatic work is exempt from his critique, and if so on what grounds, with a follow-up that presses him on whether the distinction he draws can be applied by anyone other than himself.

Second, his preferred alternative to theater is the public festival — the open-air celebration in which citizens become the spectacle themselves. But a festival is also a kind of performance, and it seems to manipulate emotion just as theater does. I want a question that asks him to articulate the difference in a way that does not merely restate the conclusion, and a follow-up that asks whether a well-designed civic festival could not drift into the very vices he attributes to theater.

Third, Rousseau's broader political philosophy insists that human beings are transformed by institutions — that the move from the state of nature to civil society changes our very nature. Does this not open the door to the possibility that theater, under the right institutional conditions, could be part of that transformation rather than an obstacle to it? And if he denies this, what is it about theater specifically that makes it uniquely resistant to being put to civic use?

I would also like questions that press him on the empirical side: his argument depends heavily on claims about the psychological effects of spectacle — that spectators identify with characters in ways that weaken their moral judgment, that the pleasure of pity in the theater substitutes for real compassion. Are these claims he thinks can be settled from the armchair, or would he accept that they are in principle testable? And if evidence went the other way, which part of his argument would give way first?

Finally, I want at least one question about whether his critique of theater applies to his own philosophical writing. The Letter is itself a kind of performance — it is artful, it dramatizes, it aims to move its readers. If all performance is suspect, is the Letter suspect too? And if not, what makes it different?

For each question, include a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give, and try to make the questions build on one another so that the interview has a narrative arc.
