In the Hart–Devlin debate, the case for legal moralism is often compressed into a single analogy: a society's shared morality is to the society what its basic political order is to the state, so private immorality stands to that moral order as treason stands to the political order, and the criminal law may enforce morality as a matter of society's self-preservation. I keep seeing the analogy used to license the conclusion that the state may criminalize conduct solely because it is widely and sincerely condemned, without any showing of harm. Before I read another argument resting on it, I want it audited. Start with a correspondence table: in the target — morality and the criminal law — what is supposed to play the role of the constitution or basic order, the state, the traitor, the act of subversion, the harm to be prevented, and the right of self-preservation? Then work through the places the mapping breaks. In particular: Is a society held together by a shared morality in anything like the way a state is held together by enforceable allegiance, or does the analogy assume the very thing at issue? Is moral disagreement a threat of the same kind as insurrection, or a normal and survivable feature of a pluralist society, and what would settle that? In the treason case the harm is independent of the wrongness of disloyalty; in the immorality case, is there any harm that is not just the wrongness itself or the offense people take at it? And does the analogy smuggle in a picture of society as an organism or a single moral agent, such that any breach is a wound to the whole? Then give your verdict: does the legal-moralist conclusion require the analogy, or can it be restated as an ordinary harm-based argument — and if it is restated that way, is it still legal moralism or a different position?
