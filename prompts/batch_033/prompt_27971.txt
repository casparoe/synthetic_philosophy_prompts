I'm a science journalist and I've been asked to run a 90-minute workshop at a public ideas festival. The topic is epistemic dependence on AI — specifically the worry that as we offload more thinking to AI tools, we lose the understanding that comes from working through problems ourselves, and we might not even notice it's gone because the outputs still look fine.

I want an activity that makes participants *feel* this erosion in real time. Here's what I'm picturing: something where small groups have to solve a multi-step reasoning problem (maybe a medical diagnosis, or a legal argument, or an engineering failure analysis — something with interdependent steps where understanding *why* step 3 follows from step 2 matters). At first they do all the reasoning themselves. Then in subsequent rounds, they're allowed to hand off sub-tasks to an "oracle" (played by another group or by a facilitator with a script) who gives correct outputs but no explanations. By the final round, they're just stitching oracle outputs together, and then I hit them with a surprise twist — the original problem changes slightly, or they're asked to explain thier reasoning to someone else, and they realize they can't.

No technology allowed — no phones, no laptops. Just people, paper, and rules. About 30 participants, mostly educated laypeople.

I need: the full setup (what problem they're solving, how the oracles work, what materials to prepare), the round-by-round rules, a timeline for 90 minutes, and a debrief that connects what happened to the philosophical literature — especially the distinction between having correct answers and grasping the relationships between them (understanding as "seeing how things hang together"), and the idea that understanding might require cognitive effort rather than just access to outputs.

Also: tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and be honest about which part of the philosophical worry this activity *can't* capture — e.g., the activity has a debrief where people notice their lost understanding, but in real life with AI, one of the worries is precisely that we *don't* notice. How do I address that gap in the discussion?
