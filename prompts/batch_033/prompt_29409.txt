I hold the view that the wrong kind of reasons problem is fatal to the buck-passing account of value — that is, to the view that for something to be good, valuable, or desirable is simply for there to be reasons to take some pro-attitude toward it: to admire it, desire it, respect it, and so on. My reasoning is as follows. The buck-passing account needs to distinguish between reasons that genuinely bear on an object's value and reasons that merely bear on the expedience of having the attitude. The demon who threatens to punish you unless you admire him gives you a reason to admire him, but that reason does not make him admirable. So the account must restrict itself to reasons of the right kind. But every attempt to draw the right-kind/wrong-kind distinction without circularity — whether by appealing to object-given versus state-given reasons, or to reasons that figure in the attitude's correctness conditions versus its pragmatic justification — either presupposes the very notion of value it was meant to replace, or misclassifies clear cases. The distinction cannot be drawn in a way that is both extensionally adequate and non-circular, and without it the buck-passing account overgenerates disastrously. So the project fails.

I want to know what would count against this conclusion. I am not asking for a bibliography of replies; I want a map of the view's exposure. Specifically: what kind of result or argument would decisively show that the WKR problem is not fatal — that the distinction can be drawn after all, or that the failure to draw it does not matter in the way I think it does? What would merely make the problem look harder or more interesting without vindicating the buck-passing account? Are there developments in the theory of reasons — for instance, work on the semantics of reason-ascriptions, or on the metaphysics of weighing explanations — that, if they turned out a certain way, would change the force of the objection? What would a committed defender of the buck-passing account concede counts against their view, and what would they dismiss as a misunderstanding of what the account is trying to do? I also want an honest assessment of whether the buck-passing literature has insulated itself against this objection — whether the distinction between right-kind and wrong-kind reasons has been built into the view in a way that makes it unfalsifiable, and if so, whether that is a problem for a conceptual analysis of this sort, or exactly what we should expect.
