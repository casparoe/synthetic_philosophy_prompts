I have no academic background in philosophy, but I have spent a lot of my free time trying to get clear about panpsychism, and I keep going in circles on the combination problem. By the combination problem I mean the subject version: if the fundamental building blocks of the world each have some small amount of experience, it is hard to see how many distinct experiencing subjects could add up to the single unified subject I am, since experiences do not seem to be the sort of thing that can be stacked or glued like bricks.

I have come across five families of answer, and I would like you to rank them from most promising to least promising as solutions to that problem specifically. Please commit to one definite ordering, with no ties, rather than telling me it depends on how the terms are defined; where a term is ambiguous, choose the reading that gives the view its best chance and say so.

Here is how I understand the five families:

1. The bonding view. Micro-experiences can fuse into a larger unified experience through a special kind of relation, sometimes called phenomenal bonding. This relation is not ordinary physical composition, but it is a real relation among subjects. The combination problem assumes no such relation exists.

2. The emergence view. The basic building blocks have some minimal form of experience, and when they are organized in the right way, a new and unified consciousness arises at the higher level. The fact that we cannot see in any transparent way how the parts produce the whole is not an objection, because emergence just is the appearance of something not fully derivable from the lower-level facts.

3. The proto-phenomenal view. The fundamental building blocks are not conscious at all. They have properties that are neither purely physical nor fully experiential, but that are suited to produce consciousness when combined in the right way. Because there are no micro-subjects, there is no problem of combining subjects in the first place.

4. The universe-first view. Instead of building consciousness up from small parts, start with the whole: the universe as a whole is the one fundamental conscious subject, and ordinary finite subjects such as you or I are limited aspects or derivatives of it. The combination problem is then replaced by a decomposition problem about how one cosmic subject differentiates into many local subjects.

5. The dissolve-the-problem view. The combination problem is an artifact of a bad picture of experiences as small objects that need to be glued together. Once we stop treating experience on the model of physical parts, the demand to explain how they combine no longer arises, so the right response is to dissolve the question rather than solve it.

For each of the five, say in a short paragraph why it lands where it does in your ranking. Then give me a bottom line: which one you would actually bet on as the best place for someone sympathetic to panpsychism to put their effort, and which one you think is closest to a dead end. If I have mischaracterized any view, correct that before ranking it, but still give the ranking.
