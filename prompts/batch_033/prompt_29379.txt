I've been noticing something in how medical conditions get classified and reclassified over time, and I'm wondering if philosophers of medicine have a name for it.

The pattern goes both ways. On the one hand: a condition exists, it causes real suffering, but for decades it's treated as just part of the normal range of how humans vary — bad luck, not a disease. Then someone develops a drug or a procedure that can treat it, and within a generation the same condition is firmly classified as a disease, with diagnostic criteria and insurance codes and everything. The biology didn't change; what changed was that we can now do something about it.

On the other hand: a condition is considered a disease for a long time, but then its prevelance in the population goes way up — say because people are living longer, or because diagnostic thresholds got looser. At some point it starts to feel strange to call something a disease when half the population over 60 has it. So it gets quietly reframed as a "risk factor" or a "normal part of aging," and doctors stop treating it as a disease per se even though the underlying biology is exactly the same as before.

I'm not just asking about medicalization, which I've heard of — that seems to only cover the first direction, and anyway I think it's more of a sociological label than an analysis of what's going on conceptually. What I'm after is whether this two-way drift, where disease status seems to track our practical stance toward a condition (can we fix it? how many people have it?) rather than any biological essence, has been picked apart by philosophers. Does this pattern have a name? And if it doesn't, what would you call it, and how exactly does it bear on the question of whether disease concepts are natural or constructed?
