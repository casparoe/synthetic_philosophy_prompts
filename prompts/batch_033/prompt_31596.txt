I am a retired physical chemist, and since leaving the laboratory I have been reading my way into the philosophy of mathematics. I have just finished working through Skolem's paradox: the downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem guarantees that if ZFC has a model at all, then it has a countable model, and yet inside any such model one can prove the usual theorem that the powerset of the natural numbers is uncountable. In my reading group, when I described this as a striking fact about the expressive limits of first-order logic, another member cut me off with the one-liner "Skolem's paradox shows that countability is relative," and a second member used it to conclude that there is no fact of the matter about whether the real numbers are uncountable — "uncountable" is merely an artifact of the model one happens to be working in.

I suspect that one sentence is being asked to do more work than it can, but I cannot yet say exactly which readings of it are trivial, which are substantive and defensible, and which are false. Could you lay out, as a menu of distinct precise claims, what that slogan might be taken to assert — about the absoluteness of the predicate "is countable," about what "there are uncountably many reals" means from inside versus outside a model, about whether the whole set-theoretic universe could be countable when viewed from outside, and any other theses you think are hiding in it — and then say which of those readings would actually license the conclusion my fellow reader drew? Please also tell me how confident you are in the overall assessment and single out the point you are least sure of.
