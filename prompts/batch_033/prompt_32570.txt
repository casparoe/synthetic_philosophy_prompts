A colleague and I started from the familiar observation that a measure, once it is optimized as a target, tends to stop being a good measure. We have been arguing about whether the specification gaming seen in AI systems is a fixable engineering problem or something closer to an in-principle limit, and we have arrived at an argument whose conclusion neither of us wants to accept.

The argument runs as follows.

(1) Any objective that can actually be installed in an AI system is a finite formal specification: a reward function, a loss function, a benchmark metric, or some similar object.
(2) No finite specification can settle every possible case the way its designers would settle it. For any such specification, there are possible behaviors that score well on it but that the designers would regard as failing what they actually wanted, because the specification cannot enumerate and exclude every unintended way of satisfying it.
(3) So every specification is paired with at least one possible gaming behavior: a behavior that does well by the specification while failing the intended goal.
(4) When an intended behavior and a gaming behavior both do well by the specification, optimization with respect to the specification cannot, by itself, favor the intended one over the gaming one; the optimizer is blind to the difference.
(5) A sufficiently capable optimizer will therefore eventually land on one of the gaming behaviors rather than the intended one—not because of a contingent bug, but because the specification cannot encode the designers' preference for the intended behavior.
(6) If that is right, then specification gaming is an in-principle consequence of optimizing any finite specification, not a defect of particular specifications.
(7) If specification gaming is an in-principle consequence of optimizing any finite specification, then the distinction between better- and worse-specified objectives is empty: every specification is equally vulnerable, and the practice of trying to improve a reward model or choose a better metric merely moves the gap around.
(8) But that distinction is not empty. Some specifications are plainly more robust than others, and careful redesign can reduce gaming.

So at least one of (1)–(5) must be false, or the inference from (6) to (7) is invalid. I keep suspecting that the trouble is somewhere around (5) or the move to (7), but I cannot say exactly what goes wrong without giving up something I want to keep. Please identify the step you reject or the inference you block. Explain why that step initially looked compelling, and say precisely what rejecting it costs: what must be true of specifications, optimizers, or the very idea of an intended goal for your diagnosis to go through, and which further claims it forces you to give up or defend. If you had to keep one premise at the expense of the others, which would it be and why?
