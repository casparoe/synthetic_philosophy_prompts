A friend of mine works in a pain clinic and has very settled views about when a pain is bad for the person feeling it. Her track record, as best I can reconstruct it: a throbbing toothache, bad. Pain asymbolia — the patient can point to the spot and calls it sharp or burning, but genuinely doesn't mind it and doesn't pull away — not bad. A patient who reports an awful, unbearable feeling but can't localize it or describe it as sharp, burning, or aching, just "pure awfulness," and is visibly distressed by it — bad. A half-second pinprick, clearly unpleasant but gone before you can react — bad, though only barely.

She's about to classify two new patients and I want to guess what she'll say. Patient A has the full sensory pain profile — sharp, localized, the lot — and all the usual pain behavior, grimacing and guarding and pulling away, but insists convincingly that it doesn't feel unpleasant and that they don't mind it. Patient B is a marathon runner who says the burn in her legs is intensely unpleasant — awful, actually — but genuinely wants it to continue, because she reads it as proof she's pushing hard enough, and she doesn't avoid it at all.

What should I predict she'll say about A and B, if she's being consistent with her track record? I'm less interested in the verdicts themselves then in the rule she seems to be running, the other rules that fit her track record just as well, and which of these two cases — or what further case — would best tell those rules apart.
