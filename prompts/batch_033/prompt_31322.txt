I've been thinking about taste and experience a good deal lately. Six months ago I had a drawn-out respiratory illness — not COVID, as far as the tests said, but something that dragged on and left me, among other things, with a noticeably altered sense of taste. My morning coffee, same beans I've used for years, same grind and brew, now tastes flatter and faintly metallic. I still drink it, but it isn't what it was. Around the same time I stumbled across Daniel Dennett's essay "Quining Qualia" — specifically his thought experiment about Chase and Sanborn, the two coffee tasters who both stop liking the company blend, one because his standards rose while the taste stayed the same, the other because his taste changed while his standards held steady. The example has colonized my thinking. I find myself unsure what to say about my own coffee, and unsure what that uncertainty means.

Here is what I find myself thinking about a range of cases, my own included. I lack a principle behind these verdicts, and I'd like you to supply one if you can, or at least lay out the candidates and tell me which of my verdicts I can't keep.

**Case A (my case).** The coffee I drink each morning now tastes different to me — flatter, metallic. I am confident this is a change in the experience itself, not merely a change in what I think about it. I didn't become a snob; my tongue went wrong. Verdict: experience changed.

**Case B (the sommelier).** A colleague who trained intensively as a sommelier over five years says that a particular Bordeaux she has drunk annually now tastes richer and more structurally distinct to her than it did when she started. She credits her perceptual learning: she can now discriminate notes she couldn't before. I accept this as a genuine change in her experience. Verdict: experience changed.

**Case C (the barista).** Another friend, a former barista, tells me that the house espresso at his old café tastes exactly the same as it did a decade ago, but he no longer enjoys it — his standards rose after years of tasting exceptional single-origin shots, and he now judges the same flavor profile as dull. I find this fully credible. Verdict: standards changed.

**Case D (the stroke).** My uncle, after a mild stroke, reports that food generally tastes blander — he knows it's a sensory change because he still craves the same dishes and feels disappointed rather than indifferent. I accept this. Verdict: experience changed.

**Case E (the music student).** A musician friend tells me that after ten years of ear training, a certain chord progression she once heard as merely pretty now strikes her as intricate and tense — though she knows the recording is unchanged. Perceptual learning, she says, has genuinely altered how the chords sound. I agree. Verdict: experience changed.

**Case F (the snob).** Another friend insists that a pop album he loved at twenty now sounds shallow and grating to him, but that his auditory experience is identical — he hears the same thing; he has simply become more demanding. I take his word for it. Verdict: standards changed.

**Case G (the undecidable pair).** Now consider Chase and Sanborn as Dennett presents them: two equally experienced tasters, equally articulate, who both just say "I no longer like the coffee." Chase says his standards rose; Sanborn says his taste changed. In this stripped-down scenario, I find I cannot tell from anything they say or do which one is in which situation. More strongly: I suspect that when two people are this symmetrically sincere and this evenly matched in descriptive ability, there may simply be no fact of the matter — the distinction between "experience changed" and "standards changed" is one we project, not one we discover. Verdict: in such a case, the distinction may not track a real difference.

**Case H (my own case, revisited).** When I try to be fully honest about Case A, I realize that to say my coffee tastes different I am comparing a present experience to a memory of a past experience. I have no independent access to the past experience to check the memory against. I am inferring from affect, expectation, and the brute sense that something is missing — which is exactly the kind of data I would use if I were trying to decide whether someone else's standards or their experience had changed. And yet I remain confident in Case A. I can't explain why. Verdict: experience changed, but I am now suspicious of my own confidence.

I think my verdicts on A through G hang together, more or less. H reveals the crack. G says that when two people are symmetric in all the ways I can specify, the Chase/Sanborn distinction may not pick out a real difference; H says that even in my own case, the grounds on which I distinguish experience-change from standards-change are the same kinds of evidence I'd use for someone else, and yet I remain confident. I also notice that E and F are structurally very similar cases (both involve long-term refinement in an aesthetic domain) but I give them opposite verdicts — and I can't say quite what makes the musician's case different from the snob's.

What I want is a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures as many of these verdicts as possible. The principle should specify conditions under which we are warranted in saying "the experience itself changed" rather than "the subject's standards or judgments changed." Please state it precisely, indicate which verdicts motivate which clauses of the principle, and apply it to cases I haven't listed, including ones where the principle yields a surprising or counterintuitive result. Most importantly, tell me honestly which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and whether I should abandon those verdicts. Finally, say what this principle implies for the larger question Dennett's paper raises — whether qualia, understood as intrinsic and immediately apprehensible features of experience, survive scrutiny or deserve to be quined. Please answer in American English.
