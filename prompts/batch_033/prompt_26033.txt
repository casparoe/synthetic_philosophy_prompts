I'm putting together a lecture on money pump arguments and diachronic rationality for my grad seminar, and I realized I should probably check whether my own views on this stuff actualy hang together before I stand up there and say something embarrassing. Here's roughly where I've landed:

(a) Money pump arguments are genuinely normative. They don't just show that certain preference structures would be costly or imprudent; they show that those preference structures are irrational. That's the whole point of them.

(b) The sophisticated chooser response works. If I have cyclical preferences—A over B, B over C, C over A—but I can foresee the full sequence of trades I'd be offered, and I refuse the first trade on that basis, then I'm off the hook. The pump doesn't get me, and that's what matters for the rationality verdict.

(c) Synchronic cyclicality is worse than diachronic cyclicality. If all three pairwise preferences are co-present at a single time, that's a straightforward coherence violation. But if my preferences shift in a cycle over time—say A > B on Monday, B > C on Wednesday, C > A on Friday, driven by changing moods or new information—that's a different kind of thing, and not the same grade of rationality failure.

(d) The normative force of a money pump doesn't depend on there actually being a clever trader waiting to exploit you. What matters is the mere possibility of exploitation: if your preference structure is such that you *could* be pumped, that structural fact alone is the problem.

(e) Rationality doesn't demand unlimited foresight. An agent isn't required to compute arbitrarily long chains of trades to count as rational. There's some reasonable bound on how far ahead you need to think.

I want a committed answer: tell me what you think the least costly revision to this set is, not a menu of options.
