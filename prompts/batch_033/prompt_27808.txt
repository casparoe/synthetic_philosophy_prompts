I chair my city's public art commission and we're in a mess. We review about 30 proposals a year — sculptures, murals, installations for parks and plazas — and our five-person committee (two artists, a city planner, a neighborhood rep, and me, a lawyer) has to say yes or no to each one. The problem is our decisions look totally inconsistent from the outside. We'll approve a weird conceptual piece one month and then reject something equally weird the next, and I can't articulate what principle we're using because I'm not sure we have one.

I read Hume's "Of the Standard of Taste" and it seems like he's got the bones of a solution: there IS a standard, it's just not a simple rule, it's grounded in the joint verdict of people who've developed certain qualities — delicacy of taste, practice, comparison, freedom from prejudice, good sense. But I need someone to turn this into an actual procedure my committee can run. What questions do we ask, in what order? What counts as a sufficient answer to each? Where does the procedure stop and say "this is just a judgment call, own it"? The constraints: we have two hours per meeting, we're looking at renderings and artist statements (not the finished work), and half the committee has no formal art training.

Then apply the procedure to two hypothetical proposals so I can see how it works: (1) a realistic bronze statue of a local historical figure, technically flawless but completely conventional, and (2) a challenging abstract installation about gentrification that uses demolition materials from a torn-down housing project — half the committee finds it visually repellent, the other half thinks it's the most important thing they've seen all year. I especially want to see a case where the procedure spits out an answer the committee won't like, because if it never does that it's probably just rubber-stamping our gut feelings.
