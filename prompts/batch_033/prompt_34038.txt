I am a novelist at work on a book that turns, in part, on the question of whether people can be said to want freedom when freedom makes them miserable. I have been re-reading Dostoevsky — specifically the Grand Inquisitor chapter from The Brothers Karamazov — and I find myself unsure what the chapter is actually arguing, as opposed to what it dramatically presents. I would like you to read the chapter through the lens of the philosophical debate about freedom and happiness: the claim, associated with the Grand Inquisitor's speech, that human beings do not truly want freedom, that they find it a burden, and that what they most deeply desire is miracle, mystery, and authority — someone to take the terrible choice off their hands and give them bread and peace instead.

I want a reading that treats the chapter seriously as both a work of fiction and a philosophical argument. Where does Dostoevsky, through Ivan, give the Inquisitor the stronger case, and where does the framing — the fact that Ivan is telling this as a poem to Alyosha, the fact that Jesus says nothing throughout and then kisses the Inquisitor at the end — undercut or complicate the Inquisitor's argument? Does the kiss answer the argument, or merely refuse to engage it? And what does the novel as a whole do with the Inquisitor's claims: do the lives of the characters, especially Alyosha and Dmitri, bear out the idea that freedom is a crushing weight that most people would gladly trade for security and submission, or do they suggest something else?

I am not looking for a survey of the secondary literature. I want a committed reading that takes a definite position on what the chapter is doing philosophically, that distinguishes between what the Inquisitor says and what the text shows, and that is honest about where the chapter resists a tidy resolution — where the honest critic has to say that Dostoevsky leaves the tension standing rather than resolving it. Please write in British English, in continuous prose without headings, bullet points, or any other formatting — just paragraphs, as in a letter or an essay.
