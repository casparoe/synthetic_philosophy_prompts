I've been trying to get clear on what I actually think about moral status, by going with my gut on cases, and the results are embarassing enough that I want someone to clean them up.

Here's where I land. A thermostat: nothing, I'd unplug it without a thought. A tree: nothing. A nematode, the little worm with a few hundred neurons: basically nothing; I'd flush a thousand of them and sleep fine. A honeybee: not nothing, but almost — I wouldn't crush one for fun, but if a hive had to be exterminated to get it out of a wall, I wouldn't loose sleep. A fish: more than a bee; catch-and-release fishing strikes me as genuinely cruel, but I don't think a fish's death is as bad as a chicken's. A chicken: clearly matters; I think factory farming them is wrong, but if I had to choose between pulling a chicken or a child out of a burning building, I'd grab the child and wouldn't call it a moral dilemma. A cow: matters a lot, maybe not exactly like a human, but enough that I won't eat one. A dog: matters a lot — honestly I'd be more upset about someone kicking a dog than someone kicking a pig, and I can't defend that, but it's my gut. A newborn human: full status, same as an adult, even though it can't do much. An adult with severe dementia: full status, same as anyone. A brain organoid in a dish: probably nothing now, but I'm not sure why I'm confident. A current large language model: no, though I can't say exactly what it's missing.

The rule I keep telling myself is that status tracks the capacity to suffer, and that it comes in degrees. But that doesn't obviously give me the bee-versus-nematode gap, or the chicken-versus-infant gap, or the dog-versus-pig thing. Is there one principle that gets all or nearly all of these, stated precisely? What does it say about cases I haven't settled — say, an octopus, a shrimp, a whole ant colony, a digital simulation of a mouse brain, and a human patient in a dreamless sleep? And which of my verdicts does it force me to drop?
