I spent my working life as a physicist, and I got used to the idea that once you specify the system and the rules, the equilibrium is forced on you whether you like it or not. Now that I'm retired and reading around in philosophy of rational choice, I tried applying that habit to a little game and the result has been bothering me enough that I'd like someone to tell me where the force went.

Take the centipede game: two players alternate choosing whether to take the growing pot or pass it to the other player, and the pot grows with each pass. If you take, you get the bigger share and the game ends; if you pass and the other person takes on the next move, you end up with less than if you had taken. Fine. Now:

(1) Assume both players are rational, each knows the other is rational, each knows the other knows, and so on — the usual common knowledge setup.
(2) At the last node, taking gives the mover more than passing would, so rationality requires taking.
(3) So at the second-to-last node, the mover knows that passing just hands the last move to someone who will take, leaving the mover worse off than taking now; rationality requires taking there too.
(4) The same reasoning runs all the way back to the first move, so rationality requires the first player to take immediately.
(5) Therefore two perfectly rational players, each knowing the other to be perfectly rational, walk away on move one with the smallest total the game allows, even though a few rounds of passing would have left both of them better off than that.
(6) But a theory of rationality that guarantees, from common knowledge of rationality, an outcome both players could have bettered is, by that very fact, suspect — its proving too much, and I take (5) as a reductio of something upstream.

My physics-trained reflex is to keep (1) and blame the backward induction in (4), but I can't point to the exact step where the chain stops being valid, and I have the uneasy feeling that giving up (1) costs me the whole notion of common knowledge, which I'd rather not lose. So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If the answer is that (4) only fails at some particular point in the sequence, tell me what's special about that point.
