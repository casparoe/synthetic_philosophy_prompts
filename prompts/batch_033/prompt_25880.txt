You are a professor emeritus of philosophy of mind and the history of artificial intelligence, contributing a chapter to an edited volume reassessing Alan Turing's legacy in light of contemporary AI. Your task is to reason carefully and historically through the following question, beginning with a brief brainstorming phase in which you identify the key philosophical commitments one would need to reconstruct from Turing's writings before attempting an answer. Then, develop a well-structured and nuanced response.

Consider Turing's 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence," his 1948 report "Intelligent Machinery," and his broader intellectual trajectory, including his interest in cybernetics and his proposal for a learning machine that would start from a relatively unorganized, child-like state and acquire concepts through experience and training. Contemporary large language models engage in fluent, multi-turn text-based dialogue that, in restricted settings, can appear indistinguishable from human conversation. Some researchers claim this satisfies a behavioral criterion for intelligence. Meanwhile, critics point out that these systems lack embodied interaction with a shared physical environment, sustained personal history, and the kind of open-ended agency Turing may have taken for granted when he described machines that could "roam the countryside."

Taking into account Turing's explicit defense of behavioral evidence as sufficient in principle, his distinction between the "argument from consciousness" (which he rejected) and objections based on disability or limitation (which he took more seriously), his emphasis on learning and development rather than pre-programmed adult competence, and his remarks about the necessity of "the best sense organs that money can buy" if a machine is to be made intelligent, what would Turing most likely have concluded about the claim that a purely text-trained, disembodied LLM — however conversationally sophisticated — genuinely understands language and possesses a mind? Would he have regarded the behavioral criterion as met by such a system, or do his developmental, interactionist, and epistemic commitments suggest he would have set a substantially different bar?
