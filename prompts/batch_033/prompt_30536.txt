I'm in a political theory reading group, and last week we were discussing a contemporary politician whose public actions seem transparently self-serving and harmful. When one member argued that the politician's behavior only makes sense if he has genuinely malicious intentions, another member shut the discussion down by saying, "This is just the banality of evil — you don't need malice to do terrible things." The phrase drew nods around the table, and we moved on.

I have since gone back to Hannah Arendt's *Eichmann in Jerusalem: A Report on the Banality of Evil*, and I am struck by how differently the phrase "the banality of evil" functions in ordinary argument compared with the specific role it plays in Arendt's account of Eichmann. It now circulates as a kind of free-floating slogan that people deploy to mean quite different things: sometimes that ordinary, unremarkable people can commit atrocities; sometimes that evil acts do not require evil motives; sometimes that bureaucratic systems diffuse responsibility so no individual is ever a straightforward villain; sometimes something more deflationary, namely that evil is not a deep metaphysical category at all.

I want to return to the group with a clear breakdown rather than a vague objection. What are the distinct, precisified claims that "the banality of evil" could be taken to express? Which of those readings are fairly trivial or uncontroversial, which are substantive and genuinely close to Arendt's own view, and which are probably false or at least overstated? Most importantly, which reading would actually be needed for the phrase to do the work it was doing in our discussion — dismissing the question about malicious intent — and does that reading survive scrutiny?
