I am a software engineer at a consumer software company, and something about our product practices has been bothering me. The company's integrity policy is strict about literal truth: marketing copy, in-app text, and even error messages go through legal review, and any claim that can be shown to be factually false gets blocked. Yet the same company ships features that are carefully worded to be accurate but are designed to mislead. The cancellation flow states at each step that the subscription remains active until the end of the billing period, but it is built so that a user who believes they have finished cancelling has not. A privacy settings screen labels every option correctly, but the visual hierarchy nudges users toward the most permissive choice, and the disclosure that matters sits in a tooltip most users never open. When I raised the contrast, I was told these patterns are just persuasive design and that we have never told anyone anything false.

I want to understand what this combination of practices actually commits the company to. It seems to presuppose that there is a real moral difference between lying and misleading — that the wrong in deception attaches to the false statement itself rather than to the false belief we produce, the foreseeable misunderstanding we exploit, or our intention that users end up with a mistaken picture. Is that a coherent view? Does the practice strictly presuppose it, or is the practice compatible with several different accounts of why deception is wrong and merely convenient under this one? And if we replaced that view with the position that misleading is no better than lying, or that what matters is the belief the user is likely to form, what concretely would have to change in how the company reviews copy, designs flows, and runs experiments?
