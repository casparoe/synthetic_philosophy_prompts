I've got really into birdwatching over the last year, and it's made me notice how easily I talk myself into patterns: I see a few black ravens and suddenly I'm half convinced they're all black, and then I catch myself noticing that every white thing around me (a shoe, a fridge, a page) is also not a raven, and wondering whether that's meant to count as evidence too. A mate who did philosophy of science at uni tried to explain Hempel's raven paradox over a pint, and I've been turning it over ever since, becuase it seems to be about something I now do all the time, which is a wierd thing to find yourself doing.

Could you write me a villanelle about the paradox? Here's what I'd like the form to do: the two refrains should be, on one side, the hypothesis that all ravens are black, and on the other, the observation of some non-black non-raven thing (a white shoe, say) that the logic of confirmation seems to force us to count as evidence for that hypothesis. The way the refrains keep taking turns and then land next to each other in the closing couplet should do the work of showing why the paradox keeps reasserting itself: by the equivalence condition, evidence for the claim that all non-black things are non-ravens is evidence for the claim that all ravens are black, so the shoe looks both obviously irrelevant and formally on a par with a black raven. Please don't just declare the shoe irrelevant to resolve it; leave the tension where the paradox leaves it. It should be accurate enough that a philosopher of science wouldn't wince, and still a decent poem for someone who's never heard of Hempel. And if you could write the answer in British English, that'd be grand.
