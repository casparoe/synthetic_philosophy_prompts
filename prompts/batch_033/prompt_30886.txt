Draft the joint statement that a hinge epistemologist (working in the tradition of Wittgenstein's On Certainty) and a traditional evidentialist epistemologist (who holds that all doxastic commitments are, in principle, open to rational evaluation — answerable to evidence, revisable on the basis of reasons, and assessable as justified or unjustified) could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration.

The document should contain four sections.

Section 1: Agreed Claims. A numbered list of substantive claims both parties accept. These should go beyond truisms. Address at least: what the skeptical paradox correctly identifies; what ordinary doubt looks like in practice and what it presupposes; whether there is a regress of justification and, if so, where it bottoms out; what it means for inquiry to proceed; and whether it is psychologically or rationally possible to doubt everything at once. Both parties should be able to read the list without feeling that their view has been mischaracterized or diluted.

Section 2: Disagreements Table. For each row, state the question neutrally, then the hinge epistemologist's answer and the evidentialist's answer, each in wording its own proponents would endorse. Cover at least the following: (a) Are hinge commitments propositional — do they have truth-conditional content that can be asserted and denied? (b) Are hinge commitments subject to epistemic norms, or do they lie outside the space of reasons? (c) Can a rational agent suspend judgment on a hinge commitment without thereby abandoning rationality? (d) Does treating hinge commitments as arational certainties dissolve the skeptical problem, or does it merely relabel the point at which justification gives out? (e) Is the status of a hinge commitment — as hinge — itself an epistemological discovery, or is it a mere description of what we happen not to doubt? (f) When one hinge commitment conflicts with another across different world-pictures or epistemic systems, is there any rational procedure for adjudicating between them?

Section 3: Cruxes. Identify the sub-questions whose resolution would cause at least one side to substantially revise its view. For each crux, specify what both sides would accept as a test, argument, or type of evidence that bears on it, and record each side's prediction about what that test or argument would show if carried through. Where possible, describe a concrete case — an empirical finding, a thought experiment, or a formal result — that both sides agree would move the debate if it were settled one way rather than another.

Section 4: Verbal Disputes. Assess whether any part of the disagreement is merely terminological. For example, both sides might agree about all the underlying facts concerning how we in fact reason and inquire yet disagree about whether the word "knowledge" applies at the level of hinges, or about whether the phrase "subject to norms" covers the kind of normative pressure that both sides agree exists. If any such merely verbal disputes are identified, state what both sides would agree on if the terminology were clarified and what residual, substantive disagreement remains. If neither side concedes any verbal dispute, state that explicitly and give each side's brief reason for regarding the disagreement as substantive rather than semantic.
