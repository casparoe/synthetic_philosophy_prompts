Health systems behave in apparently inconsistent ways when lives are at stake. During the COVID-19 pandemic, many hospitals rationed intensive care and ventilators using triage protocols that explicitly aimed to save the greatest number of lives, ranking patients by expected survival. Yet the same systems allocate donor organs partly by waiting time and partly by expected benefit, and for ordinary scarce treatments they often use first-come, first-served, even where that saves fewer lives overall. At the same time, many people are uneasy about the thought that in a choice between saving one person and saving five, the five simply have a stronger claim in virtue of being more numerous. Reconstruct the theory of how individual claims aggregate that the greatest-number triage practice presupposes. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, say whether triage, queueing, and the uneasiness can be made mutually consistent, and explain what triage and allocation policy would have to change under the view that numbers of lives saved do not by themselves add to the strength of the reasons. Please write the answer in British English.
