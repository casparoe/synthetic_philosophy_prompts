I've been thinking about Hofstadter's ant colony thought experiment—the Aunt Hillary dialogue in *Gödel, Escher, Bach*—and I'm stuck on what seems like an overlooked implication. If the argument works, it shows that a colony of ants, whose individual behaviors are mechanistic and mindless, can collectively constitute a system with genuine mental states: beliefs, desires, perhaps even a kind of consciousness. But if that's right, then it seems we should take the interests of ant colonies into account in our moral reasoning. We wouldn't casually destroy a human mind; why should we casually destroy a colony-mind? Yet no one—not even Hofstadter, as far as I can tell—draws this ethical conclusion. I'm trying to figure out whether the argument itself is flawed, or whether there's a principled reason why the metaphysical conclusion (that the colony has a mind) doesn't carry the ethical weight I'm assuming it does. Where does the reasoning go wrong?
