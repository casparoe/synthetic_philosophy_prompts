I have been trying to work out what I actually believe about which animals are conscious, and my case-by-case verdicts refuse to add up to a view. I take it as obvious that a dog is conscious, and I would say the same about a mouse and about a trout: the trout flinches from tissue damage, learns to avoid a place where it was hooked, and shows stress responses that strike me as more than reflex. I would also say an octopus is conscious, given its problem-solving and what looks like play. More controversially, I have come to think a honeybee probably has some minimal subjective experience, because it learns flexibly, communicates about locations, and adjusts its behavior in ways that do not look fully pre-programmed. But when I consider a shore crab, which also learns to avoid noxious stimuli and will tend to an injured limb, my reaction is that there is nothing it is like to be the crab — it seems like a bundle of reflexes in a shell. A jellyfish: no. A sea sponge: no. A nematode such as C. elegans, which has a nervous system and learns but has only a few hundred neurons: no. So my verdicts run: dog, mouse, trout, octopus, honeybee — yes; shore crab, jellyfish, sponge, nematode — no.

I cannot tell what principle I am applying. A behavioral criterion — flexible learning, avoidance, communication — would force the crab and the nematode in, and would leave me unsure how to exclude the jellyfish's coordinated behavior without appealing to the very integration I cannot define; a vertebrate-line criterion is broken by the octopus and the bee; a large, centralized brain criterion would exclude the bee and possibly the trout, and in any case just names a threshold without justifying it. My best guess is something like a nervous system that integrates information into a single evaluative point of view, with a valence system that steers behavior, but I suspect that phrase is just a way of ratifying whichever verdict I already hold. I am not looking for a survey of the empirical literature; I want the principle that best fits my verdicts and an honest account of where it forces me to change them. Is there one principled account that delivers all nine verdicts, or close to all of them? If not, which of my verdicts should I overturn, and why those rather than their neighbors? And what does the principle you end up with say about cases I have not settled — a jumping spider, a parrot, a squid, a frog, and an ant?
