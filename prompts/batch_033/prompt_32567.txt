At a recent workshop on technology governance, a participant invoked the precautionary principle to argue against field trials of a novel gene-drive system. When pressed, they said simply: 'Better safe than sorry.' The phrase was treated as self-evident, and the discussion moved on. I left the room uncertain what, precisely, had been claimed. 'Better safe than sorry' seems to admit of readings ranging from a platitude no reasonable person would reject to a principle that, applied consistently, would forbid nearly every innovation — including, perhaps, the very research that might tell us whether the gene drive is safe. I want to return to the next meeting with a systematic disambiguation.

Please lay out the distinct precise theses that 'better safe than sorry' could be expressing in a regulatory or technology-governance context, ordered from weakest to strongest. For each reading, specify:

- Whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible normative claim, or a thesis that is false or self-defeating.
- Whether it would, by itself, support a moratorium on the gene-drive field trials.
- The strongest objection available to someone who believes the field trials should proceed.

After laying out the readings, identify which one or ones the workshop participant was most likely invoking when they called the phrase 'settled policy,' and explain whether the appeal to policy favours one reading over others.

Finally, address a standard rejoinder: 'The precautionary principle cuts both ways — failing to deploy a technology that could prevent harm is itself a precautionary failure.' Is this rejoinder a rejection of the principle, a friendly amendment, or a confusion about which reading is in play? Answer separately for each of the readings you identified.
