I'm working on a piece about the paradox of fiction — the old puzzle about why we have genuine emotional responses to characters and events we know do not exist. The standard formulation, associated with Colin Radford's work in the 1970s, runs roughly as follows: we feel real fear, pity, or grief for fictional characters; to feel such emotions we must believe their objects are real; yet we do not believe fictional characters are real. So one of those claims has to give, and philosophers have been arguing about which one ever since — some denying that our responses are genuine emotions, others denying that emotions require belief in reality, and still others recasting the whole thing in terms of simulation, make-believe, or thought-contents.

It strikes me that Shakespeare's Hamlet is already working through versions of this problem, and I want to read two moments in the play through the lens of the philosophical debate. The first is Hamlet's reaction to the Player's speech about Hecuba in Act II, Scene ii: the actor weeps for a woman he never knew, from a story set centuries before, and Hamlet is visibly shaken by the sheer reality of the feeling — more real, he thinks, than his own grief for his murdered father. The second is the play-within-a-play in Act III, Scene ii, where Hamlet stages The Murder of Gonzago to provoke a real emotional response — guilt — in Claudius, and succeeds: fiction reaches out of its frame and changes something in the world. Between them, these scenes seem to offer a kind of working theory of how fiction moves us, one that predates the philosophical literature by centuries but has not, to my mind, been fully mined by it.

I would like a reading that takes both the play and the philosophical debate seriously. What does Shakespeare seem to think explains our emotional responses to fiction — is he closer to a simulation theory, a thought-content theory, a make-believe account, or something else? Does the play treat the paradox as genuine, or does it dissolve it? And most of all, does Shakespeare dramatize something the philosophical debate misses — something about performance, about complicity, about the way fiction can catch us off guard when we are not paying attention — that a good theory of the paradox ought to account for? I am not looking for a survey of positions; I want a committed reading that argues for a definite interpretation and defends it.
