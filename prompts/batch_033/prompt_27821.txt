You are a professional philosopher of science who specializes in post-positivist philosophy of science and, in particular, in Lakatos's methodology of scientific research programmes. I am a high school junior taking a semester-long philosophy of science elective. My final assignment is a 4,000-word term paper, and before I can begin writing I must submit a short research proposal of about 1,200 words that will be assessed on whether the question is precise, tractable, and genuinely open. I would like you to write that proposal for me.

My half-formed idea is this. Lakatos's distinction between progressive and degenerating research programmes is supposed to tell us when it is rational to keep working within a programme and when it is rational to abandon it. But whether a modification of a programme is a promising auxiliary adjustment or a merely ad hoc patch often becomes clear only later, because what matters is whether the programme continues to predict novel facts, and that takes time to reveal itself. My suspicion is that the criterion can therefore be applied only retrospectively, and I am unsure whether that makes it useless as a guide for scientists who must make decisions in the present. I want the proposal built around the question: can a retrospective criterion of progress still be normative for scientists at the moment of choice, or does Lakatos's methodology collapse into a post hoc rationalization with no prescriptive force?

Please make the proposal include all of the following.

1. A precise statement of the research question, formulated so that it has a determinate answer rather than remaining a topic.
2. A short account of why the existing positions leave the question open: what a standard defender of Lakatos would say, what the usual objection is, and why neither settles the matter. Refer to positions and arguments in your own words, and do not include a bibliography or citations, since I will add the scholarly references myself.
3. A method section describing what I would actually do, week by week, across the semester: how I would select historical episodes in which a programme looked degenerate but recovered, or looked progressive but stalled; what I would extract from those cases; and how the results would adjudicate between the two readings of Lakatos's criterion. Also explain why this case-study method is the right approach rather than the obvious alternatives, such as a purely conceptual analysis of the word normative or an abstract formal model of theory choice.
4. A statement of the possible conclusions, what a successful result would look like, and what would count as the project failing — for instance, what pattern of results from the case studies would show that the question as posed is unanswerable or badly framed.
5. An honest assessment of whether the project is too large for one semester and, if so, how to narrow it while preserving the core question.

Before you write the proposal, take time to brainstorm. In particular, develop the strongest version of the objection that the project is really just a verbal dispute about the word normative, and the strongest version of the opposite worry that the question is too big to be settled by a handful of historical cases. Then draft the proposal so that it anticipates both worries. Be careful to distinguish the claim that Lakatos's methodology is descriptive from the claim that it is retrospective; the interesting version of my question concerns the second, not the first.

End with a short section titled Where I Am Least Confident, in which you say which argument or consideration would most likely change your mind about the framing of the proposal — that is, what someone could show you that would make you reframe the question, abandon it, or substitute a different question — and where the proposal's central reasoning is most likely to fail.
