I've been arguing with a friend who works in metaphysics about whether omissions can be genuine causes, and the conversation has pushed me to write up my thoughts more carefully. The resulting essay (below) tries to dissolve the puzzle rather than solve it: my claim is that the apparent weirdness of causation by omission is just a particularly visible instance of the purpose-relativity that's present in all causal claims, and that treating omission-causation as metaphysically suspect rests on a kind of map-territory confusion. I'd appreciate critical comments from people who think carefully about these issues. I'm particularly interested in whether the argument actually lands against someone who holds a robust metaphysical realist position about causation — someone who thinks causation is a natural relation that carves at the joints — or whether I'm just talking past them. I'm also worried that the final move, where I say there's no fact of the matter about whether omissions cause things, might collapse into a causal anti-realism I don't actually want to endorse. Any suggestions for tightening the argument or flagging places where it begs the question would be very helpful.

**What We Talk About When We Talk About Omissions**

Suppose I promise to water your plants while you're away. I forget. The plants die. Did my failure to water them cause their death? Most people say yes without hesitation. But now suppose a stranger in another city also failed to water your plants. Did her omission cause their death? Most people say no, even though the counterfactual structure is identical: if she had watered them, they wouldn't have died (assuming she could have gotten there in time, and so on). The standard puzzle about causation by omission is that our judgments seem to track something beyond bare counterfactual dependence — they track expectations, promises, duties. And this makes it look like causal judgments about omissions are somehow contaminated by the normative, while "real" causation — the kind involving pushes, pulls, and collisions — is purely factual.

I want to suggest that this picture gets things exactly backwards. The problem isn't that omission-causation is weirdly normative; the problem is that we've been pretending that ordinary physical causation isn't selective in exactly the same way.

Take any paradigmatic case of "real" causation: a rock strikes a window, and the window shatters. Why do we say the rock caused the shattering rather than citing the gravitational field of Alpha Centauri, which exerts a force on every particle in the window and is part of the full physical story of why the glass molecules moved as they did? Because we're interested in the difference-makers relative to a background of normal conditions. The gravitational field of Alpha Centauri is part of the background; the rock is the thing that changed. But what counts as "background" and what counts as "change" depends on our interests, our typical expectations, and the contrast class we implicitly have in mind.

In other words, all causal claims involve a selection from an enormous space of counterfactual dependencies. When we say "C caused E," we're picking out one of the many factors without which E wouldn't have occurred, and our basis for picking it out is irreducibly tied to our explanatory purposes. Omissions just make this selection process more visible, because in omission cases the selected factor is an absence rather than a presence, and our reasons for selecting it often involve normative expectations — promises, duties — rather than merely statistical or physical expectations.

If this is right, then the question "do omissions really cause things?" is like the question "is the rock really the cause of the shattering, as opposed to the gravitational field of Alpha Centauri?" Both are real in the only sense that matters: they are the factors we have reason to select given our explanatory purposes. There's no further metaphysical fact about which factors are the "real" causes, because causation is not a natural kind waiting to be discovered — it's a tool for navigating a world of counterfactual dependencies, and like any tool, it's shaped by what we need it to do.

This doesn't mean causal claims are arbitrary or merely subjective. The world really does have a counterfactual structure, and some selections from that structure are better than others for a given purpose. But the purpose-sensitivity goes all the way down. The mistake, I think, is to treat the purpose-sensitivity in omission cases as a contaminant and the purpose-sensitivity in physical causation cases as nonexistent. Both involve it; we just notice it more in the omission cases because the normative dimension is more salient.

I realize this position is close to broadly pragmatist views of causation, though I haven't read enough of that literature to know exactly where it overlaps or diverges. I'm also aware that a metaphysician of causation might object that I'm conflating causal selection — which is pragmatic — with the existence of a causal relation — which might be metaphysical. But I think that distinction itself is part of what I'm challenging: if the causal relation just is whatever we're picking out when we make successful causal claims, and our picking-out is always purpose-relative, then there's no purpose-independent causal relation left over to be metaphysical about.

I'd be grateful for feedback, but especially on three points. First, whether the Alpha Centauri analogy actually works, or whether there's a principled difference between physical background conditions and omissions that I'm eliding. Second, whether my response to the selection-versus-relation objection is adequate or just question-begging. And third, whether there's a way to accept my conclusion without sliding into the view that causal claims are just expressions of subjective preference — that is, whether I can keep the "the world really does have counterfactual structure" part doing enough work to avoid anti-realism.
