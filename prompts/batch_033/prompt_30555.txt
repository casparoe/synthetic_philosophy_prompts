I am a software engineer on the trust and safety team at a social media company, and I have been asked to help revise our policy on posts that discuss suicide. The debate in our group is whether to remove, downrank, or leave up content in which someone argues that suicide is a rational and permissible choice. The concrete cases keep pulling in different directions: a post by a user with a terminal diagnosis who has chosen the date of their death; a long essay defending suicide as an autonomous decision; a teenager's post that reads like a goodbye; and a private group that discusses methods in a matter-of-fact way. The working proposal is to remove anything that treats suicide as a reasonable option, on the theory that it may nudge vulnerable people. I recently read Hume's essay on suicide, and it has made the debate much harder for me. As I understand it, Hume argues that suicide is not always a violation of one's duty to God, to other people, or to oneself, and that the traditional religious and moral prohibitions rest on superstition and a mistaken picture of our obligations. I am not trained in philosophy, so I would like someone to work out the extension of that position for me.

What do you think Hume would say about our platform's policy if he were alive today? In particular, would he distinguish the kind of deliberate, reflective suicide he defends from impulsive, contagious, or coerced suicide, and would that distinction justify treating the different kinds of content differently? Would he support automatic interventions such as hotline prompts and content warnings, or would he regard them as paternalistic? And what would he make of algorithmic amplification — the fact that our recommender system may spread pro-suicide content to people who never sought it out? I would like a prediction of his likely position, grounded in his actual commitments, including any places where modern circumstances would force him to draw new lines or revise his view. Please write your answer in American English.
