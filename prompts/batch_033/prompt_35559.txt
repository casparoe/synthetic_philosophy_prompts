I have been trying to get clear about how I should respond to decisions that involve tiny probabilities of enormous stakes, and my intuitions keep pulling me in different directions depending on the details of the case. I would like to know whether there is a single principle that captures all or most of my verdicts, and if not, which verdicts I should revise and why.

Here are the cases and my untutored reactions:

1. **The street mugger.** A stranger approaches me and says: "Give me ten dollars, or I will use my hidden powers to create and torture a googolplex of sentient beings." I do not pay. If I took the threat at face value, the expected disvalue would be astronomically negative, but I dismiss it entirely — the probability that this person has such powers is not merely small but, I want to say, not one I am obliged to take seriously at all.

2. **The physics experiment.** A consortium of physicists proposes a high-energy experiment. Their best models indicate a 1-in-10^25 chance that the experiment triggers a vacuum phase transition that would destroy the entire observable universe. We could instead pursue a safer experimental design, at an additional cost of fifty billion dollars, which would reduce the risk to 1-in-10^40. My reaction is: spend the money. A 1-in-10^25 chance of ending everything is too high, even though the number sounds minuscule in absolute terms.

3. **The mathematical lottery.** I am offered a ticket for one dollar. The ticket gives me a precisely defined 1-in-10^100 chance of winning 10^200 dollars, where the mechanism is a mathematically specified random process with no ambiguity about the probabilities. The expected value is 10^100 dollars, an incomprehensibly large sum. And yet I would not buy the ticket. It feels like a trick, even though the probabilities and payoffs are mathematically exact.

4. **The ordinary lottery.** A standard lottery ticket costs one dollar and offers a 1-in-10-million chance at five million dollars. The expected value is fifty cents, so it is a bad bet in expectation, but I do not find it absurd to play. If the prize were raised to twenty million, giving the ticket a positive expected value of two dollars, I would play without hesitation.

5. **The crank with a proof.** Someone emails me claiming to have a proof that settles a major open problem in my field. They offer to sell me the proof for five hundred dollars. I have no way to estimate the probability that they are telling the truth — they could be a genius, a crank, or a prankster. The value of a genuine proof would be enormous for my career. But I do not pay. The expected-value calculation seems to require a probability I cannot even begin to assign, and that very fact feels like a reason to walk away.

6. **The daily drive.** Every time I drive to the grocery store, I face a roughly 1-in-10-million chance of dying in a car crash. I do this several times a week without a second thought, and this seems perfectly reasonable.

7. **Pascal's wager, modernized.** An otherwise intelligent friend argues that I should devote my life to a particular religion because, even if the chance that its eschatology is correct is vanishingly small, the payoff is infinite and so dominates all finite considerations. I find the argument uncompelling, but I have a harder time saying exactly why it fails than I do with the street mugger — the structure of the argument feels harder to dismiss, even though I am confident the conclusion is wrong.

These verdicts seem to be in tension. Cases 2 and 6 suggest that tiny probabilities of enormous stakes should be taken perfectly seriously. Cases 1, 3, 5, and 7 suggest that sometimes they should not be. Case 4 sits comfortably in the middle. I can feel that something about the source or grounding of the probability matters — the physics experiment involves a probability embedded in a well-tested scientific model, while the mugger's probability is pulled from nowhere — but I am not sure that distinction can carry the weight I want it to, especially when I look at case 3, where the probability is mathematically precise and yet I still balk.

Is there a principle that gets all or most of these verdicts right? If no single principle can accommodate them, which verdicts should I overturn, and on what grounds? And what does the principle you propose say about a case I have not listed: a government program that, for an expenditure of a billion dollars, would reduce the annual probability of a catastrophic global pandemic from two percent to one percent?
