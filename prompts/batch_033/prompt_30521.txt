I've got an upper-level undergrad seminar in philosophy of mathematics coming up — 14 students, 80-minute block, and most of them have done at least one epistemology course. I'd like to run a structured activity that gets them to feel the force of Benacerraf's dilemma without me announcing it up front. The thing I want them to run into on their own is this: mathematical claims seem to be about mind-independent objects (numbers, sets, that kind of thing), but those objects, if they exist, are exactly the sort of thing we can't have any causal contact with; and if knowing requires some kind of causal or information-carrying connection to the facts, then our mathematical knowledge starts to look impossible even though it also looks like its the most certain knowledge we have. Ideally the students hit that wall themselves and only afterwards see the two horns. Could you build me a game, puzzle, or role-play — maybe with some students as 'knowers' and others as 'objects' or 'facts' — with concrete rules, simple materials I can actually prepare, and a timeline for the 80 minutes? The debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto the semantic horn (truth about abstracta) and the epistemic horn (causal constraints on knowledge), and leave them seeing why this isn't just a problem about being bad at math. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be honest about which part of the dilemma no classroom activity can really reproduce and I'll have to supply myself.
