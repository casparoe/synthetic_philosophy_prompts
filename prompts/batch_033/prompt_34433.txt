For a lecture in a course on rational choice and games, I need a worked example showing that iterated elimination of strictly dominated pure strategies is strictly weaker than the same procedure with mixed strategies allowed. Construct a two-player normal-form game meeting all of the following specifications.

(a) Some pure strategy survives iterated elimination of strictly dominated pure strategies but is eliminated once strictly dominated mixed strategies are permitted.
(b) The difference is not confined to that one strategy: once it is removed, at least one further pure-strategy elimination becomes available that was not available under the pure procedure, so the two procedures end with different surviving sets of pure-strategy profiles.
(c) The game has at most three strategies per player and small integer payoffs.
(d) The mixed strategy that performs the elimination assigns positive probability to at least two pure strategies, neither of which strictly dominates the target strategy on its own.

Present the matrix, then run the elimination sequence under both procedures, write out the inequality that establishes the mixed-strategy dominance, and list the surviving pure-strategy profiles in each case. Then identify the feature of the payoff matrix that is doing the work — what stops any pure strategy from doing the same job — and say what happens to the example if that feature is removed or perturbed. Finally, explain how someone who held that only pure-strategy dominance is rationally compelling would attack the example, and why you nonetheless regard it as decisive for the purpose above. Be committed throughout: assert that your game meets each specification, give a single diagnosis of the crucial feature, and do not offer alternatives.
