A colleague of mine has been thinking about virtual worlds and real harm for years, and I have been tracking her verdicts on specific cases as she gives them. I want to know what she would say about a new case, but more than that I want to understand what principle she is actually running, because I suspect she may not have made it fully explicit even to herself.

Here are her verdicts on the cases I have presented to her over time.

She said that having your virtual sword stolen through account hacking, when you paid two hundred dollars for it on a third-party market, is a real harm—not merely a setback in a game.

She said that losing the same virtual sword in fair player-versus-player combat, when you earned it entirely through gameplay, is not a real harm; it is just part of the game.

She said that being subjected to a sustained campaign of racist and sexist slurs directed at you in a virtual reality chatroom is a real harm, even though no physical contact occurs and no money is lost.

She said that losing all your virtual possessions because the game company permanently shuts down the servers is not a real harm—it is genuinely disappointing, but it is not the kind of loss that counts as a harm in the sense she cares about.

She said that a player who tricks another into accepting a wildly unfavorable trade using only in-game mechanics—no lies about the real world, no misrepresentation of who they are, just exploiting the game's trading interface as designed—has not committed a real harm.

She said that a guild leader who posts another player's real name, home address, and workplace in the game's public chat has caused a real harm, even if no one acts on the information.

She said that a player who spends months building an elaborate virtual city in a sandbox game, only to have another player deliberately destroy it using the game's own building and demolition tools, has suffered a real harm—the creative effort and the intentional destruction of it matter, even though the game permits the demolition.

Now the new case. A player in a social virtual reality world is virtually groped by another player: their avatar is touched in sexual ways, and the platform's haptic feedback system causes the controller to vibrate in a corresponding pattern. The targeted player feels violated and distressed. No real-world physical contact occurred, no money changed hands, and no real-world identifying information was exchanged. The platform's terms of service prohibit this behavior, but the act was carried out entirely through the platform's own movement and haptic systems.

What would my colleague say about this case? I want you to identify the candidate rules that fit her verdicts, say what each rule predicts about the virtual groping case, and give your best guess about her verdict with your reasons for preferring one rule over the others. If her existing verdicts genuinely underdetermine the answer, tell me what principle she would be implicitly adopting with each choice, and name the further case—one I could actually present to her—that would best distinguish the competing rules. Throughout, please separate what is genuinely settled among people who work on the metaphysics and ethics of virtual worlds from what is actively contested, and do not present a contested position as the consensus. Write in continuous prose, the way you would write an email or a letter—no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
