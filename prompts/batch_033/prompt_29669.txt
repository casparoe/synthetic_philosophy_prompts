I spent my career in computational neuroscience — modeling neural systems, fitting models to data, cross-validating — and only after retiring did I start reading philosophy in earnest. Lately I've been working through Hume's Enquiry and the main contemporary responses to the problem of induction, and I keep returning to a thought that won't leave me alone: the no-free-lunch theorems in machine learning seem to state, with mathematical precision, something very close to Hume's core point. Wolpert and Macready showed that averaged over all possible objective functions, no optimization algorithm outperforms any other; the supervised-learning version says that averaged over all possible data-generating distributions, every learning algorithm has the same expected error. In both cases, the upshot is that an algorithm's past performance gives you no reason to expect future success unless you've already restricted the class of problems you're facing — which sounds remarkably like the claim that induction has no rational justification unless we already presuppose something like the uniformity of nature.

I'd like to stress-test this connection in conversation. Please play a philosopher of science who finds the analogy between NFL theorems and Hume's problem superficial and ultimately misleading — someone who grants that both involve a kind of "no free lunch" structure but thinks the NFL results are about search in a formal space and have nothing interesting to say about Hume's genuinely epistemic challenge, which concerns our entitlement to infer unobserved instances from observed ones. Be precise and rigorous; don't concede the core disagreement, though you may grant smaller points where they don't undermine your position. Keep replies to roughly a paragraph so it feels like a real exchange. Here's my opening move: The NFL theorems show that any inductive algorithm's expected performance is exactly chance-level when we average over all possible worlds — or, more carefully, over all possible data-generating processes. That seems to capture, in a mathematically rigorous way, the idea that past success provides no rational basis for expecting future success unless we've already built in an assumption that the future resembles the past. What exactly is missing from this as a formal analogue of Hume's argument?
