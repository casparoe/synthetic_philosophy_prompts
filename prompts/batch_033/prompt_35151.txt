I teach an upper-level seminar on language and mind, and I'm putting together the final. I want one essay question on Davidson's radical interpretation that can't be answered by just reciting the usual one-line gloss of the principle of charity. It should force students to say what kind of principle charity is even supposed to be — an empirical guess about how often speakers are right, a methodological policy for the field linguist, or a constitutive condition on something's being interpretable as a belief at all — and to actually reconstruct the omniscient interpreter argument as Davidson's case for the constitutive reading, rather than just name-dropping it. I also want them to sit with the obvious worry: if charity is constitutive, does Davidson make it impossible to attribute massive error or deep irationality to a speaker, and is that a bug or a feature of the view. Please write the question, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 that seperates an A from a B by naming the specific moves each must contain — for instance, distinguishing constitutive from merely heuristic charity, stating exactly what the omniscient interpreter argument is supposed to rule out, and taking a clear stand on whether room for error survives. And please don't make it answerable by listing the standard labels.
