I'm a software engineer who went down an evolution rabbit hole after a HN thread about group selection got weirdly heated. So I read Dawkins' selfish gene stuff, and then discovered the other side: David Sloan Wilson and Sober's multilevel selection, and the older Wynne-Edwards group selection that everyone says was discredited. The weird thing to me is that both sides seem to accept the same formal machinery — the Price equation, Hamilton's rule, all that — and yet they reach totally different conclusions about whether selection "really" acts on genes or on groups. You've got Dawkins and Williams saying organisms are just vehicles and genes are the replicators; then you've got Hull saying no, you need replicators *and* interactors, and the interactors can be groups; then the multilevel selection people say it's an empirical question whether group-level selection is a strong force in, like, eusocial insects or human cooperation. And somehow after fifty years nobody has moved.

My working theory is the whole thing is a dispute about what "level of selection" even means — is it about what gets copied, what causes fitness differences, what you can partition variance by, or what's the most useful bookkeeping metaphor. If that's right its a verbal/framing disagreement, but people on both sides keep insisting it's empirical, so I'm probably missing something. Diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is — verbal, empirical, about standards of evidence, different questions under one label — and say what would actually count as progress given that diagnosis, or whether it's just stuck forever. And be honest if my working theory is wrong; I can take it.
