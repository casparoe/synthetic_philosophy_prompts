I'm presenting to my school's Philosophy and Politics Club next week, defending the claim that Amartya Sen's liberal paradox presents a genuine and unresolved problem for any theory that tries to reconcile individual rights with the Pareto principle in social decision-making. My argument has three parts: first, I walk through the formal impossibility result and the Lady Chatterley's Lover example to establish that the conflict is real — that under minimal conditions, a social choice function cannot simultaneously respect individual decisiveness over personal matters and guarantee that unanimous preferences are reflected in the social ordering. Second, I argue that the three most common responses — weakening the Pareto principle, redefining rights as game-form entitlements rather than restrictions on social preference, and restricting the domain of admissible individual preference profiles — each fail to dissolve the paradox without introducing problems of their own. Third, I claim that the paradox teaches us something beyond technical social choice theory: that rights cannot be understood purely in terms of preference satisfaction, and that any adequate framework for collective decision-making must give up on at least one of the seemingly innocuous assumptions that generate the result.

The audience will be about fifteen members of the club plus our faculty advisor. The club is split roughly into three camps. One group is libertarian-leaning: they think individual rights are foundational and that the state should be neutral between conceptions of the good; they are likely to resist any conclusion that seems to weaken the case for individual liberty or to suggest that rights claims reduce to mere preferences. A second group is more utilitarian and economics-oriented: they care about efficiency, are comfortable with cost-benefit reasoning, and will be skeptical of any argument that seems to privilege individual choice over aggregate welfare. A third group is politically engaged but not committed to any particular theoretical framework; they will be interested in the real-world implications and may press on whether the paradox is just a formal curiosity. Our faculty advisor teaches AP U.S. Government and has a background in political philosophy; she is familiar with social choice theory in a general way and will ask the hardest questions, likely about the interpretation of the result and what it actually licenses us to conclude.

Prepare me for the Q&A: what objections will each group raise, in roughly what order, phrased the way they would phrase them? For each objection, give me a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is weakest. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
