Before I commit to any theory of what distinguishes manipulation from legitimate persuasion, I need a full specification of what such a theory must get right. Write me the desiderata: the cases it must handle, the distinctions it must respect, the explanatory burdens it must discharge, with the reasoning behind each requirement. Flag which desiderata are non-negotiable features of the phenomenon itself and which are optional commitments of particular theoretical camps. Also tell me whether you suspect any two desiderata cannot be jointly satisfied, and which single requirement is hardest for the leading approaches to meet.

To be clear about the kind of detail I am after: I want the list to contend with cases like the following. A friend who knows your insecurities urges you to apply for a job by emphasizing your strengths and quietly omitting mention of your weaknesses — is that manipulation, encouragement, or something in between? A therapist uses carefully timed silence to prompt a patient to fill the uncomfortable gap with self-disclosure. A negotiator frames the same proposal in different words to different audiences without changing the substance. A partner says "I'll be sad if you go out tonight" — guilt-tripping or honest self-disclosure? A political advertisement shows drowning polar bears to motivate climate action. A parent tells a child that the family dog will be disappointed if she does not finish her homework. I am not asking you to resolve these cases; I am asking what constraints they place on an adequate theory of the distinction.

I also want the desiderata list to register that any theory of this distinction sits at the intersection of several larger debates. It must be compatible with the fact that nearly all human communication involves some element of shaping how another person thinks or feels. It must respect the difference between manipulation and coercion without collapsing one into the other. It must explain why manipulation seems pro tanto objectionable even when it leads someone to a decision that is in their own interests, and yet why we do not treat all cases of manipulation as equally serious. It should also say something about whether the distinction applies to self-directed influence — cases where a person deliberately engineers their own emotional state or motivational structure, as in certain kinds of therapy or self-help. And it should be able to handle non-conscious influence: ambient music in a store that slows your pace, default settings on a form, the order in which options are presented.

The response should be continuous prose — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — as in a letter or an essay. And I want you to identify the step in your reasoning you regard as most fragile: where would a well-chosen counterexample or a different reading of a case most likely force a revision, and what would that revision be?
