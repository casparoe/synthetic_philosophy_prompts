I'm putting together a session for my advanced philosophy of language seminar on presupposition and the politics of default terms, and I want to make Hofstadter's "A Person Paper on Purity in Language" the anchor text. For those who haven't encountered it: the piece is a Swiftian satire in which Hofstadter writes an indignant letter to the editor protesting the growing use of "person" as a supposedly inclusive generic — because, in the world of the letter, it erases the crucial distinction between whites (who are persons) and blacks (who are not). The conceit substitutes race for gender, so that sentences whose racism would be immediately obvious to any contemporary reader turn out to be structurally identical to sentences about "man" and "mankind" that pass without remark in ordinary English. The effect depends on the reader's willingness to accommodate the piece's central presupposition — that blacks are not persons — and then to experience the shock of recognizing that this very presupposition is doing exactly the work that "man" as default does in non-satirical discourse.

I'd like a close reading of the Person Paper through the lens of presupposition theory — specifically, the Stalnakerian picture of common ground and the mechanism of presupposition accommodation, together with the more recent work on the normative dimensions of presupposition (the idea, developed by Langton, Haslanger, and McGowan among others, that presuppositions can function as conversational exercitives that enact or reinforce social hierarchies rather than merely reporting them). The question I want to put to the seminar is this: does the Person Paper's satirical force depend on the reader's *failure* to accommodate the racist presupposition, or on their *successful* accommodation followed by retrospective horror? That is, is the mechanism of the satire that we cannot bring ourselves to update the common ground as the text demands, and our resistance is what exposes the parallel — or that we do update it, inhabit it, and then recognize we have been made complicit in something we disavow? And what does the answer tell us about how default-male language actually functions in non-satirical contexts: does it work by getting speakers and hearers to accommodate a presupposition they would reject if made explicit, or by exploiting a presupposition they have already internalized?

I'm less interested in a general discussion of the piece's political message and more interested in a precise account of the linguistic mechanisms by which the satire achieves its effect, and what those mechanisms reveal about the pragmatics of sexist language more broadly. If there are places where the presuppositional reading strains — where the satire's effect is better explained by other pragmatic phenomena (implicature, expressives, or something else) — I'd like those flagged as well, since part of what I want the students to see is the limits of any single theoretical lens.
