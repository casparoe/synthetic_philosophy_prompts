After retiring from a career in experimental physics, I have been working through the philosophy of quantum mechanics, and I keep returning to the Born rule problem in the many-worlds interpretation. The difficulty, as I understand it, is this. If the universal wave function always evolves unitarily and measurement is only decoherence-induced branching, then every outcome with nonzero amplitude is realized somewhere in the branching structure. Yet the Born rule tells us to expect outcomes with probability proportional to the squared modulus of the amplitude. In a theory with no collapse and no single actual outcome, it is not obvious what these probabilities are probabilities of, or why a rational agent who accepts the many-worlds interpretation should apportion credence by Born weights rather than by, say, simply counting branches. The leading strategies—decision-theoretic derivations, envariance, and self-locating-uncertainty accounts—claim to recover the Born weights without adding an independent probability postulate, but critics charge that each of them smuggles the weights in somewhere.

I would like your credence, expressed as a single number, that the Born rule can be given a satisfactory in-principle justification within the many-worlds interpretation without positing it as an independent law, where by satisfactory I mean non-circular, not merely stipulative, and likely to persuade a reasonable but skeptical philosopher of physics. Please decompose the number. I imagine the terms include at least the following: the probability that some version of the many-worlds interpretation is correct; the probability that, conditional on its being correct, the probability problem is a genuine problem rather than one that dissolves under closer analysis; the probability that at least one of the extant strategies, or a close variant, succeeds; and the probability that such a derivation would explain our Born-rule statistics rather than merely restate the Born rule in decision-theoretic or epistemic dress. Assign a rough probability to each term and show how they combine. If you think the question is ill-posed, assign a probability to that possibility and fold it into the decomposition rather than declining to answer.

Then tell me which term your final number is most sensitive to, and, most important, which argument, theorem, or result would most change your mind, and where you think your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
