This is the kind of thing I end up thinking about at three in the morning instead of sleeping. Bear with me.

I teach a seminar on Galileo, and every year we read the Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina — you know the one, where he drops that line from Cardinal Baronio about the Holy Ghost intending to teach us "how one goes to heaven, not how heaven goes." The students always want to cast Galileo as a secular hero, a proto-Enlightenment rationalist who wanted to free science from the Church. But that's not quite right. Galileo was a believing Catholic who thought he was doing the Church a favor by showing that a literal reading of certain passages was theologically unnecessary and pastorally harmful. He wasn't trying to dethrone scripture; he was trying to protect it from being embarrassed by new evidence.

Here's what I can't stop turning over. Suppose we could bring Galileo to a seminar today and hand him three documents: the Pontifical Biblical Commission's 1993 document *The Interpretation of the Bible in the Church*, which endorses the historical-critical method; Vatican II's *Dei Verbum*, which says scripture teaches without error only what pertains to our salvation; and John Paul II's 1992 address acknowledging that the Church erred in condemning Galileo. On the surface, Galileo won. The Church now says basically what he said: when demonstrated physical knowledge conflicts with a literal reading of scripture, scripture must be reinterpreted. But the *way* the modern Church does this — source criticism, form criticism, treating biblical texts as human documents shaped by the agendas and limitations of their authors — is a long way from Galileo's own hermeneutic. Galileo's model was: scripture is divinely inspired, but it accommodates itself to the understanding of ordinary people, so we shouldn't press it for astronomical information. The modern model is: scripture is a human product, albeit one through which God speaks, and its claims about the physical world reflect the prescientific worldview of its authors. Those are different claims, and I'm not sure Galileo would have been comfortable with the second one.

So here's my question. If you had to write the paper Galileo would have written in response to the Church's modern hermeneutic — the one that's been vindicating him since Vatican II — what would his verdict be? Would he see it as the logical extension of his own principles, or would he find something in it that goes too far, that dissolves the authority of scripture in a way he never intended? And if the latter, what exactly would his objection be, and would it have any force?

(2) **The modern Catholic hermeneutic** — what *Dei Verbum* and the historical-critical method actually say about scripture and science (roughly 200 words). (3) **Where Galileo would agree** — the points of genuine continuity between his position and the modern one (roughly 200 words). (4) **Where Galileo would push back** — the specific commitments of the modern approach that he would find troubling and why, with your best guess at what his counterargument would look like (roughly 300 words).
