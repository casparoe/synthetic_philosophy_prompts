I teach an upper-level undergraduate course on paradoxes and self-reference, and in two weeks we reach the surprise exam paradox. The class is about eighteen students, and I have a seventy-five-minute session. I want to open with a structured activity before the paradox is named or lectured on, one in which the students themselves produce the backward induction and then have to live with what follows. Please design a runnable in-class game along the following lines. I am willing to run a real surprise quiz with low stakes — a few extra-credit points or a bag of candy — with the day committed in advance by a sealed envelope. The students may deliberate openly and coordinate, and there should be a payoff scheme or scoring rule, not involving real money, that rewards correct predictions of the quiz day and punishes confident but wrong predictions. The rules should be arranged so that a well-coordinated group will predictably reason: it cannot be the last day, because by then they would know; so it cannot be the second-to-last; and so on — arriving at the conclusion that the announced quiz is impossible or that the announcement is inconsistent. Then the quiz should happen on a day of my choosing, and the debrief should map what occurred onto the structure of the paradox: the two clauses of the announcement, the students' knowledge of their own future reasoning, the way the elimination argument undermines its own premises, and the difference between a quiz's being psychologically unexpected and its being unknowable in the sense the argument requires.

Include exact rules and materials, a timeline for the session, the payoff scheme, a debrief sequence with the questions I should ask and when, and a list of the failure modes I should expect the first time I run it — a student who spoils the day, a group that refuses to commit, someone who cries foul that I lied — with concrete ways to handle each. Be explicit about which feature of the paradox the activity cannot reproduce, presumably the idealized epistemic state the argument assumes, and where your design leans on a contested reading of the paradox. Finally, tell me what would change your mind about the design: which objection to the activity, or which alternative resolution of the paradox, would lead you to structure it differently.
