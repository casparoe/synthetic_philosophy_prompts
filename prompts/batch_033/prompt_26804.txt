Hey, so I'm working on a novel — sci-fi, near future — where teleportation becomes commercially available but there's this underground movement of people who refuse to use it because they think it kills you and replaces you with a copy. The novel follows a married couple who split over this: one of them teleports for work, the other won't. I've been reading up on Parfit's teletransporter thought experiment to get the philosophy right, and I wrote up some notes that I'm planning to turn into an essay (maybe publish it somewhere as a companion piece to the novel). Could you read these notes and give me comments? Specifically I want to know: (a) did I get the philosophy basically right, or am I misrepresenting Parfit anywhere, (b) is there a more interesting angle I'm missing that would make the essay sharper, and (c) are there parts that drag or repeat themselves?

Here's the draft:

---

When I step into the teletransporter, a scanner records the exact state of every cell in my body. That information is transmitted to a receiver on Mars, where a new body is assembled from local matter — atom for atom identical to the one that was destroyed in the scanning process. The person who walks out on Mars has all my memories, my mannerisms, my intentions. From the outside, they're me. But am I the one who wakes up on Mars?

Parfit's move is to say that we're asking the wrong question. The question "is it me?" presupposes that personal identity is what matters in survival. But Parfit argues that what we really care about — what matters — is psychological continuity and connectedness: that there's a chain of overlapping psychological states linking my current self to my future self. Memory, intention, character, beliefs. The teletransporter preserves all of that. So if psychological continuity is what matters, the teletransporter gives me what matters in survival, regardless of whether the identity question has a determinate answer.

This is where it gets interesting for my novel. If identity isn't what matters, then the anti-teleportation movement in my book is wrong about the metaphysics. But — and this is the thing I keep coming back to — they might be right about something else. Parfit's argument works if you accept that psychological continuity is all that matters. But the person being destroyed in the scanner experiences something: their stream of consciousness ends. The copy on Mars has a new stream of consciousness that feels continuous with the old one, but it's a new one. There's a death that happens in the scanner, even if no one mourns it.

Parfit anticipates this with his division cases: if each hemisphere of your brain is transplanted into a different body, and both resulting people are psychologically continuous with you, then neither is identical to you (since identity is one-to-one). But both have what matters. So identity can't be what matters. Fair enough. But the teletransporter case is different from the division case in one crucial way: in the division case, the original isn't deliberatly destroyed. The teletransporter destroys the original as part of the process. That feels like it should matter metaphysically, not just emotionally.

I think what I'm really writing about is whether the "what matters" relation includes the continuity of the underlying substrate. If I'm uploaded to a computer, is that me? If my neurons are replaced one by one with silicon chips, is that me? Parfit would say yes, because psychological continuity is preserved. But I'm not sure my characters would agree, and I'm not sure I do either.

---
