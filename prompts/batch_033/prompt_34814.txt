The experience machine thought experiment has been at the center of debates about well-being since Nozick introduced it in 1974, and yet after half a century the field seems no closer to consensus on what it shows. The basic challenge is familiar: if you could plug into a machine that gives you any experience you want, indistinguishable from reality, and you would believe the experiences were real while inside, would you plug in? Nozick's original claim was that most of us would not, and that this reveals we value something beyond pleasant experiences — perhaps authentic connection to reality, genuine achievement, or being a certain kind of person. Hedonists about well-being have had fifty years to respond, and their replies now fall into recognizable camps: some bite the bullet and say we should plug in, and that our reluctance is a mistake; some argue the machine cannot actually deliver the experiences we want, because what we want is to actually do things, not merely to have the experience of doing them, so the machine's offer is incoherent; some diagnose our reluctance as status quo bias, loss aversion, or an evolutionary heuristic that misfires in this artificial case. Meanwhile, anti-hedonists treat the near-universal reluctance to plug in as close to a decisive refutation. Both sides have heard all the arguments, and neither moves.

I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is, not a verdict on who is right. Is this a disagreement about facts — about what the machine can actually deliver, about what humans would in fact choose under ideal conditions, about what our reluctance actually tracks? Is it a disagreement about bedrock normative intuitions that neither side can argue for, so that the thought experiment is really just a device for eliciting which intuitions you already had? Is it a verbal dispute about the meaning of "well-being," where the two sides are using the term to pick out different things? Or is it something else — perhaps a disagreement about the proper method for doing philosophy, about whether a thought experiment with a vivid scenario can serve as a reliable test of a theory at all? My working hypothesis is that the thought experiment is doing two things at once: it tests a theory of what well-being consists in, and it also tests a theory of what we care about when we deliberate. The impasse, on this view, comes from the fact that these two questions can come apart, so the case is genuinely ambiguous and each side is right about something different. But I am not confident in this diagnosis. Give me a careful analysis of the structure of the disagreement, grounded in what each side actually says, and tell me what would count as progress — a conceptual distinction, an empirical finding about how people actually reason about the case, a reformulation of the question — or whether we should instead expect the impasse to be permanent and why.
