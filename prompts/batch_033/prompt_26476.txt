A couple of friends of mine have been arguing for weeks about a thought experiment one of them came across. It runs as follows: a machine scans every detail of your body and brain, destroys the original, and then assembles an exact atom-for-atom duplicate on Mars. The duplicate possesses all your memories, your personality, and is entirely convinced it is you. The question is whether you ought to step into it.

My two friends took opposite positions.

Dan said he would categorically refuse. The person on Mars is a copy, and a copy is not the same person as the original — it is a new being who merely believes himself to be Dan. Stepping into the machine amounts to committing suicide while simultanneously creating a replacement.

Sarah said she would step in without hesitation. What matters for survival, she argued, is not the persistence of the same physical material but psychological continuity: the person on Mars remembers being Dan, possesses Dan's character, intentions, and projects. If Dan had gone to sleep and woken up on Mars with all his memories intact, nobody would claim he had died. The only difference here is the mechanism of transport.

Dan replied that Sarah was overlooking the branching case. If the machine malfunctioned and failed to destroy the original — so that there were now two Dans, one on Earth and one on Mars — Sarah would have to concede that the Earth Dan is the one who steped into the machine and is still standing there. So plainly the Mars Dan is a copy, and destroying the original simply makes the copying lethal.

Sarah responded that the branching case actually demonstates the opposite. In that scenario, both individuals are equally Dan, because both are psychologically continuous with the pre-scan Dan. What the branching case reveals, she said, is that numerical identity is not the right concept to focus on — what matters is that one's psychology continues, and it can continue in more than one place. The destruction of the original in the standard case simply means there is only one continuer, which is unproblematic.

Dan countered that this was absurd on its face: if someone informed him he was about to be tortured, but then reassured him that a psychologically continuous duplicate would also be created who would not be tortured, he would still care intensely about which one he was. So identity clearly matters, not merely psychological continuity.

Neither has shifted. I can see the force of both positions and genuinely do not know how to adjudicate this. Could you go through their exchange point by point and assess who is right about what, and whether the disagreement turns on something neither has articulated? Please write your answer in British English.
