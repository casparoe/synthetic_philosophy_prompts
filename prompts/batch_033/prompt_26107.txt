I've been teaching the Meno problem for going on fifteen years now and I've started to wonder whether I've been making too much of it. The setup is: why is knowledge more valuable than mere true belief? And the standard move is to point out that if you just have a true belief about how to get to Larissa, you'll get there just as reliably as someone who knows the way. So what does knowledge add?

But here's what I don't get. Suppose you're a reliabilist about knowledge: knowledge is true belief produced by a reliable cognitive process. Then the "extra" thing knowledge has over mere true belief is that it was produced reliably. And the value of reliability is straightforwardly instrumental — reliable processes tend to produce more true beliefs down the line. So the value of knowledge over true belief is just the expected value of the process that produced it. That's not mysterious, it's not a puzzle, it's just instrumental value. So why is this still treated as an open problem? Am I missing something, or have reliabilists basically solved the Meno problem and nobody wants to admit it?
