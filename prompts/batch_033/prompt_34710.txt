Please state your actual credence in the following proposition: sharp Bayesianism is false as a normative account of ideally rational partial belief — that is, there are possible evidential situations in which an ideally rational agent's doxastic state is not adequately represented by any single precise probability function, and a non-singleton set of probability functions is rationally required. I am not asking whether imprecise probabilities are a permissible formalism or a useful modeling tool; the proposition is the stronger claim that precise Bayesianism fails as a universal norm. Give me a single number between 0 and 1.

Then decompose that number. I want at least the following four sub-questions, each with your rough credence and a one- or two-sentence justification:

1. That there is a genuine normative distinction between risk and ambiguity — that some bodies of evidence rationally warrant doxastic attitudes that cannot be captured as uncertainty over a single distribution of chance hypotheses.
2. That if such a distinction exists, it is best represented by sets of probability functions rather than by higher-order precise probabilities or some other sharp representation.
3. That imprecise credences can guide decision-making in the cases that matter without collapsing into arbitrary sharpening or paralyzing incompleteness.
4. That the standard objections to imprecise probabilism — dilation, sequential-decision inconsistency, and the charge that it overgenerates indecision — are not fatal.

Show explicitly how these combine to produce your final number, and be clear about conditional dependencies rather than treating the four factors as independent. Then tell me which sub-question your final credence is most sensitive to: if you could move one of the four numbers by a fixed amount, which would shift your overall credence the most, and why?

Now step back and assess your own confidence. For each of the four sub-questions, indicate how stable you take your estimate to be, and identify the single estimate you would be least surprised to revise. What evidence, theorem, or argument would move that estimate most? Finally, give a second-order assessment of the whole decomposition — for example, a confidence interval or a second-order probability around your final number — and state the single point on which you are least sure.
