I work in the provenance office at a midsize museum, and I'd like you to turn Nozick's entitlement theory into a decision procedure we can actually run on an object file. Our director keeps saying the rule is simple: check that each step in the chain was just, and don't worry about the overall pattern of who ended up with what, because a just distribution gets knocked askew the moment people freely choose what to do with their money — the Wilt Chamberlain point. But that gives me nothing for the cases I actually see, where the chain has gaps, the original acquisition happened under colonial rule or in wartime, or a later clean sale might or might not scrub an earlier theft. Could you write the proceedure as a sequence of questions with, for each one, what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure returns a no, and where it has to hand off to a person because the question is a value call rather than a fact call? Also spell out what 'rectification' requires at the level of a daily workflow, not just in theory. We usually have about three working days per object, the curator and the director both sign off, and the stakes can include lawsuits and repatriation demands. Then run it on three files: (1) a Benin bronze bought by the museum in 1953 from a London dealer, no earlier provenance; (2) a painting sold by a Jewish family under duress in 1938, bought at auction in good faith in 1955, donated to us in 1970; (3) a cuneiform tablet we bought last year with an export permit, but the source country's law says all antiquities are state property. Flag the places where the procedure gives an answer my director won't like.
