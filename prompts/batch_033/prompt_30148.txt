A popular physics blog I read recently summarized the debate over whether the wavefunction is real in the following way:

"The debate about whether the quantum wavefunction represents something real or just our information has been going on since the 1920s. On one side, psi-ontic views hold that the wavefunction is a real physical object — roughly, a kind of field that directly pushes particles around, as in Bohmian mechanics. On the other side, psi-epistemic views, defended by Bohr, Heisenberg, and more recently by QBists, hold that the wavefunction is nothing more than a summary of an observer's knowledge or beliefs, updated when new measurement results come in, much like a Bayesian probability. For decades this looked like pure philosophy, but in 2012 Pusey, Barrett, and Rudolph proved that psi-epistemic views are ruled out. Their theorem shows that if the wavefunction were merely information, then two different quantum states could describe the same underlying physical situation — and they demonstrated that this assumption contradicts the predictions of quantum mechanics. The result is widely seen as a decisive refutation of the epistemic program: the wavefunction must be a real feature of the world, and the only remaining question is which realist interpretation — many-worlds, Bohmian mechanics, or objective collapse — is correct."

I'm not an expert on the foundations of quantum mechanics, but I've heard enough to suspect this summary is too tidy. In particular, I know that QBists and others continue to defend broadly epistemic views even after 2012, which makes me doubt that the PBR theorem is the clean knockout the blog describes. At the same time, the blog's author clearly knows more physics than I do, so I'm not confident in my suspicions. I'd like a careful, sentence-by-sentence accuracy audit of this summary. For each claim, I want to know: what would a philosopher of physics or a quantum foundations researcher accept as correct, what is a common simplification that distorts something important, and what is just wrong? In particular, what exactly did Pusey, Barrett, and Rudolph prove, and what does their theorem actually rule out — and what does it leave untouched? And does the blog's final conclusion — that the only remaining question is which of the three realist interpretations is correct — survive once the inaccuracies are corrected?
