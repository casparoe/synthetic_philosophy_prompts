For the past two terms I have been attending a seminar on the conceptual foundations of machine learning, and we have spent an inordinate amount of time on a single point of disagreement that I am beginning to suspect is structural rather than factual. The seminar is built around a close reading of Turing's 1950 paper—the one that introduces the imitation game—and the secondary literature it has spawned. The sticking point is Turing's proposal, in the final section, that instead of trying to programme an adult mind one should programme a child mind and then educate it. Half the seminar reads this as a piece of behaviourist engineering: the child machine is a blank slate trained by reinforcement signals, and the education Turing describes is nothing more than a training regimen designed to produce behaviour that passes the imitation game. The other half reads it as a genuinely developmental proposal: Turing, they argue, was gesturing towards something much richer—a machine that learns through a process analogous to human upbringing, with genuine conceptual growth, and the imitation game is merely a diagnostic tool, not the definition of intelligence. The same passages are quoted at one another; the same secondary sources are cited; no one moves. We have been going round this mulberry bush for weeks, and I have the strong impression, as a novelist watching philosophers argue, that the two sides are not even answering the same question. One side keeps asking what Turing thought would count as a successful machine; the other side keeps asking what Turing thought education does to a mind. I want to know what kind of disagreement this is, really. Is it a substantive disagreement about the text—one that could be settled by a more careful reading of what Turing actually wrote? Is it a verbal dispute about the word 'behaviourism'? Or is it a deeper disagreement about what a learning machine would have to be—a disagreement Turing himself did not fully resolve, and which the seminar has simply inherited? And if it is the latter, is there any way of reframing the question that would allow the two sides to make contact, or is this one of those impasses where the only honest answer is that the original text is ambiguous and both readings are viable? I would be grateful for a diagnosis, grounded in the details of Turing's argument, that does not simply split the difference but tells me what the disagreement is actually about and what, if anything, would count as progress. Please write your answer in British English.
