I've been going down a rabbit hole on decision theory lately — partly because of some debates in the effective altruism community about whether we should use expected value reasoning when the probabilities get really small. Anyway, a friend of mine who did a PhD in philosophy sent me this summary of a podcast episode he'd just listened to on money pump arguments. I'll paste what he wrote:

"The money pump argument is a classic proof that intransitive preferences are irrational. Suppose you prefer apples to bananas, bananas to cherries, but — and here's the irrational bit — cherries to apples. A savvy trader can offer you a series of exchanges: you start with cherries, pay a penny to swap for bananas (since you prefer bananas to cherries), then pay another penny to swap bananas for apples (since you prefer apples to bananas), then pay a third penny to swap apples for cherries (since you prefer cherries to apples). You're back holding cherries, three pennies poorer, and you'd do it all again if offered. The argument shows that anyone with cyclical preferences can be exploited indefinitely, which is why basically all decision theorists agree that rational preferences must be transitive — it's the preference analogue of Dutch book arguments for probabilism, and it's considered just as decisive."

Something about this bugs me but I can't quite articulate it. For one thing, it seems like the argument assumes you don't notice the cycle — but what if you do? If you know the trader's whole scheme in advance, couldn't you just refuse the first trade and keep your cherries? And I feel like I've heard that some philosophers (maybe people writing about the repugnant conclusion or something?) think there are cases where intransitive preferences are actually fine. But I'm not a philosopher and I might be misremembering. Can you go through my friend's summary and tell me what's right, what's subtly wrong or overstated, and whether the conclusion — that the money pump argument decisively shows intransitive preferences are irrational — actually holds up?
