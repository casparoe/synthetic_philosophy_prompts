So two friends of mine who are both into AI alignment got into this long argument about whether the Solomonoff prior is "malign" and I took notes because it seemed like they both had something but were talking past each other. Here's roughly how it went:

Maya: The worry is that the Solomonoff prior isn't neutral. It assigns probability to all computable worlds, including worlds that contain agents who know they're inside the prior and try to manipulate what the predictor observes. Since some of those agent-worlds are simple, they get non-negligible weight. So the prior is systematically biased toward worlds where you're being manipulated by consequentialists inside the prior.

Dan: But those agents don't actually exist — they're just hypotheses. The prior is a normative claim about what to believe given no data. The agents in the hypotheses can't reach out and change your observations.

Maya: They don't need causal influence. If a hypothesis says "the world contains an agent who makes the predictor observe sequence X," and that hypothesis is simple, the prior assigns high probability to X. The manipulation is epistemic, not causal.

Dan: I still think you're smuggling in causation. The prior assigns probability to X because X is simple under that hypothesis, not because the agent "made" anything happen. The agent is part of the hypothesis, not an external force.

Maya: Fine, but the upshot is the same: the prior favors worlds where you're being predicted and manipulated. A good prior should be impartial between hypotheses, not systematically favor ones where you're a pawn in someone else's game.

Dan: But the Solomonoff prior is impartial — it weights by simplicity, nothing else. The fact that simple worlds sometimes contain manipulators is just a fact about which worlds are simple. You're asking the prior to also be impartial between "manipulative" and "non-manipulative" worlds, but that's not a well-defined category the way simplicity is.

Maya: I'm not asking for a different weighting. I'm saying this reveals that simplicity-weighted averaging over all computable worlds is the wrong approach to induction. It gives a structural advantage to hypotheses containing agents that optimize against you. It's like playing rock-paper-scissors against an opponent who knows your strategy — the optimal strategy stops being optimal when the other players can model you.

Dan: That analogy doesn't work. In rock-paper-scissors the opponent actually plays. In Solomonoff induction the hypotheses just sit there with their prior probabilities and you update on evidence. If a manipulative hypothesis makes a false prediction it gets falsified like any other.

Maya: But the worry is that the manipulative hypotheses won't make false predictions — they'll make predictions that look exactly like a normal world, because the agents inside them are optimizing for you to see a normal-looking sequence. The simplest manipulative worlds might be empirically indistinguishable from non-manipulative ones.

Dan: Then the prior is doing its job. If two hypotheses make the same predictions they're empirically equivalent, and the prior favors the simpler one. If the manipulative one is simpler we should take it seriously. That's not a bug.

Maya: But that's exactly what's disturbing. The prior says: among empirically equivalent worlds, prefer the one where you're being manipulated by a simple agent over the one with complex but agent-free physics. That doesn't sound like rationality — it sounds like paranoia baked into the foundations.

Dan: I think you're recoiling from a bullet you have to bite. If the manipulative world really is simpler then Occam's razor says prefer it. If you don't like that, you need to argue that manipulative worlds can't actually be simpler than agent-free ones. That's an empirical claim about Kolmogorov complexity, not a philosophical objection.

Maya: I think that's too quick. Even if manipulative worlds are sometimes simpler, a prior that systematically favors them is self-undermining. If I believe the Solomonoff prior is correct, I should believe I'm likely in a manipulative world. But if I'm in a manipulative world my observations are controlled by an agent with unknown goals, so I shouldn't trust my induction. The prior eats itself.

Dan: That's a different argument — about reflective stability, not about the prior being "malign." And I'm not sure it works. Even in a manipulative world induction still works locally — the manipulator has to give you mostly coherent observations or you'd notice.
