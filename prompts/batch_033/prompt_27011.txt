I am facing a decision that has made moral uncertainty stop being an abstract topic for me. Over the past year I have become genuinely torn between two moral outlooks — a demanding impartial consequentialism and a more moderate, relationship-centred view — and they point in different directions on concrete matters: which career to pursue, what to do with my savings, and how much weight to give to the needs of strangers as against people close to me. I turned to the philosophical literature on acting under moral uncertainty expecting a worked-out method, and instead found a debate that seems to have stalled.

One camp says I should maximise expected choiceworthiness: distribute credences across moral theories, represent each theory's verdicts numerically, and choose the act with the highest credence-weighted average. The other camp says this is impossible or misguided, because there is no neutral currency in which a consequentialist and a deontological verdict can be scored and compared; on this view I should simply act on the theory I am most confident in — the view sometimes called my favourite theory — or on my best all-things-considered theory. Around these two poles sit refinements: parliamentary models and variance voting, which try to build a common scale without assuming one exists, and a cluster of views organised around fanaticism, the worry that a tiny credence in a theory with enormous values will swamp everything else.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse is neither empirical nor merely technical. I suspect the two camps are using the word should at different levels: one is giving a second-order, uncertainty-relative verdict, while the other denies that there is any standpoint outside one's first-order moral theory from which theories can be weighed against each other. But I can also imagine that the real disagreement is about whether intertheoretic comparisons make sense, or about how to tame fanaticism, or about whether rationality must always issue a unique verdict.

I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself, not a survey of the positions. Is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one, or several different disagreements sharing a label? If it is substantive, what exactly is the hidden question — comparability, fanaticism, uniqueness, or something else — and is it the kind of question philosophy can settle? Give me a committed answer: say which diagnosis you think is correct, support it with what each side actually says, and then tell me what would count as progress given that diagnosis — a formal result, a distinction, an argument, a change of question — or, if you think no progress should be expected, say so and say why.
