I'm a graduate student writing a dissertation chapter on the principle of indifference, and I've hit a wall that I suspect is structural rather than just my own confusion. The literature seems to break into two camps: one camp proposes specific fixes — Jaynes' invariance under transformations, the maximum-entropy approach, various symmetry-based refinements — and the other camp argues that the whole enterprise is hopeless and that the principle should be abandoned in favor of something else (imprecise probabilities, or just living with the ambiguity). What I cannot find is a clear, motivated statement of the requirements that any formulation of the principle of indifference must meet to count as even a candidate solution. Without that, I have no way to compare the proposals to each other, or to tell whether a given objection is fatal or just a bullet that a formulation can reasonably bite.

So I'd like you to write the desiderata. What must an adequate formulation of the principle of indifference deliver? I have a rough list started, but I need each requirement sharpened, motivated by a case or a consideration, and sorted into negotiable and non-negotiable. Here's what I'm working with: (1) it must yield a unique probability distribution for the finite symmetric case — coins, dice, cards, the standard cases where everyone agrees indifference applies; (2) it must resolve Bertrand's paradox, which means either identifying the correct parameterization for the chord-in-a-circle case (and the cube-factory case, and the others) or explaining why the paradox does not impugn the principle; (3) it must not collapse into inconsistency when applied to continuous parameter spaces; (4) it must be grounded in something deeper than "assign equal probabilities when you have no reason to favor one outcome" — because that formulation is what produced the paradoxes in the first place; (5) it must specify its domain of applicability precisely enough that we can adjudicate disputes about whether a given application is legitimate. If any of these are jointly unsatisfiable — if, for instance, the demand for uniqueness in the finite case is in tension with the only available resolutions of Bertrand's paradox — I want that flagged explicitly. And if I've missed a requirement that the literature takes seriously, I want to know what it is and why it belongs on the list.

Please state your bottom line as a single sentence beginning "Verdict:".
