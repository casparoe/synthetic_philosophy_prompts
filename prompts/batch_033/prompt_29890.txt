I am the director of a health and social care commissioning board in England. Each month my team and I decide whether to shift spending between services for different population groups — for example, between services for people with severe and complex needs, people with moderate long-term conditions, and people who are comparatively well. Every proposal changes the expected distribution of well-being across these groups, and at present our decisions are made inconsistently. I want one reusable decision procedure grounded in prioritarianism, which I understand as the view that a given improvement in someone's well-being matters more the worse off that person is, and that equality in itself has no value. I also want the procedure to avoid the mistake identified by the levelling-down objection: it must never treat a reduction in inequality as a good thing when no one is made better off.

Please write the procedure so that my staff, who are not philosophers, can run it within a two-hour monthly meeting. It should take the form of a checklist or a flowchart in words: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it must hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. It should say what to do when well-being cannot be put on a single numerical scale, and it should separate the factual question of how a proposal changes people's expected well-being from the evaluative question of how much priority to give the worse off. The procedure must be explainable in plain English to the lay chair and the finance director, who sign off every decision.

Then apply the procedure to three proposals.

(a) Levelling down: a proposal to withdraw a service used mainly by a better-off group. It makes that group worse off, makes no other group better off, and narrows the gap between groups.

(b) Unequal growth: a proposal that substantially improves the expected well-being of the worst-off group but improves a better-off group by even more, so that inequality widens.

(c) Priority transfer: a proposal that transfers resources from a moderately ill group to a severely ill group. The expected loss to the moderately ill group is 12 units of well-being and the expected gain to the severely ill group is 9 units. Assume the board has pre-agreed that a unit of well-being for the severely ill counts three times as much as a unit for the moderately ill.

For each case, show which step of the procedure settles it and state the answer. Ensure that at least one case yields an answer the board will find unwelcome, and say why the procedure requires it anyway. Finally, state explicitly what the procedure cannot do: which questions it cannot answer, where it will predictably misfire, and which residual judgements the board must make for itself.

Please write the entire answer in British English.
