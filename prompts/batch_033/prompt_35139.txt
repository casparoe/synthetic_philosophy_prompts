I run a philosophy club at my high school — about fifteen students, mostly juniors and seniors, meeting for an hour after school. We've been reading some introductory metaphysics together, and last week we stumbled onto the question of whether you could have been born to different parents, or even from a different sperm and egg, and still be you. The discussion got heated fast: half the room insisted that of course they'd still be themselves if they'd been conceived a month later, because "I'm my personality, not my biology," while the other half felt something deeply wrong with that but couldn't quite articulate why. I want to channel that energy into a structured activity for our next meeting rather than let it fizzle out.

I'm looking for a design that makes participants feel the pull of origin essentialism — the idea that your particular biological origin is essential to your identity, so that a person coming from a different sperm and egg simply wouldn't be you, no matter how similar their life turned out. Ideally the activity shouldn't start by naming the view or lecturing. Instead, the rules, roles, or choices should be arranged so that students run into the intuition themselves: they should find themselves making identity judgments that commit them to something like origin essentialism before they realize what they're doing, and then have to reckon with what those judgments imply. The debrief should map what happened onto the philosophical terrain, name the view, and help students see where it can be resisted and what resisting it costs.

A few practical constraints: we have about forty-five minutes for the activity itself and fifteen for debrief. No real money or stakes beyond maybe some candy. The room has a whiteboard and we can rearrange the chairs freely. Please also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be upfront about which features of the philosophical position the activity can't capture — for instance, whether it can do justice to the difference between epistemic and metaphysical versions of the claim, or whether it smuggles in assumptions that a careful defender of the opposing view would reject. And since I want to make sure I'm not misrepresenting the state of the debate to my club, please separate what is genuinely settled among philosophers who work on this from what is still contested, and avoid presenting a contested view as though it were the consensus.
