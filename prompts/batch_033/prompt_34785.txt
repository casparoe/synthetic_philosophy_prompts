I've been working through the literature on sphexishness — Hofstadter's term for behavior that looks intelligent but turns out to be the rigid execution of a fixed program, like the Sphex wasp that will repeat its entire burrow-inspection routine from the beginning every time you nudge its paralyzed cricket an inch while it's inside. The concept is supposed to mark an objective distinction: some behavior is genuinely flexible and responsive to reasons, and some is merely mechanical. But I've arrived at an argument that seems to show the distinction cannot bear the weight it's asked to carry, and I can't tell whether the argument is sound or where it slips.

Here is the argument:

(1) Sphexishness is meant to be an objective property of an agent's behavior — the wasp really is rigid, a deliberating human really is not — and not merely an artifact of how we choose to describe the agent.

(2) The operational mark of sphexish behavior is that the agent fails to respond to a reason it has. The wasp has just confirmed its burrow is empty and safe; the cricket's position has changed in a way the wasp itself caused and can perceive; so the wasp has a reason to skip the redundant inspection — but it inspects anyway.

(3) What an agent has reason to do depends on what it believes and what it wants. The wasp wants to secure its cricket; whether it has reason to skip the inspection depends on whether it believes the burrow is still safe.

(4) Belief-attribution to non-linguistic agents is governed by something like the principle of charity: we attribute those beliefs that make the agent's behavior come out as reasonable given its goals. If the wasp re-inspects the burrow, the charitable interpretation is that it does not believe the burrow is safe — perhaps it "believes" that burrow-safety is volatile and must be reconfirmed after any interruption, however brief.

(5) So by (4), we can always reinterpret any apparently sphexish behavior as non-sphexish: simply attribute beliefs that make the behavior reasonable. If the wasp repeats the whole routine, we say it believes the situation has reset. If a human knocks on a door they just watched someone unlock, we say they believe locks can relock spontaneously. The difference between the wasp and the human is now just a difference in which beliefs we find it natural to attribute — not a difference in the structure of their agency.

(6) But belief attribution is holistic and underdetermined by behavior, as Quine's indeterminacy arguments suggest. There is no fact of the matter about whether the wasp "really believes" the burrow is safe or not, independent of our choice of translation manual.

(7) Therefore, by (5) and (6), there is no fact of the matter about whether a given behavior is sphexish. Sphexishness is an artifact of the interpretive stance we adopt, not an objective property of the agent.

(8) But (7) contradicts (1). And if (1) is false — if sphexishness is not an objective property — then the concept cannot do the work it is routinely asked to do: it cannot ground claims that certain AI systems are "merely sphexish" and therefore not genuine agents, or that sphexishness is what free will consists in transcending.

I find the conclusion intolerable, but I cannot locate the mistake. Which step fails, and why? If the argument is valid, which premise is least costly to abandon, and what does abandoning it cost for the broader uses of the concept?
