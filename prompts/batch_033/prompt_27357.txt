I am trying to work out whether I actually believe there is a moral difference between lying and merely misleading, and my gut reactions to cases keep pulling me in different directions. I would like you to extract the principle behind my verdicts, test it, and tell me where it forces me to revise.

By "lying" I mean asserting something one believes false; by "misleading" I mean saying something true, or otherwise behaving truthfully, in a way one expects to leave or produce a false belief. Here are my verdicts.

1. The abusive ex-partner. A woman I know is hiding from an abusive former partner. He appears at my workplace and asks whether she is in today; I know she is in the back office and that he means to harm her. Lying ("She no longer works here") strikes me as fine. Misleading ("She usually works from home on Thursdays" — true as a general rule, though today she came in) also strikes me as fine. I feel no moral difference between the two here.

2. The job interview. An interviewer asks directly, "Were you fired from your last job?" Lying ("No, I left voluntarily") strikes me as not fine. Misleading ("I left during a restructuring" — true: there was a restructuring the week I left, though I was in fact fired for performance) also strikes me as not fine, but distinctly less bad than the lie.

3. The manuscript. A friend asks whether I read her entire novel. Lying ("Yes, cover to cover") strikes me as not fine. Misleading ("I worked through the opening chapters and took notes" — true, but I let her infer that I finished it) also strikes me as not fine, but only a little less bad than the lie.

4. The used car. I am selling a car with a failing transmission. The buyer asks no specific question and only remarks that it seems like a nice car. Misleading — volunteering "The brakes are new and the engine was just serviced; it runs beautifully" (all true) while saying nothing about the transmission — strikes me as not fine. And here is the puzzle: it feels worse to me than lying would have felt had he asked "Any issues?" and I had answered "None." The volunteered truths seem to exploit his trust more than a flat falsehood would have.

5. The surprise party. My partner asks where we are going tonight. Lying ("A work dinner") strikes me as fine. Misleading ("Somewhere with people you like" — true) also strikes me as fine. Again I feel no difference.

6. The reference letter. I am asked to complete a reference form for a student I found mediocre. The form asks, "Would you hire this applicant?" Lying ("Yes, without hesitation") strikes me as not fine. Misleading ("She attended every seminar and was never late" — true, and written so the reader will take it as a recommendation while I omit that her work was weak) also strikes me as not fine, and in this case I feel the two are exactly as bad as each other.

The principle I keep reaching for is something like this: what matters is not whether the vehicle is a false statement or a true-but-misleading one, but a combination of (a) how much false confidence the act predictably creates, (b) how entitled the other person is to the truth about that matter, (c) how directly the other person asked for that truth, and (d) how far the other person could protect themselves by noticing and correcting the deception. That principle seems to explain cases 1 and 5 (the other person is not entitled to the relevant truth, so the deception is permissible), case 2 (a direct question about the very fact denied makes the lie worse), and case 4 (no question and no warning makes the volunteered misleading truth worse). But it appears to predict that in case 6 the lie should be worse than the misleading answer, because the form asks a direct question; and that is not what I feel.

So: is there a principle that captures all or nearly all of these verdicts? Please state it precisely, and for each clause say which case or pair of cases motivates it. Then apply it to two cases where I do not trust my own reactions, and say what it predicts and whether I should revise my gut: (i) a friend asks, "Did you invite Sam?" and I answer, "I sent the invitation to everyone in the group chat" (true, but Sam is not in the group chat); (ii) a doctor tells a patient, "There is no evidence the tumor is malignant" (true, because the biopsy results are not back yet) while privately strongly suspecting that it is. Finally, tell me honestly which of my verdicts above your principle cannot accommodate, and whether the fault lies with the verdict or with some clause of the principle I was reaching for.
