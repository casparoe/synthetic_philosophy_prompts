Every so often I go back to the numbers debate — the one Taurek set off in the late seventies about whether you're obliged to save five people rather than one — and I'm struck by how completely it has stalled, even though both sides are working from the same cases and the same facts. Taurek's move, as I understand it, is that each of the five only stands to lose one life, their own, and there is no extra subject called 'the five' whose loss is bigger than one person's loss, so the numbers don't add up to a stronger claim. The other side says this is just an intuition with a fancy gloss: of course you save the five, and refusing to aggregate harms across people is a strange view of what it is to weigh reasons at all. But nobody has produced a genuinely new consideration in decades, and everyone in the debate already grants the obvious distinctions, so it isn't as if one side simply hasn't understood the other.

My working theory is that the real dissagreement is about whether a reason can be summed across persons in the first place, and that this is a bedrock commitment neither side can argue for without already assuming it. Is that right? Or is it a verbal dispute about what 'counting' means, or two groups answering different questions — one about what we owe each individual, the other about what there is most reason to do? I want a diagnosis of the structure of the impasse, not a verdict on Taurek, and an honest answer about whether anything — a distinction, a formal result, a new kind of case — could actually count as progress, or whether we should just expect this one to stay stuck.
