I am writing a position paper for a high-school summer philosophy program on the question of which animals are conscious. Before I submit it, I would like to know whether the set of views I have settled on is internally consistent. I currently hold all of the following:

1. Consciousness is a biological phenomenon produced by nervous systems.
2. For an animal to be conscious, its nervous system must generate a single, unified point of view. I think this requires the central integration of information, rather than many specialized subsystems operating in parallel.
3. Central integration does not require a neocortex. I think mammals and birds are both conscious, even though their forebrains are organized differently.
4. I think octopuses are conscious, even though a large majority of their neurons are located in their arms and their arms can act semi-independently of the central brain.
5. Flexible learning and novel problem-solving are strong evidence for consciousness, whereas simple withdrawal reflexes are not.
6. I think bees are not conscious, even though they have a centralized nervous system and are capable of flexible-looking learning and navigation.
7. I think there is nothing it is like to be a sea anemone or a jellyfish, which have nerve nets rather than a centralized nervous system.

Please audit this set for consistency. Identify which pairs of commitments are genuinely in tension, rank those tensions by severity, and explain which pairs only appear to be in tension and why. For each genuine tension, say what the least costly revision would be and what it would require me to give up.
