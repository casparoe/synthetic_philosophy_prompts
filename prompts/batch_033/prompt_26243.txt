I read a chapter on imprecise probabilities in an introductory philosophy of science textbook. It summarizes the view roughly as follows:

Bayesians hold that rational agents have precise degrees of belief for all propositions, obeying the probability calculus. Imprecise probabilists reject this: they argue that our evidence rarely pins down a unique probability, so we should represent belief states with sets of probability functions — your credal set. This move is motivated by the Ellsberg paradox, where people prefer known risks over unknown ones, revealing that they treat ambiguity as distinct from risk. Imprecise probabilities formalize this by letting you be indeterminate between, say, 0.2 and 0.8 regarding some event rather than picking a single number. The view is sometimes called "second-order Bayesianism" because it models uncertainty about probabilities themselves.

Critics raise two main objections. First, imprecise probabilities seem to lead to decision paralysis: if your credal set contains functions that disagree about which action maximizes expected utility, the theory gives you no determinate verdict. Defenders reply that you simply apply the maximin rule — choose the action with the best worst-case expected utility — and the problem disappears. Second, there is the problem of dilation: when you condition on a partition of possible outcomes, your probability interval for a related event can expand to [0,1], which looks like complete ignorance. This is widely regarded as a reductio of the imprecise approach, since it shows that imprecise probabilities collapse into vacuity under perfectly ordinary reasoning.

Something about this summary strikes me as off — I recall hearing that imprecise probabilists are careful to distinguish their view from hierarchical Bayesian models with second-order probabilities, and I also vaguely remember that there are multiple decision rules beyond maximin that defenders take seriously. But I don't know the literature well enough to judge whether the textbook is being unfair or just simplifying. Which parts of this summary would a careful imprecise probabilist accept, which are misrepresentations, and do the two objections land with the force the textbook suggests?
