I work as a journalist covering science and ideas. Several times a month I have to decide how much credibility and prominence to give to official statements that are contested, misleading, or demonstrably false, and I want a reusable editorial procedure rather than an ad hoc instinct. My background is in science writing, not political philosophy; I can spend thirty to sixty minutes on each decision before deadline, and my section editor is the one who finally signs off, but I am expected to bring a recommendation with reasons. I have read Hannah Arendt on political lying—her distinction between factual truths, which are contingent and fragile, and truths of reason, which are compelling and do not depend on witnesses; her argument that the capacity to lie and the capacity to act share the same root in imagination; and her account of the Pentagon Papers as a case of systematic image-making and defactualization, in which problem-solvers lost track of the facts they had created. Please turn these ideas into a step-by-step procedure I can actually run on a disputed official claim before filing. The procedure should: specify the questions to ask in order; say what counts as a sufficient answer at each step; distinguish an ordinary political lie from organized image-making or defactualization; address how the procedure should weigh the official’s likely future behavior, not only the current claim; and identify every point where the procedure cannot eliminate judgment and I or my editor must decide a values question. Then apply the procedure to three worked cases: (1) a defense ministry release describes a failed operation as a success, and a reporter has obtained internal telemetry showing the operation failed; (2) a public health agency repeatedly presents an optimistic model as a prediction while its own scientists’ current uncertainty is buried in an appendix; (3) a senior politician says a violent event was staged, offers no evidence, and frames the claim as merely asking questions. In at least one case, the procedure should give a recommendation I will not like, and you should say so. End by stating what the procedure cannot detect or settle—for example, the difference between self-deception and cynical manipulation, or when official secrecy is legitimate—and where a journalist would be misusing Arendt by treating the procedure as a proof of bad faith rather than a guide to coverage.
