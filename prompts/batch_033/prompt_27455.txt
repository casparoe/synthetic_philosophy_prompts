I've noticed something about how people talk to and about AI chatbots, and I'm trying to figure out if there's a name for the specific conceptual issue it raises.

When I use ChatGPT or similar tools, I catch myself saying things like "it thinks I'm asking about X" or "it got confused" or "it's trying to be helpful." I know, intellectually, that these systems don't have beliefs, intentions, or confusion in any literal sense — they're generating text based on statistical patterns. And yet, this way of talking feels almost unavoidable. It's not just a convenient shorthand; when the system gives a wrong answer, I feel a flash of annoyance at it, as if it were a person who made a mistake rather than a program that produced an undesirable output.

What's interesting is that this seems to cut both ways. Some people insist we should never use mental-state language for AI because it's misleading and anthropomorphizing. Others argue that for sufficiently complex systems, treating them as if they have beliefs and desires is actually the best way to predict and understand their behavior — that it's pragmatically justified even if we don't think the system literally has mental states.

But here's the specific phenomenon I'm curious about. There's a gap between two things: (1) the practical indispensability of mental-state language for interacting with these systems, and (2) the fact that we don't actually attribute genuine mental states to them. What I want to know is whether this gap itself has been named and analyzed. It's not quite the same as the general question of whether AI can think. It's more specific: even if we agree that current AI doesn't have beliefs, we still seem to need belief-talk to interact with it effectively. Is there a term for this — the situation where a framework is technically false but practically mandatory? And if there isn't, how should we think about the status of the "beliefs" we attribute in these cases? Are they useful fictions? A different category of belief-ascription? Something else entirely? I'm less interested in the label itself and more in understanding what exactly we're doing when we talk this way and whether it's philosophically defensible.
