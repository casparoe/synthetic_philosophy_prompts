I've been going down a rabbit hole on art forgery and I keep tripping over the same pattern, and I'm hoping someone can tell me whether it has a name. Here's the thing. When a forgery is exposed, people don't just revise their appraisal — they revise their perception, and they do it retroactively. After van Meegeren was revealed, critics who had praised his Vermeers started saying the paintings had always looked wrong to them: the figures were wooden, the lighting was theatrical rather than luminous, the emotion was sentimental rather than profound. They weren't saying "I now judge it differently"; they were saying "I now see it differently, and I suspect I was always dimly aware of something off." The same thing happens with literary hoaxes: once the true authorship comes out, readers start noticing clunky sentences and borrowed ideas that they sailed right past before. It even shows up in blind taste tests — tell someone the wine they just enjoyed came from a screw-top bottle and suddenly it tastes thin. I'm not asking about the general question of why we value originals over forgeries (I know Goodman and Dutton and others have covered that ground). I'm asking about this specific cognitive move: the retroactive perceptual retrofit, where a belief about provenance reaches backward and rewrites the sensory record. Does this have an established name in aesthetics or philosophy of perception? If it doesn't, what are the nearest concepts, how does this differ from each of them, and what would you call it if you had to give it a label that carves it at the joints?
