Two members of my philosophy department got into an argument at a colloquium reception about money pumps and whether they actually establish that cyclic preferences are irrational. I took notes because I thought I might want to reconstruct it later. Here is roughly how it went:

(1) Dr. Chen said that the money-pump argument is the best reason we have for requiring preferences to be transitive: if you prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A, a trader can offer you a series of trades—each of which you accept by your own lights—that leaves you back where you started but poorer, which is a clear sign of irrationality.

(2) Dr. Okonkwo replied that this only works if you do not notice the cycle. A rational agent who knows her own preferences can foresee where the sequence of trades is heading and simply decline the first trade, or decline to complete the cycle. The money pump requires the victim to be myopic, and the irrationality is the myopia, not the cyclic preferences.

(3) Chen answered that this is exactly the point: to avoid being pumped, you must adopt a standpoint outside any single moment of choice and treat your preferences across time as a unified plan. Cyclic preferences force you either to be myopic and get pumped, or to be sophisticated and act against your own current preferences at some point in the cycle—which is just another way of saying you cannot consistently act on them. Either way, they fail as action-guiding preferences.

(4) Okonkwo said this conflates two things. Having cyclic preferences does not mean you are committed to acting on each pairwise comparison whenever it is offered. You can prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A, and still have a perfectly coherent policy: never accept a trade that is part of a cycle you can identify. That policy does not require you to act against any preference; it merely requires you to have preferences about sequences of trades in addition to preferences about outcomes.

(5) Chen said that preferences about sequences just are higher-order preferences, and the question is whether they can be made consistent with the base-level cycle. If you prefer A to B but also prefer not to trade A for B when that trade would initiate a cycle, you have conflicting preferences about the same objects under different descriptions, and that is a different kind of irrationality.

(6) Okonkwo said that is simply the familiar point that preferences are intensional—they are sensitive to how options are described—and there is nothing irrational about that. We all prefer the salad when it is described as healthy and the burger when it is described as tasty, even if they are the same meal on different occasions.

At that point someone pulled them away to get more wine, and I never heard the resolution. Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, and then say who has the better overall position and what, if anything, both are missing. Please write your answer in British English.
