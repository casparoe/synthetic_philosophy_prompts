I keep noticing a pattern in how people (including me, even after I've spotted it) choose between gambles, and I'm wondering whether it has a name and a settled diagnosis. Here's a concrete version. Pair one: A gives you $1,000 for sure; B gives an 89% chance of $1,000, a 10% chance of $5,000, and a 1% chance of nothing. I take A, and so does almost everyone I ask. Pair two: C gives an 11% chance of $1,000 and an 89% chance of nothing; D gives a 10% chance of $5,000 and a 90% chance of nothing. Here almost everyone, me included, takes D. But the only thing that changed between the pairs is the common 89% branch: in pair one it pays $1,000 no matter what, in pair two it pays nothing no matter what. Since that branch is identical across the two options within each pair, it feels like it should be a wash, and your preference shouldn't reverse. Yet it does.

I've also seen a version where there's no certain option at all, just two lotteries that share a big common branch, and the same reversal happens, so I don't think its only about a sure thing. And there's a related one that feels like the same disease but with proportions: a sure $3,000 beats an 80% chance of $4,000 for most people, but a 25% chance of $3,000 loses to a 20% chance of $4,000. Both options got scaled by the same factor, so why would the preference flip? Is there a name for this specific reversal, and is the standard view that the reversal is the mistake, or that the 'common branches should canel' rule is the mistake? I'm not just after the label—I want to know how the standard view carves this up, whether the sure-thing version and the proportional version are the same phenomenon or two different ones, how it relates to the thing people call the certainty effect, and where the line is between a rational preference and a violation of a rule that maybe shouldn't be a rule.
