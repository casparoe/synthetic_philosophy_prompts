Last week I had a couple of friends over for dinner — I'm a retired ecologist, spent my career doing field work on bird behavior, and lately I've been reading a lot of philosophy of biology. Anyway, two of my guests got into it over costly signaling theory and I've been turning it over ever since. I thought I'd run the exchange by you.

Here's roughly how it went.

(1) Mark (evolutionary psychologist) was talking about a paper that used costly signaling to explain extreme charitable donations — the idea being that giving away a public, hard-to-fake sum of money honestly signals your resources and generosity. He said it's a textbook case: the cost is real, the signal is hard to fake, and the payoff in reputation makes sense.

(2) Elena (philosopher of science, works on animal behavior) pushed back and said she's starting to worry that costly signaling explanations are becoming unfalsifiable. Her point was: you can take almost any human behavior, identify something about it that's difficult or expensive, and call it a costly signal. But the theory doesn't tell you in advance which behaviors are signals and which aren't — it only tells you that if something is a signal, making it costlier makes it more credible. So it's a constraint on signaling, not an explanation of why a particular behavior exists in the first place.

(3) Mark said that's too strong. The theory does make testable predictions: when the incentive to deceive is higher, signals should be costlier, and receivers should be more skeptical of cheap signals. He said those predictions hold up cross-culturally and you can't just dismiss them.

(4) Elena granted that but said the deeper problem is that "cost" in human cases is almost always identified after the fact. With a peacock's tail you can measure the metabolic burden independently of the signaling hypothesis. With a big charitable donation, the "cost" is the money given away — but the person might have given for genuinely altruistic reasons, or for tax benefits, or because they were pressured. The theory doesn't give you a way to say whether the cost is actually for signaling or is just a side effect that gets labeled as signaling post hoc. At that point you're not explaining the behavior, you're redescribing it in adaptationist language.

(5) Mark said that's a general problem with evolutionary explanations of human behavior, not a special problem for costly signaling. Every adaptationist hypothesis has to deal with the possibility that a trait is a byproduct or a co-opted spandrel. But that doesn't mean we throw out the framework — it means we're careful about alternative hypotheses.

(6) Elena said that's fair in principle, but she thinks costly signaling has a worse version of the problem because the notion of "cost" is so elastic. If the behavior is expensive, the cost is financial. If it's risky, the cost is risk. If it's time-consuming, the cost is time. If it's painful, the cost is pain. She said the theory is unfalsifiable in practice even if it's falsifiable in principle, because there's always some dimension of cost you can point to.

At that point I poured more wine and changed the subject, but I've been chewing on it since. I'd like you to go through their exchange and say who's right about what — not just who wins overall, but at each step where the argument actually moved. I'm genuinely unsure: Mark's point about this being a general adaptationism problem seems right to me, but Elena's worry about the elasticity of "cost" also seems like it bites harder here than for, say, hypotheses about foraging efficiency or mate choice. And I wonder if they're both missing something about the original handicap principle — Zahavi's idea was that the cost isn't just correlated with honesty, it's the very mechanism that guarantees it. Does that distinction matter for their argument, or is it a red herring?
