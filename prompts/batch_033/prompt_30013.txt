Question. Quine's radical-translation scenario is standardly introduced through the example of the native utterance 'gavagai,' elicited by a passing rabbit. Explain what the example is meant to show, and distinguish carefully between the inscrutability of reference and the indeterminacy of translation (holophrastic indeterminacy). In particular: does the gavagai example, taken by itself, establish the indeterminacy of translation, or only something more local? Be explicit about whether the example operates at the sub-sentential level, the whole-sentence level, or both.

Student's answer. In radical translation, a field linguist confronts a language with which she has no prior acquaintance. She hears a speaker utter 'gavagai' just as a rabbit runs by, and from the speaker's verbal behavior and the surrounding environment she naturally takes the utterance to be about a rabbit. Quine's point is that all the behavioral evidence available to her is equally compatible with several different translations: the utterance could be translated as a claim about rabbits, but just as well as a claim about undetached rabbit parts, about momentary rabbit stages, or about the property rabbithood. This shows that reference is inscrutable — there is no fact of the matter, even in principle, about which object the term picks out. Since the reference of the terms is unsettled, the translation of whole sentences is unsettled in the same way, so translation is indeterminate. The two doctrines are really the same point made at two levels; once the gavagai example gives you inscrutability of reference, you already have indeterminacy of translation. Quine's deeper conclusion is that there is no fact of the matter about meaning at all, which is why two rival translation manuals can both fit all possible behavioral evidence while still conflicting.
