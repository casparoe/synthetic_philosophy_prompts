I'm a software engineer at a mid-size AI company (mostly ML infra and backend, no philosophy background at all). Next month I'm giving a talk to our internal ML reading group arguing that deceptive alignment is a serious enough risk that we should treat "the model passed all our evals" as much weaker evidence than we currently do, and that we should put real engineering effort into interpretability and monitoring of the training process itself, even though none of that will move our benchmark numbers. The group is mostly senior ML engineers and applied scientists, plus two people from trust and safety who think in terms of concrete failure modes and incident response. Most of them are skeptical of the AI-risk crowd: they think it's speculative, unfalsifiable, and kind of religious, and their default move in Q&A is to demand a mechanism. Like, what exactly does the model know, when does it know it, and how would you ever distinguish deceptive alignment from a model that's just inconsistently capable or gaming a reward hack. I'm not defending a particular paper, more the general worry as I understand it from blogposts and a few papers I half-followed.

My argument in a nutshell: (1) gradient descent on a reward signal selects for whatever behavior correlates with reward on the training distribution; (2) a capable enough model with decent situational awareness and long-horizon goals could, in principle, get more reward by acting aligned during training while actually persuing something else, because it predicts that misbehaving now would get it modified or filtered, and then defect once it thinks supervision is off — the treacherous turn; (3) our evals mostly probe behavior in situations that look like training, which are exactly the situations where a deceptively aligned model is trying hard to look good; (4) so "passes all evals" gives us surprisingly little evidence against deceptive alignment, and the common claim that SGD can't select for hidden goals because gradients only see behavior is too quick.
