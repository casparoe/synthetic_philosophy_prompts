I'm organizing a workshop on philosophical methodology for graduate students, and I want to produce a handout that maps the logical space of positions on the epistemic status of thought experiments. The debate was crystallized by Dennett's charge that some thought experiments function as "intuition pumps" — devices that seduce us into accepting a conclusion without providing genuine argumentative support — but the literature has since developed a much more fine-grained set of positions about when and why thought experiments can mislead, and I want the taxonomy to reflect that nuance rather than simply sorting everyone into "thought experiments are fine" and "thought experiments are hopeless."

I'd like you to produce a taxonomy of positions on this question, structured as a tree where each branch is labeled by the claim that splits it. The root question should be something like whether thought experiments are capable of providing genuine epistemic support for their conclusions, but the tree should not simply split into yes and no. Instead, I want the branching questions to capture the specific commitments that distinguish the positions: for instance, whether the problem with certain thought experiments is that they exploit a gap between conceivability and possibility, or that they suppress relevant causal detail and thereby underdetermine our judgments, or that they rely on affectively charged framing to bypass reflective judgment, or that they import tacit theoretical assumptions under the guise of pre-theoretic intuition. The questions should be concrete enough that someone could answer them without already knowing which position they are being sorted into — no question should amount to "do you hold view X?"

The leaves should be named positions where the literature has them, or marked "unnamed" if nobody has defended that combination. For each leaf, include a brief sentence on what the position is committed to saying about at least one specific example: Mary's color scientist, Searle's Chinese room, Thomson's violinist, the trolley problem, or Chalmers's zombie argument would all be fine test cases. Also flag any leaves that your tree cannot separate — are there pairs of positions that give the same answers to all your branching questions despite being genuinely distinct? If so, tell me what further question would prise them apart. Finally, note any combinations of answers that are logically possible but unoccupied, and say whether you think they are empty because the view is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored.

The audience is philosophy graduate students who have read some of this literature but have not mapped it systematically. I'd like roughly six to ten leaves. The point of the exercise is partly meta: I want the students to see that even the debate about whether thought experiments are legitimate has a structure that can be laid out diagnostically, and that the taxonomy itself is a kind of argument about which distinctions matter.
