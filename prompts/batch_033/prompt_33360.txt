A friend and I just spent an evening arguing about weakness of will. His line, which he treated as settling the matter, was that a person over time is really a sequence of distinct selves, so an akratic choice is just a conflict between two parties: the present self, who wants the cigarette or the extra hour of scrolling now, and a future self, who will absorb the cost and who is not at the table. Hyperbolic discounting, he argued, explains why the present self wins so reliably: the near reward is overvalued relative to the later cost, the future self has no vote, and nothing is there to represent its interests. So, he concluded, weakness of will is not a distinct failure of agency or self-governance; it is simply the mechanics of intertemporal bargaining between successive selves, and the only real remedy is commitment devices rather than willpower or character.

I suspect the "two selves" analogy is doing more work than he admits. Lay out the analogy explicitly: what corresponds to what between a negotiation between two separate people and the akratic choice. Then identify where the mapping breaks, and distinguish the disanalogies that matter for his conclusion from the ones that are harmless. Finally, tell me whether his deflationary conclusion — that akrasia is nothing over and above steep intertemporal discounting between successive selves — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the whole argument. If a better analogy for weakness of will is available, give me that too.
