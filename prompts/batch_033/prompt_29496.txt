I am a software engineer who works on decision-support tools for allocating social benefits, and I have been trying to make my own normative assumptions explicit before I encode any of them. I wrote down my reactions to a series of cases about when a badly-off person is owed help, and I would like to know whether a single principle can explain them. A person born with a genetic condition requiring expensive lifelong care: clearly owed help. A pedestrian struck by a drunk driver and left unable to work: clearly owed help. A child whose health is permanently damaged by severe poverty: clearly owed help. A homeowner who repeatedly ignored flood-zone warnings, declined available insurance, and lost their house in a flood: I do not think they are owed a full replacement. A person who refuses a free and effective vaccine and then becomes seriously ill: not owed. A person who gradually develops a taste for fine wine and is then perpetually dissatisfied because they cannot afford it: not owed. So far the pattern looks like: compensate for bad luck a person did not choose, but not for bad outcomes that flow from their own choices.

But the pattern breaks down in the middle cases. A careful backcountry skier who takes an avalanche-safety course, checks forecasts, and carries proper equipment is caught in a rare and unpredictable avalanche: I want to say owed help. A careful person who invests their savings in a startup after extensive research, and loses everything when the startup fails: I do not want to say owed help. Both made an informed decision to accept a risk, and I cannot articulate what separates the two. A lifelong smoker who develops lung cancer: I lean against compensation. A nonsmoker with a known genetic predisposition who develops lung cancer: owed. But the smoker began smoking as a teenager, and I am unsure how much that background should matter.

Is there a principle that captures these verdicts, or most of them? State the principle precisely, say which of my verdicts it forces me to revise, and tell me what it implies for a case I have not settled: a person who chooses to work as a freelance artist knowing the income is unstable, and later cannot afford healthcare — are they owed help, and why or why not? Finally, does the principle need separate notions of how bad the luck was and how avoidable the choice was, or can one notion do all the work? Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
