I teach an upper-level seminar on rationality and the foundations of game theory, and we are about to spend a session on Douglas Hofstadter's superrationality. The centrepiece is the Platonia dilemma: twenty people are each told that a billion dollars will go to exactly one of them if and only if exactly one of them sends a telegram by noon the next day; if none or more than one send, nobody receives anything; and no coordination is permitted. The puzzle turns on the clash between two standpoints. The individual reasoner treats the others' choices as fixed and asks whether to send; the superrational reasoner notices that all twenty players face the same symmetric problem and asks instead what rule they should all follow.

I want to close the session with a strict villanelle — nineteen lines, five tercets and a closing quatrain — that makes this clash felt rather than merely stated. Let the two refrains be the two voices: one line carrying the first-person temptation (if I alone send, the prize is mine), the other carrying the superrational resolution (we must each roll the same twenty-sided die). The form should do the work: the refrains alternate and pass each other throughout the poem, then meet in the closing couplet. That meeting is not decorative; it mirrors the structure of the dilemma itself, in which the individualist's symmetric equilibrium condition and the superrationalist's maximisation problem happen to converge on the same instruction, a one-in-twenty chance of sending, for different reasons. A reader who knows the dilemma should be able to say which refrain is which and why they end up side by side; a reader who does not should still find it a good poem. Please begin with the first line of the poem itself, with no preface or commentary, and write in British English.
