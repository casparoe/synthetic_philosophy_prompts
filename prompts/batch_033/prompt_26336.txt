I'm taking a seminar on moral responsibility this term, and we just finished Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment." One thing I can't stop thinking about is how victim impact statements fit into the picture — and whether the practice is philosophically coherent.

Here's the practice: in many jurisdictions, before a judge passes sentence, the victim or the victim's family may address the court and describe the harm they suffered — the grief, the fear, the ongoing damage to their lives. The judge is permitted, and in some jurisdictions required, to take these statements into account when determining the sentence. The statements are not introduced as evidence of the facts of the crime (those have already been established); they are introduced to convey the emotional and personal impact of the offense. Their content is often raw with resentment, indignation, and moral anger.

But the same legal system also insists on principles that seem to pull in the opposite direction. It holds that justice should be impartial and dispassionate. It holds that punishment should be proportional to the gravity of the offense and the culpability of the offender, not to how the victim happens to feel about it. It treats the fact that one victim is more eloquent, or more vengeful, or more visibly traumatized than another as a morally irrelevant factor — we don't want sentencing to turn on who cries harder on the stand. And the system explicitly excludes certain emotional responses from the determination of guilt: a jury is instructed not to convict on the basis of sympathy or outrage, and the prosecution cannot appeal to vengeance as a ground for a verdict.

So there seems to be a tension. On one hand, the practice of victim impact statements appears to treat the reactive attitudes — resentment, indignation, the sense of having been wronged — as relevant to what an offender deserves, as though these attitudes are not merely expressive but carry justificatory weight. On the other hand, the system's broader norms of impartiality and proportionality seem to treat the reactive attitudes as, at best, epistemically useful signals about the severity of a harm, and at worst, as biases that threaten the fairness of the process.

What view of moral responsibility does the practice of victim impact statements actually presuppose? In particular: does it presuppose something like Strawson's picture, on which the reactive attitudes are internal to our practices of holding one another responsible and cannot be replaced by a detached, objective stance without losing something essential to moral life? Or can the practice be reconciled with a view that assigns the reactive attitudes a more modest role — say, as heuristics that help us identify harm, but that carry no independent justificatory force? And if the practice does presuppose a Strawsonian picture, what should we make of the fact that the same legal system works so hard, in other parts of the process, to suppress the very attitudes that victim impact statements invite? Is the practice philosophically coherent, or does it reflect an unresolved tension between two incompatible pictures of what it is to hold someone responsible? And if we were to take the Strawsonian picture seriously all the way through, what else in the criminal justice system would have to change?
