I've been reading the literature on whether the brain is a computer — or more precisely, whether cognition is a computational process in any sense that makes the computational level of description more than a loose metaphor or a temporary engineering analogy. This debate has been running since at least the 1960s, and the cast of characters is familiar: Putnam's early functionalism and his later rejection of it, Searle's Chinese Room, the Lucas-Penrose arguments from Gödel's incompleteness theorems, the connectionist revolt against classical computationalism in the 1980s, the embodied and enactive cognition movements, and more recently the claim that the success of deep learning vindicates something like computationalism after all — or at least shows that the brain doesn't need to be a symbol-manipulation device for the computational picture to earn its keep. I've read papers from both sides over the years, and my honest summary is that almost every argument is now well-rehearsed and nobody's credence seems to move. The anti-computationalists point to the symbol-grounding problem, or the frame problem, or the supposed non-computability of certain cognitive capacities, or the claim that computation requires a homuncular interpreter to assign semantic content to the states being manipulated. The computationalists reply that these are engineering problems rather than in-principle objections, or that the anti-computationalist is demanding too much of a theory and ignoring how much explanatory work the computational framework actually does in cognitive science. Round and round it goes, and I find it hard to tell whether anyone is even wrong.

I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. What kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it a verbal dispute about the word "computation" — where one side means something like Turing-machine-equivalent symbol manipulation over representations with semantic content, and the other means something much thinner, like the implementation of input-output functions that can be described mathematically, and the whole argument dissolves once you force both sides to operationalize? Is it a disagreement about an empirical fact that we simply haven't figured out how to test, and if so, what fact? Is it a clash of research-program aesthetics where each side is really arguing about what kind of explanation would be intellectually satisfying — mechanistic decomposition versus dynamical systems description, say — and the surface arguments about computation are just rationalizations for a deeper temperamental divide? Or is it a disagreement about where the burden of proof lies — whether the default presumption should be that the brain computes, given the success of computational explanation elsewhere, or that it doesn't, given that biological systems weren't designed by engineers? I have my own hypothesis (I suspect the term "computation" is doing too much work and the debate would mostly dissolve if both sides had to specify exactly which claims they are affirming and denying, with most of the remaining residue turning on an empirical question about the role of representation in neural processing that we don't yet have the tools to answer), but I want to hear yours. Support the diagnosis with what each side actually says, and give me an honest answer about what, if anything, could move the debate forward — a new empirical result from neuroscience, a sharper conceptual distinction that carves the question at a different joint, a formal result that constrains what any computational system can do, or whether we should simply expect the impasse to continue indefinitely and redirect our attention to questions that are better-posed.
