Military decorations like the Medal of Honor or the Victoria Cross are awarded for "conspicuous gallantry above and beyond the call of duty." The phrasing presupposes that there is a call of duty, that it can be located, and that it can be exceeded. But soldiers already have an obligation to face lethal risk in combat. So what is the "call of duty" that the medal-winner is supposed to have gone beyond? If the baseline duty already includes risking death, then throwing yourself on a grenade might simply be an especially effective way of discharging that duty, not something beyond it. If the baseline duty does not include throwing yourself on a grenade, then the military is conceding that soldiers are not required to do everything they can — which sits uneasily with the severity of sanctions for cowardice, desertion, or refusal to obey orders.

What theory of the distinction between duty and supererogation does the practice of awarding such medals presuppose? Is it a threshold view (duty requires a certain level of risk or sacrifice, and beyond that is supererogatory), a role-based view (duty is defined by the specific role, and the medal-winner transcended it), or an effort-based view (duty requires trying, but the medal-winner tried harder or in a way that was not reasonably expected)? And is the practice stable — does it collapse into inconsistency once you try to specify the line between what duty demands and what the medal honors? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say what would have to change in military law, doctrine, or culture if we concluded that there is no coherent distinction between duty and supererogation in this context.
