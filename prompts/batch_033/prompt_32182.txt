Analyze the German Federal Constitutional Court's 2006 ruling that struck down § 14(3) of the Aviation Security Act—the provision that would have authorized the military to shoot down a hijacked passenger aircraft about to be used as a weapon against targets on the ground—through the lens of philosophical theories of incommensurable values and hard choices. Your task is to produce a case analysis that runs in both directions: the theory should illuminate the ruling, and the ruling should test the theory.

You are a tenured professor of philosophy with a joint appointment in law, teaching a seminar on the structure of practical reasoning. You have spent your career working on value pluralism and the possibility of rational choice when values conflict. Take your time with this. Begin by sketching the theoretical landscape you intend to use—what competing accounts of incommensurability and hard choices are on the table (e.g., Raz's incomparability thesis, Chang's parity view, the commensurability-by-covering-considerations approach, the lexical-priority view)—and then apply them systematically to the 2006 ruling. Be precise about the mapping: what exactly was the choice the Court faced, what were the candidate values at stake, and what kind of conflict between them did the Court take itself to be confronting?

In your analysis, engage with the following. The Court grounded its ruling in the Basic Law's guarantee of human dignity, arguing that the state may not treat innocent passengers as means to an end—specifically, as the Court put it, by the state's using their killing as a means to save others, they are treated as mere objects, which denies them the value that is due to a human being for his or her own sake. What theory of the relationship between the values at stake does this reasoning presuppose? Is the Court claiming that the lives of the passengers and the lives of the potential victims on the ground are incommensurable, or that they are comparable but subject to a lexical prohibition on certain kinds of trade-off, or something else entirely? Where does the Court's position sit relative to the standard philosophical views on incommensurability, and which view does it most threaten or most support?

Then, push in the other direction. What does the episode reveal about the philosophical theories themselves? For instance, does the Court's reasoning expose a gap in accounts that treat hard choices as resolvable through parity or covering considerations—namely, that some normative systems deliberately forbid the comparison that the theory says is required? Does the ruling suggest that "hardness" in a choice can be not a feature of the metaphysics of value but a feature of a deontic prohibition on deliberation, and if so, should philosophical theories of hard choices be revised to accommodate this? Or does the fact that the Court felt compelled to offer a reasoned argument at all—rather than simply asserting a bare prohibition—show that even lexical-priority views cannot escape the demand for a comparative justification of the priority ordering itself?

Conclude by offering a clear assessment: does the German Constitutional Court's ruling function as a confirmation of one family of theories about incommensurable values, a counterexample that demands theoretical revision, or a case that the existing theories were never designed to handle—and what follows for the project of theorizing hard choices?
