I just got back from this endless argument with my friend about lying vs. misleading, and I'm still kind of fuming about it. She's convinced there's a real moral difference — like, if your partner asks where you were and you say "oh, just out with people from work," when actually you were having drinks with your ex who also happens to be a coworker, that's fine because you didn't technically say anything false. I kept pushing her on whether the distinction holds up under pressure, and she kept retreating to "well it's not the *same* as lying." We went in circles for like two hours.

Anyway, the whole thing made me think about Kazuo Ishiguro's *The Remains of the Day*. Stevens the butler is basically a case study in misleading without lying — he's so committed to "dignity" that he never quite says anything false, but he spends the entire novel obscuring his actual feelings, his employer's Nazi sympathies, his relationship with Miss Kenton, everything. When he's asked directly about Lord Darlington's past, he deflects and changes the subject. When Miss Kenton tries to get him to admit he's upset about her marrying someone else, he says something technically true about the household staffing arrangements. The man is a master of the true-but-misleading statement, and the novel shows the wreckage that results from it.

I'm trying to figure out whether the novel, read seriously through the lens of the philosophical debate about lying and misleading, actually ends up undermining my friend's position — or whether it complicates things in ways neither of us were really accounting for. Like, does Stevens's particular kind of misleading (self-deception mixed with strategic omission, all wrapped in a code of professional ethics) fit neatly into the standard philosophical categories, or does the novel reveal something about the ethics of misleading that the debate misses? And here's the thing I keep going back and forth on: if Stevens had outright lied to Miss Kenton about his feelings, would that have been *worse* than what he actually did? The novel makes his actual behavior feel almost more cruel than a clean lie would have been, but I can't tell if that's just literary atmospherics or if there's a genuine moral insight there. I'd love a reading that takes both the novel and the philosophy seriously — and that's honest about where the novel resists the philosophical framework rather than just using it as an illustration.
