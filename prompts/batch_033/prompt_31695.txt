I sit on the bargaining committee of my union local. Our contract with the employer expires in nine months, and the committee is split over whether to adopt, before talks even begin, a public resolution committing us not to accept any wage increase below six percent and no increase in our share of health-insurance premiums. The proponents say that if we tie our own hands in public, management will see that we cannot retreat, and that this is the only way to keep us from being slowly talked down over months of negotiation. The opponents say management has already been putting out public statements that wages are frozen, so our resolution would simply dare them to make their own no-concessions pledge, and the two fixed positions would carry us into a strike that most people on both sides do not actually want.

I have no academic background, but I have been trying to think this through on my own, and I keep coming back to the idea that commitments can race each other: each side makes itself immovable in order to gain ground, and the result is that both become unable to move, even when moving would be better for everyone. I do not have the vocabulary to turn that thought into a decision the committee can act on. Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before our next meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame for the months before and during negotiations, and it should engage the strongest case for adopting the public no-concessions resolution rather than simply dismissing it. The committee is practical and will set aside anything that reads like an academic essay.
