I cover philosophy and the social sciences for a general-interest magazine, and I recently encountered a version of an argument that I cannot shake. It begins with Nozick's utility monster — the familiar objection to utilitarianism — but then generalizes in a way that seems to threaten not just utilitarianism but any moral theory that allows one person's interests to be weighed against another's. I will lay it out as it was presented to me.

(1) Any moral theory that takes individual welfare seriously must allow that, at least sometimes, a sufficiently large benefit to one person can justify imposing a small cost on another. If it could not, the theory would be paralyzed whenever interests conflict — it could never justify, say, diverting a modest amount of one person's tax contribution to fund a life-saving medical procedure for someone else.

(2) For any theory that accepts (1), there must be some way of comparing the magnitudes of different people's welfare gains and losses. Call this the theory's trade-off function.

(3) For any trade-off function, we can conceive of a being — a utility monster — whose capacity to derive welfare from resources is so vastly greater than that of ordinary humans that, for any finite set of ordinary humans and any finite set of resources, the function would require transferring all resources to the monster. The monster's welfare gain from consuming everything would outweigh the combined welfare loss of everyone else, no matter how numerous the others or how acute their deprivation.

(4) A moral theory that would require transferring all resources to a single being while everyone else is left with nothing — and would require this regardless of how many ordinary humans there are, how badly they are suffering, or how well the monster is already doing — is not a plausible moral theory.

(5) Therefore, no plausible moral theory can accept (1): no plausible theory can permit interpersonal trade-offs at all.

(6) But (5) is itself absurd. A moral theory that cannot sanction any interpersonal trade-off — that cannot say it is worth mildly inconveniencing one person to save another's life — is not a moral theory at all.

(7) So the argument must contain a mistake, but where?

I suspect the move from (2) to (3) is doing the real work, and that the issue lies somewhere in whether a utility monster is genuinely conceivable in the way the argument requires. But I cannot decide whether the monster is impossible for reasons that rescue the trade-off function — diminishing marginal utility, perhaps, or distributional constraints built into any defensible function — or whether ruling out the monster already commits you to substantive moral constraints that beg the question. I would like to know which step you would reject, and what the rejection costs.
