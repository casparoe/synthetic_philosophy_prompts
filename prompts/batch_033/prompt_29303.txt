I saw The Zone of Interest a few months ago and I keep trying to figure out what it does with Arendt, because everyone I talk to seems to have a different read. We did Eichmann in Jerusalem in a political theory course, and the banality of evil line gets thrown around a lot, but the film is about Rudolf Höss, not Eichmann, and Höss doesn't fit the picture of the shallow, cliché-spouting functionary Arendt describes. He's a competent administrator, a father who reads to his kids, a man who worries about his garden and his wife's happiness while the camp runs on the other side of the wall.

I'm writing an essay and I dont want to just say the film illustrates Arendt or that it refutes her. I want a careful reading of the film through the banality of evil thesis: where it dramatizes the idea, where it complicates it, and where it seems to be doing something Arendt wouldn't have done. For instance, the sound design keeps the camp present but off-screen the whole time, and the family's comfort seems to depend on not noticing. Is that the thoughtlessness Arendt meant, or is it something more active, like the ongoing work of keeping yourself from knowing? And what about the ending, with the cut to the present-day museum cleaners? I don't need quotes or citations, just a close reading that takes both the film and Arendt seriously, and an honest account of where the film resists the Arendtian frame. What does the film add to the debate that the thesis alone doesn't?
