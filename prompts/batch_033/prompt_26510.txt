I spent forty years as a research neuroscientist studying the dopaminergic reward system and its role in decision-making. Since retiring, I have been volunteering at a nonprofit that provides structured life-planning conversations to adults at transition points—career changes, retirement, serious medical diagnoses, the dissolution of a long partnership. Our counselors are intelligent generalists, not trained in philosophy, and they see roughly eight clients per week for one-hour sessions.

I have been reading the philosophical literature on well-being, and I am struck by how Nozick's experience machine thought experiment and the subsequent debate among hedonistic, desire-satisfaction, and objective-list theories could genuinely help a person think through a concrete life decision. But the literature is dialectical and abstract; I cannot hand our counselors Parfit or Sumner and expect them to extract a usable tool.

I want a reusable decision procedure—a sequence of questions with explicit stopping rules—that a counselor can walk a client through in about twenty minutes. The procedure should operationalize the central insight of the experience machine literature: that there is a distinction between a life that feels good from the inside and a life that is good, and that this distinction bears on actual decisions. It should draw on the conceptual resources of the main theories of well-being without naming them or presupposing any one of them, and it should help the client notice when their reasons for a choice collapse into purely hedonic or desire-based considerations in a way they might not, on reflection, endorse.

The procedure must handle at least the following hard cases:

- A client whose stated values and their felt motivational pull point in opposite directions (e.g., "I believe family matters most, but I feel most alive when I am working").
- A client who responds to every question with some version of "but that is what I want" or "that would make me happy."
- A client who, when the experience machine is described to them in simplified terms, insists they would enter it without hesitation and sees no force in the distinction the procedure is trying to draw.

For each question in the procedure, specify what counts as a sufficient answer to move forward, where the procedure stops with a tentative conclusion, and where it hands the decision back to the client with an honest acknowledgment that the procedure has nothing further to contribute.

Then apply the procedure to two cases:

1. A mid-career software engineer considering leaving a well-compensated, comfortable position to teach mathematics in a public high school—a change that would reduce their income by more than half, strain their marriage, and eliminate their savings rate, but which they describe as "what I am supposed to do."

2. A recent widow deciding whether to relocate to be near her adult children, who want her close by, or to move to a retirement community in a warm climate where she knows no one but has dreamed of living since she was young.

Finally, I ask that you identify where this procedure is most likely to fail: which step rests on a contested philosophical claim, where a client with a different temperament or cultural background would reach a different conclusion for reasons the procedure cannot adjudicate, and what argument or empirical finding would lead you to redesign the procedure substantially. I want to know what would change your mind about the structure you have built.
