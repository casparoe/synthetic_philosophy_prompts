I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so apologies if I'm trampling over a hundred careful distinctions without knowing it. I've been working on a book about memory and self-narration — the way we're constantly editing our own pasts without noticing — and I fell down a research rabbit hole into the philosophy of mind, specifically this thing called the color phi phenomenon and the Orwellian/Stalinesque debate about the timing of conscious experience. For anyone who hasn't encountered it: you flash a red dot and then a green dot in quick succession at different positions, and subjects report seeing a single dot that moves smoothly and changes color mid-trajectory. The question is, when did the color change enter conscious experience? The Orwellian story says you consciously experienced red, then green, and then your memory of the first experience got quietly rewritten after the fact — the Ministry of Truth retroactively edits your experiential record. The Stalinesque story says consciousness itself was delayed by a couple hundred milliseconds, and the edited version — the moving dot that changes color — is what was presented to the theater of consciousness in the first place, before any memory tampering. Dennett's move, as I understand it, is that there's no possible experiment that could tell these apart, and the whole question is built on a mistake about there being a "finish line" where consciousness happens.

Here's what's bugging me. I've now read a bunch of the back-and-forth and it seems like everybody agrees on the data, everybody agrees on the neuroscience (such as it is), and yet the argument has been going since the early nineties and nobodys moved an inch. The two accounts are functionally equivalent in every testable way, and yet the people in the debate seem to think they're having a real dispute about how the mind actually works. I'm trying to figure out whether this is a substantive disagreement about the architecture of cognition, or a verbal dispute about what "conscious experience" means, or something weirder — like two camps with different pictures of what an explanation of consciousness is supposed to do, arguing past each other under a single heading. As a novelist I'm obviously drawn to the Orwellian picture (it's so much better for fiction — the idea that we're all walking around with a constantly revised experiential autobiography), but I don't want to build a whole book on a metaphor if the underlying philosophy is actually settled in the other direction and I just can't tell.

So my question: diagnose the structure of this disagreement. What kind of impasse is it — factual, conceptual, methodological, or what? Is there something hidden doing the work, like a disagreement about the metaphysics of time and experience that neither side is making explicit? Has anything happened in the last decade that actually moves the needle, or is this one of those debates that will just sit there forever because the question itself is badly posed? And if it's the latter, what's the better question I should be asking about the timing of experience instead — one that would actually help me write a psychologically accurate character?
