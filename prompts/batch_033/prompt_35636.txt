I'm a teaching assistant for an advanced undergraduate course in formal epistemology and rational choice theory. For the midterm, I set the following question:

"Explain what Dutch book arguments and representation theorems such as those of von Neumann–Morgenstern and Savage each establish, and assess whether, taken together, they show that a rational agent ought to maximize expected utility. Be explicit about the assumptions each argument makes and about any gap between the formal results and the normative conclusion."

One student submitted this answer:

"The Dutch book argument shows that if an agent's degrees of belief violate the probability axioms, there is a set of bets, each fair by the agent's own lights, that guarantees a net loss. This gives a pragmatic reason to require probabilistic coherence: incoherent credences make you exploitable. The representation theorems go further. Von Neumann and Morgenstern proved that any agent whose preferences over lotteries satisfy completeness, transitivity, continuity, and independence can be represented as maximizing expected utility relative to a utility function unique up to positive affine transformation. Savage proved an analogous result for acts under uncertainty, but, like von Neumann and Morgenstern, he assumes that objective probabilities are already given and then shows how to recover the agent's utility function from her preferences. Since these axioms are intuitively compelling, the theorems show that rational agents maximize expected utility. The main limitation is that the axioms are not beyond dispute: Allais-type preferences violate independence, and some think completeness is too demanding. But the formal results themselves are not in question."

I'd rather have a committed, specific verdict than a list of possible readings, so don't hedge between grades.
