I've been dealing with chronic pain for the past two years, and my neurologist recently mentioned pain asymbolia during a conversation about treatment options — specifically, whether it might be possible to target the suffering without eliminating the sensation itself. I've been reading up on it, and I came across a popular neuroscience book that summarizes the significance of the condition like this:

"Pain asymbolia is a rare condition, usually caused by damage to the insular cortex, in which patients report feeling pain but don't experience it as unpleasant. They'll say things like 'It hurts, but it doesn't bother me,' and they won't try to escape the painful stimulus. This shows that the sensory and affective dimensions of pain are produced by distinct brain systems — the sensory component tells you where and how intense the damage is, while the affective component is what makes pain matter to you. Since the affective component is separable, pain isn't intrinsically bad; its badness is a contingent feature added by a specific brain circuit. In principle, we could develop treatments that block the affective component while leaving the sensory component intact, eliminating suffering without losing pain's protective function."

The book draws the moral that suffering is not a necessary part of having a body that can be damaged, but a kind of biological add-on we might eventually opt out of.

Something about this summary feels too clean to me, and given what's at stake for me personally, I want to be sure I'm not being sold an oversimplified story. I'd like you to go through it and tell me: which claims are well-supported by the pain asymbolia evidence, which are plausible interpretations that go beyond the data, and which, if any, are straightforward mischaracterizations of what the condition shows?
