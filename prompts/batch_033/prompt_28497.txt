The split-brain literature has been accumulating case studies since the 1960s, and at this point the empirical picture is fairly stable. We know what commissurotomy patients do: the left hand unbuttons a shirt while the right hand buttons it; an object presented to the left visual field can be pointed to but not named; a stimulus shown to the right hemisphere can influence behavior while the patient confabulates a reason. What remains completely unsettled—and, as far as I can tell, has barely moved in decades—is the interpretation. Are there two conscious subjects in one skull? One subject with a disunified stream of consciousness? One subject whose consciousness switches between hemispheres? Or is the question itself somehow ill-posed?

I've been reading this literature for a while and I'm struck by how little progress there's been on the central interpretive question. The same positions—the two-minds view (Sperry, Schechter), the unified-subject view (Bayne), and various deflationary approaches that treat the question as confused—have been trading the same arguments for years. Everyone agrees on the behavioral data. Everyone agrees that consciousness is the thing we're trying to locate. And yet nobody budges.

My working hypothesis is that this isn't an empirical disagreement in disguise. No new experiment seems likely to settle it, since we already know what the patients do and say, and further neuroimaging seems unlikely to reveal a subject of experience sitting in one place or another. I suspect the real disagreement is about what would count as a satisfactory answer in the first place—about what kind of thing we're asking for when we ask how many subjects are in there. Some theorists want an answer that tracks something like a narrative or agential center of gravity; others want an answer that tracks something like information integration or unified access; still others think the question is like asking how many clouds are in a partly cloudy sky—the concept of a subject doesn't carve nature at the joints in the way the question presupposes.

I want a diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement, not a verdict on who's right. Given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress? A conceptual distinction that dissolves the question? A formal framework that makes the disagreement precise enough to test? Or is this one of those debates where we should expect the impasse to persist indefinitely, and if so, what does that tell us about the concept of a subject of experience that we're all relying on?
