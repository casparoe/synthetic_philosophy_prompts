I keep seeing people argue about free will by pointing at one case — the wasp, or the addict, or someone driving on autopilot — and I want the full spec before I read any more of that. I'm trying to put together a list of requirements that any theory of free will built around the flexibility/rigidity contrast (flexibility vs sphexishness, mechanical routine, being stuck in a loop) has to satisfy to be worth taking seriously. Don't give me a literature review or citations; I want the criteria, with the case or consideration behind each, and a clear call on which are non-negotiable and which are just one camp's preferences.

Here's the rough territory. The standard sphex wasp story: the wasp drags a paralyzed cricket to the mouth of her burrow, leaves it there, goes inside to check the burrow, comes back out, and drags the cricket in. If you nudge the cricket a couple inches away while she's inside checking, she comes out, drags it back to the threshold, and goes inside to check again — and again, indefinately. It looks smart but it's a fixed loop; she never just skips the check and drags it in. The question I want the requirements for: what does a theory of free will (or agency, or whatever you call the thing the wasp lacks and we supposedly have) have to deliver if it's going to use that flexibility/rigidity contrast as its backbone? I want a theory good enough to draw the line between the wasp and us without saying anything obviously false about either.

Things I'd want the list to cover, at minimum:
- Which cases it has to get right: the wasp, addiction, compulsion, habit, driving on autopilot, but also fast expert action that isn't deliberated at all.
- Which distinctions it can't collapse — flexibility vs mere randomness or noise, flexibility vs unpredictability, responsiveness to reasons vs behavioral plasticity, and whether free will is all-or-nothing or comes in degrees.
- What results from animal cognition it can't contradict, if it's going to put the line anywhere near humans rather than somewhere down in the wasp's neighborhood.
- Whether the wasp is even a good foil, or whether the real requirement is something like attention, self-control, or caring about what one does, which the wasp story doesn't actually touch.

For each requirement, say why it's a requirement. Then flag any pairs that can't be jointly satisfied — I suspect there's a real tension between wanting free will to involve reliable commitment (not being blown around by every stimulus) and wanting it to involve flexibility (not being stuck in a loop), and I want to know whether that's a real conflict or just sloppy talk. Finally, tell me which single requirement does the most to knock out the popular positions, and run a couple of standard contenders against the list briefly — a reasons-responsiveness view, a simple libertarian view that equates free will with indeterminism, and whatever the Dennett-style line around the wasp example amounts to.

Oh, and write it in American English.
