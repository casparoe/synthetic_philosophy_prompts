I'm an epistemologist who runs a reading group on the epistemology of disagreement at a mid-sized philosophy department. Over the past year, two senior members of the group — one a committed conciliationist in the tradition of Richard Feldman and Adam Elga, the other a committed steadfast in the tradition of Thomas Kelly — have been engaged in a structured adversarial collaboration. They've met monthly, exchanged written responses, and worked through a series of cases (from restaurant-check disagreements to religious and political belief) under the supervision of the rest of the group as neutral referees. The collaboration is now winding down, and they've asked me to draft the joint statement they should both be able to sign.

I want you to produce that document. You are to act as a professional epistemologist who is fluent in both the conciliationist and the steadfast literature, who is committed to neither side, but who is deeply sympathetic to the concerns of each. Take your time, think through the architecture of the document, and produce something that neither party would reject as a caricature of their view.

The document should have three parts. First, a numbered list of agreed claims — claims about evidence, epistemic peerhood, higher-order evidence, and the normative significance of disagreement that both camps can genuinely sign. These should be substantive, not trivial. Avoid formulations so vague that they paper over real disagreements, but don't smuggle in conciliationist or steadfast conclusions under the guise of neutrality. Second, a disagreements table. For each point of genuine disagreement, give: (a) the conciliationist's formulation of their view, in wording the conciliationist would accept as accurate; (b) the steadfast's formulation of their view, in wording the steadfast would accept as accurate; and (c) a neutral framing of the point of disagreement — what exactly the dispute is about, stripped of question-begging terminology. The table should capture disputes about the equal-weight view, the independence principle, the role of first-person access to one's own reasons, and the implications of the uniqueness thesis. Third, a list of cruxes — sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side substantially. For each crux, specify: (i) what the conciliationist predicts the answer will be; (ii) what the steadfast predicts the answer will be; and (iii) an experimental design, argument, or case study that both sides agree would genuinely bear on the crux. I suspect one crux concerns whether higher-order evidence about one's reliability can be rationally assessed without relying on the very first-order reasoning under dispute; another may concern what happens when we iterate disagreement across multiple independent peers. But don't limit yourself to these — include whatever cruxes the collaboration itself actually revealed.

Finally, if you judge that any part of the dispute is merely verbal — a disagreement in vocabulary that dissolves under careful translation — add a short section identifying which part and why. But don't force this: if the dispute is substantive through and through, say so.
