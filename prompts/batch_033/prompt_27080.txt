I'm a journalist doing a piece for a science-and-ideas outlet on why we can read a letter across a thousand different fonts — the old letter-spirit problem of what makes every a an a. I want to build one of those "which theory of the letter do you actually hold?" interactive quizzes for the piece. Please write eight to ten questions, each describing a concrete glyph case in words (single-storey vs double-storey a, a blackletter a that's basically a cage of strokes, a Cyrillic letter visually indistinguishable from the Latin a, an uppercase A, a handwritten a with the bowl left open, an @ sign, a Greek alpha, a brand logo that replaces an a with a triangle-and-bar, that sort of thing) and giving two or three answer options about whether it's an a, whether it's the same letter as another sample, or whether it's just a stylistic variant. Than write a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto positions: a feature-list or geometric view, a prototype or family-resemblance view, a topological or gestalt view, a functional or role view (it's an a if it does the job of an a in its script), a motor or production view, and a style-constitutive view that treats style as part of the letter's identity. No question should just ask "do you define a by its features?" — the point is to sort readers by what they say about cases, not by which label they like. Build the scoring from the logical space of answers rather than from the list of names, flag any answer combinations nobody seems to hold and say whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored, and tell me which positions the quiz can't really separate.
