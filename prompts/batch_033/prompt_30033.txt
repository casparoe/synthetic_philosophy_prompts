I'm retired now — spent thirty-odd years in a lab, mostly protein crystallography — and I've recently gotten into philosophy. I've been trying to work out what I actually think about lying versus misleading by collecting my gut reactions to cases first, the way I used to collect data before I'd trust a model. Here are the cases and my verdicts.

1. An ex-partner of my friend's shows up at my door, and I'm fairly sure he means her harm. She's hiding in the back room. If I say "No, she isn't here," that's a lie, and I think it's fine. If instead I say "She said she was going to stay with her sister" — true, she did say that yesterday before changing her mind — that's misleading, and I also think it's fine, and actually it feels like the better thing to say. I can't say why.

2. A neighbor I barely know asks whether I enjoyed the book she lent me. I say "I can see why you liked it" — true, though I couldn't get through it. Fine. If I'd said "I loved it," that would be a lie and I'd also think it was fine, but the first one feels cleaner.

3. A student asks whether I can write her a strong letter for a job. I can write a letter, but it won't be strong. I say "I can write you a letter" — true, but it lets her hear "a strong letter." I think that's not fine. Saying "Sure, I'll write a strong one" would be a lie, also not fine, and maybe worse.

4. My sister asks whether her biopsy came back clean. It didn't. I say "The doctor wants to talk to you in person" — true, and misleading. I think that's not fine. Saying "Yes, all clear" would be a lie, also not fine, and I'm genuinely not sure which is worse.

5. A company spokesperson says "There is no proof our pesticide causes nerve damage" when the company's own studies strongly suggest it does but stop short of proof. That's technically true, but I think it's worse than if they'd just said "It doesn't cause nerve damage." The weaselly use of "proof" makes it worse to me.

6. A collaborator asks whether I've sent him the revised draft. I say "It went out this morning" when I haven't finished it. A lie, not fine, but somehow less bad than the letter case.

7. In a paper I reviewed, the authors plotted real data on a truncated y-axis so a small effect looked enormous. Nothing on the graph is fabricated. I keep wanting to say it's as bad as fabricating the data, but I'm not sure.

Here's what I can't reconcile. I think the misleading answer to the ex-partner is better than the lie, but I think the company's misleading statement is worse than a lie would be. And I keep going back and forth on the graph. Is there a priniciple that gets all of these, or nearly all? State it precisely, and if it has clauses, say which of my cases motivates each clause. Then tell me what it says about the graph case, and about a collegue who asks if I'm coming to her retirement party and I say "I'll be out of town that weekend" — true, but I only booked the trip after I heard about the party, so I'd have an excuse. And tell me which of my verdicts the principle can't accommodate.
