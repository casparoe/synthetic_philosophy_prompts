I'm working on a paper about causation by omission and I've arrived at the following set of commitments. Before I commit to them in print, I'd like a consistency audit. Here they are:

(1) Omissions can be genuine causes. The gardener's failure to water the plant caused it to die, and this is causal in the same sense — not a metaphorical or merely pragmatic sense — as a fire causing a death. If we deny that omissions are causes, we lose the ability to make sense of a huge range of ordinary and scientific causal claims.

(2) The counterfactual analysis of causation is broadly correct: c causes e when e counterfactually depends on c, or when there is a chain of counterfactual dependence linking c to e. I am aware of preemption cases and other hard problems, but I take the counterfactual approach to be the right framework and the problems to be solvable within it.

(3) The fundamental ontology is purely positive. At the most basic level of reality, there are no negative facts, no absences, no lacks. Negative truths are made true by positive facts together with the laws, or by something else that does not require negative entities in the fundamental inventory.

(4) There is a morally significant distinction between doing harm and merely allowing harm to occur. It is not just that doings are typically worse or that the distinction is merely epistemic; the doing/allowing distinction marks a genuine normative difference in the structure of our obligations.

(5) The best explanation of the doing/allowing distinction traces it to a difference in the causal structure of the two kinds of case. Specifically, in cases of doing harm, the agent's action is a cause of the harm; in cases of merely allowing harm, the agent's omission is not a cause of the harm — or if it is, it is a cause in a different, normatively weaker sense.

(6) Causal claims require truthmakers. If it is true that the gardener's failure to water caused the plant's death, then something about the world — not merely our practices of assertion or our interests — must make that claim true.

I can see some obvious tensions, but I suspect there are less obvious ones that matter more. Please go through these and identify every pair that is in genuine tension, rank them by severity, and say what would have to be revised in each case to restore consistency. Also flag any pair that looks like it is in tension but is not, and explain why. I would rather know now than after I have written the paper.
