I'm about to turn 18 so my goverment teacher has been on us nonstop about registering to vote. But the thing is, I keep coming back to this: in a big national election, my one vote basically never changes the outcome. The chance is so small it might as well be zero. And yet the whole culture treats voting as this serious civic duty — schools run registration drives, campaigns spend real money on turnout, people act like you're a bad person if you skip it. So here's what I want to know: what is the practice of voting actually presupposing about how a single action can matter? Does it secretly assume my vote works acausally — like, by voting I'm part of a big correlated block of people who think like me, and that's where the power comes from, not from my individual ballot? Is it the same kind of logic as those acausal threat cases where someone can threaten you without any way to actually carry it out, and you should still give in because your choice tells you which world you're in? Or does it assume I should adopt a surrogate goal, something like becoming the kind of person who votes, since the actual goal of changing the outcome is basically hopeless? Or is it just running on a plain expected-value view where even a tiny chance of deciding a huge election is worth the cost of an afternoon? Reconstruct the implicit theory of action or value that the practice runs on, and tell me which parts of the practice strictly need that theory and which parts are compatible with several different ones. Then say what would actually have to change if we swapped in a straight causal expected-value view — would voting stop being a duty, would turnout drives stop making sense, would schools have to stop pushing registration? And flag any inconsistency you find, like why we treat voting as a duty but don't treat other super-low-probability good deeds the same way.
