I spent forty years in experimental physics before retiring, and I've recently turned to philosophy — specifically aesthetics — with the habits I picked up in the lab. Before I read another theory, I want to know what I'm asking a theory to do. So: what would an adequate theory of tragic pleasure have to get right? By "tragic pleasure" I mean the familiar but puzzling fact that we voluntarily seek out, pay for, and apparently enjoy artworks — tragedies, certainly, but also sad films, mournful music, harrowing novels — that depict suffering, grief, and death, the very things we would go to considerable lengths to avoid in real life.

Please act as a professional philosopher of art. Think carefully — begin by brainstorming the full range of phenomena any theory must cover, including edge cases and apparent counterexamples — and then produce a structured list of desiderata. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, and indicate whether you think it is non-negotiable or merely a preference of a particular camp. I am especially interested in whether any pair of these requirements cannot be jointly satisfied; if some subset is in tension, that is itself a result worth knowing before I wade into the literature.
