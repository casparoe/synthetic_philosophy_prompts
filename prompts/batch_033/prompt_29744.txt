I have no academic training, but I have spent a long time reading and thinking about the doomsday argument, and I have noticed that careful people who agree on the underlying arithmetic still divide sharply over whether the argument should change anyone's mind. I would like a document that forces the two sides to say, precisely, what they can jointly sign, where they part ways, and what would move either of them.

Please draft an adversarial-collaboration statement between the following two positions about the doomsday argument:

1. The proponent, who holds that the argument supplies a genuine and substantial reason to raise our credence that humanity will die out much sooner than we would otherwise expect.
2. The critic, who holds that the argument supplies no reason at all to update, and that the apparent shift is an artifact of a mistaken or unjustified assumption.

The statement should have three parts.

Part 1, Agreed claims. Give a numbered list of substantive claims both sides can sign. These should include, at minimum: a neutral statement of what the argument says, understood as probabilistic reasoning about one's own position in the sequence of all humans who will ever live; what the argument does and does not claim (for example, that it does not identify a date or a particular catastrophe, and that by itself it says nothing about which mechanism would end humanity); and the empirical starting points both accept, including the rough scale of how many humans have lived so far and the fact that the future could plausibly contain vastly more humans than the past. The agreed claims should be specific enough that both sides are staking something, not just agreeing on definitions.

Part 2, Disagreements. Provide a table or structured list of the precise points of disagreement. For each point, give the proponent's wording of their own view and the critic's wording of their own view, each stated so that its own side would sign it. Cover at least these points: whether it is legitimate to treat one's own birth rank as if it were drawn uniformly from the set of all humans who will ever live (the self-sampling assumption); whether there is any principled way to choose the reference class, and how much the conclusion depends on that choice; and whether the argument does real work or only re-encodes a prior that already expects a short future.

Part 3, Cruxes. Identify the sub-questions such that settling one would genuinely move at least one side. For each crux, give a concrete case, test, or argument that both sides agree bears on it, and state in advance what each side predicts that case or argument would show. Include at least these cruxes: the reference-class problem; whether the argument should change when we consider non-human observers, digital minds, or observers in other civilizations; and whether the argument would still seem forceful to a randomly selected member of a species that in fact goes on to have a very long future.

Finally, if any part of the dispute turns out to be verbal rather than substantive, say exactly which part and why.
