As a longtime reader of LessWrong and related forums, I have seen the doomsday argument and the Self-Indication Assumption treated too often as tribal badges, and I want the document that would result if two careful people actually sat down to isolate where the disagreement lives. Please draft the joint statement that a committed advocate of the doomsday argument and a committed proponent of the Self-Indication Assumption could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on whether our observed birth rank among humans is strong evidence that humanity will go extinct relatively soon.

The advocate's core claim, to be stated in the document in its strongest defensible form, is: conditional on the total number of humans who will ever live, my birth rank is roughly uniform among those humans; a rank near one hundred billion is far more likely if the total is a few hundred billion than if it is in the trillions; so, for a wide range of priors, observing that we are this early substantially raises the probability of near-term extinction. The critic's core claim, also to be stated in its strongest form, is: my existence itself is more strongly predicted by worlds that contain more observers, and this self-indication effect gives larger-population hypotheses a prior boost that exactly cancels the birth-rank likelihood, so the doomsday inference evaporates; what remains is ordinary reasoning about extinction risk from non-anthropic evidence.

Write the document they should have produced after a year of arguing. It should include:

1. A numbered agreed-claims section. Cover at least: the conditional likelihood-ratio core of the doomsday argument that both sides accept as mathematics; precise statements of the Self-Sampling Assumption and the Self-Indication Assumption as update rules, written so a neutral third party could implement them; what both sides agree the dispute is not about, including ordinary Bayesian updating, the reality of extinction risks, and the fact that the two assumptions differ; and any claims about reference classes, model uncertainty, or the formal cancellation result that both can sign.

2. A disagreements table. Each row should state one residual disagreement in neutral framing, with the advocate's formulation and the critic's formulation each written so its own side would accept the wording. Flag any rows that, on inspection, turn out to be verbal or to hinge only on a choice of formalism with no empirical content.

3. A crux list. Identify the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. I suspect the main cruxes are: whether the Self-Indication Assumption double-counts observers or is the only way to avoid ignoring the evidence of one's own existence; whether any non-arbitrary reference class can be selected for the sampling, and whether the doomsday conclusion is robust to plausible variations in that class; and whether the doomsday likelihood survives once model uncertainty about the far future is handled with a defensible prior over total human population. If you find a different crux more load-bearing, say so and justify it.

4. For each crux, a concrete case, argument, or empirical consideration both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Use cases from the anthropics literature where they help, such as a duplication scenario with a fair coin or a scenario in which one theory postulates vastly more observers than another and the question is whether to update toward it without any new local evidence, but describe each case self-contained enough to be evaluated without external sources. Where no test can be agreed, say so explicitly and explain what that impasse indicates about the structure of the disagreement.
