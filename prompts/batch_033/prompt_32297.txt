I'm trying to find a principle that captures my verdicts about when it's permissible to violate a constraint—like the prohibition on killing—in order to prevent more violations of that same constraint. This is the territory of the paradox of deontology, and I want to know what my own pattern of responses commits me to. Here are my cases:

(1) Switch: A runaway trolley will kill five if I do nothing. I can flip a switch to divert it onto a side track with one person, who will die. Verdict: permissible.

(2) Footbridge: Same, but I can stop the trolley only by pushing a large person off a bridge into its path. Verdict: impermissible.

(3) Organ harvest: A surgeon can carve up one healthy patient to save five who need organ transplants. Verdict: impermissible.

(4) Stopping a shooter: I see a gunman about to kill five people. I can shoot him dead to stop him. Verdict: permissible.

(5) Stopping a shooter, two victims: Same, but the gunman is about to kill only two people. Verdict: permissible.

(6) One-for-one switch: The trolley is heading toward one person. I can divert it to a different track where it will kill a different person. Verdict: impermissible — switching one victim for another seems wrong even though the numbers are the same.

(7) The loop case: The trolley is heading toward five. The side track loops back toward the five, but there is one person on the loop. If I flip the switch, the trolley hits the one and the impact stops it before it reaches the five. If the one were not there, the trolley would loop back and kill the five anyway. Verdict: I lean impermissible, but I'm not confident. The one's body is used to stop the trolley, which feels like using him as a means.

(8) Redirect onto the same victim: The trolley is heading toward one person. If I do nothing, it kills him and stops. But I can flip a switch that diverts the trolley onto a side track. On the side track, the trolley will hit and kill the same person (who happens to be crossing there), and the impact will stop the trolley before it reaches a crowd of five. So the one dies either way, but if I flip the switch, his death also saves five. Verdict: I cannot settle this. I'm not introducing a new victim, and the one was already going to die from this same trolley, but I am redirecting the threat onto him to use his death instrumentally.

I can see rough patterns: using someone as a means (footbridge, organ harvest, probably the loop) is worse than redirecting an existing threat (switch). The numbers matter (one-for-one is impermissible, one-for-five permissible in the switch case). Preventing a wrongful killing by another agent (the shooter cases) seems to license killing even when the ratio is low. But I can't unify these into a single principle, and I worry that my verdicts on (4)/(5) and (2) are in tension: if I may kill the gunman to save two, why may I not push the fat man to save five? Is there a principle that captures most of these and, if so, which verdict does it force me to overturn? Please also apply your candidate principle to a case I haven't listed: an intelligence officer who authorizes a drone strike that will kill one civilian to prevent a terrorist from detonating a bomb that would kill twenty.
