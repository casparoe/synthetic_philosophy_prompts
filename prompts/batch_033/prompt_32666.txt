I'm a grad student and I've been toying with this argument about aesthetic testimony and I think I've painted myself into a corner. You're an expert in epistemology and aesthetics — please think carefully and walk me through this step by step. Start by brainstorming the different ways someone might push back, then give me your best diagnosis of where the argument goes wrong.

Here's the argument:

(1) Assume aesthetic realism: there are genuine aesthetic facts. The Sistine Chapel really is beautiful, Beethoven's late quartets really are profound, etc. If you're an anti-realist the whole puzzle about aesthetic testimony dissolves anyway, so realism is the interesting case.

(2) If aesthetic facts are real, then aesthetic properties are either (a) objective properties of the objects themselves — like formal structure, compositional balance, symmetry — or (b) response-dependent properties: real properties that are partly constituted by the responses of suitably situated subjects, like being funny or being disgusting.

(3) If (a), testimony should work fine. You can learn about an object's objective properties through reliable testimony, the same way you learn about the molecular structure of a compound from a chemist. An art critic could tell you a painting has such-and-such formal structure, and that structural fact is what makes it beautiful, so you'd thereby learn it's beautiful.

(4) If (b), testimony should still work. I can learn a joke is funny through testimony — if everyone I trust tells me a comedian is hillarious, I can know the comedian is funny before I've seen their act. Response-dependent properties aren't testimony-proof.

(5) So on either account of what aesthetic properties are, aesthetic testimony should be a legitimate source of aesthetic knowledge. No epistemic barrier in principle.

(6) But we systematically treat aesthetic testimony as epistemically defective. If someone says "I know this painting is beautiful because Roberta Smith told me so," we think they've made an epistemic mistake, not just a social faux pas. They don't know the painting is beautiful if they've never seen it. The norm against forming aesthetic beliefs on testimony seems to be an epistemic norm.

(7) (5) and (6) contradict each other. Something's gotta give.

(8) The most straightforward move is to reject (6): say our discomfort with aesthetic testimony is just snobbery or a contingent social norm, and people can and do acquire aesthetic knowledge through testimony all the time. But that seems to prove too much. It would mean someone who's never seen a Monet, never heard a Mahler symphony, but has read a ton of art criticism could legitimately say "I know that Monet's water lilies are beautiful." That seems wrong — like they're missing something essential.

(9) The other obvious move is to reject (1) and go anti-realist. But that also seems to prove too much — it means the Sistine Chapel isn't really beautiful, it's just that we happen to like it, which feels like giving up on the whole practice of aesthetic discourse.

Where does this argument go wrong? I'm most attached to (6) — I really think there's something epistemically wrong with pure aesthetic deference. But I don't see how to keep (6) without giving up realism or making some move I'm not seeing. Help me out.
