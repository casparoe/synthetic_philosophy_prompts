I serve on the organizing committee for an annual jurisprudence workshop, and this year we've invited two senior legal philosophers to conduct an adversarial collaboration on statutory interpretation. One is a committed textualist; the other is a committed purposivist. They have been debating each other in print for over a decade — on originalism's boundaries, on the use of legislative history, on how to handle unanticipated applications — and we have asked them to produce a single joint document they can both sign. Not a compromise paper, but a clear map of where they agree, where they still diverge, and what would actually move either of them.

We have given them the following brief. The statement should open with a numbered list of claims both can accept without reservation: claims about what statutory interpretation is supposed to accomplish, about the canonical easy cases and about the genuinely hard ones whose classification they do not dispute, about what the structure of a legislature and the judicial role under the Constitution do and do not already settle, and about the acknowledged limits of the opposing view — each side stating what it concedes the other gets right. The statement should then present a disagreements table. For each point of remaining disagreement, it should state the textualist's position and the purposivist's position in wording each side would endorse as its own, and then isolate the specific sub-question — the crux — such that a resolution of that sub-question would cause at least one side to revise its view. Finally, for each crux, the document should describe at least one kind of case, argument, or empirical finding that both sides agree would bear on it, and record in advance what each side predicts such evidence would reveal.

We have also asked them to do some housekeeping. We suspect that some of their long-running disputes are partly verbal — for example, they may mean quite different things by "legislative intent" or "plain meaning" or "the purpose of the statute" — and we want them to flag any disagreement that reduces to a terminological difference once the relevant terms are explicitly operationalized. We likewise suspect that some of their disagreements are not conceptual at all but normative: about the proper role of unelected judges in a democracy, about whether predictability matters more than fidelity to statutory design in particular classes of cases, or about the relative weight of stability and adaptation across different areas of law. We want those flagged too, with the normative premises laid bare.

Please draft this joint statement. Approach it as a neutral facilitator who has listened to both sides extensively and can render each position honestly in language its own proponent would recognize. The aim is not to blur their differences or to produce a mushy middle-ground document, but to make the disagreement maximally precise — so that a reader can see exactly what turns on what, and so that the two philosophers themselves can identify which further work would actually change someone's mind.
