I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for the better part of a decade, and one move I see deployed with clockwork regularity is someone saying "all observation is theory-laden" as though it settles whatever argument about objectivity, convergence, or perceptual access to reality was on the table. Most recently this happened in a thread about whether expert radiologists and novices literally see the same thing when they look at the same chest X-ray. Someone argued that since the expert has learned to parse the visual field into clinically meaningful patterns and the novice hasn't, their perceptual experiences must differ in content — and then added "all observation is theory-laden, so of course they don't see the same image," and the thread moved on. I've also seen the slogan deployed to dismiss the idea that scientific instruments give us theory-neutral data, to argue that people from different cultures inhabit genuinely different perceptual worlds, and to claim that predictive-processing models of the brain vindicate the idea that we all hallucinate our reality and merely correct the hallucination against sensory input when things go wrong.

But I'm increasingly convinced this slogan functions as a motte-and-bailey. The motte is something like "perception involves unconscious inference, so what you end up perceiving depends on priors your visual system brings to the stimulus" — which is true and important but doesn't get you most of the conclusions people want. The bailey is something like "your beliefs, desires, and ideologies can directly change the contents of your perceptual experience, so that a bigot literally sees a weapon where there's a phone, or a creationist literally sees fossils as having been placed there by God." Between these extremes there are several distinct theses the slogan could be asserting, and I suspect people slide between them without noticing — or even without knowing the distinctions exist.

I want you to separate the precise claims that "all observation is theory-laden" could be making, ordered from weakest to strongest. For each reading, specify: (a) whether it's a claim about the causal history of perception, the epistemic status of perceptual judgments, or the phenomenal content of perceptual experience itself; (b) whether it's trivially true, an open empirical question (and if so, which way the evidence currently leans), or almost certainly false; and (c) whether it actually supports the specific claim that the expert radiologist and the novice don't see the same thing. Then tell me which reading the person in my thread was probably invoking, whether that reading can bear the argumentative weight they placed on it, and which reading — if any — they should have invoked if they wanted their conclusion to follow. Keep the response under 800 words.
