I retired from a career in marine invertebrate systematics about three years ago — spent most of it describing polychaete worms, if you can believe someone pays you for that — and I've been using the free time to finally read some philosophy of biology. I started with Hull and Ghiselin and worked forward. And I've hit a wall that I can't seem to get past. The species problem, as the philosophers frame it, isnt about finding better operational criteria — it's about what kind of thing a species *is*. Are they individuals, are they natural kinds, are they homeostatic property clusters... But here's what bothers me. When I was working, if a grad student asked me whether two morphotypes were the same species, I'd pick a concept — usually the biological species concept, sometimes a phylogenetic one if we had sequence data — and run with it. The choice felt pragmatic, not principled. And the fact that different concepts give incompatible answers in plenty of borderline cases doesn't seem like a "pluralism is fine" situation to me. It looks like the concepts are tracking different things, which means "species" doesn't refer to any one thing in nature.

So here's my worry: if species aren't a natural kind, and different species concepts don't converge on the same carve-up of the world, then what exactly are we counting when we say "we're losing dozens of species a day"? Doesn't the whole conservation biology framework presuppose that species are real, objective units out there? And if philosophy of biology has spent fifty years showing they aren't — or at least that the notion is irreducibly gerrymandered — shouldn't that bother conservation biologists a lot more than it seems to? I feel like I must be missing something basic about how the conceptual problem and the applied problem fit together, or don't.
