In discussions of climate policy and intergenerational justice, I keep encountering a particular analogy, and I want a rigorous assessment of how much weight it can bear. A colleague presses it to argue that pure time preference — weighting a benefit less simply because it arrives later — is indefensible. The analogy runs as follows. Discounting the welfare of future generations merely because that welfare lies further in the future is said to be no different in principle from discounting the welfare of your own future self merely because that self's welfare lies further in the future. Since the latter is widely taken to be irrational — the version of you that exists on Tuesday is not a lesser claimant than the version that exists on Monday merely because it arrives later — the former, the analogy concludes, should be rejected as well. Some proponents strengthen the analogy by adding that your future self is, for all prudentially relevant purposes, a different person from your present self, because what matters in survival is psychological connectedness rather than strict identity; on that view, the intrapersonal and intergenerational cases are not merely analogous but nearly the same case, and rejecting the analogy amounts to giving temporal position a moral weight it cannot bear.

Please audit this analogy in detail. Provide a correspondence table: what in the intrapersonal case is being mapped onto what in the intergenerational case — the present self, the future self, the relation between them, the discount rate, and the normative principle doing the work. Then set out where the mapping breaks. In particular, address the following questions. Does the intrapersonal case actually have the normative structure its users claim, or does the claim that pure time preference within a single life is indefensible depend on contested positions in the theory of prudence and personal identity? Does the analogy quietly import a view about what makes a person the same over time, or about what makes different persons' claims count equally, that the intergenerational case need not share? Which of the disanalogies matter for the intended conclusion, and which are mere differences that leave the argument intact? Finally, state clearly whether the conclusion — that pure time preference is indefensible — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was doing the argumentative work. If a better analogy would do the job more honestly, say what it is and where it, too, would fail.
