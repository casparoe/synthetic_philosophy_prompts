ok so I'm running a philosophy-and-film night and we're doing Arrival (the Villeneuve one). The angle I pitched was philosophy of language, specifically Davidson on radical interpretation. But now that I'm actually preparing, I'm worried I've set myself up for a mess.

Here's the thing. Davidson says radical interpretation — figuring out someone's language from zero, no translator, no dictionary — is only possible because you assume the speaker is mostly rational and mostly right about the world. The principle of charity. Without it, you can't break into the circle of belief and meaning. Fair enough.

But Arrival is about exactly this scenario — linguist meets aliens, has to figure out their language from scratch — and the whole point of the movie is that the aliens *don't* think like us. Their written language is atemporal, they percieve time non-linearly, and learning their language literally changes Louise's brain so she experiences time differently too. So the film seems to be saying: radical interpretation can work even across a genuinely radical difference in cognitive structure. Which sure looks like it contradicts Davidson.

But I keep going back and forth. Maybe the heptapods are actually perfectly rational by Davidson's lights — they cooperate, they answer questions, they have goals — and the time thing is more like them knowing different physics, not having a different conceptual scheme. In which case the film isn't a counterexample at all, it's just a vivid illustration of how interpretation works when the other party knows something you don't.

Or maybe the film is doing exactly what Davidson argued you can't do — presenting a coherent picture of minds with a radically different conceptual scheme — and the fact that it *feels* coherent to viewers is philosophically interesting. Like, maybe art can make feel possible what argument says is impossible, and that's worth taking seriously rather than just dismissing as a confusion.

I guess what I want is: if you had to lead a discussion that takes both Davidson and the film seriously, where would you land? Where does the film genuinely put pressure on Davidson's view, and where does Davidson's view actually illuminate what Louise is doing in those scenes with the logograms? And is there something about the *experience* of trying to interpret — the disorientation, the way it changes you — that the film captures and Davidson's philosophy doesn't? I'm not looking for citations or a reading list, I want to work through the ideas.
