In a metaphysics seminar on material constitution, my instructor kept meeting objections with the sentence "constitution is not identity," as though that one phrase settled whether the statue and the lump of clay on the pedestal are one object or two. The more I think about it, the less clear I am about what the sentence is even asserting. Sometimes it sounds like a trivial remark about English vocabulary: "constitution" and "identity" are different words with different meanings. Sometimes it sounds like a formal point about relations: identity is symmetric and reflexive, while constitution is neither, so the two relations cannot be the same relation. And sometimes it sounds like a substantive thesis about the case at hand: the statue and the clay are two distinct material objects that coincide, which is precisely the conclusion the sentence was being used to establish. I want to walk into the next session with a precise menu of readings. Lay out the distinct claims "constitution is not identity" could be used to express, from the most deflationary to the most substantive; for each, say whether it is a platitude, a defensible but contestable metaphysical thesis, or something probably false; and then say which of those readings, if any, would actually license the move from the slogan to the distinctness of the statue and the clay — and whether that move simply assumes the point at issue.
