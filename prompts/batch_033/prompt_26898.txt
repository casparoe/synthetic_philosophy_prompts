Okay so I need help after a long argument I had with my friend last night about advance directives. The case we kept coming back to: a woman writes an advance directive in her fifties saying that if she ever develops severe dementia she refuses antibiotics, feeding tubes, and other life-prolonging treatment, even if she seems happy. Twenty years later she has advanced Alzheimer's; she doesn't recognize her family, but she's content, she likes her music and her pudding, and she sometimes says she wants to stay. She gets pneumonia. My friend kept saying the woman in the bed is the one who matters, and if she's content and wants to live it would be cruel to let her die on the authority of a "person" who no longer exists. I kept saying the whole point of writing the directive was that the earlier self gets to set the terms, and if we ignore it autonomy just becomes a mood ring. We argued for three hours and never even got clear on whether the then-self and the now-self are the same person in the sense that matters, or whether a decision can reach across a change like that at all.

So here's what I want. Act as a professional philosopher who works on personal identity and medical ethics, and draft the document my friend and I should of produced: the joint statement of a good-faith adversarial collaboration between the precedent-autonomy camp (roughly, the earlier self's directive should bind) and the current-interests camp (roughly, the later self's present wishes and well-being should prevail). Don't write a survey and don't adjudicate; write the text both sides could actually sign. Structure it as a numbered list of agreed claims followed by a disagreements table. The agreed claims should be substantive, not truisms: they should cover what each side grants about the case (the directive was informed and autonomous, the later person has real present interests, the directive can't just be ignored wholesale) and what each side grants about the other's strongest point. Then, for each genuine disagreement, give the exact wording each side would accept for its own view, with no strawmen. After that, list the cruxes — I suspect at least three: whether personal identity or psychological continuity is what matters for decision-making authority; whether autonomy can be exercised diachronically so as to bind a later self, or only synchronically; and whether someone's well-being can include critical interests (like dying with dignity, or not being remembered as a person who lingered that way) that the current self no longer endorses. For each crux, describe a concrete case or consideration both sides would agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side would predict or say about it. If you think part of the dispute is merely verbal, say which part and why. Take your time and brainstorm the cruxes before you commit to them; be honest about places where the camps are closer than people pretend.
