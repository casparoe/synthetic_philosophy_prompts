Two friends of mine, both phil grad students, got into it at a bar last week over consequentialist cluelessness. I took notes on my phone cause I wanted to think about it later, and now I'm going through them. Here's roughly how it went.

Sam kicked things off by saying he's been reading some of the recent literature on cluelessness and he's starting to think it's a much bigger deal than most people admit — like, if you really take it seriously, consequentialism stops being action-guiding at all.

Maya pushed back: "Come on, you don't need to know the exact consequences of everything forever. You use expected value with the best evidence you have. That's how everyone already thinks about climate policy or public health."

Sam: "But that's exactly the point. Climate policy is a case where we can model things and trace causal chains. The cluelessness worry is about all the chains we can't trace. When I choose between two job offers, the downstream effects of who I meet, what ideas I end up having, the person who gets hired instead of me — all of that is totally opaque, and it plausibly swamps the stuff I can actually evaluate."

Maya: "Sure, but it's opaque in the same way for both options, so it washes out. You don't have to model every single ripple — you just need to know there's no systematic reason the ripples from job A would be better than the ripples from job B."

Sam: "But you don't know that! Maybe one job puts you in a social network that, I don't know, makes some political movement slightly more likely to succeed in twenty years. You have literally no idea. The expected value of the ripples from A and B could be wildly different, and you'd never know."

Maya: "That's just radical skepticism dressed up. If you apply that standard everywhere, you can't cross the street. The fact that you can't rule out some bizarre butterfly effect doesn't mean you should treat it as live."

Sam: "No, it's not radical skepticism — I'm not saying you can't know anything. I'm saying consequentialism specifically tells you to maximize expected value over all consequences, and when the consequences you can't assess are plausibly larger than the ones you can, the theory gives you no guidance. That's an internal problem for consequentialism, not a general epistemic problem."

Maya: "I think you're sneaking in a premise that the unknown effects are large. They might be tiny. Most of what we do probably doesn't have massive ripple effects."

Sam: "But that's an empirical claim you're just asserting! You don't know that either. That's the whole point — we're clueless about the magnitudes too."

I want to know whether Sam has a real point or whether Maya is right that this is just skepticism in disguise. And if they're both partly right, where exactly does each argument go wrong?
