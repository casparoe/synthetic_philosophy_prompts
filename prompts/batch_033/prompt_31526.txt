I teach an upper-level undergraduate metaphysics course, and I'm putting together the final. I want one essay question on causation by omission. We've spent a few weeks on this: we covered counterfactual theories of causation, the contrast between causes and background conditions, and the puzzle that if my not watering the plant caused it to die, then it looks like the Queen of England's not watering it did too. I don't want a question that lets them just recite "omissions are absences of events, so they can't be causes" or "counterfactual dependence is all there is to it." I want something that forces them to actually work through whether a broadly counterfactual theory can treat omissions as causes without loosing its claim to be a reductive, mind-independent account of causation, and to take a real stand on the selection problem.

Could you write the question, a model answer of around 700 words, and a marking scheme that seperates an A from a B by naming the specific moves each kind of answer must contain? The exam gives them 90 minutes, so it should be answerable in that time. It would also be nice if the question gave them a short concrete scenario to hang the discussion on rather than just saying "discuss causation by omission." And if there are standard student mistakes on this material, maybe a one-line note on what they are and how the marking scheme catches them.
