I have been reading Thomas More's Utopia (the 1551 Robinson translation, in a modernized edition) and find myself stuck on a passage in Book II that describes the Utopians' practices concerning the sick and dying. I will paraphrase the passage as best I can, since I do not have the exact wording to hand.

The relevant section runs roughly as follows. When a person in Utopia falls ill with a disease that is both incurable and accompanied by unremitting pain, certain magistrates and priests come to visit them. They do not simply offer comfort; they deliver what amounts to an exhortation. They tell the sufferer that they have become a burden to themselves and to those around them, that their life now consists only in suffering, and that they are, in effect, already dead to the purposes and pleasures of living. The visitor urges the person to take their own life, framing this as a rational and even virtuous act — a release from a condition that no longer deserves the name of life. If the person is persuaded, they may end their life either by refusing food or by taking a drug that brings about a painless sleep from which they do not wake. If, however, the person refuses this counsel, they are not coerced or abandoned; the Utopians continue to care for them with the same solicitude as before.

What puzzles me is the exact character of the interaction More is describing. On one hand, this looks like a form of voluntary euthanasia or assisted suicide: the final decision rests with the sufferer, and no one forces them. On the other hand, the practice is embedded in a social ritual in which figures of considerable authority — priests and magistrates — come to the bedside and make a case that the person's life has lost its value and that continuing to live would be a kind of failure. The exhortation is described in terms that seem almost moralistic: the sufferer is told they owe it to themselves and to the community to let go. I am struggling to decide whether this is best understood as a genuinely voluntary practice that respects individual autonomy, or as a form of social pressure that makes a show of respecting choice while stacking the decision heavily in one direction. Is there a way of reading the passage that takes both elements seriously without reducing one to the other? And does the fact that the visitors are priests and magistrates — rather than, say, physicians or family members — tell us something specific about how More understood the moral authority involved in such a decision?
