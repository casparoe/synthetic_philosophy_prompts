I've basically come around to Williams's side on the integrity objection, and I want to stress-test it rather than defend it. The position: utilitarianism (and impartialist moral theory more broadly) asks the agent to step back from his ground projects and personal commitments and treat them as just more material for the impartial calculus, and that demand is itself a serious moral cost — not just an awkward fact about the theory's recommended psychology but a real objection to it. The drowning-wife case is what does the work for me: the husband who saves his wife and then, pressed to justify it, reaches for a principle about spouses being permissible to save in such situations has had one thought too many, and something has already gone wrong with him or with the theory even when the theory returns the right answer.

What would count against this? I want the full map of the position's exposure, not a defense of it. If the consequentialist move of separating a criterion of rightness from a decision procedure genuinely dissolves the objection, what would have to be shown for me to accept that the objection was only ever about decision procedures? What would a Williams-style defender have to conceed counts against them — that the case is under-described, that the objection leans on a particular ideal of authentic agency that might be parochial or even morally suspect, or that integrity can be dangerous when someone's ground projects are awful? Are there cases where we positively want the agent to have the extra thought, where its absence is the failure, and does that cut against the objection or just mark its boundary? And give me an honest verdict on whether the position has been insulated by successive qualifications (from utilitarianism to consequentialism, from a claim about wrongness to a claim about alienated lives) to the point where nothing could count against it — and if so, whether that's a genuine problem for a normative position or just what positions in ethics look like.

Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say which ones a committed defender of the integrity objection should themselves admit are real costs rather than things only critics bring up.
