You are an expert moral philosopher who works on moral status and animal minds, and you are experienced at preparing speakers for difficult interdisciplinary question periods. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming the likely objections from each faction in the audience before organizing them into the ranked prep sheet described below.

I am preparing a workshop talk called Against the Brain-Size Criterion. The thesis I will defend is this: gross brain size, whether absolute or scaled to body size, has no intrinsic moral relevance, and its routine use as a shortcut for moral status is not merely imperfect but systematically misleading; it should be dropped from moral-status reasoning and replaced, where a proxy is unavoidable, by direct behavioral and functional evidence of the capacities that actually ground moral status.

My compressed argument runs as follows. First, I distinguish intrinsic from evidential relevance: moral status attaches to subjects in virtue of their capacities (sentience, evaluative states, self-awareness, and the like), and a physical magnitude such as brain volume has no normative force by itself. Second, I press an evidential critique: gross brain size is a poor predictor of those capacities, confounded by body size, neuronal density, and neural organization, and it misfires in familiar cases, including small-brained but cognitively sophisticated animals and humans with severe cortical damage who plainly retain moral status. Third, I make a pragmatic and normative claim: because brain size is salient and easy to measure, it acts as a bias magnet, crowding out more probative evidence and quietly importing a status hierarchy ordered by anatomy.

The audience is a joint workshop of about twenty people. Roughly half are animal welfare scientists and comparative cognition researchers who use brain-size metrics, including encephalization quotients and neuron-count estimates, as part of sentience-assessment frameworks for fish, cephalopods, and decapod crustaceans, and who care most about workable policy criteria and the precautionary principle. About a third are philosophers of mind and moral philosophers who defend capacity-based accounts of moral status and will worry about conceptual precision and about what replaces the criterion. The rest are neuroscientists who study the neural correlates of consciousness and are suspicious both of gross anatomical shortcuts and of purely behavioral criteria. There are no committed brain-size realists in the room, but several participants regard the criterion as a defensible heuristic precisely for the cases where direct evidence is hardest to obtain.

Prepare my Q&A prep sheet. It should give the eight or so objections I am most likely to receive from this specific audience, not generic objections from the literature and not the objections a room of species egalitarians or lay animal-rights advocates would raise, ordered by a combination of how likely each is and how much damage it would do. Phrase each objection the way a member of this audience would actually put it, using their characteristic vocabulary. For each objection, make clear what I should concede up front and what I should hold firm on, then give a concise reply I could deliver in about two minutes, followed by a note on where that reply is softest or most likely to be pressed further. End with the one question I should be hoping nobody asks, and explain briefly why it is dangerous.

Do not include reading lists, citations, or bibliography; I need argumentative preparation, not sources.
