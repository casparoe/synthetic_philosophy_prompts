I run a programme evaluation unit at a mid-sized foundation. We fund interventions—job training, health outreach, educational software—and then try to determine whether they caused the outcomes we see. My analysts know their statistics, but I have noticed they default to one causal framework without realising there is a choice: they treat causation as a counterfactual (would the outcome have happened without the programme?) and never consider the interventionist alternative (is there a well-defined manipulation of the programme variable that changes the outcome, and can we isolate it from confounders?). I gather that the philosophical literature on causation has explored this distinction in depth, and that it matters practically—some causal claims that look fine under one framework are suspicious under the other, and vice versa.

Write me a reusable decision procedure my team can follow when assessing a causal claim about a programme we have funded. It should walk an analyst through the questions that distinguish what the two frameworks demand, flag where they agree and where they diverge, and specify what kind of evidence is needed to satisfy each. The procedure must be usable by someone with graduate-level statistical training but no philosophy background, in under an hour per case. Mark the steps where judgment cannot be eliminated.

Then apply the procedure to two cases:

1. A job-training programme in a single city where we have a randomised controlled trial with clean compliance.
2. A national advocacy campaign for vaccine uptake where randomisation is impossible and we are working with observational data and an instrumental variable whose exclusion restriction is debated.

Your response should follow this structure:

- **Distinction in Practice** (roughly 15%): a short, non-technical explanation of the counterfactual/interventionist distinction as it bears on programme evaluation—just enough for an analyst to see why the procedure asks the questions it does.
- **The Procedure** (roughly 45%): the step-by-step decision procedure, presented as numbered questions with decision rules, branching logic, and explicit handoff points where an analyst must consult a supervisor or the procedure cannot settle the matter.
- **Application: Job Training** (roughly 20%): run the procedure on this case, showing each step and the conclusion reached.
- **Application: Vaccine Advocacy** (roughly 20%): run the procedure on this case, showing where the procedure forces a conclusion weaker or more awkward than the team's default counterfactual reasoning would deliver, and what the analyst should report as a result.
