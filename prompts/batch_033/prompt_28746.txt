At a recent workshop, two colleagues got into a sharp disagreement about the "scale argument" in insect sentience, and I have been turning it over ever since. I would like a point-by-point adjudication of their exchange.

The first, a philosopher of biology, argued that the sheer number of insects — conservatively around 10^18 — makes the question of insect sentience morally urgent even under very low probability estimates. If there is even a one-in-a-thousand chance that a typical insect is sentient, the expected number of sentient beings affected by our actions is on the order of 10^15, dwarfing the human and vertebrate animal populations combined. She said that standard expected-value reasoning therefore demands we treat insect welfare as a serious moral concern, and that dismissing it amounts to scope neglect.

The second, a decision theorist, replied that the argument is structurally a Pascal's mugging. When probabilities are sufficiently poorly grounded, he said, allowing them to drive decisions through expected-value calculations makes one vulnerable to arbitrarily costly demands. The probability of insect sentience is not merely low, he argued — it is radically indeterminate, because we lack a settled theory of what sentience requires. Without a well-defined probability, expected-value reasoning is being applied to a number that is essentially made up.

The first countered that the probability is not made up: we have nociceptive evidence, behavioral evidence, and neuroanatomical evidence that bears on the question. She said the situation is analogous to betting on a horse race given partial information — the probability is imprecise but not arbitrary, and imprecise probabilities still constrain expected-value reasoning.

The second responded that the analogy fails. In the horse race case, we know what winning consists in and we have a reference class. For insect sentience, we cannot even agree on the necessary and sufficient conditions for sentience, so we cannot meaningfully assign a probability that a given insect satisfies them. This is closer, he said, to assigning a probability to the claim that the universe contains a being that is "glork," where "glork" is undefined.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move. Who has the better of which arguments, and what — if anything — are both parties missing?
