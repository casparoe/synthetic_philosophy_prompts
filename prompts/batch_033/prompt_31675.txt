The long-running dispute about how to make sense of probability in the Everettian many-worlds interpretation puzzles me. Everyone in the debate accepts the same unitary quantum mechanics, the same decoherence story, and the same empirical predictions. One camp holds that the Born rule can be derived from decision theory: a rational agent who knows that every measurement outcome occurs and that rewards are spread across branches is rationally compelled to assign credences proportional to the squared amplitudes. A second camp replies that this is circular, because the decision-theoretic axioms only sound plausible if one has already decided that branches should be weighted by their amplitudes, so the derivation assumes what it sets out to prove. A third camp grounds the probabilities in self-locating uncertainty: before a measurement, I should be uncertain about which branch I shall find myself in, and the natural measure over possible future selves is the branch weight. Critics answer that in a deterministic branching universe there is no fact of the matter about which descendant is me, so the supposed uncertainty is misplaced. A fourth camp simply postulates the Born rule and remains silent about what the probabilities mean.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not really about physics. It is about what 'probability' could mean in a theory with no chance events: objective chance, rational credence, self-locating credence, or a measure of existence. Is that diagnosis right, or does it collapse several questions that the participants genuinely keep apart? Is there a substantive question underneath — for example, whether rational constraints can force branch-weight credences — that could in principle be settled, and what would settle it? I am looking for a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement, not a survey of the interpretations, and an honest account of what, if anything, would count as progress.
