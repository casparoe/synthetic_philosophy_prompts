I recently came across the following summary of Daniel Dennett's multiple drafts model in an introductory textbook on the philosophy of mind, and I'm trying to figure out how much of it to trust:

"Dennett's central claim is that consciousness is an illusion. He argues that there is no 'Cartesian theater' in the brain — no single place where sensory information comes together and is presented to an inner observer, a little homunculus watching the show. Instead, the brain processes information in multiple parallel streams, constantly producing and revising 'drafts' of experience, none of which is the definitive version of what we are conscious of at any given moment. Because there is no central place where it all comes together, there is no unified self either. Dennett replaces the self with the idea of a 'center of narrative gravity,' which he describes as a useful fiction we construct to make sense of our own behavior. The upshot is that the first-person perspective — the feeling that there is something it is like to be me — is not a real feature of the world but a kind of user illusion generated by the brain's storytelling machinery."

I can tell this summary is trying to be fair, but I've heard that Dennett has been widely misread and that he explicitly denies he is an eliminativist about consciousness. Which parts of this summary would Dennett accept, which would he push back on, and where does the summary subtly slide from what he actually argues to something stronger than his position?
