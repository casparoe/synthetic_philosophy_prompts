You are a professional philosopher specializing in Wittgenstein, Kripke, and the philosophy of language. Please think through the following question carefully, working step by step.

In teaching the rule-following considerations, one often encounters a compelling analogy: a rule is like a rail extending into the future. Once you have laid the rail, it determines every subsequent move — the train has no genuine choice, and there is a definite fact of the matter about whether a given move stays on the rails or goes off them. The analogy is meant to capture the intuition that a rule's past applications fully determine its correct future applications, and that there is a fact of the matter about what the rule requires in any new case. But Kripke's Wittgenstein argues that this is precisely the picture we must abandon: nothing in my past usage of "plus" rails me into answering "125" rather than "5" when I am asked "68 + 57."

I want a full audit of this analogy. (1) Give me the correspondence table: what in the rule-following situation maps to the rails, the act of laying them, the train, the switch points, the destination, and the fact that rails are physical objects whose location is publicly observable? (2) Identify the disanalogies. In particular, the rail picture imports a track-builder — someone who laid the rails. Does the rule-following paradox gain any of its force from the fact that in the linguistic case there is no such track-builder, or are the disanalogies deeper than that? (3) What does the skeptical solution — that we call someone a rule-follower in virtue of community agreement, not in virtue of rails — do to the analogy? Does it replace it with a different one, and if so, what would that replacement analogy be? (4) Give your verdict: if the rail analogy is ultimately misleading about what rule-following consists in, is there a better analogy, or is the whole project of finding an analogy for rule-following part of the problem?
