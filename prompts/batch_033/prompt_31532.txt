I am preparing a long-form interview for a series on philosophers whose less-celebrated commitments sit awkwardly with their canonical work, and the guest is Henry Sidgwick — not only the author of The Methods of Ethics (1874), but the Sidgwick who co-founded the Society for Psychical Research in 1882, served as its first and longest-serving president, and spent decades organising and scrutinising investigations of mediums, apparitions, telepathy and apparent evidence for survival after death. I do not want a biographical tour or a restatement of his utilitarianism; I want the conversation to press on the live tensions between his ethical epistemology and his psychical research.

Draft eight to ten questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give. Please ensure the set includes at least one question on whether his utilitarian impartialism gives him a moral reason to want evidence of posthumous survival, or a moral reason to distrust his own wanting it; at least one on the evidential standards he applies to trance phenomena and telepathy as against the standards he applies to ordinary testimony and intuition in his ethical method; at least one on the asymmetry that he became convinced of telepathy yet remained cautious about treating mediumship as proof of survival, and what that asymmetry reveals about where he set the bar; and at least one on the charge that the whole research programme was an attempt to secure by empirical means the moral order whose absence his own dualism of practical reason left as a gap. Design at least two of the follow-ups so that they force him to choose between his caution as a methodologist and his hope for a theistic moral order. Conclude by saying how confident you are in the predicted answers overall, which single predicted answer you are least sure of, and which follow-up you would most want to revise if he answered differently from expectation. Please write everything in British English.
