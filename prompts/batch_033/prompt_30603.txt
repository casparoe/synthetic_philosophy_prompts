I run an after-school discussion club at a high school — around sixteen students, most of them fifteen to seventeen, one hour a week. I'm not trained in philosophy; I just like puzzles that don't let go. For a while I've been turning over one I read years ago in a book of brain-teasers, and I want to run a session that makes the group feel why it's so strange rather than just hear me describe it.

The puzzle, as I remember it: on Friday a teacher announces that there will be a short test on one day of the coming school week, and that it will be a surprise — the students won't be able to work out the day beforehand. The class reasons that it can't be Friday, since if Thursday evening arrives and the test still hasn't happened, only Friday remains, so they would know. With Friday ruled out, the same step rules out Thursday, then Wednesday, and so on back through the whole week. So they conclude that no surprise test can be given at all. Then the teacher hands out the test on Wednesday, and everyone is surprised. The step-by-step reasoning feels airtight, yet the teacher does exactly what it said couldn't be done, and I can't shake that.

I want the students to hit that contradiction themselves, inside a game, before anyone names it. Please answer as an expert philosopher who has taught this puzzle many times and knows where people get stuck. Think carefully, and start by brainstorming two or three possible mechanics for a game, then choose the one that draws the reasoning out of the participants instead of out of me. Then give me the complete runnable plan: the exact announcement I make at the start; the rules and a scoring scheme that doesn't use real money; what I prepare beforehand (we have paper, index cards, and an envelope, and I'd rather not need more); a minute-by-minute timeline for the hour; how many rounds we play and what happens in each; and a debrief that maps what happened in the room onto the steps of the students' reasoning. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — for instance someone refusing to play along, or the group not producing the backwards argument on its own — and how I should handle each. Finally, be honest about which part of the puzzle the activity can't reproduce, so I know what I have to supply in the debrief. By the end of the hour I want them to feel that each step of the elimination argument looked forced, and that the surprise still happened anyway.
