I serve on a state bioethics commission that reviews proposals for new markets in morally contested goods. In the past three years we have seen proposals for a regulated kidney market, a futures exchange for hospital beds during pandemics, and a platform for buying and selling rights to personal health data. Our enabling statute requires us to determine whether a proposed market would cause "impermissible commodification" of the good in question, but it provides no definition of that term. Our decisions must be consistent across cases and must state reasons that can survive judicial review. I have read enough of the philosophical literature — Sandel on the corrosive effects of markets, Satz on noxious markets, Radin on contested commodification, Brennan and Jaworski on the asymmetry thesis — to know that "commodification" is deeply contested and that not every market in a morally significant good is objectionable in the same way or to the same degree. But I do not know how to turn that literature into an operational procedure we can actually run at a hearing with a stack of briefing documents and a deadline.

Please write a decision procedure my commission can follow. It should be a sequence of questions we ask about each proposal, in order, with clear guidance on what counts as a sufficient answer at each step and where the procedure stops with a rejection or a provisional approval. The procedure should distinguish cases where commodification itself is the core problem from cases where the real objection is exploitation, inequality, harm to third parties, or inadequate consent — because our statute only authorizes us to block markets on commodification grounds, and if the real problem is something else, we need to say so and refer the matter elsewhere. Flag the points where the procedure cannot eliminate judgment and the commissioners must make a value call. Then run the procedure on three proposals:

(1) A regulated market in kidneys from living donors, with a public agency as the sole purchaser paying a fixed price, allocation by medical need rather than ability to pay, and extensive health screening and follow-up care for donors.

(2) A proposal to allow pharmaceutical companies to pay healthy volunteers for Phase I drug trials at market rates rather than the current flat per-diem, with the stated aim of recruiting participants more representative of the population.

(3) A platform that allows parents to transfer legal parental rights to pre-screened prospective adoptive parents for a negotiated price, subject to a mandatory waiting period, home study, and judicial approval of the placement.

For each, tell me what answer the procedure yields, where the hardest judgment calls land, and whether the commission is on solid ground blocking the proposal under "commodification" or whether we would be stretching the term to cover concerns better addressed under another heading.
