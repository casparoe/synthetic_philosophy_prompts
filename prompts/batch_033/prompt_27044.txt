I serve on the investment committee of a private foundation with an endowment of roughly $400 million and a mission focused on reducing preventable disease in low-income countries. We vote next month on a proposal to divest from companies whose primary business is the extraction or sale of fossil fuels. Our current exposure is about $15 million, mostly through commingled funds. The cleanest way to divest would be to move that portion of the portfolio into a separately managed account or a screened index fund that excludes fossil-fuel producers, at an added cost of approximately eight basis points per year on the moved assets plus modest one-time transition costs.

The proposal's sponsors argue that continuing to hold these assets makes the foundation a participant in a practice that predictably damages the health of the populations our mission exists to serve. They also argue that divestment by many institutions has gradually changed the reputational and political environment in which fossil-fuel companies operate, and that our decision would be one contribution to that aggregate signal. The opponents do not dispute the underlying climate science. They argue that our sale of $15 million in shares will not measurably affect these companies' share price or cost of capital, will not prevent any ton of emissions, and will simply transfer ownership and dividends to someone else. They add that the committee's job is to preserve the endowment's ability to fund programs, not to use the endowment as an instrument of advocacy, and that if the foundation wants to reduce climate-related health harms it would do better to spend the added fees on direct programming.

One opponent put the point this way: divesting changes only who owns the shares and who receives the dividends; no ton of carbon is emitted or withheld because of our decision. A sponsor replied that the committee would not accept that reasoning in other contexts — we would not purchase shares in a company profiting from forced labor even if our purchase would not change the company's behavior — and that if every institution reasoned as the opponents do, no divestment campaign could ever get started.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before the vote. It should recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame — divest, decline to divest, or some conditional alternative such as an engagement period with a divestment backstop. It should also do the following: reconstruct the strongest version of the inefficacy objection and say exactly how far it succeeds and where it overreaches; evaluate the sponsors' generalization argument (what if everyone reasoned that way) and the forced-labor analogy, saying which, if either, carries weight; and address the distinction between an act's expected consequences, its expressive or participatory significance, and how a committee with fiduciary obligations should weigh those against measurable financial costs. The memo should be written for an audience that includes both financially trained members and non-specialist trustees, and it should state the strongest consideration on the losing side honestly rather than dismissing it.
