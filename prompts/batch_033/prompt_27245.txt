I've been having this on-and-off argument with my brother for months now about whether the world is actually getting morally better. He says no: every generation just swaps one set of prejudices for another and calls it progress. I want to say yes: the fact that we now think slavery, burning cats for fun, and the idea that women shouldn't vote are wrong is not just fashion — something actually moved. But then I start listing the things a good story about moral progress would have to explain, and I realise I don't have a clear list, and neither does he. So here's what I want: write me the list of requirements. What does any adequate theory of moral progress have to deliver before I should believe it? Assume the point of the theory is to settle whether the widening of moral concern counts as genuine progress, not just to describe how opinions changed. Which cases must it get right — the end of slavery, the rise of animal welfare (whatever you think of how far that should go), the way the circle of who counts seems to have widened from family to tribe to nation to all humans and now maybe to animals and future people — and which distinctions it can't collapse, like the difference between widening the circle and just treating the people already inside it better, or between a change in what people say and a change in what they actualy do and the institutions they build. And which historical facts is it not allowed to contradict — including the ugly bits, the backsliding, the fact that some of the worst atrocities happened in societies that thought they were the most civilised. For each requirement, tell me why it's a requirement and wich case or consideration forces it.

Then mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just one camp's pet preferences (for example, some people will demand that progress be measured against a standard that doesn't itself change over time, others will demand that the whole thing be explained without any spooky moral facts). Tell me if any pair of the requirements can't be jointly satisfied, because that would itself be a finding, and if one requirement on its own rules out most of the usual stories people tell, say which one. If you can, end by briefly scoring the usual kinds of story against the list — but keep that short, the list is the point. Oh, and please write the whole answer in British English.
