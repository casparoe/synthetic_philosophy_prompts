I recently resigned from a position on an editorial board after watching, over the course of a decade, how the commercial pressure to generate engagement transformed the paper's relationship with its readers — from a demanding but recognisably human exchange into something abstract and frictionless, where articles seemed to address no one in particular while simultaneously inviting everyone to hold an opinion they were not expected to act on. This prompted me to reread Kierkegaard's Two Ages, and I was struck by how much of his account of the press and 'the public' seems to describe, with uncomfortable precision, the dynamics of algorithmically driven social media. I would like you to analyse a specific contemporary phenomenon through his framework: the cycle in which a post or clip, stripped of context, is surfaced by an engagement-maximising algorithm, attracts moralised commentary from people with no connection to the event, escalates into a day-long national controversy, and then vanishes, leaving consequences for the individuals named but no residue of considered judgment or institutional response — the whole sequence having been driven, one might say, by the passion of chatter rather than by anyone's genuine decision.

Specifically: map his concepts of the abstract public, levelling, envy as the negatively unifying principle, and the reduction of individuals to mere spectators or 'third parties' onto this phenomenon. Then tell me where the analogy strains. Kierkegaard thought the press created the public by giving it a medium and a daily rhythm; what does it do to the analysis that the algorithm has no editor, no identifiable intent, and is tuned to nothing but engagement — that 'the press' in his sense has been replaced by something more diffuse and less accountable? Does the fact that the algorithm can be gamed, and often is, reintroduce something like intentionality, or does it make the levelling process even more thoroughgoing than he imagined? Finally, I would like your view on whether the episode vindicates his claim that the only remedy is the recovery of the single individual — or whether it suggests his diagnosis is so total that it leaves no room for the remedy it prescribes.
