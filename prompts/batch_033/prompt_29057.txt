Two editors at the magazine where I work got into a protracted argument about a book review we were preparing to run. The reviewer is a close friend of the author, and the review was strikingly positive — not factually inaccurate, but noticeably more generous than the other reviews we had commissioned of the same book.

Marta argued that we should spike the review. Her claim was that the friendship gave the reviewer a reason to evaluate the book favorably, but it was the wrong kind of reason: it did not make the book any better, so it could not justify a higher assessment of its quality. The review, she said, was compromised.

David pushed back. He said Marta was assuming that only truth-tracking considerations count as reasons for an evaluation, but that is not how evaluation works in practice. Friendship gives a reviewer genuine epistemic access — knowledge of what the author was trying to do, a disposition to read charitably, an ability to see strengths that a hostile reader would overlook. Those are perfectly good reasons for a positive evaluation, and they flow directly from the friendship.

Marta replied that this conflates two distinct things. Reading charitably in order to understand the book accurately is one thing — it helps you track the truth about the book's merits. But loyalty to a friend is another — it makes you want the book to be good, which biases your judgment. The first is a right-kind reason: it bears on whether the book actually has the merits you attribute to it. The second is a wrong-kind reason: it bears on the benefits of holding the evaluation, not on its correctness. In this case, she said, it was obvious which one was doing the work.

David said this distinction was shouldering a lot of weight and he was not sure it could bear it. He asked: what is the test for whether a reason is of the right kind or the wrong kind? If Marta could not supply one, then her objection amounted to an intuition that friendship feels like the wrong kind of reason — which is not an argument.

Marta offered this test: a right-kind reason for evaluating X positively is a fact that would figure in a correct explanation of why X possesses the features that make it good. A wrong-kind reason is a fact that makes it advantageous, pleasant, or socially required to evaluate X positively, but that does not enter into the explanation of X's merits. The friendship, she said, falls squarely in the second category: you can fully explain why the book is good or bad without mentioning that the reviewer and the author are friends.

David said this test merely pushes the problem back. What counts as a feature that makes something good? If I value a book partly because it was written by someone I care about, why is that not a feature that makes the book good for me? Marta's test, he argued, assumes an impersonal standard of goodness that simply rules out relational properties as merit-constituting — which begs the question against anyone who thinks that a book's being written by your friend is itself part of what makes it worth valuing.

At this point they were going in circles, and I had to leave before they resolved anything. I am still unsure who had the better argument. Can you adjudicate their exchange move by move — identifying who is right about which specific claims, where each argument goes wrong if it does, and whether there is a principled way to settle the underlying issue they were really fighting about?
