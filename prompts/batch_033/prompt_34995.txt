I am drafting a short essay and would welcome comments on the following sketch before I expand it. The working thesis is that underdetermination of theory by evidence is less a self-contained argument for anti-realism than a diagnostic problem. In its weak form, where rivals are underdetermined only by the evidence so far available, it shows that theory choice is provisional and revisable, which few realists deny. In its strong form, where any theory is said to have an empirically equivalent rival even given all possible evidence, it either relies on so thin a notion of empirical equivalence that the rivals differ only in philosophically negligible ways, or it assumes without argument that empirically equivalent theories are equally supported. The constructive upshot I want to defend is that the real residue of the problem is a demand for an account of why non-empirical virtues—simplicity, coherence, unifying power, fertility—carry epistemic weight in theory choice; and any adequate account must explain how those virtues could be truth-conducive without quietly presupposing the realism the argument was meant to challenge.

I would be grateful for comments on three points: (i) whether the weak/strong distinction is drawn correctly and my criticism of the strong form is fair; (ii) whether the constructive claim actually follows from the criticisms, or whether I have merely assumed that non-empirical virtues require epistemic justification; and (iii) whether the essay, as sketched, would be worth developing for a knowledgeable reader, and where it is most vulnerable. Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested in the literature, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
