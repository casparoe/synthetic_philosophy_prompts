I have come to accept Van Fraassen's Reflection principle as a genuine constraint on rational credence, not merely a useful heuristic. In its simplest form: my current credence in a proposition should equal my current expectation of my future credence in that proposition, or equivalently, conditional on my future credence in A being r, my present credence in A should be r. The version I accept is the usual qualified one, restricted to future selves who are rational, at least as well informed as I am now, and formed their views by a route I can endorse.

I want a map of what would count against even this qualified principle. I am not interested only in the familiar drug-and-brainwashing counterexamples that the qualification is designed to exclude; I want the pressure points that remain once those are set aside. Specifically: which cases (forgetting, Sleeping Beauty, subtler forms of anticipated bias), which formal results (the fate of the Dutch-book argument, the principle's relation to conditionalization, the question of whether it coheres with the Principal Principle in undermining scenarios), and which intuitions should move me — sorted from fatal to merely awkward. For each, I want to know whether a committed defender of Reflection would concede that it counts against the principle, or would dismiss it as a misapplication. I would personally find most persuasive a demonstration that Reflection is not implied by any independently motivated norm of coherence and that the arguments offered for it already presuppose the very deference they are meant to establish. Finally, tell me honestly whether the standard qualifications have insulated the principle into unfalsifiability, and whether that would be a problem for a norm of this kind — that is, whether Reflection risks anything at all, or has been made compatible with every possible outcome.
