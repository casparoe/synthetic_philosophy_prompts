I lead AI governance at a mid-sized software company, and I need a decision procedure my team can reuse for every system we ship. We deploy a range of systems built on large language models, from classifiers and summarizers to long-running workflow executors, and our risk policy treats these differently: systems classified as agentic require red-teaming, a human-approval gate before any nontrivial action, and sign-off from our safety committee, while tool systems go through ordinary software review. The policy's current definition — an agentic system is one that pursues goals with autonomy — has become useless at decision time, and different teams exploit its vagueness in both directions.

The people running the procedure are product managers and engineers, not philosophers. They will have a system design document and a working demo, and they need a classification within an afternoon. The result has to be defensible to our lawyers and to the engineers, who will often dislike the classification they receive.

Please write the procedure: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops and returns tool or agent, and which steps are judgment calls about values rather than facts that cannot be eliminated and must be handed to a person — and who that person should be. Ground the procedure in the distinctions that actually separate tool-like from agent-like systems, such as goal-directedness, autonomy, persistence of goals, who selects the actions, and whether the system is optimizing over states of the world or computing outputs for a user, and whatever else you think does real work. Be explicit about which distinctions you are keeping, which you are dropping, and where the simplification will misfire.

The procedure must classify the system as deployed, not the underlying model: the same model can be a tool in one product and an agent in another. It should also handle the hard cases we repeatedly hit: a system given a goal but confined to a sandbox, a tool with one high-stakes action taken without human approval, and a stateless model called in a loop by an external script.

Then run the procedure on three proposed systems:

1. A customer-support assistant that answers questions and can issue refunds up to $50 by calling our billing API without any human in the loop.
2. A coding assistant that, given a GitHub issue, plans the change, edits several files, commits them, and opens a pull request, all before a human looks at it.
3. A document-processing pipeline that ingests contracts, extracts clauses, and flags anomalies, with a human approving every flag before it is reported to a client.

Make sure at least one of the applications returns a classification you expect us to resist, and say plainly what the procedure cannot settle.
