Reconstructing from memory, the passage runs roughly like this: "Condorcet proved mathematically that if you gather a group of people who are each more likely than not to answer a yes-or-no question correctly, then the majority will be more reliable than any individual member of the group, and as the group grows the probability that the majority is correct approaches certainty. That is the deep reason crowds are wise: for any binary decision, a large enough group will almost surely settle it correctly, which is why democratic votes, juries, and prediction markets deserve more trust than individual experts, and why a larger decision-making body is always an improvement over a smaller one."

Could you go through the summary sentence by sentence and tell me what a social choice theorist would accept, what they would insist on qualifying, and what is simply not a consequence of the theorem?
