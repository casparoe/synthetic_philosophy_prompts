Exam question (25 points):

State the Kelly criterion for a repeated binary bet, explain the mathematical argument that it maximizes the asymptotic growth rate of wealth, and critically evaluate whether maximizing expected logarithmic growth is rationally mandatory for an agent with a non-logarithmic utility function over wealth. Be precise about what the criterion does and does not maximize.

Student answer:

The Kelly criterion gives the fraction of one's bankroll to stake on a favorable bet that is repeated many times. For a binary bet that pays net odds b, with win probability p and loss probability q = 1 − p, the Kelly fraction is f* = (bp − q)/b; for an even-money bet this reduces to f* = p − q, the bettor's edge. The standard rationale is that this fraction maximizes the expected logarithm of wealth. If an agent's utility function is logarithmic in wealth, then Kelly betting is optimal by construction. By the law of large numbers, the realized per-period logarithmic growth rate of a bettor using a fixed fraction f converges to its expectation E[log(1 + fX)], so maximizing expected log wealth is the same as maximizing the asymptotic growth rate of the bankroll. Because log is concave and tends to −∞ at zero wealth, the rule never risks the entire bankroll and therefore balances growth against ruin. Since what matters for long-run wealth is the growth rate, Kelly also maximizes expected wealth in the long run. It follows that a rational agent facing a long sequence of independent favorable bets should always bet full Kelly. The only complication is that p and b must be estimated in practice; estimation error justifies betting fractional Kelly, but that is a practical concession rather than a challenge to the criterion's normative status. Even if an agent's utility function is not logarithmic, she should still use Kelly. The market's offered odds do not change her preferences; what matters is that the Kelly policy's asymptotic growth rate exceeds that of every other fixed-fraction policy, so it is the rational policy no matter what her utility function is.
