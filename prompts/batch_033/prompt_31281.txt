**Exam question (from the final exam):**

Explain the procreation asymmetry as formulated by David Benatar. What role does it play in his argument for the conclusion that coming into existence is always a harm, and what is one significant objection to the asymmetry? (12 points)

---

**Student's answer:**

The procreation asymmetry, as developed by David Benatar in *Better Never to Have Been*, is a thesis about the different ways pleasure and pain count in decisions about bringing people into existence. It can be stated as four claims:

(1) The presence of pain is bad.
(2) The presence of pleasure is good.
(3) The absence of pain is good, even if there is no one who experiences that good.
(4) The absence of pleasure is not bad, provided there is no one who is deprived of it.

The crucial asymmetry is between (3) and (4). In the case where a person never exists, the pain they avoid counts as a genuine good — it makes that state of affairs better — but the pleasure they miss out on does not count as a bad. This asymmetry is supposed to capture a widely shared intuition: we think we have a reason not to bring a child into existence if they would suffer terribly, but we do not think we have a reason to bring a child into existence merely because they would be happy. The asymmetry explains this difference in our moral reasoning.

Benatar deploys the asymmetry as the central premise in his argument that coming into existence is always a net harm. His reasoning runs as follows. For any possible life, non-existence guarantees the absence of all the pain that life would contain, and by (3) this absence is good. Meanwhile, the absence of all the pleasure that life would contain is, by (4), not bad. Therefore non-existence is always preferable to existence, regardless of how good the life in question would be. Even the best human life contains some pain, and the asymmetry tells us that avoiding that pain is good in a way that missing out on pleasure is not bad. So it is always better never to have been.

One important objection is that the asymmetry is unmotivated. Critics contend that if the absence of pain is good even without a subject to enjoy that good, then by parity of reasoning the absence of pleasure ought to be bad even without a subject to suffer that bad. Benatar treats pleasure and pain asymmetrically, but he gives no independent justification for doing so. If we reject the asymmetry, the antinatalist argument collapses, because then the absent pleasures of non-existence would count against it just as the absent pains count in its favor, and the comparison between existence and non-existence would depend on the particular balance of pleasure and pain in each life.
