Write a dialogue between two political philosophers walking out of a packed arena after a Wilt Chamberlain game. One defends Nozick's entitlement theory of justice; the other defends a patterned principle of distributive justice, such as strict equality or a Rawlsian difference principle. Their dispute centers on Nozick's Wilt Chamberlain argument: begin with a distribution that satisfies your favored pattern; allow fans to pay Chamberlain twenty-five cents each for the pleasure of watching him; the resulting distribution violates the pattern, although each transfer looked free and unobjectionable. Nozick takes this to show that liberty upsets all patterns and that preserving a pattern requires continuous interference with people's lives.

Have the two philosophers engage the argument rather than restate it. The defender of patterns should press the point that the example's starting point is stipulated just but may be unrealistic, and that genuinely voluntary transfers presuppose just background conditions, not merely a patterned distribution. The entitlement theorist should press the point that any after-the-fact correction of the resulting distribution seems to undo choices the fans and Chamberlain had every right to make. Let them also dispute whether the example shows that patterned principles are incompatible with liberty in principle, or only that they require taxation or institutional design rather than prohibition. Make each character respond to the other's strongest formulation, and do not end with a judge's summary or a declared winner.
