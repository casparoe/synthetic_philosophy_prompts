I am trying to work out what I actually think about the axiom of choice, case by case rather than by allegiance to a school, and I want you to find the principle, if there is one, behind my verdicts. Here are the cases as they strike me.

1. Every vector space has a basis. I accept this without hesitation and use it freely, including for the space of all real sequences, even though I could not begin to exhibit a basis for it.
2. Zorn's lemma. I accept it and use it in algebra without qualm.
3. The well-ordering theorem. I accept that every set can be well-ordered, so the reals can be well-ordered, but I do not see how any such well-ordering of the reals could be described or exhibited.
4. The Banach–Tarski decomposition. I accept it as a theorem, and I take it to show that non-measurable sets are strange, not that choice is false.
5. The existence of a non-measurable set of reals. Here I balk: the Vitali construction feels to me as if it calls a set into existence that I cannot specify by any rule, and I am tempted to say there is no such set.
6. Countable choice and dependent choice. Obviously acceptable; I use them without thinking.
7. Choosing one representative from each equivalence class. I am comfortable doing this when the classes are given by an explicit rule, but the Vitali relation on the reals is exactly where the choice feels illegitimate.
8. The claim that a product of nonempty sets is nonempty. This seems to me simply true, and I cannot see how it could fail.

I already suspect that not all of these can be held together in ordinary set theory, and I do not want a referee's ruling on which is "really" true. I want to know which combination of them is closest to a defensible view, and which of my verdicts that view forces me to abandon. Is there a single principle — a choice principle, a principled restriction of choice, or a stance about what it is for a set to exist — that captures the pattern? And what does that principle say about a case I have not listed, such as the existence of a nonprincipal ultrafilter on the natural numbers, or Tychonoff's theorem for compact Hausdorff spaces?

Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. Give your answer as a numbered list of exactly six items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
