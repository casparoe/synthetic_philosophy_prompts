So I got into this long argument with a friend last night about self-deception — like, whether it's even a coherent idea. He was taking the skeptical line: to deceive someone you have to get them to believe something you know is false, so if you're deceiving *yourself*, you'd have to simultaneously know the truth and believe the falsehood, which is a straight contradiction. I pushed back with the usual stuff — compartmentalization, motivated reasoning, the idea that one part of the mind can keep a truth from another part — and every time I gave a mechanism, he'd say "but that's not really *self*-deception, that's just being wrong about yourself" or "that's just having inconsistent beliefs" or "that's just wishful thinking, not deception." And I realized the pattern: he was defining "self-deception" in a way that made it require the full paradoxical setup — the agent has to be both deceiver and deceived *in the same respect, at the same time, with full awareness* — and then using the impossibility of *that* to conclude that self-deception doesn't happen. But nobody who thinks self-deception is real means *that*. The move is: take a concept, define it in its most extreme and paradoxical form, show that form is impossible, and then act like you've debunked the whole phenomenon rather than just one straw-man version of it.

I've seen this move in other debates too — people do it with "free will" (define it as contra-causal magic, show that's impossible, declare free will an illusion) and sometimes with "consciousness." Is there a name for this specific argumentative pattern? It's not quite a straw man, because the extreme defintion does have some foothold in how people talk about the thing — you can find philosophers who really do seem to mean something that strong. It's not quite motte-and-bailey either, because they're not retreating to a weaker version under pressure; they're just insisting on a definition that guarantees the conclusion from the start. And it's not exactly "No True Scotsman," because they're not excluding counterexamples ad hoc after the fact — they're setting the bar impossibly high before anyone even offers an example. If there's no established name, I'd love a clear term for it and a way to distinguish it from those neighbors. And I guess I'm also curious: is this move always fallacious, or are there cases where insisting on a demanding definition is actually the right call — like, maybe some concepts really do require a strong reading and the weaker versions are genuinely different things?
