I'm a novelist, and I'm stuck. My current book has a lot of scenes where characters try to prove something about themselves to someone else—loyalty, competence, love, status—through some kind of costly display, and I keep getting the note from my editor that the scenes don't convince. "He wouldn't be moved by that," she says, or "this feels like a gesture not a signal." I realized I don't actually have a grip on when a costly action should persuade the character it's aimed at, and when it should look like a cheap bid.

I read about the handicap principle years ago—the idea that signals are honest when they're costly enough that a low-quality individual couldn't afford to send them, so the signal carries information. I get the peacocks tail and the engagement ring. But applying it to the scenes I'm actually writing is a mess. Human beings signal with money, time, risk, embarrassment, cruelty, self-sabotage. The "cost" isn't always obvious and the "quality" being signaled isn't always obvious either. I need a procedure I can run on a scene I'm drafting—grounded in the actual logic of costly signaling, not just writing advice with a jargon gloss—that tells me whether a character's display would genuinely land as honest. Something I can use in ten minutes, not a research program.

Please give me exactly five steps, each a sentence or two, no introduction, no conclusion. I want the steps to be genuinely derived from the theory, and please flag clearly which steps are mechanical and which depend on my own judgment as a writer. Then run the procedure on two scenes: (1) a wealthy man takes a woman to a restaurant where he clearly doesn't belong—can't pronounce the dishes, uses the wrong fork—to show her he's willing to be humiliated for her; (2) a politician donates a large sum to a cause he publicly opposes, does it anonymously, and then tells exactly one person, swearing them to secrecy.
