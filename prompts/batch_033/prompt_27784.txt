I'm a journalist working on a longform piece about the science and philosophy of dreaming, and I've found myself stuck on a conceptual knot that I suspect can only be untied by someone who moves comfortably between the two registers. I've been reading the exchange between Norman Malcolm and Daniel Dennett on whether dreams are experiences — Malcolm arguing in *Dreaming* (1959) that the concept of being asleep simply rules out having experiences, since sleep is by definition a state in which one is not conscious, and that dream reports are therefore not reports of past mental events but something more like stories we find ourselves inclined to tell upon waking; Dennett responding, in "Are Dreams Experiences?" (1976), with the cassette theory and related thought experiments, suggesting that the dream narrative could be composed and inserted into memory at the moment of awakening, with no prior conscious episode corresponding to it. The debate has stayed with me because it seems to sit exactly at the boundary where conceptual analysis and empirical neuroscience ought to be able to talk to each other, but in practice the conversation rarely happens.

I have a decent working knowledge of cognitive neuroscience — I can follow papers on memory consolidation, hippocampal replay, the global workspace theory of consciousness, the distinction between REM and non-REM dream reports, prefrontal deactivation during REM sleep, and the neural correlates of consciousness more broadly. What I'd like you to do is restate the Malcolm–Dennett debate in that neuroscientific vocabulary as faithfully as you can. For Malcolm's side, translate his claim that sleep precludes experience into talk of cortical activation thresholds, the breakdown of the global workspace, or whatever constructs are appropriate; for Dennett's side, translate the cassette theory into the language of memory consolidation, offline replay, and the composition of narrative memories without concurrent conscious access. Then give me the residue report: which claims translate cleanly, which ones you had to distort or fudge to get across, and which ones have no counterpart at all in the neuroscientific framework. I'm especially interested in whether the residue cuts both ways — whether there is also something the neuroscientific picture wants to say about dreaming that has no natural expression in the philosophical vocabulary Malcolm and Dennett were using. Finally, I'd like you to tell me where your translation is most likely to fail: what empirical finding (say, a reliable neural signature of conscious experience during sleep that we haven't yet identified) or philosophical argument would force you to redraw the mapping, and why that particular vulnerability rather than some other.
