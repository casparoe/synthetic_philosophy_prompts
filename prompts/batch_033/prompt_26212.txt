I'm putting together a systematic view of practical reasons and rationality, and before I commit to it in writing I want an audit of whether the pieces actually fit together. Here are the commitments I'm working with:

(1) **Factualism about reasons.** Reasons for action are facts — states of the world — not mental states like beliefs or desires. If the fact that the bridge will collapse is a reason not to cross it, that reason is the fact itself, not my belief that the bridge will collapse.

(2) **Free-floating reasons.** There can be reasons that no agent has ever entertained, recognized, or reasoned from. A reason doesn't need a reasoner. The fact that a particular plant compound inhibits a disease is a reason to administer it, even if no one has discovered the compound yet.

(3) **Objectivism about 'ought'.** What an agent ought to do is determined by the balance of all the reasons that actually apply to their situation — not by the reasons they believe or have evidence to believe apply. If the bridge is sound but all your evidence says it will collapse, you don't thereby have a reason not to cross it; you merely have a reason to gather more evidence.

(4) **Reasons-first account of rationality.** To be rational just is to respond correctly to reasons. A rational belief is one supported by the balance of epistemic reasons; a rational action is one supported by the balance of practical reasons. There is no independent domain of "rationality" over and above proper responsiveness to reasons.

(5) **Structural requirements of rationality.** There are rational requirements that govern combinations of attitudes regardless of the reasons for or against the individual attitudes — for example, means-end coherence (if you intend an end and believe some means is necessary, you're rationally required to intend the means) and enkrasia (if you believe you ought to φ, you're rationally required to intend to φ). These requirements aren't themselves reasons for the attitudes they govern; they're constraints of a different kind.

(6) **No overridingness of morality.** Moral reasons are a subset of practical reasons and carry no special structural priority over other kinds of reasons. The balance of reasons can, in principle, favor an immoral action — though in practice this may be rare. Morality doesn't settle the question of what one ought to do all-things-considered.

I want you to go through these six commitments and tell me whether they form a consistent package. Here's the structure I want your response to follow:

**1. Pairwise scan (roughly 2 paragraphs).** Go through the pairs of commitments that might pull against each other and flag which ones are worth examining. Don't yet resolve anything — just identify the candidate tensions. A table or list is fine here.

**2. Apparent tensions that dissolve (roughly 2–3 paragraphs).** For each candidate tension that turns out, on closer inspection, not to be a genuine problem, explain why the apparent conflict is illusory. Be specific about what someone would be conflating if they thought there was a problem here.

**3. Genuine tensions, ranked by severity (roughly 3–4 paragraphs).** Take the remaining tensions and order them from most to least severe. For each, explain exactly what makes the two commitments hard to hold simultaneously, and what a defender of the overall package would have to say to keep both. Don't just note that they're in tension — trace the argument that puts pressure on the pair.

**4. Repair options (roughly 2 paragraphs).** Identify the single revision to my set of commitments that would resolve the most tension at the lowest philosophical cost. Then identify which commitment, if I were forced to drop exactly one, would be the least damaging to the overall spirit of the view. These might be the same thing, but they might not — if they differ, explain why.
