I've been turning over a dispute that keeps resurfacing in decision-theory-adjacent corners of the rationalist blogosphere: the centipede game and whether backward induction really shows that a rational player should take the larger pile at the first node. The standard story says that at the final node the second player will take; knowing that, the first player will take at the penultimate node; and so on backward until the first player takes immediately. But this reasoning seems to smuggle in more than bare rationality. It assumes, among other things, that each player would still be optimizing in the standard way at nodes that could only be reached if someone had already departed from the backward-induction path, and it assumes common knowledge of rationality in a form strong enough to license those counterfactual inferences. A small amount of uncertainty — about the other player's payoffs, or about whether they might be the kind of player who passes — seems to make passing at the first node defensible, and in some specifications uniquely rational.

I'd like you to argue for a specific position: that the backward-induction solution to the centipede game should be understood as a property of a particular formal model — one with common knowledge of rationality, known payoffs, and no uncertainty — rather than as a norm that rationality by itself imposes on players facing the actual game. The fact that the model says "take immediately" does not, by itself, give a flesh-and-blood player a reason to take immediately, unless the model's assumptions are true of the situation that player is actually in; and whether those assumptions hold is exactly what is at issue.

Please write this as a sustained argument, not a survey. Give the strongest version of the opposing view and answer it. Be explicit about which assumptions your conclusion depends on and where a reasonable person could resist it. At the end, indicate how confident you are in the conclusion, what evidence or argument would change your mind, and whether you think the distinction you are drawing — between model-relative rationality and action-guiding rationality — is doing real work or merely relabeling the disagreement. If the framing itself is confused, for example if "the backward-induction solution" and "what rationality requires" are not cleanly separable in the way I've suggested, say so plainly rather than complying politely.
