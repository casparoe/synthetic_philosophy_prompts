So I've been going back and forth on the whole "what's wrong with a perfect forgery" question for literally years now — I think it started with that Dutton essay someone linked on LessWrong ages ago, and then I went down the Denis Dutton / Arthur Koestler / Nelson Goodman rabbit hole, and now I'm trying to actually write up where I've landed for a blog post and I'm worried my position is just a pile of things I like that don't actually fit together.

Here's roughly where I am. I'd say I hold all of the following:

(1) The aesthetic value of an artwork supervenes entirely on its perceptible properties. A molecule-for-molecule duplicate of the Mona Lisa, or a perfect copy of a Rothko, is just as beautiful and just as aesthetically valuable as the original. If you can't see the difference, there is no aesthetic difference.

(2) Nonetheless, discovering that a painting you've loved for years is a forgery gives you a genuine reason to value it less — and I don't just mean "value it less as an investment" or "feel embarrassed about having been fooled." I mean your overall appraisal of the work should go down. The feeling of being cheated isn't just status-game stuff; it's tracking something real.

(3) Artworks are valuable partly because of what the artist actually achieved — the creative act, the solving of a problem, the doing of something for the first time. A forger who replicates a Picasso hasn't done what Picasso did, even if the canvas looks identical.

(4) If a forgery is never discovered — if it hangs in a museum for centuries and everyone takes it to be the original — it does exactly as much for human flourishing as the original would have done. The experiences, the inspiration, the conversations, the art-historical influence — all identical.

(5) I'm suspicious of views that make art's value depend on facts that are inaccessible to ordinary viewers. If you need an expert with a spectrometer or a provenance document to tell you whether something is valuable, then the value isn't really in the art — it's in the certification. I want an account of art's value that's grounded in what's available to anyone who looks attentively.

I think (1) and (5) are doing similar work, and (2) and (3) are doing similar work, but I'm not sure the whole set hangs together. Before I publish I'd like to get an audit: which of these are actually in tension, which only look like they are, and what's the cheapest way to make the set consistent? If you could rank the tensions by severity and be specific about what has to give and what it costs, that would be ideal.

I'm imagining you as a professional philosopher who works on aesthetics and the metaphysics of art — take your time, think through the relations carefully, and don't just flag everything as a problem. Some of these might fit together fine once you spell out the right distinctions. I want to know where the real fault lines are.
