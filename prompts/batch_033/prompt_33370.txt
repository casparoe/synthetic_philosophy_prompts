I've been reading the Wealth of Nations alongside the standard histories of the American Revolution, and I keep being struck by how neatly Smith's account of colonies maps onto the imperial crisis — and how much it also seems to miss. As I understand him, Smith argues that Britain's monopoly of American trade and its political dominion over the colonies were, as a system, a net burden on the British public: the profits were captured by merchants while the costs of colonial administration and defense were socialized, and the rational alternatives were either full political incorporation on equal terms or a voluntary, amicable separation. He also predicted, while the crisis was still unfolding, that trying to hold the colonies by force would end in losing them.

I'd like a careful case analysis of the imperial crisis — from the end of the Seven Years' War through the Declaration of Independence — using Smith's account of colonies as the lens. Be disciplined about the mapping: who, in Smith's framework, are the relevant actors and interests; what does his analysis say the British state actually faced and should have done; and which features of the crisis does it genuinely explain? I have in mind the Stamp Act, the quarrel over who would pay for colonial defense, the Navigation Acts and the trade monopoly, and the merchant lobbies that pushed for repeal. And where does the framework misdescribe or fall silent? In particular, how much of the actual rupture was about the economics of empire, and how much about constitutional principle, parliamentary sovereignty, and the colonists' claim that they were being denied the rights of Englishmen — categories Smith seems poorly equipped to handle?

Then run the analysis in the other direction and give a committed verdict: is the American Revolution, on balance, a confirmation of Smith's diagnosis, an anomaly for it, or a case that exposes the limits of a political-economic explanation of imperial breakdown? I want a definite position with reasons, not a survey of possible readings — including an honest account of where the theory is genuinely silent rather than stretched to speak. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble and no restating of the question.
