I've been going down a rabbit hole with Bongard problems lately — those little puzzles where you see six boxes on the left and six on the right, and you have to figure out the rule that separates them. They got me thinking about what makes a concept learnable from just a handful of examples. I've been going thru my own intuitions on a bunch of cases and I can't quite extract the principle. Here's where I've landed:

Case A: Left side has circles, right side has triangles. I'd learn this from two examples. The concept "circle" is perfectly projectible.

Case B: Left side has convex shapes, right side has concave ones. Same — convexity feels like a natural concept, I'd pick it up fast.

Case C: Left side has shapes with an odd number of sides, right side has even. This feels learnable too, though maybe it takes an extra example or two. Still a natural concept.

Case D: Left side has small triangles and large circles, right side has large triangles and small circles. This feels off. I mean, I could learn it, but it feels like a disjunctive mess — "small-if-triangle-or-large-if-circle" on the left, the opposite on the right. I want to say this concept is less natural, less projectible.

Case E: Left side has shapes whose English names start with a vowel (circle, octagon, ellipse) and right side has shapes whose names start with a consonant (triangle, square, pentagon). This feels completely unlearnable from the drawings alone. The concept isn't in the images at all — it's a fact about our labeling, not about the shapes.

Case F: Here's where I get stuck. Left side has a single large shape, right side has two small shapes. It's a compound concept — one-vs-two AND large-vs-small. My gut says it's learnable, about as natural as Case C. But why? Case D is also a compound concept (shape and size interacting) and it felt awful. What's the difference?

Case G: Goodman's grue. Emeralds observed before 2050 are green, after 2050 they're blue. Not projectible. But "all emeralds are green" is projectible. The two hypotheses are symmetric in the data we have — every green emerald observed so far is also grue. But only one feels like a real induction. I'm with Goodman on the verdict but I can't say why.

Case H: Last one. I'm thinking of a Bongard problem where the rule is "shapes that are topologically equivalent to a disk" (no holes) vs. "shapes with at least one hole." This feels extremely natural — maybe even more natural than Case C. But it's also a fairly abstract mathematical property.

So here's what I'm asking: is there a single principle, or a small set of principles, that captures which of these concepts are projectible from few examples and which aren't? And if you can give me the principle, what does it predict about a case I haven't listed — say, a Bongard problem where the rule is "shapes that are symmetric about the vertical axis" vs. "shapes that aren't"? And please say which of my verdicts the principle has to overturn, if any.

Oh, and please answer in American English. I'm a retired physicist and I've only recently gotten into this stuff, so don't assume I know all the jargon.
