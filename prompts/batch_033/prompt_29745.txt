I have spent the last year in a working group on formal approaches to ethics, and our discussion of Harsanyi's aggregation theorem has settled into a stalemate that strikes me as philosophically interesting in itself. The mathematics is not in dispute. If both the individuals and a social observer satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, and the social ordering respects a Pareto condition over lotteries, then the social utility function must be expressible as a weighted sum of the individual utility functions, up to an additive constant. Everyone in the room accepts this, and everyone agrees that the theorem is valid. What is contested is what, if anything, the theorem establishes. One side — call it the representation camp, associated with Sen's informational critique and with a good deal of formal social choice theory — holds that the result is normatively inert. On this view, the theorem says only that, once arbitrary von Neumann–Morgenstern representations of individual preferences have been chosen, any social utility function satisfying the axioms can be written in that additive form; but because such representations are unique only up to positive affine transformations, the weights in the sum are artifacts of scaling, and the theorem neither uses nor delivers interpersonal comparability of welfare. So it cannot be a derivation of utilitarianism. The other side — call it the derivation camp, associated with Harsanyi himself and with later defenders such as Broome — replies that this criticism asks a theorem to do something no theorem can do. What the result shows, they argue, is that if there is to be a single coherent social preference over lotteries that tracks individual preferences in the specified way, it must have the additively separable form; that structural constraint is itself a substantive step toward utilitarianism, and the choice of weights is precisely the remaining ethical task, not a refutation of the theorem's significance. The two sides do not disagree about any formula, and yet neither has moved in decades.

I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is, not a verdict on who wins. Is it a substantive normative disagreement, a verbal disagreement about what "utility" or "utilitarianism" means, a disagreement about the normative status of mathematical results and what a foundation requires, or two groups answering different questions — one about the representation of preferences, the other about the justification of a moral principle — under a single heading? I also suspect there is a hidden technical question about whether the Pareto condition should be applied ex ante, to preferences over lotteries, or ex post, to outcomes, and about whether that choice is doing more of the work than either side acknowledges. Given your diagnosis, tell me what would actually count as progress: a sharper formal theorem, a distinction that dissolves the apparent conflict, a reframing of what the theorem is supposed to show, or an argument that the debate is structured so that no progress should be expected.
