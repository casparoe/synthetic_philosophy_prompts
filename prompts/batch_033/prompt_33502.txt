I'm a philosophy professor preparing a paper on the limits of debate as an AI alignment strategy, and my intended audience is machine learning researchers. I need the core philosophical debate about "obfuscated arguments" — the worry that there may exist arguments that are systematically easier to construct than to evaluate, and that this asymmetry could undermine the debate protocol even when one of the debaters is honest — restated in the vocabulary that ML researchers actually use day to day. I am not asking for an analogy or a popularization. I want a systematic translation: every substantive claim the philosophers make should be rendered in the vocabulary of adversarial robustness, computational asymmetry between generation and verification, oracle access, query complexity, the P versus NP intuition, and related concepts from learning theory, insofar as an honest translation permits.

The response should have three sections, in this order, with the approximate lengths indicated:

1. **Translation** (roughly 60% of the response). Restate the positive case for debate (that a human judge, by comparing two adversarial agents, can surface truth beyond their own evaluative competence), the obfuscated-arguments objection, and the standard replies — for instance, that the honest debater can flag the obfuscation for the judge, or that the judge can be recursively amplified. Where a philosophical claim resists translation — where there is genuinely no counterpart in the target vocabulary — say so explicitly rather than forcing a false cognate.

2. **Residue Report** (roughly 25%). Catalogue what survived the translation intact, what survived only with distortion (and what the distortion was), and what could not be expressed at all. I am especially interested in whether the normative dimension of the debate — the claim that debate is supposed to yield truth in a way that constitutes an alignment solution — has any counterpart in how ML researchers think about adversarial training or robustness, or whether it evaporates.

3. **Assessment** (roughly 15%). Give your verdict on the residue. Is the philosophical worry about obfuscated arguments isomorphic to a known open problem in ML — for example, the fundamental hardness of adversarial robustness, or the gap between train and test distributions — or does it depend on assumptions about the judge, about the space of possible arguments, about what counts as "truth" that have no analogue in any actual learning system? Does the translation exercise reveal the philosophical debate to be more or less substantive than it first appeared?
