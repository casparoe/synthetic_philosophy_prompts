I'm a software engineer, so my default way of handling decisions is to enumerate options, assign probabilities and utilities, and take the highest expected value. But I've been stuck on a big personal decision — whether to move to a different country with my partner — and it feels like a recursive bug. If I go, the person I become will probably have different preferences, different friendships, maybe even different values, so my current utility function is like a legacy codebase trying to evaluate a runtime it was never compiled for. I've seen people online call this kind of choice a transformative experience: the choice itself changes what you care about, so you can't really evaluate it from the outside.

I keep flip-flopping between two instincts. One says: ask people who already made the move — they have the data. But the other says those people have been rewritten by the choice, so their advice is coming from a different value system than mine. In between there's a pile of other strategies I've seen thrown around. (a) Just do the expected-value math with your current preferences and stop overthinking it. (b) Put heavy weight on the testimony of people who already made the move. (c) Run cheap simluations first — visit for a month, learn some of the language — and treat that as evidence, even though the simulation can't reproduce the transformation. (d) Minimize future regret by picking whichever option leaves the most doors open. (e) Go with whatever your present self most wants, on the grounds that you shouldn't betray who you are now. (f) Try to pick which future self you'd rather become, and vote for that one. If you had to allocate 100 points of decision weight across these strategies for a genuinely preference-changing choice like this, how would you split them, and why does each one get what it gets? You can also add a strategy I missed and take points from the others.
