I have a set of firm judgments about who should be helped when harms to different people point in different directions, and I suspect they hang together, but I cannot yet state the principle behind them. Going case by case: if five strangers are trapped in one place and one stranger in another, and I can reach only one group, I should rescue the five — the numbers count there. If I could save five patients by killing one healthy person and harvesting his organs, I must not do it — the numbers do not count there. If a technician will suffer a severe and painful electric shock unless I interrupt a live championship broadcast for fifteen minutes, spoiling the finish for millions of viewers, I should interrupt it — a million mild disappointments do not add up to a reason to let one person be badly injured. If one person needs a scarce drug to survive while two others need the same drug only to avoid a week of severe headaches, I should give it to the one — severity can outweigh numbers. But when I must choose between curing one person of a terminal illness and curing two people of an equally terminal illness, I cure the two. I keep trying to turn these into a Scanlon-style anti-aggregation principle, but my verdicts force qualifications, and I am currently tempted by a threshold view: aggregate across persons, except that using someone as a mere means is never permitted, and no number of harms below some threshold of seriousness outweighs a harm above it. Is that the right principle, and how should the threshold be stated? In particular, what does it say about the case I keep going back and forth on, where I must choose between one person becoming quadriplegic and two people each losing a finger?
