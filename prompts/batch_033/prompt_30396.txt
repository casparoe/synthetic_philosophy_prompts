Analyze the 2008 financial crisis through Keynes's distinction between risk and uncertainty. The facts I have in mind are the familiar ones: in the run-up to the crisis, banks, rating agencies, and regulators came to rely on quantitative risk models—above all Value-at-Risk—and on credit ratings that attached default probabilities to mortgage-backed securities and collateralized debt obligations; when U.S. house prices fell and interbank lending froze in 2007–08, losses repeatedly exceeded what the models had treated as extreme scenarios, and institutions behaved as if the probabilities they had been using were not genuine knowledge. Give a disciplined mapping rather than a broad narrative. Spell out Keynes's distinction between measurable risk and non-measurable uncertainty, including the role of the weight of argument if you think it matters. Then identify who in the pre-crisis system was treating uncertainty as if it were calculable risk, what Keynes's view implies they should have done differently, and which features of the crisis the distinction explains. End with an honest account of where the lens breaks: what about 2008 the distinction does not by itself explain, and whether the episode is better read as a vindication of Keynes's distinction or as evidence that it is too blunt to guide institutions. Reach and defend a conclusion.
