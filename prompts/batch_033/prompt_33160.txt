I'm reading the old 1891 James Legge translation of the Zhuangzi for a reading group, and this bit in chapter six (the "Great and Most Honoured Master") has me stuck:

"The True men of old knew nothing of the love of life or of the hatred of death. Entrance into life occasioned them no joy; the exit from it awakened no resistance. Composedly they went and came."

I get that it's partly about not fearing death, but I dont see what the first clause about "the love of life" is doing. Is Zhuangzi saying a True Man doesnt love life either? If so, why isnt this just a general numbness, rather than something specific about how to face death? And is "Entrance into life occasioned them no joy" supposed to be a grim claim about birth, or is it saying that birth and death are both just transformations of the same stuff, so neither is cause for celebration or despair? Also, what exactly does "Composedly they went and came" mean here — is "went and came" just a poetic way of saying they lived and died, or does it pick out something else about returning to the Dao? I'd like someone to walk me through the actual conceptual move in this passage, especially what "love of life" amounts to in this context: is it craving to stay alive, or ordinary enjoyment of being alive? And does the text here actually claim that death isnt bad, or only that the True Man isnt disturbed by it? I dont need a summary of the whole chapter — just help unpacking this.
