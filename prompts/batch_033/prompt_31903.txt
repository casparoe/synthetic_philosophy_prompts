I've spent the last couple of years reading political philosophy after a career in experimental physics, and I've arrived at a set of views that I think of as broadly conservative in a Burkean sense, but I'm not sure they all fit together. I'd like someone to audit them for consistency. Here they are:

(1) I accept Chesterton's fence as a defeasible but strong presumption: you should not reform or abolish an inherited institution, practice, or norm unless you can articulate why it was established and what function it served.

(2) I believe that the accumulated experience embedded in long-standing institutions constitutes a genuine form of knowledge — a kind of distributed wisdom — that is not fully available to any individual reasoner, no matter how intelligent.

(3) I hold that some reforms are plainly justified, and that the case for them does not require reconstructing the original rationale for the institution they replace. For example, the abolition of chattel slavery did not require a nuanced account of the social function of slavery before one could judge it wrong.

(4) I believe that the burden of proof in any proposed reform always falls on the reformer, never on the defender of the status quo.

(5) I am committed to the view, partly from my scientific training, that the best way to learn about a complex system is to perturb it — that is, to experiment — and that this applies to social systems as well.

(6) I think that the social world is significantly more complex and less predictable than most physical systems I worked with, and that the track record of top-down social engineering is poor enough to warrant a general skepticism toward it.

I'd like you to go through these and identify which pairs or subsets are in genuine tension, which tensions are only apparent and dissolve on closer inspection, and how severe the remaining tensions are. Then tell me what the least costly revision would be to restore consistency, and what I'd lose by making it.
