I am a novelist, and for the book I am writing I have been spending time with a team of engineers who build anomaly detection systems for industrial equipment. Their daily work is to distinguish, from a stream of sensor readings — vibration, temperature, pressure, load — between a machine that is still the same machine doing the same kind of work under new conditions, and a machine whose underlying mechanism has genuinely changed: a bearing beginning to degrade, a valve now opening in response to a different cause, a controller whose logic has been altered. What puzzles me is that the algorithms see only surfaces: a shift in a distribution, a correlation that has broken, a prediction that has begun to fail. Yet the engineers speak as though the algorithm has detected a change in the mechanism itself, and they sometimes override it — insisting that the mechanism has not changed although the readings have drifted, or that it has changed although the readings have returned to normal. Last week they argued for an hour about whether a pressure drop was the same compressor responding to a colder intake or a different failure mode, and the disagreement turned on what "the same mechanism" could mean.

I want to understand what account of sameness of mechanism this practice presupposes. When the team treats a changed distribution as a mere change in inputs or environment rather than a change in mechanism, what criterion are they implicitly applying? Does the practice strictly presuppose a particular theory — for example, that a mechanism is individuated by its components and their organization, or by the invariant causal relationships among its variables — or is it compatible with several rival theories and merely easier to run under one of them? And are the parts of the practice consistent with one another: does the algorithm's statistical criterion of change presuppose the same notion of mechanism as the engineers' talk of bearings and valves, or do two different notions operate at different points in the routine?

Finally, I would like to know what would have to change if the team adopted, explicitly, one or another of these views. If a mechanism simply is the joint distribution of its observables, then "anomaly" and "mechanism change" collapse into distribution change, and the engineer's override becomes a hunch with no target. If a mechanism is instead a set of components and activities with a stable organization, then no statistical test on surface readings can by itself detect mechanism change, and the practice is committed to inferring something unobserved. I am trying to write these engineers accurately, so I need a reconstruction of the implicit philosophy their routines would make true — or that they would be tacitly denying every time they trust the algorithm.
