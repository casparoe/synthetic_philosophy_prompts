I'm in a philosophy of medicine seminar and last week two of my classmates got into it after class about the therapy/enhancement distinction, and I've been turning it over in my head ever since. It started when we were discussing coverage decisions for ADHD medications.

Elena said the therapy/enhancement distinction is basically the only thing keeping health insurance from becoming a lifestyle subscription service. If you can't say that treating a disease is different from making a well person better-than-well, then there's no principled limit on what gets covered, and the whole risk-pooling model collapses. Marcus pushed back and said the distinction is a phantom: it just picks out whatever we happen to think is normal human functioning, and "normal" is a statistical notion, not a moral one. He pointed out that if the average person's vision is 20/20, then LASIK is enhancement, but if the average shifts because everyone gets LASIK, suddenly it's... what, therapy for the unaided? That's absurd.

Elena said that's not quite right because you can define normal functioning in terms of species-typical functioning, not the statistical average—the idea being that there's a biological baseline set by what human organisms are organized to do, and disease is a departure from that. Marcus said that's even worse: species-typical functioning would rule out eyeglasses for most of human history, and it also means the standard is set by whatever evolution happened to produce, which is a naturalistic fallacy. Elena came back and said that's too quick: the line doesn't have to be sharp to be real, and the fact that we can argue about borderline cases (is mild ADHD a disorder or just the low end of a normal distribution?) doesn't mean there's no difference between treating depression and taking modafinil to pull an all-nighter.

Marcus said that's a pragmatic fudge, not a principled distinction. He said the real work is being done by suffering and functional impairment, not by some concept of "normal," and once you admit that, you've already conceded that enhancement is sometimes justified—because if you live in a society where everyone else is cognitively enhanced, your unenhanced state might involve genuine suffering and functional impairment relative to the social baseline. Elena said that's exactly the kind of ratchet she's worried about: once you let the social baseline set the standard, you're in an arms race where everyone has to enhance just to keep up, and that's a different kind of harm from the one Marcus is pointing at—it's a collective action problem, not an individual welfare issue.

They went back and forth for another ten minutes and I honestly couldn't tell who was getting the better of it. Can you go through their exchange and adjudicate it move by move—who's right about what, which points land, which ones don't, and whether there's a way to reconcile their positions?
