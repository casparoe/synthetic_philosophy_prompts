I teach a philosophy of science course, upper-level undergrads, about 18 students, 80-minute sessions. We're about to hit the Duhem-Quine thesis and underdetermination of theory by evidence, and I want them to feel the problem in their bones before they read a word of it. I've found that if I just lecture it, half of them nod along and the other half think it's a cheap skeptical trick they can ignore.

Design an in-class activity where the students, split into small groups, are given the same set of data points—plotted on a graph, ideally—and asked to come up with the "best" theory, meaning a function or curve that accounts for the data. The setup should be such that multiple very different curves fit the data equally well, and the groups only discover this when they compare results. Then I want a second round: I give them one or two new data points that rule out some of the curves but still leave at least two incompatible ones standing, so they see that more evidence doesn't necessarily settle which theory is right.

The activity should map onto the philosophical point: for any finite body of evidence there are indefinitely many theories consistent with it, and choosing among them involves extra-empirical criteria—simplicity, elegance, conservatism, fit with background assumptions—that the groups will have implicitly used without noticing. The debrief should draw those out and name them.

Specifics I need: what the dataset should actually look like (give me coordinates or a clear description I can plot on the board), how to structure the groups and the timing within 80 minutes, what the facilitator says and doesn't say at each stage, and a debrief that connects specific moments in the activity to Duhem, Quine, and the broader point about empirical equivalence. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong—groups that all happen to pick the same curve, groups that get frustrated and want me to just tell them the answer, whatever—and how to handle it in the moment. And be honest about what the activity can't capture: I know the real thesis is about empirical equivalence given all possible evidence, not just a finite set, and a classroom game can't reproduce that limit case. Say where the analogy breaks and what I'll need to supply in discussion afterward.

I want a definate design, not a menu of options—tell me what you'd actually run and why you chose it over the alternatives.
