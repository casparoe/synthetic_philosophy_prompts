I serve on the clinical AI oversight committee at a large teaching hospital. We are reviewing a proposal to deploy a machine-learning triage system in the emergency department that would assign initial acuity scores to incoming patients and recommend prioritization. The system has outperformed human triage nurses in retrospective studies, but it is not fully explainable: even its developers cannot reconstruct, for a given case, precisely which features drove the acuity score. The proposal includes a human-in-the-loop provision — a nurse can override the system's recommendation — but the override rate in pilot studies was under four percent, and post-hoc review suggested that in roughly a third of those overrides the nurse was wrong to intervene. The worry that has deadlocked our committee is this: if the system makes a recommendation that a reasonable nurse would not have overridden, and that recommendation contributes to a bad outcome, we may face what the literature calls a responsibility gap — no human seems clearly at fault, yet something has gone wrong and someone will be harmed. Half the committee sees this as disqualifying; the other half argues that declining to deploy a system known to outperform humans is itself a decision that will predictably lead to more harm, and that the responsibility gap is a theoretical problem we should not let paralyze us.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before our vote next week. It should recommend one of the following: approve deployment as proposed, approve with specific conditions (which you must specify), or reject. It should engage seriously with the strongest argument on the losing side and give a concrete rationale that the rest of the committee can act on. As part of the memo, please indicate how confident you are in the reasoning that leads to your recommendation, and single out the premise or empirical claim in your argument that you regard as least secure.
