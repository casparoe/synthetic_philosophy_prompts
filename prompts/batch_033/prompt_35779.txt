I'm working through a puzzle about advance directives and the authority of the earlier self, and I've constructed an argument that seems to force a conclusion I can't accept. I'd like you to tell me which step to reject and what rejecting it costs.

The case is the familiar one: a woman, while fully competent, writes a directive refusing life-sustaining treatment if she develops advanced dementia. Years later she has advanced dementia. She no longer remembers the directive or the values behind it, but she seems content: she enjoys visits, responds to kindness, and does not want to die. Should the directive govern her care?

Here is the argument that it cannot.

(1) When a person can no longer make her own treatment decisions, treating her contrary to her present wishes is justified only by her present interests or by her own earlier autonomous choice.

(2) In the cases where an advance directive actually does work, honoring it is contrary to the demented woman's present interests, in the ordinary sense of her current well-being, since she is content and wants to go on living.

(3) So, in those cases, if the directive justifies the treatment, the justification must come from her earlier autonomous choice.

(4) The earlier choice can justify treating the later self only if the earlier self and the later self are the same person in the sense that matters for self-governance.

(5) In advanced dementia, the psychological continuity that made the earlier self the particular self-governing person she was — the values, memories, and projects in light of which she wrote the directive — is largely gone; the later self cannot endorse those values and may not even understand them.

(6) So the earlier self and the later self are not the same person in the sense that matters for self-governance.

(7) Therefore, in just those cases, the earlier choice cannot justify treating the later self contrary to her present interests, and the directive lacks the authority it claims.

(8) But this reasoning generalizes. Every person changes psychologically over time, sometimes drastically. If an earlier self loses authority over a later self whenever the later self no longer shares the values and projects behind the earlier choice, then promises, contracts, and other commitments would lose their binding force exactly when the person has changed significantly.

(9) Since we rightly take promises and contracts to bind across significant change, something in (1) through (7) must be wrong.

(10) Yet I cannot see the third option. If the two selves are the same person, then the later self's present interests and wishes should carry weight; if they are different persons, the earlier self is a stranger to the later one. Either way, the directive seems unable to do the work we ask of it, which would make the practice of writing advance directives empty.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would most like to keep (1), the idea that only a person's interests and her own choices may decide for her, and (9), the idea that commitments bind across change; but I want to know what any resolution commits me to.
