So my roommate and I were talking about the atomic bombings — it came up because we were watching that Oppenheimer movie — and our neighbor from downstairs joined us and things got heated.

Roommate: What Truman did was straightforwardly murder. You don't intentionally incinerate a hundred thousand civilians, including children, as a means to getting a government to surrender. That's not war, that's terrorism dressed up in a uniform.

Neighbor: That's absurd. The alternative was an invasion that would have killed way more people — estimates were in the millions. Truman chose the lesser evil. You can't just pretend the counterfactual doesn't exist.

Roommate: That's exactly the argument Anscombe went after when she protested Oxford giving Truman an honorary degree. You can't kill innocent people as a means to an end, even a good end. The civilians weren't collateral damage — they were the means of sending a message. If you'd be willing to nuke a city of your own country to save a larger number of people, you might at least be consistent, but nobody would accept that.

Neighbor: So you're saying the act itself is intrinsically wrong regardless of consequences? That's just deontology, and deontology has the same problem it always has — it treats rules as more important than the actual lives at stake. If the rule says "don't kill civilians" and following it causes more civilians to die, the rule is the problem.

Roommate: The rule didn't cause more civilians to die. The Japanese military leadership's refusal to surrender caused that. Truman's choice was whether to kill civilians intentionally. He chose yes. The fact that other people might have died later from other causes doesn't make his action any less of a choice to murder.

Neighbor: This is just word games. He made a choice in a situation where every option involved death. You're framing it as "he chose to murder" when the alternative was letting an even larger number die through invasion and blockade — you're privileging the deaths he directly caused over the deaths he would have allowed. That's a psychological distinction, not a moral one.

Roommate: It's not psychological. It's the distinction between intending death and merely foreseeing it. That distinction is basic to law, to ordinary morality, to everything. If a doctor gives a dying patient morphine for pain knowing it might hasten death, that's not the same as injecting them with potassium chloride to kill them. Same outcome, differnt intention, totally different moral category.

Neighbor: But the doctor analogy doesn't work, because the doctor isn't choosing between two groups of patients. Truman was. And the intention distinction gets really thin when you're certain about the foreseen consequence. If you drop a bomb on a city, you're not "merely foreseeing" civilian deaths — you're guaranteeing them. At that point the distinction between intended and foreseen is just a way to feel better about yourself.

I honestly couldn't figure out who was winning. Can you go through their exchange and adjudicate it move by move — who's right about what, and who's more right overall?
