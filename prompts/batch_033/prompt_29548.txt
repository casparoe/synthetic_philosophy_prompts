I'm a novelist and I've been asked to give a craft talk on creativity, and I'd like a consistency check before I stand up in front of a room full of writers. Heres roughly where I've landed: (1) I think Hofstadter is basically right that creativity is knob-twiddling—you take an existing theme or skeleton and vary it, the way a jazz solo is a string of variations on a standard or a novel is a set of moves within a genre. (2) I think there's no such thing as making something from nothing; every so-called original writer is just good at hiding their sources. (3) I think fixed constraints—a sonnet, a locked-room mystery, an Oulipo rule—tend to produce more interesting work than total freedom. (4) I think the value of a work lives in the variations themselves, in the distance from the theme, not in the theme. (5) I think that if creativity is knob-twiddling, then in principle a machine that twiddles knobs well could be as creative as I am, and we shouldn't gatekeep the word. (6) But I also think the thing that makes a novel mine is the choice of which theme to start from and which knobs to twiddle—and that choice is where the real originality is, not in the twiddling. Do these hang together? Give me an audit: which pairs are actually in tension, which only look like they are, and what's the cheapest fix and what it costs? Rank the real tensions by severity, and don't assume I have a favorite among them.
