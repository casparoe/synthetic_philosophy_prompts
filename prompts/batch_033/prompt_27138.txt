I work as a product manager on a consumer app with a few million monthly actives, and I've read enough rationalist-blog stuff to flinch whenever someone in a meeting says the data "shows what users really want." The pattern is always the same: we have clicks, session lenght, retention, uninstalls, and either someone treats that as a straight readout of preferences, or someone says we should just "infer the reward function" from behavior the way you would in inverse reinforcement learning, and then optimize that. I'm the one who has to turn whatever this is into a roadmap decision next quarter, and I keep feeling like the assumptions are being smuggled in without anyone naming them.

What I want is a reusable procedure for going from observed user behavior to a defensible claim about what users prefer or want. It has to be something my team — PMs, data scientists, and one engineer who thinks this whole project is made up — can actually run in about a week per decision, before the VP of product signs off. Ground it in what IRL and preference inference actually know: the identifiability problem (many reward functions explain the same policy), noise and rationality assumptions, discounting, partial observability, the difference between what a policy reveals under constraints and what it reveals about preferences. The deliverable is a sequence of questions or checks, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with "we can't infer that from this data," and where it hands the decision to a human because it's a values call rather than an inference.

Then run it on three cases we actually fight about: (1) users spend a ton of time on a feature but rate it poorly in surveys and say it makes the app feel like a slot machine; (2) a highly requested power feature gets almost no usage from a segment of users even though we're pretty sure it's good for them; (3) an option that lowers short-term engagement but seems to improve week-12 retention. I want at least one case where the procedure gives me an answer I don't like, and an honest statement of what the procedure cannot settle — including whether "what users want" is even a well-formed question in some of these cases.
