I've been reading LessWrong and the broader rationalist blogosphere for close to a decade, and I've watched the same failure mode play out in comment sections, subreddits, and group chats more times than I can count: a thread generates hundreds of comments of increasingly acrimonious disagreement, and eventually someone — often too late — points out that the whole thing was a dispute about what to call something. Other times, the failure runs the other way: someone prematurely dismisses a disagreement as "just semantics" when there's actually a substantive empirical or normative question underneath, and the move functions as a conversation-stopper rather than a conversation-solver.

I want a tool I can use myself and point other people to. Please write me a decision procedure for determining whether a disagreement is substantive or merely verbal. The procedure should be something a reasonably careful participant in an online discussion can run in a few minutes, without special training in philosophy of language. It should take the form of a series of questions to ask in order, with clear stopping rules: at what point the procedure says "this is probably a verbal dispute — here's how to dissolve it" and at what point it says "this is probably substantive — the object-level argument is worth having."

Ground the procedure in whatever the philosophical literature on verbal disputes and metaontology has actually established — but translate it into operational steps. Do not just summarize the taxonomy; I want the protocol. The procedure should handle the following hard cases:

- Disagreements where the two sides are using the same word with different extensions (they're talking past each other without realizing it).
- Disagreements where one side denies there's a fact of the matter at all, while the other insists there is.
- Disagreements where the disputants agree on every empirical fact and every value judgment but disagree about which conceptual scheme to adopt — and the question is whether that remaining disagreement is itself substantive or merely a choice of labeling.
- Disagreements that are partly verbal and partly substantive, where the procedure needs to separate the two components rather than issuing a binary verdict.

The procedure should flag the points where judgment cannot be eliminated — where even a perfectly executed protocol hands the question back to a human because the judgment call is itself substantive. It should be honest about the cases where it will misfire, and about what kind of error it is biased toward (false positives for "verbal" or false positives for "substantive").

Then apply the procedure to three worked examples:

1. **The hot dog sandwich debate.** The classic internet argument. Is a hot dog a sandwich? This one is low-stakes and familiar, and it should let the procedure show its basic moves on a clean case.

2. **AI understanding.** A dispute about whether a large language model that passes every behavioral test we can devise "understands" language. One side says the word "understands" requires something about internal representations or grounding; the other says functional equivalence is sufficient. I want to see whether the procedure classifies this as verbal, substantive, or mixed — and, if verbal, whether it can specify what the substantive disagreement is that the word "understands" is obscuring.

3. **Fetal personhood.** A dispute about whether a fetus is a "person." This is the one I care about most, because I suspect the procedure will reveal that the dispute is partly a verbal disagreement about the word "person" and partly a genuine normative disagreement about what matters morally — and I want to see whether the procedure can cleanly separate those two components and, if so, what remains of the argument after the verbal part is dissolved.

I want the procedure to be something I can literally copy-paste into a comment the next time a thread is going in circles. The worked examples should be concrete enough that someone reading the comment can see how to apply the procedure themselves, not just admire it in the abstract.
