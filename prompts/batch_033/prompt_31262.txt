I've been arguing with a friend about the dollar auction—the game in which two players bid for a dollar, the highest bidder wins the dollar, and the second-highest bidder must also pay their final bid but receives nothing. The game is usually introduced in discussions of escalation of commitment, and it is often offered as an illustration of the sunk cost fallacy. But here is what I cannot get straight.

Suppose I have already bid ninety cents and my opponent has bid one dollar. If I drop out, I lose ninety cents for nothing. If I raise to one dollar and ten cents and my opponent then drops out, I have paid $1.10 for a dollar, which is a net loss of only ten cents. Compared with losing ninety cents, that looks like the clearly better option. The same reasoning seems to apply at every later stage: once both players are in, each additional small raise appears rational because it is less bad than surrendering the larger amount already committed. Doesn't this show that the sunk cost fallacy is not really a fallacy—that continuing to invest because of what you have already put in can be the rational thing to do? If so, why do decision theorists still describe escalation of commitment as an irrational bias rather than as the correct response to being caught in a dollar auction?
