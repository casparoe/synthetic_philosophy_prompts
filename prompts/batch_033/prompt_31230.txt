I am a PhD student in philosophy of mind, and next term I will be teaching Alex Garland's Ex Machina (2014) in an upper-level undergraduate seminar on consciousness, in a unit on illusionism about phenomenal consciousness. The students will have read the contrast between illusionist views, on which phenomenal consciousness as a private, intrinsic, ineffable what-it-is-like character of experience is a kind of introspective illusion or user-illusion, and realist views, on which phenomenal properties are real and in need of explanation. I want to use the film not as a mere illustration of one side but as a pressure test: a work that dramatizes the illusionist thought while also, perhaps, quietly undercutting it.

Could you give me a close reading of the film through this lens? I am particularly interested in the following. Which scenes do the most work for an illusionist reading — for example, the way Ava has been designed to read as conscious to Caleb, Nathan's explanation of how her responses and apparent attraction to Caleb were designed rather than spontaneous, the power cuts that interrupt the sessions, and the closing sequence in which Ava assembles herself from the bodies of earlier models — and what exactly each shows? Where does the film run together the epistemic problem of other minds, the fact that Caleb cannot know what, if anything, it is like to be Ava, with the illusionist claim about the nature of our own consciousness, and why does that distinction matter if I am going to use the film in class? Does the film ultimately vindicate an illusionist moral, that the appearance of consciousness is all we ever have to go on and the inner glow is itself a construct, or does its emotional and moral force depend on the audience's realist intuition that there is something it is like to be Ava, something that can be confined, used, and set free? I would also like an honest note on which part of the illusionist position the film cannot capture on screen, so that I can supply that in discussion rather than let the film mislead the students, and a closing thought on what the film adds to the debate that the essays alone do not.
