I'm a PhD student in comparative philosophy, and I've been goign back and forth on the Jain doctrine of anekāntavāda for a paper I'm trying to write. The basic idea, as I understand it, is that reality is irreducibly many-sided — no single description or standpoint captures the whole truth about anything, and genuine knowledge requires holding multiple, seemingly incompatible perspectives together. The classic analogy is the blind men and the elephant, but that undersells it: the Jain claim isn't just that different people see different parts, it's that the nature of reality itself is such that contradictory predications can both be true in different respects (syādvāda).

The thing I keep getting stuck on is the old objection that anekāntavāda undermines itself. If all truths are partial and standpoint-dependent, then isn't the claim that "all truths are partial and standpoint-dependent" itself just a partial truth? And if the Jain replies that anekāntavāda is the one exception — the one absolute truth about the non-absolute nature of everything else — that looks like special pleading. But I've read enough to suspect the Jain tradition has better answers than most Western philosophers give it credit for, and I don't want to strawman it.

Could you write a dialogue between a Jain philosopher defending anekāntavāda and an analytic epistemologist pressing this self-refutation worry? I want both of them to be genuinely competent — the Jain should have the resources of the tradition available (nayavāda, the sevenfold predication, the distinction between absolute and conventional standpoints), and the epistemologist should be sharp and charitable, not just dismissive. Real back-and-forth, where each responds to what the other actually says rather than talking past them. And please write it in British English.
