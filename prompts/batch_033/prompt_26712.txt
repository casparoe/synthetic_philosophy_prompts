Write a villanelle about Lewis Carroll's "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles." In the dialogue, the Tortoise accepts premise A (a Euclidean axiom) and premise B ("If A then Z") but refuses to accept the conclusion Z. When Achilles protests that logic compels the move, the Tortoise asks him to write that logical compulsion down as premise C: "If A and B are true, then Z must be true." The Tortoise now accepts A, B, and C but still refuses Z — and the regress is underway. No matter how many times Achilles reformulates the rule of inference as a new premise, the Tortoise grants it and still withholds the conclusion.

The villanelle's two refrains should embody the Tortoise's two recurring moves: one granting whatever premise has just been offered, the other refusing to take the step to Z. The form should do the philosophical work: just as the refrains return no matter how the intervening lines develop, the regress reasserts itself no matter how many new premises are added. The poem should be accurate enough that someone who knows the original dialogue recognizes the structure of the problem in the structure of the verse — the distinction between premises and rules, and why treating a rule as a premise can never force the move it was meant to license — and someone who does not know the dialogue should still find it a compelling poem about a mind that grants everything and concedes nothing.
