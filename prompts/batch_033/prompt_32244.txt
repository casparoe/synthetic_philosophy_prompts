I spend too much time on rationalist-adjacent forums, and there's an argument I read a while ago that I keep coming back to. I'd like someone to actually referee it move by move instead of just giving the standard "it's a known impossibility result" line.

The setup: there's three possible states of the world — A reads a certain book, B reads it, or nobody reads it. A thinks the book is corrupting and would rather nobody read it, but if someone has to, better A than B; so A's ranking is nobody reads > A reads > B reads. B thinks everyone should read it and would most like A to read it, but would settle for reading it himself; so B's ranking is A reads > B reads > nobody reads. A is given a decisive say over whether A reads (so society has to rank "nobody reads" above "A reads," since that's A's preference over that pair), and B is given a decisive say over whether B reads (so society ranks "B reads" above "nobody reads"). But both A and B prefer "A reads" to "B reads," so by the Pareto principle society must rank "A reads" above "B reads." Put those together and you get nobody reads > A reads > B reads > nobody reads. A cycle, so no social ordering can satisfy all three conditions at once.

The two people in the thread, call them Marta and Dev, went back and forth like this:

Marta: "The lesson is that Pareto has to go. A right is basically a veto over some pair of outcomes, and Pareto is a demand that everyone's preferences always get aggregated. Those are going to collide whenever someone's preference pokes into someone else's veto zone. Between the two, Pareto is the one to drop, because rights are doing the actual moral work."

Dev: "I don't think Pareto is the culprit. Look at A's ranking — A cares whether B reads the book. That's a meddlesome preference, it's about someone else's private choice. If you throw out preferences people have over other people's private lives, the cycle disappears. The real problem is the unrestricted domain assumption: social choice shouldn't take every possible preference profile as input."

Marta: "That's circular. Nothing in the formalism tells you which preferences are 'meddlesome.' You need a prior account of what is someone's own business to filter the domain, and that prior account just is the rights theory the decisive-pair condition was supposed to encode. So you haven't escaped the paradox, you've just hidden the rights theory inside the domain restriction."

Dev: "Fair, but I think the decisive-pair condition is the thing to reject. A right isn't a preference that society has to rank above another; it's a constraint on which options are available. Give A control over whether A reads and B control over whether B reads, and on the natural reading you get B reads — which both of them agree is worse than A reads. But that's just a Pareto-inefficient outcome, not a cycle. Rights and efficiency can conflict; that's not an impossibility, it's a normal tradeoff."

Marta: "But now you've got a rights system that deliberately picks an outcome both people would agree to overturn. If the rights are waivable, they'll trade — B waives his claim to read, A grudgingly reads — and the paradox dissolves, but only because you let them contract around the rights. If the rights are unwaivable, you're stuck defending an outcome that is worse for both of them. So which horn do you want? If waivable, the original theorem doesn't bite; if not, you've got a different bullet to chew."

They went around like this for a while and never landed it. I later found out its a known result, but knowing that didn't tell me which of these moves is actually right.
