I cover the science-and-ideas beat for a magazine and I keep running into this thing I don't have a word for. Here's what it looks like. I'm at a conference where a physicist is debating a young-earth creationist. The physicist brings up radiometric dating, the creationist says decay rates might have been different in the past, the physicist says that would change the fine-structure constant and we'd see the effects in spectra from distant stars, and so on. But at some point the creationist says something like "but couldn't God have created the world last Thursday with the appearance of age, with the light from distant stars already in transit, with the fossils already in the ground?" And the physicist doesn't produce a counter-argument. He just sort of stares, and then says "well, if you're going to go there, I don't know what to say." And the debate is effectively over — not because one side won, but because the shared framework for having a debate collapsed.

I've seen the same structure in other places. A climate scientist and someone who thinks the temperature records are being systematically faked by a global conspiracy. An evolutionary biologist and someone who thinks the fossil record was planted by Satan. In each case there's a moment where one side invokes a possibility that the other side can't refute with evidence but also can't treat as a live option without giving up the whole enterprise of doing science.

But it's not just a science-versus-religion thing. I've seen it within science too. There's a famous story about a physicist who, when told about an experimental result that seemed to violate conservation of energy, said he'd sooner doubt the experiment than the principle. And that wasn't presented as dogmatism — it was presented as good scientific judgment.

So here's what I'm trying to get at. These aren't just strongly held beliefs, and they aren't just unfalsifiable claims. They seem to be beliefs that play a special structural role: they're the things you have to take for granted for a certain kind of inquiry or debate to get off the ground. Challenging them doesn't produce a counterargument; it produces a breakdown of the conversation. And this isn't necessarily irrational — it might be that you literally can't do the inquiry without holding them fixed.

Is there an established name for this in epistemology? I've heard the term "axiom" but that feels too formal and too much like something you consciously choose. I've heard "presupposition" but that seems too broad — lots of things are presupposed in conversation without having this special immunity-to-revision quality. What I'm after is: what exactly is this class of beliefs, what distinguishes them from ordinary empirical beliefs and from mere dogmatic committments, and if there's a literature on this, what's the core insight I should take away?
