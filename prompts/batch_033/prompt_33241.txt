You are a journalist who covers science and ideas, with enough technical literacy to follow the algorithmic fairness literature, and you have been asked to serve as the neutral drafter and facilitator for an adversarial collaboration. Before you draft anything, think carefully and brainstorm in notes: what would each side insist on being true to its own position, what would each side refuse to sign, and which apparent disagreements are really differences in wording.

The collaboration is between two researchers who have argued for months about what to make of the impossibility results in algorithmic fairness. The technical starting point is the result that a binary risk score cannot, in general, satisfy all three of the following across two groups: predictive parity (equal positive predictive value among those classified high risk), parity in false positive rates, and parity in false negative rates. The incompatibility arises whenever the base rate of the outcome differs between the groups and the score is not a perfect predictor, and it holds regardless of the algorithm used to produce the score.

One researcher, whom we will call the abolitionist, reads this as a decisive case against deploying algorithmic risk scores in high-stakes allocation decisions such as bail, lending, hiring, or admissions: no such instrument can be fair in the ways that matter, so the responsible response is to stop using them, or at most to treat any deployment as a political choice among harms rather than a technical solution. The other researcher, whom we will call the compatibilist, reads the same results as a constraint rather than a refutation: they show only that several group-level statistical criteria cannot all be optimized at once, and that the right response is to choose and justify a criterion for the specific decision context, not to abandon risk scores or conclude that algorithmic fairness is impossible.

Draft the document this collaboration should produce. It must contain the following.

1. A numbered section of agreed claims. These should be substantive, not vacuous, and should cover: what the theorems actually establish, including the degenerate cases; what they do not establish, for example about individual-level or procedural notions of fairness; and what both sides accept about the empirical stakes of the dispute.

2. A disagreements table. Each row should state one precise point of disagreement, with the abolitionist's position and the compatibilist's position phrased so that each side would accept the wording of its own view. Do not slant either side's wording.

3. A cruxes section. Identify the sub-questions such that a settled answer to one of them would move at least one side. For each crux, specify the concrete case, dataset, or argument that both sides agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction in advance.

4. An exposure section. For each side, state which evidence, theorem, or argument would be most likely to lead that side to revise, and where that side's own reasoning is most likely to fail.

5. A closing note on which parts of the dispute, if any, are verbal rather than substantive, and which parts would survive even perfect clarity about the mathematics.

Write in the voice of a journalist: technically precise but accessible to a reader who has not read the primary papers, and scrupulous about not putting words in either side's mouth. Do not include a bibliography or reading list; the document should stand on the arguments themselves.
