I've been having a running argument with a friend who's trying to work out which creatures deserve serious moral consideration, and she's landed on a set of verdicts she treats as fixed points. She's confident that humans have full moral status; that chimpanzees, bonobos, and bottlenose dolphins have very high moral status; that African elephants have high moral status; that dogs, pigs, common ravens, and octopuses each have moderate moral status; that mice and zebra finches have low but non-zero moral status; and that honeybees, fruit flies, and nematodes have no moral status to speak of. She also insists — and this came up because someone in our group raised the "aliens" objection — that if we ever encountered a being with a brain the size of a walnut that could hold a conversation, compose music, and make moral judgments, it would have full moral status regardless of its brain size. So whatever rule she's running, it isn't a simple "bigger brain, more status" formula, and she's aware of that.

The case she can't settle is the sperm whale. The sperm whale has the largest brain of any animal ever to have existed — roughly six times the mass of a human brain — but its encephalization quotient is modest (comparable to a walrus or a capybara), and a substantial fraction of that brain is devoted to acoustic processing for echolocation rather than to the kinds of integrative processing we associate with higher cognition. Sperm whales live in complex matrilineal social groups, have distinct cultural dialects, and may possess a form of collective intelligence, but the evidence for individual-level capacities like mirror self-recognition or means-end reasoning is sparse and contested. She's asked me what she should say about the sperm whale if she wants to be consistent with her other verdicts, and I honestly don't know.

I'm less interested in the verdict itself than in the rule she seems to be running, the other rules that fit her existing verdicts equally well, and which of those rules would yield different answers for the sperm whale. What I'd really like is a mapping of the candidate principles — absolute brain mass, encephalization quotient, cortical or forebrain neuron count, some measure of cognitive or behavioral complexity, or a hybrid — and an analysis of which of her fixed points do the most work in narrowing the field, which ones are compatible with too many principles to help, and whether the sperm whale case would force her to revise any of her existing verdicts if she wants to remain consistent.
