The unilateralist's curse has been invoked to argue for caution in domains ranging from gain-of-function research to geoengineering to the development of advanced AI. The core idea is that when any single actor can take an action that affects everyone, and actors have private information about whether the action is net-beneficial, the probability that someone will take a harmful action increases with the number of actors — even if each actor is rational and well-intentioned. But the literature on the curse remains relatively thin compared to the weight it is sometimes asked to bear in policy arguments.

Suppose you are advising a research group with about five person-years to devote to theoretical work on the unilateralist's curse and its implications for institutional design. Their goal is to produce work that could actually change how we structure decision-making in high-stakes, multi-actor domains. Which open questions should they prioritize, and in what order?

I want the reasoning behind the ranking, not just the list. For each question you prioritize, address: (1) Why is it upstream of practical recommendations — what would we do differently depending on the answer? (2) How tractable is it given current tools in decision theory, game theory, and political philosophy? (3) What empirical or theoretical result over the next few years would cause you to move it up or down the ranking?

In framing your answer, I would also like you to take a stand on a higher-level question: is the most urgent work on the unilateralist's curse about refining the formal model itself (e.g., relaxing assumptions about signal independence, exploring continuous action spaces, modeling sequential decisions), or about designing and evaluating institutional remedies (e.g., threshold rules, deference norms, information-pooling mechanisms)? Or does that distinction break down under scrutiny?
