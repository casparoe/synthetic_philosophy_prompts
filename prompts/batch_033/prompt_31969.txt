I've been going back and forth on Schellenberg's divine hiddenness argument, and one of the main responses to it, the free-will defense, is really bugging me. The way I understand it, the idea is that God stays hidden because if he revealed himself unambiguously, our belief would be coerced—we wouldn't freely choose to love him, and a loving God wouldn't want forced love.

But isn't this just empirically false? The Bible is crammed with people who had direct, unambiguous encounters with God—Adam and Eve walked with him in the garden, Moses spoke to him face-to-face, the Israelites saw the pillar of fire, Judas spent three years with Jesus—and they still managed to disobey, sin, and in some cases reject him outright. So knowing God exists pretty clearly doesn't destroy free will or force anyone into a genuine relationship. It seems like the free-will defense is making a psychological claim that's just flatly contradicted by the tradition it's supposed to be defending.

Why do philosophers still take the free-will defense seriously as a response to Schellenberg? I feel like I must be misunderstanding something about how the defense actually works, because it seems way too easy to knock down otherwise. Happy to be told I'm framing the whole thing wrong.
