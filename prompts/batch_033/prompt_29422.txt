Compose a dialogue in which two philosophers of biology — one committed to a selected-effects (etiological) account of biological function, the other to a causal-role account — try to get clear about where their views genuinely differ. The exchange should be a real conversation, not a pair of set speeches: each should respond to the other's actual arguments, and each should be forced to state at least one revision or clarification of their view as a direct result of the other's pressure.

Have the dialogue work through three hard cases, with the burden of reply distributed fairly between them:

1. Malfunction. A human heart that is structurally incapable of pumping blood still seems to have the function of pumping blood. The selected-effects theorist should press this case against the causal-role theorist, and the causal-role theorist should offer a substantive reply rather than simply conceding.

2. Novel traits. A trait that has only just appeared and currently contributes to its bearer's reproductive success, but has no history of selection for that contribution, seems to have a function now. The causal-role theorist should press this case against the selected-effects theorist, and the selected-effects theorist should offer a substantive reply rather than simply conceding.

3. Function and accident. The human nose can support eyeglasses, but supporting eyeglasses is not its function. The two should work through what each account says about this case, and what it shows about how each draws — or fails to draw — the distinction between a function and a merely useful accident.

By the end, neither view should have collapsed into the other, but both characters should hold somewhat more precise positions than they did at the start. Leave the remaining disagreement sharp rather than resolving it artificially. Aim for roughly 1,000 words.
