I've been turning over an argument that seems airtight until the last step, where it starts proving way too much. (1) If you're a Bayesian, your credence in a claim is your prior conditional on your total evidence. (2) So two people with the same prior who both update by conditioning can end up with different credences only because their evidence differed. (3) Suppose Alice and Bob, same prior, each have private evidence, and they publicly announce their current credences about some claim, honestly, with each seeing that the other heard. After that, each knows the other's credence, each knows the other knows, and so on: there credences are common knowledge. (4) Aumann's agreement theorem says that if two rational Bayesians share a prior and their posterior credences in a claim are common knowledge, then those posteriors must be equal. (5) So after this exchange, Alice and Bob can't rationally disagree. (6) But that's obviously false: careful people lay out exactly how confident they are all the time and still walk away disagreeing. So either every such disagreement involves hidden irrationality or different priors, or one of the steps is doing something it shouldn't. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
