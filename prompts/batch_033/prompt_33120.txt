I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am preparing a feature on Molyneux's problem: the question whether a person born blind, who can distinguish a sphere from a cube by touch, would recognize them by sight immediately after gaining sight. The feature will discuss why the problem has persisted from the seventeenth century into contemporary cognitive science, and I want my treatment of the original reasoning to be precise rather than a loose paraphrase.

I have been working with the passage in which Locke introduced the problem in the second edition of his Essay Concerning Human Understanding (1694), Book II, Chapter IX, Section 8:

"Suppose a Man born blind, and now adult, and taught by his touch to distinguish between a Cube, and a Sphere of the same metal, and nighly of the same bigness, so as to tell, when he felt one and t’other; which is the Cube, which the Sphere. Suppose then the Cube and Sphere placed on a Table, and the Blind Man to be made to see. Quaere, Whether by his sight, before he touch’d them, he could now distinguish, and tell, which is the Globe, which the Cube."

Locke then gives Molyneux's answer:

"Not. For though he has obtain’d the experience of, how a Globe, how a Cube affects his touch; yet he has not yet attained the Experience, that what affects his touch so or so, must affect his sight so or so; Or that a protuberant angle in the Cube, that pressed his hand unequally, shall appear to his eye, as it does in the Cube."

I find it difficult to state exactly what the negative answer rests on. I can see at least two readings, and I am not certain which is closer to the text, or whether there is a third I am missing. On one reading, the answer is an empirical prediction about a particular person's performance: the man will not, in fact, be able to name the shapes on first sight, because he has not had the opportunity to learn a correlation between particular tactile sensations and particular visual appearances. On another reading, it is a stronger conceptual point: no amount of tactile experience could by itself give the man the relevant visual ideas, because the idea of a cube as touched and the idea of a cube as seen are distinct ideas connected only by further experience or reasoning.

What I would like help with is pinning down which of these — or what other — claims the passage actually commits Locke and Molyneux to. Specifically: (1) What exactly is the "Experience" the man is said not yet to have attained, and would a single episode of seeing the shapes while touching them count as acquiring it? (2) Why does the passage say that what affects his touch "must affect his sight so or so"? What notion of necessity, if any, is in play, and is that consistent with the empirical reading? (3) How much weight should be placed on the phrase "taught by his touch"? Does the answer presuppose that the man's tactile distinctions are the product of learning, and would the problem change if they were not?

I am less interested in the history of the correspondence than in a precise reconstruction of the reasoning inside the passage, because I want my feature to explain what the original answer did and did not rule out before I contrast it with later positions and with modern experimental findings.
