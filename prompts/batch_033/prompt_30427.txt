I am a PhD candidate in social epistemology, currently spending a year embedded in the patient-relations office of a large teaching hospital as part of an applied philosophy fellowship. The office receives about two dozen complaints each month from patients who believe their reports of symptoms — pain, medication side effects, postoperative changes — were disbelieved, minimized, or left out of the medical record. The complainants are disproportionately women, people of color, older patients, and patients with psychiatric diagnoses. My supervisor has asked me to turn what I know about epistemic injustice, and specifically Miranda Fricker's distinction between testimonial and hermeneutical injustice, into a procedure that caseworkers with no philosophical training can actually run on a complaint. The stakes are real: an upheld complaint can trigger a chart review, a meeting with the clinical team, and in serious cases a referral to the hospital's quality and safety committee, while a dismissed complaint can leave a patient unheard and a medical error uncorrected. Caseworkers have about an hour per complaint, and the office director must personally approve any escalation.

Please write the procedure itself: an ordered list of questions a caseworker should ask, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure stops with no further action, where it must hand the decision to a human being because the question is one of values or institutional priorities rather than facts, and where it should flag residual uncertainty. I want the procedure to preserve the distinctions that do the real work in Fricker's account — above all, the difference between a credibility deficit that tracks the evidence and one that tracks identity prejudice, and the difference between a patient who was disbelieved and a patient who could not make their experience intelligible in the vocabulary available to them — while dropping the theoretical apparatus that would not survive contact with a busy office. Then run the procedure on three complaints: (1) a Black woman in her forties whose report of severe postoperative pain was documented as anxiety and who was discharged without adequate analgesia; (2) an elderly man with a history of alcohol use whose repeated statements that he felt wrong after a medication change were dismissed until he was readmitted with a serious drug interaction; and (3) a patient who says a junior doctor did not believe her account of a rare but documented side effect, where the doctor's skepticism rested on the rarity of the side effect and on an inconsistency between the patient's complaint and her own earlier chart note. Make sure at least one of the three yields a recommendation the office would prefer not to make, and say plainly what the procedure cannot settle and where it is most likely to misfire.
