A book on philosophy of mind that I read summarizes Dennett’s “Quining Qualia” argument roughly like this: his coffee-tasting case with Chase and Sanborn shows that when two people report a changed taste experience, there is no fact of the matter about whether the change lies in their raw subjective feel or in their later judgment or standards. Since even the subjects themselves cannot tell which has changed, Dennett concludes that the whole notion of qualia as private, intrinsic, introspectively knowable states is empirically empty and probably incoherent. According to the summary, Dennett’s view is that qualia talk is not just unverifiable but meaningless, and that first-person reports of conscious experience should be replaced by third-person descriptions of dispositions and brain states. I am suspicious about several steps here, but I do not know enough about the original argument to identify exactly where the summary is fair and where it slides into something stronger than Dennett establishes. Go through the summary sentence by sentence and assess: which claims would Dennett or a careful defender of his argument accept, which are overstated or subtly misstated, and does the final conclusion about replacing first-person reports follow from anything established by the Chase and Sanborn case?
