**Question:**

The "paradox of tragedy" is the puzzle that we voluntarily seek out artworks — tragedies in theater, literature, and film — that reliably produce feelings of sadness, pity, and fear, and we seem to enjoy or value the experience. This appears to conflict with the ordinary assumption that we avoid painful emotions. Explain two major approaches to resolving this paradox: one from the historical tradition and one from contemporary philosophy. For each, describe how the approach is supposed to work. Then choose one of the two approaches and raise what you consider the strongest objection to it, explaining whether you think the objection succeeds.

**Student Answer:**

The paradox of tragedy has been a central problem in aesthetics at least since Aristotle. Two influential approaches come from Aristotle himself and from the contemporary philosopher Susan Feagin, who offers a meta-response account.

Aristotle, in the Poetics, introduces the concept of catharsis. On his view, tragedy produces pity and fear in the audience, and through the experience of the drama, these emotions are purged or cleansed. The idea is that we carry around these emotions in a latent or disordered state, and tragedy provides a structured outlet for them. By the end of the play, we feel a kind of relief: the emotions have been discharged, and we return to a calmer psychological state. The pleasure of tragedy, then, is not pleasure in the suffering depicted but pleasure in the release that follows the build-up of emotion. Aristotle's view is essentially a hydraulic model — emotions are like fluids that build up pressure and need to be released, and tragedy provides the mechanism for that release.

A distinct contemporary approach is Feagin's meta-response theory. On this account, our direct emotional response to the tragic events is indeed painful — we feel sadness and pity for the characters. But we also have a second-order response to our own first-order responses: we are pleased, or at least satisfied, to discover that we are the kind of person who can feel sympathy for others, who can be moved by the representation of suffering. This meta-response — the pleasure we take in our own capacity for empathy and moral sensitivity — is what makes the overall experience of tragedy positive and valuable. We do not enjoy the suffering; we enjoy the confirmation that we are not indifferent to it.

I think the strongest objection targets Feagin's view. The objection is that the meta-response theory seems to predict that we would value any experience that confirms our moral sensitivity, but we do not. Consider a documentary that depicts real-world atrocities in graphic detail. Watching such a documentary might also confirm that we are sympathetic and morally sensitive people, yet most people do not describe the experience as enjoyable or seek it out in the way they seek out tragedy. If the meta-response were the source of tragedy's value, we should find these experiences equally rewarding. The fact that we do not suggests that Feagin's account is incomplete at best. I think this objection is damaging because it shows that the meta-response cannot be the whole story: there must be something specific to the artistic context of tragedy that makes the meta-response pleasurable, and Feagin's account does not adequately identify what that something is.
