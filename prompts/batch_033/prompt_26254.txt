For a political philosophy seminar on democratic persuasion, I'd like a decision tree that takes a student from their intuitions about concrete cases of political influence to a named position on what distinguishes manipulation from legitimate persuasion. The tree should have 8–12 leaf nodes.

The positions that must appear as leaves include: the non-rational-influence view (manipulation works through emotion or bias rather than reason), the bypassing-autonomy view (manipulation subverts the target's capacity for self-governance), the disrespect/dignity view (manipulation treats the target as a mere means), the deception-based view (manipulation essentially involves misleading the target), the reasons-responsiveness view (manipulation bypasses the target's capacity to respond to reasons), and the view that the distinction is purely conventional or context-relative with no principled basis. If you think there are other positions that carve out distinct logical space — for instance, a view that locates the wrong in the manipulator's intention rather than in the mechanism or the effect — include them as well.

The diagnostic questions must be built entirely around concrete cases, not around theoretical labels. Use cases drawn from political life: campaign ads that use ominous music and unflattering imagery without making false claims; door-to-door canvassing that tailors its arguments to a voter's known anxieties; social media microtargeting that shows different policy framings to different personality profiles; a politician's tearful personal anecdote deployed to shift debate away from a policy failure; a party platform that frames a tax cut as "relief" or "freedom money"; a get-out-the-vote message that tells recipients their neighbors will be informed whether they voted. For each case, the student simply answers: "Is this manipulation, legitimate persuasion, or does it depend on further details?" — and if it depends, the follow-up question should specify what further detail matters.

Key design constraint: build the tree from the logical space of answers to the cases, not from the list of positions. First map the combinations of answers the cases allow, then locate the actual positions within that space. Any region of logical space that no defended position occupies should be flagged and briefly diagnosed: is the combination incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored in the literature? Also flag any pair of positions that your tree cannot reliably separate using the cases you selected, and explain what additional case or question would be needed to split them.

The audience is advanced undergraduates who have taken one course in political philosophy but have not read the manipulation literature. The questions should be answerable by someone who has never heard the theoretical labels. The deliverable is the tree itself — a branching structure with questions at the nodes and positions at the leaves — accompanied by a brief commentary on the unoccupied regions and the inseparable pairs.
