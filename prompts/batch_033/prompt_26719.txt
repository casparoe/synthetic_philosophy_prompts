I spend a lot of time reading rationalist-adjacent forums, though I have never studied philosophy or decision theory in any formal way. One phrase I keep seeing there is "shut up and multiply." I recently watched it get used to end an argument about a version of Pascal's mugging. Someone proposed that if a stranger asks you for ten dollars and says he will, with probability one in a trillion, use the money to build a machine that later gives you a trillion trillion dollars, you should pay, because the expected value is enormous. Several people replied that this was obviously absurd. The original poster just answered "shut up and multiply," as if doing the multiplication settled the matter. I have a nagging sense that the slogan is smuggling in several different claims at once, but I cannot pull them apart myself.

I would like you to unpack it for me. What are the distinct precise claims "shut up and multiply" could be making — for example, about whether the product of a probability and a payoff is always the thing to act on, about when it is legitimate to trust the numbers you are multiplying, about what to do when a calculation gives a result that feels insane, and so on? For each reading, tell me whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible principle, or something false or at least doubtful. Then say which of these readings would actually be needed to get the Pascal's-mugger conclusion, and whether the slogan, on that reading, just assumes the very thing it is being used to prove.

Please keep the answer under 700 words and present the readings as a list, with a one-sentence verdict on each.
