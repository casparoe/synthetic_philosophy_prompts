Two members of my reading group, Marta and Daniel, argued late into the evening about the philosophical zombie argument against physicalism. I could see the force of several moves on both sides and was unable to referee, so I have written the exchange out as accurately as I can and would like a point-by-point adjudication.

By "philosophical zombie" they meant a being physically identical to a conscious human being, down to the last particle and pattern of activity, yet lacking phenomenal consciousness: there is nothing it is like to be that being. By "physicalism" they meant the thesis that every fact, including every fact about consciousness, is fixed by the physical facts.

(1) Marta opened with the argument. Zombies are conceivable: the description of a physical duplicate with no experience seems internally consistent. If zombies are conceivable, they are metaphysically possible. If they are possible, then there is a possible world physically identical to ours but different in its conscious facts, so the physical facts do not determine the phenomenal facts. Physicalism is false.

(2) Daniel replied that conceivability is a poor guide to possibility. People once found it conceivable that water might not be H₂O, or that the Morning Star might not be the Evening Star; both are impossible. Conceivability tracks our concepts and our information, not modal reality, so Marta's first inference fails.

(3) Marta answered that those cases turn on incomplete empirical information about identities in the actual world. The zombie case is different: it holds every physical fact fixed and varies only the phenomenal facts. No further information about brains could show the zombie world impossible, because by stipulation there is nothing physical left to discover.

(4) Daniel said this is exactly the signature of an epistemic gap rather than a metaphysical one. We cannot derive the phenomenal facts a priori from the physical facts, so we can conceive of their coming apart. But the physicalist can explain this by the character of phenomenal concepts: they are recognitional or indexical concepts that pick out experiences from the first-person point of view and do not reveal the physical nature of what they refer to. The gap is in our concepts, not in the world.

(5) Marta responded that this relocates the problem without solving it. If the physical facts genuinely necessitate the phenomenal facts, something must make that necessitation hold. The phenomenal-concept story explains why the entailment is not transparent to us; it does not supply the entailment itself. What is left is a brute necessity, and "brute necessity" is just a label for an unexplained connection asserted to be necessary. Worse, the strategy is too flexible. A defender of vitalism could say that the gap between physical facts and facts about life is real but hidden by our biological concepts, and a defender of irreducible moral facts could say the same about the gap between natural facts and moral facts. A strategy that can insulate any alleged non-physical necessity from refutation proves too much.

(6) Daniel answered that the strategy is not ad hoc, because it is independently motivated. Consider a scientist who has complete physical knowledge of color vision but has never seen red: on first seeing red, she gains a new phenomenal way of thinking about a state she already knew under physical descriptions, rather than learning a new fact about the world. That is exactly the sort of conceptual gap the strategy posits. And in any case, Marta's inference from conceivability to possibility is only as strong as the conceivability in question. We can entertain the supposition that there is a largest prime, and nothing in the surface content of the supposition announces the contradiction; reflection exposes it. Unless Marta can show that zombie-conceivability survives ideal reflection, her inference is no better than the cases she dismisses.

At that point they had gone around the same circle twice, and I could not tell how much had actually been settled.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move. For each of the six moves, say whether it is clearly right, clearly wrong, or partly right, and briefly justify the rating. Then say what, if anything, both parties are missing, and who is more right overall.
