**Question:**

Explain the Epicurean argument that death is not bad for the person who dies. Then critically evaluate the deprivation account as a response: does it successfully refute the Epicurean position, or does the Epicurean have resources to push back?

**Student's answer:**

The Epicurean argument, found in Epicurus's Letter to Menoeceus, holds that death is nothing to the person who dies. The argument can be reconstructed as follows. Premise 1: something can be bad for a person only if that person experiences it. Premise 2: a person never experiences their own death, because when death is present, the person is not, and when the person is present, death is not. Conclusion: therefore, death is not bad for the person who dies. The argument is elegantly simple: it takes a plausible claim about the nature of harm—that harm requires an experiencing subject—and applies it to the case of death, where the subject is absent.

The deprivation account, developed by Thomas Nagel, challenges the first premise. Nagel argues that something can be bad for a person even if they never experience it. Death is bad not because it is a painful experience but because it deprives the person of the goods they would have enjoyed had they continued living. The badness of death is comparative: we compare the actual world, in which the person dies at a certain time, with a counterfactual world in which they live longer, and we see that the person is worse off in the actual world. Nagel's key insight is that the value of a life is not just about the quality of experiences within it but about what the person gets to have overall—a longer life containing more goods is better than a shorter one, even if the shorter one is experienced just as positively.

The deprivation account faces a well-known objection: the timing problem. If death is a deprivation, when exactly is it bad for the person? It cannot be before death, because the person is still alive and has not yet been deprived. It cannot be after death, because the person no longer exists and there is no subject to bear the harm. Nagel's response is that death is bad for the person at all times after they die, because it is always true at those times that the person is deprived of the goods they would have had. This may seem odd, but Nagel argues that it is no odder than saying that someone's achievements are good for them even after they die.

A further challenge comes from the symmetry argument, originally advanced by Lucretius. Lucretius points out that we do not regard our prenatal nonexistence—the time before we were born—as a bad thing. But prenatal and posthumous nonexistence are, in themselves, symmetrical: both are periods during which we do not exist. So if posthumous nonexistence is bad, prenatal nonexistence should be bad too. Since we rightly reject the latter, we should reject the former. Nagel responds to this by arguing that there is an important asymmetry: while we could have been born earlier and thus lived longer, we could not have been born later and lived longer, because being born later would mean being a different person. Our birth date is essential to our identity in a way that our death date is not.

In my view, the deprivation account largely succeeds. The Epicurean's experiential requirement is too restrictive: we can clearly make sense of harms that are not experienced, such as being betrayed behind one's back or having one's reputation destroyed without one's knowledge. Death is a limiting case of such unexperienced harms. The timing problem is a genuine difficulty, but Nagel's response, while counterintuitive, is not obviously incoherent. And the symmetry argument can be resisted. So the Epicurean position, while powerful, is ultimately unpersuasive.
