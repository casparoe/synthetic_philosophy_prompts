I'm preparing a seminar on science and religion, and I want to put Bertolt Brecht's play *Life of Galileo* into conversation with Galileo's actual views on how scripture relates to natural philosophy, as laid out in his Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina. The historical Galileo held a subtle position: scripture and nature both proceed from God, but scripture is written in language accommodated to ordinary understanding and often speaks figuratively, whereas nature is inexorable and must be read literally. So when physical demonstration conflicts with a literal reading of a biblical passage, the proper response is to reinterpret the passage — not to reject the demonstration. Brecht's play, by contrast, stages the conflict as a more straightforward collision between truth and institutional power, with the Church functioning as a political obstacle to knowledge rather than as a partner in a hermeneutic dispute. I'd like you to read the play through the lens of Galileo's actual hermeneutic arguments. Where does Brecht capture something real about Galileo's predicament, and where does he transform a theological-epistemological debate into a political drama about courage and cowardice? In particular, does the play's treatment of Galileo's recantation — the moral center of the piece — implicitly depend on a view of the science-scripture relationship that the historical Galileo would have found alien? Please approach this as a scholar comfortable with both early modern intellectual history and Brechtian theater. Think carefully about specific scenes and avoid reducing either the play or the philosophical position to a caricature.
