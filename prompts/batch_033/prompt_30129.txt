I keep noticing a pattern that I don't have a word for, and I'm hoping someone can help me carve it precisely.

Here's the phenomenon. A person realizes that having a certain disposition or character trait would be genuinely advantageous for achieving their goals, even though that disposition would lead them to make, by their own current standards, worse decisions. So they set out to actually acquire that trait — not to fake it, not merely to constrain their future options, but to genuinely change how they evaluate choices. The crucial thing is that the trait has to be real to work: if they're only pretending, the advantage disappears, because other people — or their own future self — can detect the pretense.

Some instances of what I mean:
- Someone makes themselves genuinely vengeful, knowing this will lead them to retaliate in ways that are costly and pointless when considered in isolation, because being known as vengeful is the only credible deterrent against people who would otherwise exploit them.
- Someone cultivates genuine overconfidence about their abilities, because they know that overconfident people get more opportunities and persist longer through setbacks, even though the overconfidence will cause them to misjudge risks and waste effort on doomed projects.
- A person trains themselves to be genuinely unable to tell a convincing lie, because they know a high-stakes situation is coming where they will need to be believed, and being detectably honest is the only way. They will now be worse off in all the low-stakes situations where a small lie would have helped.

This isn't quite precommitment in the Ulysses-and-the-sirens sense: precommitment is about constraining your options or raising the costs of certain choices while keeping your preferences the same. Here the person is changing their preferences or their decision-making psychology itself. It also doesn't seem to be quite the Parfit hitchhiker case — there the transformation is targeted at a single future decision, namely paying the driver, whereas I'm describing a more diffuse change in disposition that ramifies across many decisions, many of which will now go worse than they would have.

Is there an established name for this specific phenomenon — strategically acquiring a disposition that makes you a worse decision-maker by your own prior lights, because the strategic benefits depend on the disposition's being genuine? If not, what would you call it, and how exactly should we distinguish it from precommitment, from rational irrationality, and from the Parfit cases? I'm less interested in the label than in getting the boundaries right: what counts and what doesn't, and whether the phenomenon is even coherent once you press on it.

One request: if any of this rests on a confusion — for example, if changing your preferences strategically collapses into ordinary preference change and isn't a distinct category, or if there's a reason this cannot be rational in the way I'm gesturing at — please say so plainly rather than trying to salvage the question. I'd rather learn that the framing is muddled than get a polite answer to a bad question.
