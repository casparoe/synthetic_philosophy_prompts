So I'm teaching this intro to ethics module and I've been trying to get students to see that the ticking time bomb debate isn't just about abstract hypotheticals — that it plays out in the way we tell stories about national security. I'm screening Zero Dark Thirty next week and I want to use it as more than just a conversation starter. The film's been read every which way: as an apologia for torture, as an exposé of it, as a kind of moral Rorschach test. What I'm actually interested in is whether the film does philosophical work that the standard debate can't. You know the standard debate: one side says torture is absolutely wrong no matter what, the other says in a genuine ticking time bomb case — nuclear device, millions of lives — it's not just permissible but obligatory. The film doesn't stage that debate; it shows something messier. Maya doesn't argue with Dan about the ethics of what he's doing; she just watches, and then she uses the intelligence, and then she's the one who finds bin Laden. The film seems to be saying: here's what it looks like, here's what it costs, here's what it got us — now what do you think?

I'd like a reading of Zero Dark Thirty through the lens of the philosophical literature on torture and the ticking time bomb. In particular: does the film function as an argument, and if so, for what? Does it undermine the absolutist position by showing that torture can produce actionable intelligence? Or does it undermine the consequentialist position by showing the psychological and institutional damage in a way that the tidy ticking time bomb hypothetical screens out? Are there scenes where the film seems to be taking a stance, and scenes where it pointedly refuses to? And here's what I really want to know: does the film expose a limitation in the standard philosophical framing — that the debate is set up as a choice between abstract principles, when the actual moral texture of torture involves things like complicity, moral luck, institutional momentum, and the way people live with themselves afterward, which the hypothetical just brackets? I want to know whether the film is doing philosophy or just providing an illustration, and if it's doing philosophy, whether it's doing a kind that the papers can't.
