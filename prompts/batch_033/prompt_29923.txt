Next month I am presenting to the technical advisory committee of an aquaculture certification scheme that is deciding whether to add fish and decapod crustacean welfare criteria to its standards. I co-authored the evidence review that prompted the discussion, and I have been asked to defend the position that farmed fish and decapod crustaceans should be treated as sentient for welfare purposes, and that several routine practices — slaughter without prior stunning, eyestalk ablation in shrimp broodstock, out-of-water transport, and stocking densities that suppress normal behavior — should be phased out or regulated rather than defended as ordinary industry practice. The decapod claims are the genuinely contested part, and they are where I expect the hardest questioning. I have spent my career on the physiology and husbandry side of this, but I have never had to defend the welfare conclusion in front of people whose production targets and budgets depend on the answer.

The committee is mixed: two fish health veterinarians, three production biologists (one salmon, two shrimp), an economist who models the cost of compliance, and an academic neuroscientist who works on crustacean nociception and has argued in print that the evidence for invertebrate consciousness is overstated. They are not hostile to welfare language in principle; they already accept water-quality and slaughter-hygiene standards. But they care about measurable outcomes, biosecurity, feed conversion and cost per kilo, and they are wary of getting ahead of the scientific consensus or inviting regulation. I expect their resistance to be empirical and operational — burden of proof, welfare metrics, species generalization, economic feasibility — rather than philosophical.

Write my Q&A prep sheet: the objections I will actually get from this audience, not the ones from philosophers or animal-advocacy groups, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are; each phrased the way one of these committee members would put it and attributed to the kind of member most likely to raise it; each with a two-minute reply, a note on where that reply is weakest, and what I should concede up front rather than defend. My argument in brief: the best current evidence satisfies reasonable criteria for treating these animals as sentient under a precautionary standard; the practices at issue would cause pain if the animals are sentient; and given the number of animals involved, the error of ignoring welfare is worse than the error of over-regulating. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks.
