Consider Frank Jackson's knowledge argument: Mary, a brilliant neuroscientist, has spent her whole life in a black-and-white room and has learned every physical fact about color vision. Upon leaving the room and seeing red for the first time, she exclaims that she has learned something new. The argument concludes that physicalism is false because there are facts about conscious experience not entailed by the complete set of physical facts.

The philosophical literature contains a number of distinct responses to this argument. Your task is to rank these responses by how much they concede to the core anti-physicalist intuition that drives the argument — that is, by how much each response grants that Mary genuinely acquires something she lacked before, where "something" might be factual knowledge, a concept, an ability, a mode of presentation, or nothing at all. Rank them from most concessive (grants the most to the intuition that Mary's pre-release picture of the world was incomplete in a deep sense) to least concessive (denies that anything epistemically significant happens when Mary first sees red).

The responses you should rank are:

(a) Property dualism / the original Jackson position: Mary learns a new, irreducible, non-physical fact about what it is like to see red.
(b) The ability hypothesis (Lewis, Nemirow): Mary gains only abilities — knowing how to imagine, recognize, and remember red experiences — not any new factual or propositional knowledge.
(c) The acquaintance hypothesis (Conee, Bigelow): Mary gains acquaintance with the property of phenomenal redness, which is a form of non-propositional knowledge distinct from both factual knowledge and mere ability.
(d) The phenomenal concept strategy (Loar, Papineau, Balog): Mary acquires a new phenomenal concept (a recognitional or demonstrative concept) that refers to a physical property, so she gains a new way of thinking about an old physical fact.
(e) Type-A physicalism / the "no-new-knowledge" view (Dennett, Churchland): Mary does not learn anything genuinely new — her reaction reflects a failure of imagination or an illusion of epistemic progress.
(f) Type-B physicalism / the "old fact, new guise" view: Mary learns an old physical fact under a new mode of presentation; there is an epistemic gap but no ontological gap, and the intuition that she learns something new is explained without abandoning physicalism.
(g) Russellian monism / panpsychist responses: The physical facts Mary knew were relational and dispositional; upon seeing red she learns the intrinsic categorical nature that underlies those dispositions, so she does learn something new, but what she learns is the intrinsic nature of the physical itself.

Please structure your response in exactly the following sections:

1. Clarification of the ranking dimension (approx. 100–150 words): Explain more precisely what it means to be "more concessive" or "less concessive" to the anti-physicalist intuition. Distinguish, for instance, between conceding that Mary gains new factual knowledge about the world, conceding that she gains a new cognitive relation to reality without new facts, and conceding nothing at all. This section sets the standard by which the ranking is made.

2. Tier list (approx. 50–100 words): Present the seven responses grouped into three or four tiers, from most concessive to least concessive, with a short label for each tier (e.g., "Tier 1: Full concession — Mary gains new non-physical facts"). List the letter labels of the responses that fall into each tier.

3. Justification for each placement (approx. 300–400 words total): For each response, in the order of the ranking, give one or two sentences explaining why it lands in its tier rather than higher or lower. Pay special attention to adjacent responses that might seem close — explain what tips one above the other. If any two responses are effectively tied on this dimension, note that and justify why they cannot be separated.
