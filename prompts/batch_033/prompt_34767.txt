I cover the behavioral sciences for a general-interest magazine, and I have been reading my way through the experimental philosophy literature, where the Knobe effect keeps coming up: the finding that people are readier to call a foreseen side effect intentional when it is bad than when it is good. Rather than only report other people's results, I decided to collect my own verdicts on a set of cases. They are below, and I would like to know whether a single principle captures them, or nearly does, and what that principle commits me to in a case I have not been able to settle.

Case 1: The executive and the wetland. A chief executive approves a cost-saving expansion after being told it will drain and destroy a nearby wetland. She says the wetland is not her concern; her only aim is the profit margin. The wetland is destroyed. I judge that she intentionally destroyed the wetland. I am confident about this.

Case 2: The executive and the creek. The same executive, on a different occasion, approves a project after being told it will, as a side effect, restore a long-polluted creek. She again says the creek is not her concern; her only aim is the margin. The creek is restored. I judge that she did not intentionally restore the creek. I am just as confident as in the first case, and I cannot say what distinguishes the two.

Case 3: The reluctant physician. A physician prescribes the only effective drug for a serious infection, knowing it will cause severe nausea. She wishes she could spare the patient the nausea and regards it as a genuine cost. The nausea occurs. I judge that she did not intentionally cause the nausea. I am confident.

Case 4: The trivial irritant. An office manager orders new carpeting, knowing it will give off a faint odor for an afternoon. She does not care about the odor. The odor occurs. I judge that she did not intentionally cause the odor, or if she did, only in a sense too thin to matter. I am moderately confident.

Case 5: The nurse and the patient's mood. A nurse administers a painkiller, knowing it will also lift the patient's mood. She says the mood is beside the point; her job is pain management. The patient's mood lifts. I find myself inclined to say she did intentionally lift the patient's mood, even though the effect is good and not her aim. I am tentative about this one.

Case 6: The general and the civilians. A general orders a strike on a munitions depot. The only feasible route to the depot runs through a residential block, and she is told civilians will be killed. She says the civilians are not her concern. They are killed. I judge that she intentionally killed the civilians. Here the deaths are a means rather than a side effect, so I am confident, but I include the case because any principle should say why this one is easy while the executive cases are hard.

There are tensions among these verdicts. Cases 1 and 3 both involve bad, foreseen, unwanted effects, yet I call one intentional and the other not; the difference seems to be that the executive was indifferent where the physician was not, but I do not know why indifference should matter more than foreknowledge. Cases 2 and 5 both involve good, foreseen, unwanted effects, yet I call one not intentional and the other intentional; there the difference seems to be something about the nurse's professional role, but I cannot state it precisely. Case 4 makes me doubt that badness alone does the work.

My question is this: is there a single principle, stated in terms of foresight, desire, indifference, norms, role, blame, or some combination, that yields all six verdicts, or close to it? If saving every verdict requires a gerrymandered principle, I would rather be told which verdicts to overturn and why. Once the principle is stated, please apply it to a further case I have not settled: a data analyst illegally accesses a competitor's server, and as a side effect of the intrusion the competitor discovers and patches a security flaw. The analyst says he does not care about the patch. Did he intentionally patch the flaw? I genuinely do not know.
