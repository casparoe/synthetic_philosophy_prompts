I am a PhD student in political theory, working on democratic institutional design and the case for sortition. I have reached a set of positions on lottocracy that I am preparing to defend in a seminar, but before I do, I would like an audit of whether they hang together. I have tried to state them as plainly as I can, and I genuinely cannot tell where the strain lies, so I would be grateful for a systematic examination rather than a general assessment.

Here is where I have landed.

(1) I hold an epistemic conception of democracy: a decision procedure is justified chiefly by the quality of the decisions it tends to produce, not by its capacity to express citizens' antecedent preferences.

(2) I think the fundamental defect of electoral democracy is that election is an aristocratic selection mechanism. It systematically favours the wealthy, the highly educated, the well-connected, and the rhetorically gifted, and this bias is internal to election itself rather than a correctable effect of campaign finance or party organisation.

(3) I therefore think the main legislative function ought to be discharged by a citizens' assembly chosen by lot from the adult population, serving for a fixed term, remunerated, and embedded in structured deliberation with expert testimony and cross-examination.

(4) I think the legitimacy of such an assembly rests on its being a statistical mirror of the citizenry: citizens can see themselves, or someone like themselves, among the authors of the laws.

(5) I think descriptive representation matters only instrumentally. A body's mirroring the population is valuable because it brings a wider range of cognitive perspectives and social knowledge into the room, which improves decision quality, not because a legislature ought to mirror the population for its own sake.

(6) I nevertheless think elected officials should retain agenda-setting and executive functions, because elections supply accountability for outcomes in a way that sortition cannot. I would remove from elected officials only the final say over ordinary legislation.

(7) I am sceptical that ordinary voters are competent to decide complex policy questions directly, which is why I oppose binding referenda on complex matters. But I am confident that ordinary citizens selected by lot can, after structured deliberation, make competent decisions on those same complex questions.

(8) I think political equality, in a large modern state, is best secured by giving every citizen the same chance of selection for the legislative assembly, rather than by trying to equalise influence over each decision once the assembly is seated.

I would like you to go through these commitments and identify which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be. For each genuine tension, please say how severe it is, what would have to give to resolve it, and what the cheapest revision would cost in terms of the rest of the view. Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally serious, and please do not simply reassure me that the position is broadly fine. Finally, I would like you to say which single revision to the set would do the most to restore coherence, and at what cost. I would also be grateful if the answer could be written in British English.
