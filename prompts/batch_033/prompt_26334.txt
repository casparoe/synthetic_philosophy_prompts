I'm preparing to interview a philosopher who has recently published a book arguing that Alan Turing's discussion of extrasensory perception in "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" (1950) is not a bizarre aside but a serious and illuminating part of his philosophy of mind. The book's central claim is that Turing's willingness to take ESP as a genuine empirical challenge, rather than dismissing it as a conceptual confusion, reveals something important about his naturalism and about what he thought it means to think.

Please act as an expert on Turing's philosophy and on mid-century parapsychology debates. Think carefully and draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond having the guest restate the book's thesis. I want questions that press on the hardest points. In particular, please include:

- At least one question about whether Turing was genuinely convinced by J.B. Rhine's statistical evidence for telepathy, or whether the ESP section is better read as ironic or as a rhetorical strategy — and a follow-up to the answer the guest is most likely to give.
- At least one question about what the ESP discussion implies for the relationship between the imitation game and Turing's earlier work on computability: if a telepath could beat the game, does that mean "thinking" for Turing includes non-computational processes?
- At least one question about how the guest handles the fact that Turing's proposed solution — the "telepathy-proof room" — seems to treat ESP as a merely practical obstacle rather than a conceptual one, which sits uneasily with the book's claim that the passage is philosophically deep.
- At least one question that pushes the guest to say what Turing got wrong in his treatment of ESP, and what contemporary philosophy of mind could still learn from it.

Before writing the final questions, please begin by brainstorming the strongest objections to the guest's position and the replies she is most likely to give, so that the questions can anticipate and cut through those replies.
