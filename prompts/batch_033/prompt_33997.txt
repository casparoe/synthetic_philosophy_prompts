I'm teaching a grad seminar on social choice theory this semester — about a dozen students, mix of philosophy and econ — and we've just spent two weeks on Sen's liberal paradox. You know the setup: unrestricted domain, the Pareto principle, and minimal liberalism (at least two people each get to be decisive over at least one pair of social alternatives) turn out to be jointly inconsistent. Sen's own prude-and-lewd example makes it vivid enough, but now I want to give them something that helps them figure out where they actually stand, rather than just nodding along with whichever response I happened to assign that week.

So here's what I'm after: a decision-tree handout that starts from "yes, I accept that the three conditions can't all hold" and then walks the student through a series of branching questions until they land at a named family of responses. The questions shouldn't be "do you reject the Pareto principle?" — they should be concrete. Give them a case (the prude/lewd one, or a variant you construct) and ask: would you override the prude's preference here? Would you say the lewd's preference shouldn't even count as the kind of thing a right protects? That sort of thing. Each leaf gets a name for the position (rights-as-constraints, waiving, domain restriction, rejecting Pareto in the presence of rights, etc.) and a one-sentence note on the most awkward bullet that position has to bite.

Build the tree from the logical space, not from a list of names. If there are answer-combinations nobody defends, say why — incoherent, just unappealing, or underexplored. And flag any leaves the tree can't cleanly seperate (I know some of these views blur at the edges).

Also, be careful to distinguish what's settled — the formal impossibility result itself, the fact that those three conditions conflict — from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this. Don't present one resolution as the consensus if it's not.
