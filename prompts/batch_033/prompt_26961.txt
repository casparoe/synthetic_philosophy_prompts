I've been defending heterophenomenology in my lab's journal club — Dennett's line that the right way to do a science of consciousness is to treat subjects' verbal reports as a text to be interpreted, not as authoritative readouts of some inner theater. You take the intentional stance toward the subject, you gather their sincere reports about what it's like for them, and those reports are the data you're trying to explain. But you don't grant that the reports are *true* descriptions of some private realm; they're expressions of beliefs, and beliefs can be wrong, even about your own experience. So far so good.

But I've started worrying that I can't actually say what would falsify this methodology. If someone says "but you've left out the *what-it's-like-ness* itself," the heterophenomenologist just says: describe what-it's-like-ness in more detail and I'll add that to the text I'm explaining. If someone says "there's an aspect of my experience I can't put into words," the heterophenomenologist says: then there's nothing for me to explain, because the data is the report. It feels like the view has been set up so that every objection gets absorbed as more data rather than counting as a counterexample.

So my question: what would actually count against heterophenomenology as a methodology? I want the specific things that, if they obtained, should make a heterophenomenologist abandon the view — not just the standard hard-problem complaints, but the precise empirical or conceptual results that would break it. Are there kinds of conscious phenomena it structurally can't handle? Is there a point where the gap between report and experience becomes too wide to ignore? And if the view really is unfalsifiable, is that a problem for a *methodological* proposal (as opposed to an empirical theory), or is it fine for a method to be something you adopt rather than test?
