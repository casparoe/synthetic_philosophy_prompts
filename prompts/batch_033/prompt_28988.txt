I've been defending a version of illusionism about phenomenal consciousness — the view that there are no phenomenal properties, no raw feel over and above representational and access-level facts, plus the introspective impression that something extra is there. Not the strawman version where people are wrong that they're conscious; the version where introspection systematically misrepresents what's actually present, the way a desktop interface makes it useful to treat files as being literally inside folders.

I want a map of what would count against this view. Not the flat-footed "but obviously I'm conscious" response, unless there's a version of it that actually lands. I want the empirical findings, introspective cases, and conceptual arguments that should move me, sorted by how decisive they are. Specifically:

- Are there actual or possible findings in neuroscience or psychophysics that illusionism predicts one way and realism about phenomenal consciousness predicts another? What would falsify it, or at least make it costlier?
- Which introspective phenomena are hardest to describe away — for instance, pain doesn't just seem to carry content, it seems to hurt — and do any of them rise to the level of a defeater rather than an awkward case?
- The who-is-fooled objection: if the illusion is real, something has to be the subject that's taken in by it. How bad is this, and is there an illusionist reply that doesn't just relabel the problem?
- What about the meta-problem — the fact that we're so strongly inclined to believe in phenomenal consciousness in the first place? Is that a problem for illusionism or a prediction of it?
- Does illusionism collapse into realism once you grant that there's a robust, reportable difference between the access machinery and whatever the illusion presents as being there?

For what it's worth, I'm most worried about the who-is-fooled objection, so don't let me off easy there.

Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say which ones an illusionist should themselves concede as counting against the view, not just the ones critics think are problems. Be honest about whether the view has been insulated — wether "it's an illusion" can be deployed against any counterexample, and whether that's a vice here. Commit to a verdict: is the view genuinely exposed to evidence, and which finding or argument would make you abandon it if you held it?
