I retired from experimental physics several years ago and have been working my way through the philosophical literature on well-being — what it is for a life to go well. I find that my instincts on particular cases pull me in different directions, and I would like to know whether there is a coherent principle behind them, or whether I need to revise some of my verdicts. Below are the cases as I find myself reacting to them. I have not started from any explicit theory; these are just the judgments I arrive at when I try to take each case on its own terms.

1. **The Experience Machine.** I would not plug into Nozick's machine. A life of simulated experiences, however pleasant and however convincing, is not as good a life for the person living it as a corresponding life of genuine achievement, genuine relationships, and genuine engagement with the world. The difference is not merely that I have a preference for reality; I think the simulated life is objectively worse.

2. **The Deceived Spouse.** Alice has been married for forty years. Her husband treats her with consistent affection and consideration, and she experiences her marriage as loving and secure. In fact, her husband has carried on a series of affairs throughout the marriage, but he has been so careful that Alice never suspects a thing. Betty has an identical experiential history — the same sequence of kind words, the same gestures, the same felt sense of being loved — but Betty's husband has been entirely faithful. I judge that Alice's life has gone worse than Betty's, even though their experiences are indistinguishable from the inside. The infidelity is a harm to Alice even though she never encounters it.

3. **The Posthumously Recognized Composer.** A composer writes a symphony, dies believing it to be a competent but unremarkable piece of work, and takes no particular satisfaction in it. Fifty years after his death, the symphony is discovered and widely recognized as a masterpiece. I judge that the composer's life was better — that it went better for him — than the life of an otherwise identical composer whose symphony genuinely is mediocre and is never celebrated. The achievement counts, even though he never knew about it.

4. **The Deluded Painter.** A painter spends his career producing derivative, technically uninspired work, but he is surrounded by flatterers and sycophants who convince him he is a genius. He dies feeling triumphant. His paintings are never exhibited after his death and are eventually destroyed. I judge that his life went worse than he thought it did, and worse than the life of someone who produced genuinely good work and received honest but modest recognition.

5. **The Fraudulent Charity.** David founds and runs a charitable organization. He works tirelessly, sacrifices income and leisure, and goes to his grave believing he has saved thousands of lives. Unbeknownst to him, his business partner has been embezzling the funds from the start, and not a single dollar has reached the intended beneficiaries. I judge that David's life went worse than the life of someone who ran a genuinely effective charity with the same experiential history. The failure is real, and it matters.

6. **The Miserable Achiever.** A medical researcher spends her career in grueling, isolating work. She makes a genuine breakthrough that, after her death, saves millions of lives. But she experiences her work as a joyless slog, she feels no sense of progress or meaning, and she dies bitter and unhappy, convinced her contribution was trivial. Here I am genuinely torn. On the one hand, my verdicts on cases 2 through 5 suggest that the objective facts about achievement should matter, and by that standard her life contains a great good. On the other hand, a life that is experienced as miserable from the inside seems deficient in a way that mere objective success cannot fully repair. I do not know how to weigh these considerations against each other, and I find myself going back and forth.

7. **The Contentment Pill.** Suppose a person takes a drug that induces a state of serene, unshakeable contentment with whatever life they happen to be living. They then spend sixty years on a couch, perfectly happy, doing nothing, forming no deep relationships, achieving nothing. I judge that this is a very bad life — worse than a life of modest achievement, modest relationships, and modest pleasure. The contentment does not redeem it.

8. **The Networked Virtual World.** Suppose a community of people voluntarily enter a shared virtual environment. They form genuine friendships, fall in love, undertake collaborative projects, and achieve things that matter within that virtual world — building institutions, creating art, helping one another. They know they are in a simulation, and they remain in contact with one another as real people. I find myself inclined to say that these can be good lives, perhaps as good as non-virtual lives. But this seems to be in tension with my verdict on the Experience Machine. If the problem with the Experience Machine is that its contents are not real, then why should a virtual world be any different? The only difference I can point to is that the networked world involves real other people and allows for genuine agency — the outcomes depend on what you actually do. But I am not sure whether this distinction can bear the weight I am placing on it, especially since one could imagine a networked experience machine that also involves real other people and responsive outcomes.

I would like to know: is there a principle, or a small set of principles, that captures all of these verdicts (or at least the ones I am confident in)? If some of my verdicts must be revised to achieve consistency, which ones are the best candidates for revision, and what does the resulting view look like? Please state the principle precisely, identify which cases motivate each of its clauses, and say what it predicts about cases I have not listed — particularly cases at the boundary between the Experience Machine and the Networked Virtual World.
