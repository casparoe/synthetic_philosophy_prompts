I keep running into a version of the claim that consciousness is the brain's user interface, and I want it audited rather than accepted or dismissed wholesale. The picture runs as follows. A modern computer is a mass of operations in silicon, but what the user meets is a desktop of icons, folders, a pointer, and a progress bar. The icons do not resemble the voltages, and the progress bar does not report the true state of the machine, yet the arrangement lets a person steer the system without knowing anything about its substrate. The proposal is that conscious experience stands to neural processing the way that desktop stands to the machine: a serialized, low-bandwidth, heavily edited summary of massively parallel processes, present not to do the work but to make the work steerable. The people I hear this from then draw strong conclusions. Introspection, they say, can only ever reveal the interface and never the machinery; the self is a kind of icon on the screen, a useful fiction rather than an agent; and the hard problem of consciousness is a confusion, roughly on a par with asking what the recycle bin feels like from the inside.

I would like a careful audit of how far this analogy actually carries. Please lay out the mapping explicitly—what corresponds to the icons, the desktop, the pointer, the user, the underlying code, the display, and the designer—and then identify the disanalogies. In particular, I want to know which features of the source domain the analogy quietly imports into the target without argument, which disanalogies matter for the three conclusions above and which are merely superficial, and whether those conclusions survive once the disanalogies are on the table. If the interface analogy fails in important respects, say whether some other analogy, such as a control panel or a map drawn by the very system that uses it, would do the same work with fewer costs.
