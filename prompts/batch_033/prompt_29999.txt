After a Kierkegaard reading group last Tuesday, two of my colleagues — Miriam, who works on philosophy of religion, and David, a postdoc in ethics — got into it about Fear and Trembling, and they were still going at it when the rest of us had already moved on to the wine. I ended up standing there listening for another twenty minutes, and now I can't stop turning their argument over in my head.

Miriam's line was that the teleological suspension of the ethical is Johannes de Silentio's way of saying that from the standpoint of faith, the ethical isn't the highest authority. Abraham's willingness to sacrifice Isaac isn't murder, she said, because his absolute duty to God suspends the universal ethical obligation. The book, on her reading, is a defense of the religious life as genuinely higher than the ethical life — not a rejection of ethics, but a reordering of it under a higher telos. David pushed back hard. He said she was missing the whole point of the psuedonym. Johannes de Silentio keeps insisting he cannot understand Abraham, cannot make the movement of faith, and the book presents faith as a paradox that can't be justified to anyone standing outside it. If you read it as a divine-command ethics manual, he said, you're flattening the absurd into something comfortable. Miriam shot back: then why call it a teleological suspension? "Teleological" means it has a purpose — the telos is the absolute relation to God, and that's a higher-order reason, not just irationality. And Johannes clearly admires Abraham; he calls him the father of faith, the knight of faith. You don't write a whole book in admiration of something you think is unjustifiable. David said admiration isn't the same as providing a moral theory anyone can apply. The knight of faith is a paradox precisely because he can't be imitated in any straightforward way — Johannes even says the ethical itself is the temptation for Abraham, the thing that would hold him back from God. The suspension isn't a generalizable permission. Miriam leaned in at that point and said: if the suspension can't be generalized and can't be understood from the outside, then what's the difference between Abraham and a delusional person who thinks God told him to kill his child? If the book's point is just "faith is absurd and we can't judge it," then Kierkegaard hasn't given us any way to distinguish genuine faith from madness — and that can't be what he intended. David replied that maybe that's exactly the problem Kierkegaard wants us to sit with, and the text deliberately refuses to resolve it. Johannes says he can't understand Abraham, and that's the whole point: faith can't be domesticated into ethics. But that doesn't mean Kierkegaard is endorsing divine-command morality; it means he's showing the collision and leaving it there.

They turned to me to settle it. I mumbled something about needing to reread Problema I, but honestly I'd rather get an outside take before I weigh in with them again. Could you go through their exchange point by point — who's right about what, where does each overstate, and what are they both missing?
