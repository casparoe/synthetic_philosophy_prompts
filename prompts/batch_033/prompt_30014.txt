I couldn't follow all of it, but they asked me to settle it and I honestly couldn't.

Dan said it's a textbook information cascade: a few early teams at big companies moved to microservices for reasons that probably made sense for them, and everyone after that saw the choices piling up and rationally concluded the crowd must know something, so they suppressed they're own doubts and followed. He said that's not irrational — from the inside, 'everyone is doing it' is real evidence, because those choices embed other people's information. Priya pushed back and said that's way too generous: most teams around here aren't adopting microservices because they're learning from earlier adopters; they're adopting because there's now an ecosystem (tooling, hiring pool, blog posts, conference talks) and because nobody wants to be the engineer who argued for the boring monolith when it goes wrong. She said that's network effects and reputational herding, not an information cascade, and the distinction matters because an information cascade should be fragile, whereas this looks sticky. Dan came back and said the distinction is cleaner in textbooks than in real life: even if the motives are mixed, a new team deciding today still sees a huge installed base and is rational to treat it as evidence, and the fact that a couple of loud reversals could flip the whole industry is exactly the fragility cascade models predict. Priya then said no, the installed base is not 100 independent votes — if each team is mostly copying the teams before it, then by the time you're the 100th adopter you're learning almost nothing new from number 99, so the crowd's size is a kind of illusion. She said that's precisely why calling it 'rational' is misleading: every individual step can look reasonable while the group ends up somewhere nobody privately believes in. Dan said sure, but that's a feature of the model, not a bug — a cascade can be wrong and still be rational for each participant, and that's what he's claiming is happening here.
