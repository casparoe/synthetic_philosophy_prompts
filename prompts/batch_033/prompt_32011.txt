Before I read another paper in the units of selection debate, I want the specification against which I can test each position. Write, as precisely as you can, the requirements that any adequate theory of what units natural selection acts on must meet: the phenomena it has to accommodate, the cases it cannot get wrong, the distinctions it cannot collapse, and the results it must be consistent with. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it. Use at least the following as test cases, and add any others you think are load-bearing: segregation distorters and other selfish genetic elements, transposable elements, cancer as a within-organism lineage, eusocial colonies, and species-level traits such as geographic range or extinction rate. Among the distinctions, say explicitly whether and why the theory must keep separate: replicators, vehicles, and interactors; selection of a trait versus selection for it; levels of selection versus units of selection; and genuine group adaptation versus a statistical side effect. Include any constraints that follow from the formal decomposition of selection into within-level and between-level components, and from the major transitions in individuality. Then mark each requirement as non-negotiable or as a preference of a particular camp, and say which pairs of requirements, if any, cannot be jointly satisfied by a single theory. Finish with a short scorecard of genic selectionism, multilevel selection theory, and the replicator/interactor framework against the list. Most of all, be self-critical about the list itself: tell me which requirement you would be first to demote under pressure, what argument or finding would lead you to a different verdict about that requirement, and what sort of empirical or theoretical result would force you to add a requirement you had left off.
