I've been noticing a pattern in conversation that I can't put my finger on, and I'm hoping there's a name for it — or at least a map of the territory. A lot of sentences have the surface form "if P, then Q" but don't seem to be conditionals at all in the usual sense. Like, if I say "If you're thirsty, there's beer in the fridge," I'm not claiming the beer's existence depends on your thirst — the beer is there either way. Or "If I remember correctly, the meeting is at three" — my remembering correctly isn't a condition for the meeting time; I'm just hedging. Or "If you don't mind my asking, how much did that cost?" — the question isn't logically conditional on your not minding. These feel different from genuine conditionals like "If you drop that glass, it will break," but they also feel different from each other. The beer-in-the-fridge case seems to be about relevance: the information is offered on the condition that it's relevant to you. The hedging case seems to be about epistemic modesty. The politness case seems to be about managing face. My question is: has anyone produced a taxonomy of these non-conditional uses of "if," and is there an account of what unifies them — or are they just a grab-bag of unrelated phenomena that happen to share a word? I'm less after a label and more after the conceptual lay of the land.
