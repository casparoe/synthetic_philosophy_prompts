I've been reading through the counterpart theory vs. transworld identity literature — Lewis on one side, Plantinga and Kripke on the other — and I'm struck by how little the debate has moved since the 70s. For context: the question is how to make sense of de re modal claims like the one about Humphrey possibly winning the election. Lewis says Humphrey exists only in the actual world, and the modal claim is true because Humphrey has a counterpart in some other possible world who wins. Plantinga and Kripke say no — Humphrey himself exists in other possible worlds (transworld identity), and the claim is true because there's a world where Humphrey himself wins. Lewis fires back that transworld identity is mysterious: what makes some individual in another world identical to Humphrey rather than merely similar? Plantinga replies that counterpart theory gets the truth-conditions wrong — it's about someone else winning, not Humphrey.

And around and around it goes. Both sides seem to agree on all the modal facts, all the logical machinery, and what sentences come out true. They just disagree about what makes them true. My suspiscion is that this is really a disagreement about what metaphysical explanation requires — whether we need "genuine" identity across worlds or whether similarity-based representation is enough. But I'm not sure if that diagnosis is right, or if there's something else going on.

I'd like you to act as a professional metaphysician who's been watching this debate for decades. Think carefully and diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it substantive or merely verbal? Are the two sides answering different questions under one heading? Is there a hidden empirical or formal question that could settle it? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a new distinction, a formal result, or is the whole thing just a clash of theoretical virtues that no argument can resolve? Be specific about what each side is really after, and don't just summarize the positions.
