I work on risk-scoring models — the sort used to decide who gets bail, a loan, or extra monitoring — and I keep hitting the same wall. We wanted three things from the score, and each of them seemed obviously fair on it's own. First, the score should mean the same thing whoever it's applied to: a 7 out of 10 should carry the same risk for someone from group A as for someone from group B. Second, the people who wouldn't have had the bad outcome should be wrongly labelled high-risk at the same rate in both groups. Third, the people who would have had the bad outcome should be missed at the same rate in both groups. We treated these as just three ways of saying unbiased, and assumed any decent model would satisfy all three. But we could never get all three at once — it only seemed possible in degenerate cases, like when the two groups had the same underlying rate of the outcome, or when the model made no mistakes at all. A colleague hit the same thing with a hiring screen, so it wasn't just our data. Is there an established name or theorem for this exact configuration? I don't just want a label — give me your best definite account of what the result is, when it bites, and how the three requirements pull against each other, because I need to explain to my team why they can't treat them as interchangeable.
