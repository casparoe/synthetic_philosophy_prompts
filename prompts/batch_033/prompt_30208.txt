I cover the replication debates for a general-interest science and ideas magazine, and I am drafting a long piece on a pattern that keeps appearing in my interviews: two scrupulous analysts, looking at the same preregistered replication dataset, can issue opposite public verdicts about whether an effect survived, not because anyone is incompetent or dishonest but because their statistical frameworks differ. I do not want to relitigate any particular replication in the article. I want a clean, self-contained sidebar example that isolates the mechanism for a reader who has never thought about the difference between a p-value and a posterior probability.

Please construct a small numerical thought experiment, the kind I could run in a box beside the main text, in which a frequentist and a Bayesian analyzing exactly the same replication data reach opposite conclusions about whether the effect is real, with the Bayesian concluding that the replication supports the effect and the frequentist concluding that it does not. Constraints: the data are fixed in advance and the analyses are preregistered, with no optional stopping, p-hacking, or multiple comparisons anywhere in the story; the frequentist is committed by preregistration to a standard two-sided test of the replication data alone, so this is not a post hoc choice; the Bayesian's prior must be one a thoughtful analyst could defend in public, not a degenerate or conclusion-assuming prior; the numbers should be plausible for a small-to-medium psychological effect and a realistic replication sample; and neither analyst may make an elementary error, such as treating a p-value as the probability that the null is true or conflating a Bayes factor with a posterior probability. Please give the actual numbers—means, standard deviations, sample sizes, the p-value, and the Bayes factor or posterior probability—so a motivated reader could check the arithmetic.

After presenting the scenario, walk through both analyses in enough detail that each verdict follows from the numbers. Then tell me which structural feature of the case is doing the real work, what happens to the disagreement if that feature is removed (for instance, if the Bayesian were forced to use a default or weakly informative prior instead), and how a sophisticated defender of each side would criticize the other's conclusion.
