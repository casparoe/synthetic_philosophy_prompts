I am a novelist researching a book about a man trying to understand an act he committed but cannot fully explain, and I have been reading the philosophical literature on free will and moral responsibility. What strikes me, as an outsider, is how durable the impasse between compatibilists and incompatibilists remains. The compatibilist grants that determinism may be true and insists that what matters for freedom and blame is something like self-control, responsiveness to reasons, and the capacity to act from one's own settled character. The incompatibilist grants that people often have those capacities and insists they are beside the point: if the past and the laws fix what I do, then I could not have done otherwise in the only sense that could ground blame, so the compatibilist has changed the subject. Both sides seem to accept the same facts about the world and the same phenomenology of choosing, and yet neither moves. I am less interested in taking a side than in understanding the shape of the disagreement, because I want to write the argument convincingly from the inside. Is this a verbal dispute about the term 'free will,' a substantive dispute about the conditions under which blame is deserved, or two camps answering different questions — one about the varieties of free will worth wanting, the other about the availability of an alternative possibility — under a single label? My working hypothesis is that the debate stalls because each side takes its own favored reading of 'could have done otherwise' to be the only one that matters, while the other side hears a demand it regards as incoherent or beside the point. If that diagnosis is right, what would count as progress — a distinction that lets the two questions be asked separately, a decisive case, a formal result — or should I write the character as having discovered that the question was badly posed from the start?
