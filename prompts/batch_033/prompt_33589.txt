I've been going down a rabbit hole about Elizabeth Anscombe's protest against Oxford awarding an honorary degree to Harry Truman in 1956, and I keep running into the same summary of her argument on blogs and in forum discussions. Here's a representative version from a piece I read:

"Anscombe's position was that Truman was a mass murderer, and the argument is straightforward: dropping atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki killed hundreds of thousands of civilians, and intentionally killing civilians is murder. Since she was a devout Catholic, she appealed to the doctrine of double effect — you can't do evil that good may come of it, and the civilian deaths were the means to the end of forcing Japan's surrender, not an unintended side effect of hitting a military target. The bombs were dropped on cities, after all. So on her view, Truman was morally equivalent to the Nazi leaders we'd just finished hanging at Nuremberg for the same thing — deliberately targeting civilian populations. The fact that we don't see it that way, she argued, just shows that our moral judgments are contaminated by national self-interest and winner's bias."

I find the argument compelling in broad strokes, but something about the summary makes me uneasy. I've read enough philosophy to know that when a popularizer condenses a serious philosopher's argument into a single paragraph, distinctions tend to get flattened, and I'm especially suspicious of the way the summary treats Anscombe's Catholicism as though it's doing all the philosophical work. The comparison to the Nuremberg defendants also feels like it might be eliding a distinction she actually drew. Can you audit this summary for me? Go through it claim by claim and tell me what a careful reader of Anscombe would accept, what they'd wince at, and what's just wrong. I'm less interested in whether you agree with her conclusion than in whether the summary accurately represents the argument she actually gave, and I'd like you to take a definite position on each claim rather than just laying out interpretive possibilities and leaving me to pick.
