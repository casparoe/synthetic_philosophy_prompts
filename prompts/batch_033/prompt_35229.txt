The search for extraterrestrial intelligence and the literature on existential risk both deploy a standard argument: if intelligent civilizations were common, we should have seen signs of them by now — radio signals, megastructures, self-replicating probes; we see none; so intelligent life is probably rare, and something (a "Great Filter") must suppress its emergence or survival somewhere between abiogenesis and interstellar colonization. The argument treats our finding ourselves on a quiet planet, looking out at silent skies, as evidence that the universe is mostly empty.

But this inference presupposes a particular stance in a philosophical debate about how to reason from one's own existence. On one view, we should treat ourselves as a random draw from the set of all observers — so that finding ourselves early, or alone, is informative. On the rival view, the very fact that we exist counts as evidence that the set of observers is large — so that an empty sky is less surprising, because in a large universe with many civilizations most observers would still find themselves on a planet that hasn't yet been visited, for straightforward geometric reasons.
