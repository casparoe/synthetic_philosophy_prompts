The debate between Norman Malcolm and Daniel Dennett over whether dreams are experiences has, by my count, been open for nearly half a century without anything like a consensus. Malcolm argued, in his 1959 book *Dreaming*, that the very concept of an experience occurring during sleep is incoherent: we have no behavioral criteria for ascribing mental states to a sleeping person independently of their waking report, so the waking report does not describe a prior conscious event—it is the criterion for the dream. Dennett, in his 1976 paper "Are Dreams Experiences?", concedes that the naive picture of a private cinema running during sleep is empirically suspect, but he does not follow Malcolm all the way to eliminativism. Instead he offers a thought experiment—the "cassette" theory—on which dream narratives are composed and inserted into memory by unconscious processes at the moment of awakening, with no conscious experience during sleep at all. The point of the cassette theory is partly to show that the received view is an empirical hypothesis rather than a conceptual truth, but Dennett also seems to suggest that the question "were dreams experiences?" may be ill-posed once we see how little work the word "experience" is doing.

What puzzles me is that Malcolm and Dennett appear to agree on a great deal. Both reject the folk picture of dreaming as a conscious sequence playing out in real time during sleep. Both agree that waking testimony is the only epistemic access we have to dreams. Both agree that nothing in the phenomenology of waking up and recalling a dream settles whether an experience occurred during sleep or was generated at waking. And yet they land in opposite places: Malcolm says the notion of a dream-as-experience is conceptually confused, while Dennett says it is an empirical possibility—just one that may be false and may not matter. The debate has not so much been resolved as abandoned; people cite both papers, note the impasse, and move on to other questions about consciousness.

I want a diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement. Is it a disagreement about the metaphysics of mental events—about when, or whether, a mental event occurs if all its downstream effects are post-sleep? Is it a disagreement about the semantics of "experience"—about what the word requires, such that Malcolm and Dennett are applying different criteria under the same label? Is it a disagreement about the epistemology of first-person reports—about what waking testimony entitles us to infer? Or is it a verbal dispute that looked substantive only because "experience" was being asked to carry several distinct jobs at once?

Given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress? Would a sharpened distinction between conscious occurrence and memorial access dissolve the question? Would an empirical result—say, about the neural correlates of dream recall during REM sleep—speak to it, or would it merely change the subject? Or is the right conclusion that the question "are dreams experiences?" was poorly framed from the start, and that both Malcolm and Dennett were right to walk away from it, but for different reasons that have never been made explicit?
