Below are notes for a short essay I am considering writing, and I would welcome comments before I develop them further.

Working title: "The repugnant conclusion is a test of your aggregation axiom, not of your stomach for large numbers."

Notes:

1. The repugnant conclusion is usually introduced as a reductio of total utilitarianism: for any population of lives at a very high welfare level, there is some vastly larger population of lives barely worth living that totalism ranks as better, because its summed welfare is larger. The standard response is to find this repugnant and abandon totalism.

2. I want to reframe the dialectic. The repugnant conclusion is not a separate, intuitively loaded discovery about large numbers; it is the additive-aggregation axiom made vivid. If welfare is cardinally measurable, interpersonally comparable, and additive across persons, the conclusion is arithmetic: for any finite positive total Q and any positive per-person welfare q, there is some N such that N·q > Q. The only further substantive claim is that lives with small positive welfare are possible. So the repugnance is generated by additivity plus the existence of small-positive lives, not by totalism as some additional empirical or normative commitment.

3. This means rejecting the repugnant conclusion, at least as a direct implication of totalism, commits you to rejecting at least one of the following: the sum-ranking principle; cardinal interpersonal comparability; the claim that barely-worth-living lives have positive welfare; or the completeness of all-things-considered betterness across populations of different sizes. Each rejection has known costs, and the interesting question is which cost is cheapest, not whether the conclusion feels bad.

4. My diagnostic claim is that many people who report finding the conclusion repugnant never actually accepted additive cardinal welfare in the first place. They are running a person-affecting or threshold intuition while arguing inside a consequentialist frame, and the feeling of repugnance is largely the feeling of additivity doing work they did not sign off on.

5. The constructive upshot: the repugnant conclusion should be treated less as an intuition pump against totalism and more as a consistency check. A person-affecting view avoids it by refusing to count the addition of happy lives as an improvement; depending on how that refusal is formulated, it can require giving up completeness of the betterness ranking, or it commits one to treating the creation of a happy person as never making the outcome better. A threshold view avoids it by holding that barely-worth-living lives contribute no positive value below the threshold, at the cost of needing a sharp, principled line. The debate should be about those costs, not about who can summon the more vivid mental image of a barely-worth-living life.

I would particularly value comments on:

- Accuracy: is the arithmetic in (2) stated correctly, and is the menu in (3) the right and reasonably exhaustive one?
- Interest: is this reframe worth writing up, or is it a restatement of a point that people working on population axiology already grant?
- Weakest step: where would a sophisticated defender of the repugnant conclusion's argumentative force push back, and what would they say?
- Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this literature, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
