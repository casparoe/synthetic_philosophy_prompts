Write a dialogue between a global workspace theorist and a defender of phenomenal overflow — the view that there can be phenomenally conscious perception beyond what gets globally accessed, so that consciousness overflows the workspace. Set it right after the two of them have just run a Sperling-style partial-report experiment together: they flashed a 3x4 grid of letters, took whole-report trials, then ran the cued condition where a tone played right after the display disappears tells the subject which row to report. Theyre staring at the same numbers and drawing opposite conclusions.

The overflow person should push the point that the tone arrives after the letters are gone, so it can't change what was seen — it can only change which part of an already-formed conscious percept gets read out. The fact that subjects can report around three of four letters from whichever row was cued, while whole report only yields four or five letters total, suggests that more letters were phenomenally conscious than access can get at. The workspace person should resist: the pre-cue representation is a high-capacity non-conscious buffer (iconic memory or something like it), and the impression of having seen all twelve letters is itself constructed at the moment of access — a refrigerator-light sort of illusion, like change blindness. The overflow person should answer that this just relabels the phenomenon and ignores what subjects themselves say, and that treating 'conscious' as 'whatever can be reported' is exactly what's in dispute.

Make them actually engage: the workspace person should grant that the buffer has content, and the overflow person should get pressed on what the unaccessed phenomenal state is doing if it can't be reported or reasoned from at the time. Let the argument bottom out in a standoff about whether access is the mechanism of consciousness or just the way we find out about it. Both should be sharp and charitable, neither a strawman, and they should respond to each other's actual points rather than trading paragraphs. Aim for around 1,500 words.
