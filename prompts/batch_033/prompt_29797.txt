I'm giving a talk at my department's ethics colloquium next month and I'm already dreading the Q&A. The title is "Suffering Risks and the Case for Prioritizing the Prevention of Worst-Case Futures." The basic argument I'm making is: (1) the expected value of the far future is vast, so what happens in the long run dominates any plausible ethical calculus; (2) among possible long-run futures, some contain astronomical amounts of suffering — think misaligned AI optimizing for something that involves sentient creatures in agony, or a spacefaring civilization that systematically fails to care about the welfare of digital minds; (3) the asymmetry between suffering and happiness (or at least a weak version of it) suggests that preventing these worst-case futures should loom larger in our priorities than ensuring we reach the best-case ones; and (4) this means that within the longtermist portfolio, s-risk reduction deserves at least as much attention as extinction risk reduction, and possibly more. I'll gesture at some empirical work on how likely various s-risk scenarios are but the talk is mostly philosophical.

The audience: my department has a big ethics group. There's a utilitarian who works on population ethics and has defended the total view. There's a Kantian who thinks consequentialism is a nonstarter. There's a virtue ethicist who will probably hate the whole enterprise. There's a senior person who works on moral epistemology and has written skeptically about "grand ethical theorizing about the distant future." And there's a mixed bag of grad students, some sympathetic to EA, some hostile. I've given this talk to EA audiences and the pushback was mostly inside baseball — should we be longtermists, how low are the probabilities, is Bostrom's argument for the asymmetry any good. This audience won't care about any of that. They're going to hit me on the philosophical foundations, and I need to be ready.

So: give me a Q&A prep sheet. The objections I'll actually get from these people, ordered by how much damage they'd do to my argument if I fumble them — not just how likely they are. Phrase each one the way the person would actually ask it (tell me who's asking). For each objection: a ~two-minute reply, a candid note on where the reply is weakest, and whether there's anything I should just concede at the outset. Then tell me the question I should be praying nobody thinks to ask. And please, throughout, distinguish what's genuinely settled among people working on this stuff from what's still a live fight — I don't want to stand up there and present a contested view as if the field has moved past it.
