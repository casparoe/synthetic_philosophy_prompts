So I was reading about Kavka's toxin puzzle in a philosophy group and my friend took the ball and ran with it in a direction that's been bugging me for weeks. Quick recap of the original: a billionaire says he'll give you a million dollars tomorrow morning if, at midnight tonight, you genuinely intend to drink a toxin tomorrow afternoon that will make you miserably sick for 24 hours. The catch is you dont actually have to drink it — the money arrives before you'd have to act. So at midnight, you're trying to form an intention to do something you know you'll have zero reason to do once you have the money. Seems impossible. Most people agree you can't pull it off.

But here's the argument my friend built on top of it, which seems to show that basically all promises and commitments are in the same boat:

(1) You can only intend to φ if you believe you will have sufficient reason to φ when the time comes.
(2) In the toxin case, at midnight you believe that tomorrow afternoon you will have no reason to drink (the money is already yours, drinking only adds misery), so by (1) you cannot intend to drink.
(3) Now consider any promise to do something burdensome in the future — helping a friend move next Saturday, say. You make the promise, the friend is grateful, the social benefit is banked immediately. When Saturday arrives, the benefit is in the past.
(4) At the moment of promising, you can look ahead and see that on Saturday, your reasons to help will consist of: (a) the friend needs help, (b) you said you would. But (a) was true before you promised and you didn't volunteer then, so it wasn't sufficient. And (b) is only a reason if you already take your promise to bind you — but whether it binds you depends on whether you could form the intention to keep it, which is the very thing we're questioning.
(5) So at the moment of promising, you believe that on Saturday you will lack sufficient reason to help, just as in the toxin case.
(6) By (1), you cannot intend to help on Saturday.
(7) But a promise without the intention to keep it isn't a sincere promise — it's a prediction at best, manipulation at worst.
(8) Therefore sincere promises to do burdensome things are rationally impossible.

That's the argument. But people make and keep such promises all the time, and we don't think they're all irrational or insincere. So something has to give. I'm torn between rejecting (1) — but then what's the right theory of intention formation? — and saying there's a disanalogy between the toxin case and promising that my friend's step (4) is papering over. But I can't quite articulate what the disanalogy is without begging the question. Which step do you reject, and what's the bill?
