So I've been going down a Quine rabbithole lately — rereading "Two Dogmas" and the gavagai stuff from *Word and Object* — and I keep coming back to this question that's been bugging me for weeks. What would Quine have made of large language models?

Here's the setup. Quine's indeterminacy of translation argument says that when you're trying to translate a completely alien language, all you have to go on are the native speakers' verbal dispositions — what they're disposed to assent to or dissent from under various stimulations. And the punchline is that you could construct multiple incompatible translation manuals that are all consistent with the totality of behavioral evidence. There's no fact of the matter about which one is correct. "Gavagai" could mean rabbit, or undetached rabbit parts, or the universal rabbithood, and the radical translator can never settle it, because the stimulus meaning is the same for all of them. The indeterminacy starts at home too — Quine thought it applied to the home language just as much, which is the really spooky part.

Now fast forward sixty years and we have LLMs that do machine translation at superhuman levels without anyone ever writing down a translation manual. They're trained on massive parallel corpora — basically, they learn to map strings to strings by soaking in statistical regularities across languages. No linguist sat down and formulated analytic hypotheses. No one even tried to settle whether "gavagai" means rabbit or undetached rabbit parts; the model just learns that in context X, token sequence A tends to co-occur with token sequence B, and out comes fluent translation.

And here's what I can't shake: does this count as a vindication of Quine's view, or a refutation of it? On one hand, it seems like the LLM is doing exactly what Quine said was the only thing you could do — going by behavioral dispositions (in this case, corpus statistics rather than field linguistics, but the principle seems similar). And the fact that it works perfectly well without settling the "real" meaning of anything seems like a point in his favor: meaning really is indeterminate, and it doesn't matter. On the other hand, Quine seemed to think indeterminacy had real skeptical bite — that it showed something deep and unsettling about the underdetermination of semantics by physics. But if the whole thing works fine in practice and nobody cares about the indeterminacy, maybe it was a pseudo-problem all along? Or maybe the LLM is itself a proof that translation manuals are wildly underdetermined and yet we converge on one anyway for pragmatic reasons, which was sort of Quine's point about the home language?

I'm especially curious what Quine would have said about the fact that LLMs don't even have the stimulus-meaning framework he built everything on — there's no "assent under stimulation," no surface irritations, just next-token prediction over a text corpus that's already linguistic all the way down. Would he have said that's a completely different kind of thing from translation as he understood it, and therefore his thesis doesn't even apply? Or would he have revised his account of observation sentences to accommodate the fact that you can get translation off the ground without anything like ostensive learning? And what about the fact that LLM "translation" sometimes produces outputs that are fluently wrong in ways that would be invisible to a monolingual speaker — does that vindicate the indeterminacy claim, or is it just a boring engineering problem?

I'd like a careful reconstruction of what you think Quine's position would be, drawing on his actual commitments from his work (not just the vibe of "Quine was a behaviorist so he'd love it" or "Quine would hate it because he was a dinosaur"). Where would he double down, where would he concede, and what would he find genuinely surprising? Also: if you think there's a reading of Quine where LLMs just bypass the whole problem and make indeterminacy an irrelevant curiosity, I want to hear that argued seriously — I'm not just looking for the "Quine would approve" take.
