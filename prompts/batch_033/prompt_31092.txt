I'm a retired physicist, and since I stopped doing research I've been reading philosophy of physics for fun. The other night two friends of mine got into a long arguement at the pub, and I'd like someone to go through it properly.

Marta has been reading about psi-ontic versus psi-epistemic views of the quantum state, and she's come down on the side that the wavefunction is a real thing. Jon thinks it's a bookkeeping device for our information. The argument went roughly like this.

(1) Marta: Take the double-slit experiment with single electrons. The wavefunction of one electron goes through both slits and interferes with itself. If the wavefunction were just a summary of your ignorance — like a probability distribution — you'd expect the two possibilities to add as plain probabilities, giving you two smeared blobs, not interference fringes. Interference is something real waves do. So the wavefunction has to be physically real, not just information in someone's head.

(2) Jon: Careful. The mathematics is wavelike, but the question is what the mathematics is about. A map has contour lines without the countryside containing any actual lines. The interference pattern is a pattern in where the electron shows up on the screen; the wavefunction is the tool we use to predict that. And think about collapse. When you measure, the wavefunction updates everywhere at once. If it were a real physical field, that would be a faster-than-light physical process. If it's your information, there's no mystery: my probability distribution over a shuffled deck 'collapses' the moment I peek at the top card, and nobody thinks the deck is a nonlocal field.

(3) Marta: The deck analogy is exactly the kind of view the PBR theorem kills. Pusey, Barrett and Rudolph showed that you can't treat distinct wavefunctions as overlapping probability distributions over the same underlying physical states — at least not while keeping the assumption that independently prepared systems are independent. If the wavefunction were merely ignorance, two different wavefunctions could describe the very same underlying state of affairs. PBR rules that out. So the simple 'it's just information' view is false, and the natural fallback is that the wavefunction is part of reality.

(4) Jon: I'll grant the naive ignorance view is dead. But 'not merely epistemic' doesn't mean 'a physical object in the way you want'. There are options in between. In Bohmian mechanics, the particles are the real things and the wavefunction is more like a law of nature that guides them — real the way a law is real, but not a thing. And in QBism the wavefunction is a single agent's degrees of belief; the PBR setup sneaks in assumptions those people reject. So 'real' is doing too much work in your conclusion.

(5) Marta: But the law reading still concedes the wavefunction is part of how the world is, not merely how we describe it — that's already a form of realism about it. And if the wavefunction is just someone's degrees of belief, you owe me a story about why those beliefs obey Schrodinger's equation, and what the electron is doing when nobody's looking. You can't dodge the question by saying it's personal.

(6) Jon: And you can't dodge the measurement problem by saying the wavefunction is real. If it's real and it always follows Schrodinger's equation, you get many worlds, which is a lot of ontology to buy. If it's real and it collapses, you need a collapse mechanism nobody has found. My bookkeeping view at least keeps that mess in the description, where it belongs.

I could see the force of both, and I honestly couldn't sort out who was right about what. Could you go through their exchange move by move and adjudicate it — which points are sound, where each of them overreaches, and who comes out more right overall? Please answer in British English, by the way.
