So I'm taking this course on philosophy of mind and we've been reading Dennett, and there's this idea that keeps coming up in discussion section — "free-floating rationales." The claim is that there can be reasons that explain why something is the way it is, even though no agent ever entertained those reasons. Natural selection is the poster child: the reason why the giraffe's neck is long is that it lets them reach high foliage, but no one — not the giraffe, not nature, not God — ever thought "hmm, longer neck would help here" and then built one. The reasons are real, they do explanatory work, but they float free of any reasoner.

Here's the thing. One of my classmates has gotten really into this and now uses it to make a bold claim about AI safety. His line is basically: "Look, evolution is just a blind process of variation and selection, but over time it produces organisms that act for reasons — even though evolution itself never reasoned about anything. So we already know that rational-looking behavior can emerge from mindless processes. The scary AI alignment problem is overblown; nature's been running mindless optimization for eons and it hasn't killed us all." Then he pulls out this anaology: evolution is like a scientist who runs experiments but doesn't understand what she's doing. Random mutations are her guesses, the environment is her lab, selective survival is her data, and the resulting adaptations are her discoveries. The scientist doesn't know she's doing science, but she's doing it anyway — and the "reasons" for each adaptation are real and discoverable, even though she never held them in her head.

I'm suspicious. It feels like the analogy is smuggling in a lot, and I'm not sure the conclusion about AI follows even if the analogy holds up. Can you do a proper audit? I want: (a) a table that lines up what corresponds to what between an evolutionary process and a reasoning process, (b) the places where the mapping breaks, (c) which of those breaks actually matter for the AI-safety claim versus which are just nitpicks, and (d) whether there's a better analogy for what's going on with free-floating rationales that doesn't stack the deck the way this one seems to. Also, if the whole free-floating rationales idea is less about evolution *being* reasoning and more about something else entirely, I'd like to know that too.
