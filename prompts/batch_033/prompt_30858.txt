Here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) I'm a virtue ethicist in the standard sense that the primary evaluative notion is the virtuous person: an act is right iff it is what a virtuous agent would characteristically do in the circumstances.

(2) I'm fairly conviced by the situationist evidence. The classic studies — hurried seminarians failing to stop for someone in need, people who've just found a coin in a phone booth being far more likely to help — show that small situational features have surprisingly large effects on whether ordinary people help.

(3) I don't think (2) damages virtue ethics much, because virtue ethics never claimed most people are virtuous. Virtues are rare, and these experiments are mostly telling us about how non-virtuous people behave under pressure.

(4) I also hold that virtues, when they do exist, are robust, global dispositions: a kind person is reliably kind across home, work, and with strangers — not just kind-in-one-narrow-setting.

(5) At the same time, I'm drawn to the local traits view — the idea that the best-supported empirical traits are narrow and situation-specific, like "honest-with-friends" rather than "honest" full stop. I take this to be compatible with my virtue ethics.

(6) I think practical wisdom (phronesis) is a stable trait that the virtuous have and most people lack, and that it consists largely in correctly reading situations — which is exactly why the virtuous person's outward behaviour varies a lot by context.

(7) I also think the rightness of an act can't be hostage to silly local features like whether the agent just found a coin. If an act is right, its right because its what a practically wise person would do, not because of incidental situational details.
