I'm not an academic, just someone who reads moral philosophy on my own, and I keep noticing a pattern I don't have a word for. Take the famous story where a man's wife and a stranger are both drowning and he grabs his wife first. When someone asks him to justify that, the standard move is to show that saving your wife is allowed by some general rule, or that it makes things go best overall, so he's not just playing favourites. But there's also this other complaint that runs the other way: that if the man actually had to run that calculation before jumping in, something's already gone wrong. If his wife found out he saved her because the sums came out right rather than just because she's his wife, she'd have a right to be upset. The extra step — checking whether a general rule permits what his feelings already told him to do — seems to change the act itself.

Then there's a nearby point I also keep seeing. A person wants to be a painter, and someone points out that the money and time would do more good spent on bed nets. She's told that if she can't set that aside when the sums come out a certain way, she's being self-indulgent. But the reply is that being able to set it aside so easily would mean it was never really hers — that a life built around things you're always ready to drop isn't a life of your own at all.

Are these two points the same idea wearing different clothes, or are they seperate? Is there an established name or names for them? I'm not after just a label — I want to know exactly what the objection is, what it's not (it's clearly not just 'loyalty is fine' or 'the sums are sometimes wrong'), and where the line between the two falls, if there is one.

Please write the answer in British English.
