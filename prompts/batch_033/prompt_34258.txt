Two people in my philosophy of logic reading group got into a long argument about the problem of logical omniscience, and I'm not sure who's right. Here's roughly how it went.

Maya started by saying she thinks the problem of logical omniscience is underrated. She said it's not just that standard epistemic logic idealizes agents as perfect reasoners — it's that the possible-worlds semantics can't even *represent* the difference between knowing something and knowing something logically equivalent to it. A proposition is a set of worlds, so P and P∧(Q∨¬Q) are literally the same proposition in the model. The model can't distinguish between knowing that it's raining and knowing that it's raining and either it's Tuesday or it's not. So the problem isn't about computational limits; it's about the metaphysics of the model.

Eli pushed back. He said that's the granularity problem, which is a different issue from logical omniscience. Logical omniscience is about closure: that if you know φ and φ entails ψ, you know ψ. That's enforced by the K axiom and the necessitation rule. The granularity problem is about whether propositions are too coarse-grained, which is a problem about hyperintensionality. You can have one without the other. You could have a semantics with fine-grained propositions where closure still holds, or you could have a coarse-grained semantics where closure fails.

Maya said that's too neat. The reason closure holds in the first place is that the semantics identifies logically equivalent propositions. If you think of knowledge as a relation to a set of worlds, then of course it's closed under entailment: if all φ-worlds are ψ-worlds and you've ruled out all non-φ-worlds, you've ruled out all non-ψ-worlds too. The granularity and the closure are two sides of the same coin. You can't fix the closure problem without addressing the granularity, and if you address the granularity you don't automatically get closure anymore.

Eli said that's not right. Even if you keep the coarse-grained semantics, you can break closure by putting extra conditions on the accessibility relation. For instance, you can make the accessibility relation non-transitive, or you can use a neighborhood semantics. The point is that the semantics and the logic are separable. The possible-worlds framework is just a tool; you can make it do what you want by tweaking the parameters.

Maya replied that this just proves her point. If you're going to use neighborhood semantics or break transitivity, you're not really using the possible-worlds framework in its standard form anymore. The elegance of the framework — the reason epistemologists use it — is precisely that knowledge gets a neat Boolean structure. If you break that, you might as well use a syntactic approach or an impossible-worlds framework from the start. The fact that you have to mutilate the framework to avoid logical omniscience shows that the problem is fundamental.

Eli said that's not "mutilating" the framework, it's just using it flexibly. The framework is a modeling tool, not a metaphysical commitment. And anyway, the real problem isn't logical omniscience per se — it's logical *ignorance*. The interesting phenomenon isn't that agents know too much; it's that they fail to know things that are obvious consequences of what they know. That's a psychological phenomenon, and it's unfair to expect a formal model of knowledge to capture it in full detail.

Maya said that's exactly backwards. Logical omniscience is the problem because it's a feature of the model that doesn't correspond to anything in the modeled phenomenon. Logical ignorance is the phenomenon we're trying to capture. The model fails because it makes a false prediction about every actual agent. That's not "unfair" — it's a counterexample.

At this point they were going in circles and I had to leave. Can you go through their exchange and say who's right about which specific points, and whether there's a way to resolve the disagreement that they're both missing?
