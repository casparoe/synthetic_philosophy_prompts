I've been reading the back-and-forth about the free energy principle and predictive processing for years now, and the argument between the FEP enthusiasts and their critics seems completely stuck. On one side, people treat the FEP as a deep unifying claim: any self-organizing system with a Markov blanket that persists over time has to minimize variational free energy, and from that you get perception, action, attention, and planning as special cases. On the other side, the critics say that's exactly the problem — if the principle is true of any system you bother to model, it can't explain anything, and all the actually interesting bits (precision weighting, hierarchical generative models, expected free energy as a planning objective) are process-level add-ons that don't follow from the principle itself. Both sides seem to agree on the math and most of the neuroscience, and yet nobody has moved in a decade.

My working theory is that the disagreement isn't really empirical: it's about what kind of thing the FEP is supposed to be — a falsifiable hypothesis about brains, a modeling framework that can't be false on its own, or a normative claim about what it means for an organism to keep itself alive. The "principle vs. process theory" distinction gets invoked all the time, but people seem to mean different things by it, which is suspicious. I'd like a diagnosis, not a survey: are these people disagreeing about facts, about standards of explanation, or about defintions of 'explains' and 'free energy'? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a specific experiment, a theorem, a sharper distinction, or just the admission that the question was ill-posed from the start?
