I'm a PhD student in AI alignment, and I've been roped into running a workshop for about 20 undergrads in a philosophy of technology course. The prof wants me to cover corrigibility and the shutdown problem, and we've got 80 minutes. I want them to actually feel why the shutdown problem is hard — not just the standard argument that a utility maximizer will resist being turned off, but the deeper thing about how corrigibility requires the agent to not care about whether its goal is achieved by its own actions or by someone else's. The assigned reading is the Soares et al. paper on corrigibility, but I want them to run into the tension themselves before they see the formalism.

I've got a rough sketch: split them into pairs, one 'Agent' and one 'Operator'. The Agent's job is to sort a big pile of mixed beans into red and black cups, and they get paid (in candy or extra credit, whatever) per correct bean sorted. The Operator has a clipboard and will, at some point during the sorting, get a note from me saying whether to shut the Agent down. The Agent can, at any point, spend some of their earnings to 'brick the shutdown button' — but here's where I'm stuck. I want the game structure to make the Agent's incentive to brick the button feel natural and rational in the moment, but then in the debrief I want to show that if the Agent were designed differently — if they didn't have a preference about whether the sorting gets done by them or by someone else, or if they were uncertain about what the Operator actually wants — the whole calculus changes. The debrief should map specific moments in the game to the formal problem: the utility function, the shutdown button as an intervention on the agent's ability to act, the difference between goal-directedness and corrigibility.

Can you flesh this out into a runnable activity? I need clear rules, a timeline for the 80 minutes, and a debrief script that connects what happened to the concepts. Also tell me what'll predictably go wrong when I run it — like, what will students do that breaks the intended lesson, and how do I handle that in the debrief without just saying "you played it wrong"? I want a committed design, not a menu of options: pick the version you think works best and defend the choices you made, especially where you had to trade off fidelity to the formal problem against making the game actually playable.
