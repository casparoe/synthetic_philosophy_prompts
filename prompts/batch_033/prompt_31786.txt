So I'm a software engineer — tech lead at a mid-size company, no formal philosophy background — and I keep getting pulled into these monthly kill-or-keep meetings for projects that are behind schedule and over budget. The official wisdom in the room is always the same: sunk costs are sunk, so ignore them and only look forward. But when I try to actually apply that to a real project its not clean at all. Some of the money we already spent did buy stuff that matters going forward: we learned where the hard parts are, the team built up context on the codebase, customers are expecting the thing because we announced it, and switching to something else has real migration costs of its own. So I can't tell whether I'm being rational or just dressing up throwing good money after bad.

Could you write me a reusable decision procedure for these kill-or-keep calls, grounded in whatever economics or philosophy actually says about the sunk cost fallacy? It should be a checklist I can run in about an hour, with the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where it stops with a kill, where it stops with a keep, and where it should punt to a human because the question is a values call rather than a facts call. I want you to be honest about which steps are judgment calls disguised as procedure, and about whether honoring sunk costs is actually always irrational or whether there are legit cases where past investment should change the decision. Then run it on two or three of these: (a) a two-year internal tool rewrite that's maybe 60–70% done, but a SaaS product now covers 80% of what we needed it for; (b) a customer-facing feature we publicly promised and some customers pre-paid for, now way over budget, and a competitor just shipped something close; (c) a small spike we funded that produced no product but taught us a lot about which approach not to take. Include at least one case where the procedure tells me to kill something the team will hate killing. These decisions are usually a few hundred thousand to a couple million in future spend, a team of five to eight people, the eng manager and a product person have to sign off on the call, and the folks in the room mostly have skin in the game because they've been building the thing.
