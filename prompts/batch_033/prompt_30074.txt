I've been trying to get clear about van Fraassen's Reflection principle — roughly, the idea that you should treat your own future credences the way you'd treat a trusted expert, so your current credence should equal what you expect your future credence to be. I don't want a survey of the literature and I don't need citations; I want you to help me find the principle I actually hold. Here are my gut reactions to some cases.

I'm 0.35 that a biopsy will come back positive. I know that when the result comes in tomorrow I'll be either 0.95 or 0.03, and I don't know which. It feels right that my 0.35 now has to be the average of those two possibilities, weighted by how likely I now think each is. So I defer to post-result me.

I've just spent an hour reviewing a drug interaction and I'm 0.8 that beta blockers are contraindicated in asthma. I also know that in a week I'll have forgotten the mechanism and will be back to 0.5. I don't think I should now drop to 0.5 just because future me will be there. Future me is just less informed.

Before a party I'm 0.6 that the capital of Burkina Faso is Ouagadougou. I know that after two drinks I'll be 0.9 confident about it, because alcohol makes me overconfident about trivia. I don't think I should move to 0.9 now. Drunk me isn't a better reasoner than sober me.

Tonight I'm 0.7 that the chapter I've been writing is good. I know that tomorrow, when I'm hungry, I'll be 0.3 about it, because I get pessimistic when I haven't eaten. I don't think I should split the difference. I know the 0.3 is a hunger effect, not a re-evaluation of the work.

Now the one that trips me up. I'm 0.5 that a certain policy reduces poverty. I know that tomorrow I'm going to read a well-written but one-sided article about it — someone has already warned me it cherry-picks studies — and I know from experience that after reading it I'll be 0.75. The article does contain some real evidence I haven't seen, so future me will be better informed in one respect, but I also know the selection of evidence is biased in a predictable direction. I feel like I should move a little toward 0.75 now, but not all the way. But I can't articulate the rule that says "move a little" here while saying "defer completely" in the biopsy case and "don't move at all" in the forgetting and drunk cases.

Here's another one. I'm 0.4 that a certain number-theoretic conjecture is provable with current methods. I know that after I spend a year actually working on it, I'll be either 0.8 or 0.2. I think I should defer to the future me who has done the work — my current 0.4 should be the expectation of that future credence — even though I can't now predict which way it will go. That feels obviously right in a way the article case doesn't, and I can't say what the difference is.

And a last one, a bit wierd. I'm 0.6 that a friend will keep a promise. I know that in a month I'll have had a falling-out with them, unrelated to the promise, and that I'll then be 0.2 that they'll keep it, mostly because I'll be angry at them. I don't think I should defer to angry future me about the promise. But I notice this is different from the hunger case only in that the emotion is directed at the person, so I'm not sure my principle can handle it without ad hoc clauses.

So: is there one priniciple about when to defer to your future self that gets all of these, or close? State it as precisely as you can, then run it on an extra case I haven't listed: tomorrow I'll receive new evidence about whether a job offer will come through, but I'll also have forgotten one of the pieces of evidence I'm currently relying on, so future me will be better informed in one way and worse in another. What does your principle say there? And which of my verdicts, if any, does it force me to give up? I'd rather have a clean principle that overturns one or two of my gut calls than a gerrymandered one that saves them all, so please be honest about that tradeoff.
