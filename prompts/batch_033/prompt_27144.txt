I'm trying to get clear on what a satisfactory solution to the aggregation problem within a broadly Scanlonian contractualist framework actually has to deliver. Before I evaluate any particular proposal, I want the full specification: what any adequate account of when and why harms may be aggregated must get right.

For each desideratum, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark whether it is non-negotiable (any view that fails it is not a contender) or a preference that some serious positions reject. Flag any pairs you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied.

I want the list to address at least the following pressure points, though you should add any others that belong:

- The canonical cases: the transmitter-room case (one person's severe harm versus arbitrarily many people's very mild inconvenience), the many-versus-one rescue cases (five strangers versus one, each facing the same harm), and the lifetime-wellbeing variants where many tiny burdens across persons sum to more total harm than one large burden on a single person. What must a theory say about each, and why does each case pull in a different direction?

- The same-person / different-person distinction. Contractualism is often said to permit aggregating harms within a single life but to forbid aggregating harms across different lives. Is this a genuine requirement of the view, a useful heuristic that breaks down under pressure, or a mistake that a better theory would abandon?

- The relationship between the individualist restriction — that principles must be justifiable to each person individually — and the conclusion that we should save the greater number. A theory that simply asserts both without showing how the former yields the latter hasn't done the work. What exactly must that derivation accomplish, and what constraints does it place on a candidate solution?

- Whether the theory must deliver determinate verdicts in all pairwise comparisons, or may leave some cases as indeterminate; and if the latter, which kinds of indeterminacy are tolerable and which amount to failure.

- Consistency with the broader contractualist architecture: the reasonable-rejection test, the idea of justifiability to others, and the distinction between substantive and procedural readings of reasonable rejection. If a proposed solution to aggregation requires revising one of these, is that a cost, a benefit, or neutral?

Please separate what is settled among people working on this from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position as the consensus. Where a requirement is itself controversial — where some careful writers deny it is a requirement at all — say so.

At the very end, give a one-sentence statement of the hardest unresolved problem any such theory faces, enclosed as \answer{...}.
