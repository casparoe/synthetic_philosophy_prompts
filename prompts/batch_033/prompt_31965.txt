I am a teaching assistant for an upper-level epistemology course, and the instructor has asked me to grade a midterm question on peer disagreement. The question was worth 20 points. I would like a careful second assessment so that the score and the written comments are both defensible. Below is the exam question exactly as it appeared, followed by the student's answer exactly as submitted.

Exam question (20 points): Explain conciliationism about peer disagreement. Your answer should (i) say what an epistemic peer is; (ii) distinguish conciliationism in general from the Equal Weight View in particular, and illustrate the latter with an example; (iii) explain the main steadfast objection to conciliationism and the conciliationist reply that appeals to the Independence principle; and (iv) discuss the worry that the Equal Weight View is self-undermining, and assess whether that worry is fatal.

Student answer:

Conciliationism is the view that, when you learn that an epistemic peer disagrees with you about some proposition, you should significantly reduce your confidence in your own view, perhaps even suspending judgment. An epistemic peer is someone who is roughly your equal in overall intelligence and who has been exposed to the same evidence as you. The Equal Weight View is the most prominent form of conciliationism; it says that you should give your own judgment and your peer's judgment exactly the same weight. For example, if I am 0.9 confident that P and my peer is 0.1 confident that P, the Equal Weight View requires me to move to 0.5. The main alternative is the steadfast view, which holds that peer disagreement does not by itself require you to revise, because you have direct first-person access to your own reasoning, and your peer's reasoning is not similarly available to you. The conciliationist replies that this is question-begging: whether your reasoning is sound is exactly what the disagreement puts in doubt, so you cannot appeal to your own first-order reasoning to dismiss your peer's judgment. This is the point of the Independence principle, which says that when you evaluate whether your peer is likely to be mistaken on this occasion, you must do so without relying on the very reasoning under dispute. The main problem for the Equal Weight View is that it applies to itself. Suppose you meet someone who is your peer and who rejects the Equal Weight View; the view then tells you to give their judgment equal weight, which forces you to lower your confidence in the Equal Weight View itself. A principle that tells you to lower your confidence in itself cannot be coherent, so conciliationism undermines itself. This leaves the steadfast view as the only viable option.
