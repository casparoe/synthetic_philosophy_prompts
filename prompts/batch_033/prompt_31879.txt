I teach a seminar called "The Moral Imagination" to graduate fiction students, and on the final I set this question:

"Describe the 'torture vs. dust specks' thought experiment and say what it is meant to test about the aggregation of very small harms. Then take and defend a position: is the intuition that no number of dust specks can outweigh a single case of torture defensible, or does it rest on a failure of imagination about very large numbers?"

One of my students submitted this answer:

"The torture vs. dust specks problem, introduced by Derek Parfit, asks whether it is better to torture one innocent person for fifty years or to cause a barely noticeable dust speck in the eye of each of 3^^^3 people. A straightforward act-utilitarian seems committed to choosing the dust specks: if each speck carries even a tiny negative value, then summed across 3^^^3 people, that total will exceed the negative value of fifty years of torture. Yet most people's intuition is that no number of dust specks could outweigh a single case of torture, so the problem is often read as a challenge to the aggregation of small harms.

The right response, I think, is to deny that dust specks and torture are quantities of a single underlying badness. Torture is a qualitatively different kind of harm, not merely a very large amount of what makes a dust speck bad. This is the lexical view: some harms take priority over others regardless of quantity. On this view, harms below a certain level of seriousness do not register in the moral ledger at all, so the dust specks count for nothing and the comparison is settled in favor of avoiding torture.

The only alternative is to bite the utilitarian bullet, which would force us to say that enough stubbed toes outweigh a murder. That seems absurd, so the aggregation principle must be rejected. The problem is therefore best understood as an argument for a lexical or threshold conception of harm."

As the instructor, I want you to grade the answer the way I would: assign a grade and then give a point-by-point assessment. Do not give a general impression; walk through the answer claim by claim, saying what it gets right, what it gets wrong or unsupported, and what it misses. Finally, say where your own grading is most likely to fail: which argument or consideration, had the student included it, would most change your verdict, and which of your specific judgments is shakiest.
