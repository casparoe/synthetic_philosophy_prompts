I've just joined my hospital's committee that reviews determinations of death, and I've been reading back through our recent cases to work out what rule we actually apply, because the members describe it in different ways: some say death is the irreversible loss of all functions of the entire brain, some say it is the irreversible loss of brainstem function, some say it is the irreversible loss of the capacity for consciousness, and some say it is the irreversible cessation of circulation and respiration. Our record includes these cases. We declared death after a patient was found pulseless and apneic and could not be resuscitated despite half an hour of full efforts. We declared death by neurologic criteria in a patient on a ventilator whose pupils were fixed and dilated, who had no corneal, gag, or cough reflexes, no oculocephalic or oculovestibular responses, and no respiratory effort during an apnea test, while his heart was still beating. We refused to declare death in a patient on veno-arterial ECMO whose own heart had stopped and who had no arterial pulsatility, but who was awake and following commands; the note recorded that circulation maintained by a machine still counts as circulation. Now we have a patient with a massive basilar-artery stroke. Every clinical sign of brainstem function is gone, including any respiratory drive, but her EEG shows organized cortical activity, and her temperature regulation and some endocrine function appear preserved. Should we declare her dead if we want to be consistent with our own record? Which of our earlier cases is the closest analog? And if the record underdetermines the answer, what principle would we be adopting with each choice, and what further case would most cleanly settle which rule we are really running? Please answer in continuous prose, as you would write a letter or an email, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
