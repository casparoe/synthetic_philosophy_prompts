Question (from the exam):

"Explain Hume's problem of induction and Goodman's 'new riddle of induction.' Are these two problems fundamentally the same, or does Goodman identify something genuinely new? Defend your answer."

Student's answer:

"Hume's problem of induction is that we have no rational justification for inductive inferences. When we reason from 'all observed Fs have been G' to 'all Fs are G,' we are assuming that the future will resemble the past — but that assumption itself can only be justified inductively, which is circular. So induction cannot be given a non-circular justification.

Goodman's 'new riddle' uses the predicate 'grue,' defined as: an object is grue if it is observed before time t and is green, or is not observed before t and is blue. All emeralds we've observed so far have been green, but they've also been grue — because every emerald observed before t is green, which makes it grue by definition. So the same evidence supports both 'all emeralds are green' and 'all emeralds are grue,' even though these hypotheses make contradictory predictions about emeralds first observed after t. This shows that inductive inference is underdetermined by evidence: the same data can support incompatible generalizations.

Are these the same problem? In one sense yes: both show that induction lacks a rational foundation. Hume shows we can't justify the principle of induction; Goodman shows that even if we accept induction, we can't say which generalizations it licenses. But I think Goodman's problem is genuinely new. Hume's problem is about whether induction works at all; Goodman's is about which inductions are the right ones. Hume asks 'why induce?'; Goodman asks 'what do we induce?' Goodman's riddle survives even if we solve Hume's problem — for instance, even if we adopt a pragmatic justification of induction, we still need a principled way to rule out grue-like predicates.

So my view is that Goodman's problem is distinct from and deeper than Hume's: Hume worried about the justification of induction, but Goodman shows that the very idea of 'projecting' a predicate from observed to unobserved cases is ill-defined until we can say which predicates are projectible — and we can't do that without circularity."
