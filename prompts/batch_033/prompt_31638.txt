I am a novelist at work on a book about a writer who discovers that his characters are aware of being written. The novel's central device is a manuscript that refers to its own composition in ways that destabilize the boundary between fiction and reality, and I have been reading philosophical work on self-reference and paradox to understand the logical structure of what I am trying to depict.

In the course of this research, I encountered Saul Kripke's "Outline of a Theory of Truth," which addresses the Liar paradox by allowing sentences to be neither true nor false — truth-value gaps. The Liar sentence ("This sentence is false") is not assigned a truth value, and the paradox is dissolved because the sentence is not forced into either category.

But I am puzzled by the so-called strengthened Liar. Consider the sentence: "This sentence is not true." If Kripke's theory declares it neither true nor false, then it is not true — which is precisely what the sentence asserts. So it appears to be true after all, and the paradox returns. I have read that Kripke's theory has resources to address this, but I do not see how. If the response is simply that "not true" in the object language differs from "not true" in the metalanguage, this seems like a merely verbal maneuver that does not resolve the underlying conceptual problem. What am I missing?
