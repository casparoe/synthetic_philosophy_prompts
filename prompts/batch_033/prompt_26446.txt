I'm a philosophy professor and I sit on my university's Academic Priorities Committee — it's the group that vets new degree programs before they go to the faculty senate. We've been stuck on this one proposal for three meetings now. It's for an interdisciplinary PhD in "Mind, Language, and Computation," pooling faculty from philosophy, linguistics, and computer science. The proposal document itself is genuinely strong: well-designed coursework, an impressive roster of affiliated faculty with solid publication records, and a unusually detailed placement plan that maps out specific industry and academic pathways. If I were just reading it on its own terms, I'd vote yes without much hesitation.

But one committee member — she's an epidemiologist, not a philosopher — keeps hammering on what she calls the "outside view." She dug up data on every new interdisciplinary PhD program launched at R1 universities in the last fifteen years, and the picture is sobering. Roughly two-thirds never hit their enrollment targets, about half were dissolved or quietly absorbed back into their parent departments within a decade, and even the survivors had placement rates well below the established disciplinary programs they drew from. Her argument is basically: every one of those failed programs had a compelling proposal too, with enthusiastic faculty and a careful design, and the base rates don't care how good this particular one looks to us. Another bloc on the committee thinks she's just dressing up risk-aversion as methodology — that you can't reduce a specific, carefully-crafted proposal to a reference class, and that if we followed her logic we'd never approve anything new at all.

I need to draft the committee's decision memo. It should recommend approve, reject, or approve with conditions (a sunset clause, a review trigger after year three, a reduced initial cohort size — whatever makes sense). The epidemiologist's outside-view argument is the strongest consideration on whatever side I end up recommending against, so the memo has to engage it seriously rather than just dismissing it or changing the subject. Please begin with the memo itself, no preface or commentary.
