I run a small quantitative trading desk at a family office. We take positions across equities, currencies, and volatility products, and we generate our own forecasts — point estimates with confidence intervals, not full probability distributions. My team is numerate but not academic: they can handle a spreadsheet, basic probability, and a bit of algebra, but they are not going to derive anything from scratch.

I have recently been persuaded that maximising expected log wealth — the Kelly criterion and its generalisations — is the right organising principle for position sizing under repeated risk. But the gap between the theory and our actual workflow is wide. The textbook version assumes known probabilities, one bet at a time, and binary outcomes. We face none of those conditions: our edge estimates are noisy, we run fifteen to twenty-five positions at once, and many of our payoffs are continuous.

I need a practical position-sizing procedure my team can run each week, grounded in Kelly principles but adapted to our reality. The procedure should be a sequence of steps: what numbers to extract from our forecasts, how to combine them, what adjustments to apply for estimation error, and how to handle simultaneous positions. It should be honest about which steps are genuine applications of the theory and which are judgment calls dressed up as arithmetic. It should flag the cases where the procedure is likely to misfire — for instance, when payoffs are highly skewed or when we have a strong directional view but no good way to estimate the variance of our edge.

Please run the procedure on three worked examples:

1. A binary event: we give a 65% probability to a merger closing, with a 12% return if it closes and a 20% loss if it fails.
2. A continuous trade: we forecast the euro will appreciate by 2% against the dollar over the quarter, with a standard error of 4% on that forecast.
3. A case where the procedure gives an answer we will not like — for instance, telling us to bet trivially small on a high-conviction forecast because our edge estimate is too noisy, or telling us to bet so large on a moderate-conviction trade that we will struggle to explain it to the principal.

For each case, show the arithmetic and explain what the procedure is doing at each step.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot handle: what kinds of situations in our actual workflow it simply cannot address, and what we would need to add — conceptually or mathematically — to handle them.

Please take the role of an expert on decision theory and the Kelly criterion who also has practical experience implementing these ideas in real trading contexts. Think through the steps carefully, and do not paper over trade-offs. Write the answer in British English.
