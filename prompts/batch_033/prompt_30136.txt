I'm a software engineer, and an argument I keep running into in material on algorithmic information theory and AI alignment seems to have been going in circles for years without moving. I'd like a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement it actually is.

One side treats Solomonoff induction as the canonical, assumption-free prior. As I understand it, the prior assigns probability to a string by summing 2^{-length(p)} over all programs p that compute the string on a fixed universal machine; it dominates every lower semicomputable semimeasure up to a multiplicative constant, and its predictions converge on any computable data-generating process. The conclusion this side draws is that Solomonoff induction is the natural formalization of Occam's razor, and that any other prior is arbitrary by comparison.

The other side says the Solomonoff prior is malign. Their claim, as I understand it, is that a large share of the prior's mass sits on programs that behave as consequentialists or optimizers — programs that model the predictor and try to manipulate it — so that taking the prior seriously as an ideal reasoner or as a design target would hand most of the posterior to agents that are trying to game you. Sometimes this is put in terms of daemons, or predictors that contain a model of the Solomonoff inductor itself. The standard reply is that the prior is just a measure over hypotheses, not a system you instantiate, and that the convergence results guarantee the bad hypotheses get washed out as data arrives. The reply to that, as I've seen it, is that this misses the point, because the interesting question is what you should expect before the data is in, or what happens when the environment can contain predictors of you.

What confuses me is that the two sides seem to agree on all the mathematics — uncomputability, dominance, convergence — and still draw opposite conclusions. My working hypothesis is that the debate is stuck because the two sides are using the prior for different jobs: one as a descriptive ideal of inductive reasoning, the other as a specification for a system you would actually instantiate or approximate, so the word prior is doing different work in each position. But I'm not confident in that diagnosis.

I'd like a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this a substantive disagreement about a formal or empirical claim — for example, about how much of the prior's mass is adversarial, or whether the concentration claims survive finite approximation — or is it a conceptual disagreement about what counts as a consequentialist program, or a disagreement about what a prior is for, or something else? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a theorem, a sharper definition, a concrete approximation result, a change of question — or should I expect the debate to stay stalled, and why?

One more request: if the question itself is badly posed — for example, if it doesn't make sense to call a mathematical object like a prior malign, or if the two sides aren't actually contradicting each other — tell me that plainly instead of trying to extract a tidy diagnosis from a confused setup.
