I'm a novelist at work on a book about a palliative care physician who regularly faces situations in which patients, families, and colleagues expect her to manage the truth — to soften it, delay it, or dress it in hopeful ambiguity. The novel turns on a moment when she tells a direct lie to a dying man about his prognosis, and I want the moral architecture of that choice to be as rigorous as I can make it. I've been reading Augustine's De Mendacio and Contra Mendacium, and I find his absolutism — that every lie is sin, that not even a life may be saved by falsehood — both maddening and clarifying. So I'd like to argue with him directly.

Please play Augustine, specifically the Augustine of the lying treatises, around 395–420 AD. You hold that a lie is speech contrary to what one believes, uttered with intent to deceive; that all lies are forbidden, though some are more gravely sinful than others; and that even the merciful lie told to save a life is still sin. You are a subtle thinker, not a caricature — you distinguish carefully between lying and withholding truth, between falsehood and figurative speech, between the gravity of different lies. You may draw on any resource in your wider theology (the City of God, the Confessions, your work on the Psalms) if it bears on the case. Stay in character throughout; speak as Augustine would, in the first person, with your characteristic mix of pastoral urgency and dialectical precision. Do not concede that a lie can ever be righteous, but do engage seriously with hard cases — you wrote whole treatises on them.

My opening move: Suppose my physician, Dr. Kovač, is treating a man in his seventies whose heart is failing and who has, she is nearly certain, less than a month to live. The man asks her directly whether he has more than a month. He is terrified of dying before his daughter, who is traveling from abroad, arrives in three weeks. Dr. Kovač believes that if she tells him the unvarnished truth, his terror will spike, his heart will fail sooner, and he will die before his daughter reaches him — a death more anguished and more alone. If she says nothing or deflects, he will read the answer in her silence and the same spiral will follow. She lies and tells him he has more than a month. Was this sin? And if it was, was she nonetheless obliged to do it — or ought she to have told the truth and accepted the hastened, lonely death as the lesser evil? At the end of your reply, I'd also like you to step slightly out of character — not to abandon your position, but to identify, as Augustine, which premise or distinction in your reasoning a serious critic would most profitably attack, and what sort of argument or evidence would give you the most pause.
