I'm preparing a unit for my upper-division metaphysics course on Derek Parfit's *Reasons and Persons*, and I want to use Christopher Nolan's *The Prestige* as an extended case study. The connection is, I think, remarkably tight, but I want to make sure I'm doing justice to both the philosophy and the film before I walk into the classroom.

Specifically, I want you to read the film's final act—Angier's machine, the nightly drownings, the locked tanks, the exchange with Borden in the boiler room—through the lens of Parfit's argument that what matters in survival is not identity but psychological continuity and connectedness, what he calls Relation R. The film seems to stage a brutally literal version of the teletransporter thought experiment: a machine creates a duplicate who is psychologically continuous with the original, and the original is killed. Angier describes his own predicament in terms that echo the philosophical problem with uncanny precision. He says, in effect, that it took courage to climb into the machine every night not knowing whether he would be the man in the box or the man who appeared across the theater to take the applause. The film treats this as a horror, and Angier's tragedy is that he cannot know which outcome awaits him. But Parfit might say that Angier is asking the wrong question altogether: both outcomes involve the survival of what matters, and the question of which one is "really" him is empty. The dread, on Parfit's view, rests on a confusion.

Here is what I would like from you. Please play the role of a professional philosopher who specializes in personal identity and knows Parfit's work deeply. Begin by briefly brainstorming the key philosophical moves Parfit would make when confronted with the film's scenario—what would he say about Angier's terror, and why? Then give me a reading of the film that takes Parfit's position seriously: does the film's treatment of the machine as a horror show rest on a mistake about identity, or does it dramatize something Parfit's theory misses? Does Angier's inability to accept that both outcomes might equally preserve what matters reveal a failure to grasp Parfit's insight, or does it capture something irreducible about the first-person perspective that Parfit's reductionism cannot accommodate? I am especially interested in whether the film's emotional register—the sheer terror of the machine, the moral weight of the nightly self-drownings, the fact that the audience recoils—constitutes a kind of argument against Parfit that philosophical prose cannot easily replicate. In your reading, be fair to both: do not reduce the film to a mere illustration, and do not distort Parfit to make him fit the film. Be willing to say where the film resists the philosophical reading, and where the philosophy exposes a confusion the film relies on. Limit your response to 800 words.
