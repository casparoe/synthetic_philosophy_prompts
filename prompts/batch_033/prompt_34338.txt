I'm a journalist who covers economic policy and regulatory debates, and I keep encountering philosophers — especially in discussions of healthcare rationing, environmental risk, and the value of statistical life — who invoke T.M. Scanlon's contractualism as a serious challenge to the way cost-benefit analysis handles tradeoffs between concentrated harms and diffuse benefits. I have a working grasp of the central idea: Scanlon's "individualist restriction" says, roughly, that when we ask whether a principle could be reasonably rejected, we compare the strongest individual complaint against the strongest individual complaint on the other side — we don't simply add up many small complaints and weigh the sum against a single large one. That means you can't justify imposing a grave harm on one person by pointing out that a very large number of others will each suffer a tiny inconvenience if you don't. This runs directly counter to the aggregative logic of CBA, where enough pennies of willingness-to-pay can in principle outweigh a life.

What I'd like you to do is restate this debate — Scanlon's case for the individualist restriction and the core objections to it (the "aggregation problem" as it cuts against his view) — entirely in the vocabulary I use every day: willingness-to-pay, compensating variation, Kaldor-Hicks efficiency, distributional weights, social welfare functions, the value of a statistical life, and so on. Treat it as a full translation exercise: map each philosophical claim onto the closest economic concept you can find, and flag where the fit is imperfect. Then give me the residue report. Which parts of Scanlon's argument translate cleanly into the cost-benefit framework — for instance, does the individualist restriction simply amount to an extreme form of inequality aversion built into the social welfare function, or is it something else? Which parts require serious distortion to fit, and which have no counterpart at all in the economist's toolkit?
