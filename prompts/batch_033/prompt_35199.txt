I've been arguing with a colleague about how to build an anomaly detector for a small neural circuit that generates a rhythmic motor pattern, and we keep getting stuck before we even write any code. The question is what "same mechanism" is supposed to mean when we say two anomalous episodes are "the same mechanism misbehaving" rather than "two different mechanisms that happen to produce the same weird output." We've got a pile of candidate criteria for mechanism identity:

(a) same physical parts in the same spatiotemporal organization, so swapping in a different neuron makes it a different mechanism;
(b) same stable input–output profile, so if it does the same job it's the same mechanism;
(c) same causal structure that stays invariant under interventions, whatever the parts happen to be made of;
(d) same computational stages or algorithm, abstracting away from the hardware;
(e) same etiological function, i.e., the mechanism is whatever was selected or maintained to do that job;
(f) same failure modes, so mechanisms are individuated by how and when they break.

Could you rank these from best to worst as a criterion for our anomaly detector, with a sentence or two of reasoning for each? And where would you draw the line—below which rank would a criteria be actively misleading? The two cases that keep tripping us up are (i) a lesion silences one pathway and a redundant pathway takes over, restoring the same behavior, and (ii) two individuals show the same behavioral anomaly but have damage in different regions. I'd also like to know whether you think any one of these criteria, or some combination, handles both cases, or whether "same mechanism" is just doing too much work here and we should abandon it for something finer-grained.
