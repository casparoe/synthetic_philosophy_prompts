I'm a software engineer, and I keep running into Monte Carlo simulations where the sample mean never settles down. I'd like to know what principle, if any, captures my gut verdicts on these gambles. In each, a fair coin is flipped until it first lands heads; if that first heads is on flip n, you receive the stated payoff.

1. St. Petersburg game: payoff $2^n. I'd pay at most about $25 to play, and I don't think that's a mistake even though the expected value is infinite.
2. Pasadena game: payoff $(-1)^{n+1} 2^n / n, so the probability-weighted terms are 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + …, which converges to about 0.69 only conditionally; I gather the expectation itself is undefined. I wouldn't pay even $1 to play.
3. The same game with the signs flipped: payoff $(-1)^n 2^n / n, so the probability-weighted terms sum to about −0.69 in the given order, again only conditionally. I also wouldn't pay $1 to avoid it.
4. Any of these games with all payoffs capped at $10 million: I'll happily price it by its ordinary finite expected value.
5. Two independent copies of the Pasadena game bundled together: I still refuse to put a price on the bundle.

Is there one principle about when an expected value may guide a price that gets all of these, or close? What does it say about a game with no sign alternation and payoff $2^n / n, and about a variant whose probability-weighted terms are the same as the Pasadena game's but rearranged so their partial sums tend to +100? Which of my verdicts, if any, does that principle force me to drop? Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and don't present a contested view as the consensus.
