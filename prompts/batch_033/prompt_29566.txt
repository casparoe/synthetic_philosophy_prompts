Something's been bugging me since I had knee surgery a couple months ago. The hospital made a big deal of the 0-to-10 pain scale — every few hours a nurse would ask my number and type it into the chart like it was a lab result. But when it came to actually doing anything about the pain, the number only seemed to count if it came with visible backup: wincing, a high heart rate, swelling, something on the monitor. If I was just lying there still and said I was at an 8, it got charted and then nothing happened; the guy in the next bed moaning at a 6 got meds right away. I'm a Black woman, and I couldn't shake the feeling that who got believed wasn't only about the moaning — the older white man across the hall said 5 and got something, and another Black woman in the waiting room told me she'd learned she has to perform her pain or nobody acts on it. So the hospital treats a patient's self-report as important enough to record but not important enough to act on unless its confirmed from the outside, and that outside confirmation seems to be required more from some people than others. What view of evidence, or of knowledge, does that practice presuppose? Does it presuppose that pain is the kind of thing other people can know better from the outside than the person feeling it can from the inside, or that some people's reports just count for less? Is the practice even coherent — why ask for a number if you're going to override it? And what would have to change in how the place runs if they treated the patient's report as authoritative rather than just a clue? When you answer, could you separate what's actually settled among people who work on this from what's genuinely contested — I don't want a contested view passed off as the consensus.
