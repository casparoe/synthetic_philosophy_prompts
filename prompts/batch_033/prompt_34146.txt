I am teaching an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the philosophy of death, and I want to replace the usual "summarize the debate" paper with a diagnostic exercise that forces students to take positions on cases before they learn the labels. Please write a decision tree that takes a student from a single opening question to a named position on whether death is bad for the person who dies. Build the tree from the logical space of possible answers rather than from a list of names: first articulate the separating questions, then locate the actual positions within that space, and note any combinations of answers that no one seems to occupy, with a one-sentence diagnosis of whether each unoccupied combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Every branch node should be phrased as a question a student could answer on the basis of a concrete case or a plain claim, without already knowing which position it leads to; position labels should appear only at the leaves. Each leaf should carry the position's name and one sentence stating its most awkward commitment.

The leaves should include at least these positions: the Epicurean view that death is not bad for the one who dies; the deprivationist view that death is bad because it takes away goods the person would otherwise have had; a desire-satisfaction version of the view that death is bad insofar as it frustrates a person's actual desires or projects; and a view on which death is bad only when it cuts short a life that would have been worth continuing. Use the following cases among the diagnostic questions: prenatal nonexistence (the Lucretian asymmetry), a period of dreamless unconsciousness, cryonic suspension with the prospect of revival, posthumous destruction of one's reputation, and a death that occurs just before a cure that would have restored many good years. Keep the instrument to roughly seven to nine branch nodes and six to eight leaves. Flag explicitly any positions the tree cannot separate from one another, and state what further question would separate them.

The students will have encountered only the Epicurean arguments and a short statement of the deprivationist reply, so the questions must not presuppose familiarity with the wider technical literature; the point is to sort readers by how they respond to cases, not by which label they happen to recognize.
