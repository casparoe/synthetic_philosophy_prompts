I'm the president of my school's Philosophy and Politics Club, and in two weeks I'm giving a twenty-minute talk at a joint meeting with the Math Club. My thesis is that the Condorcet jury theorem is not just a formal curiosity but provides a genuine, non-question-begging justification for certain democratic institutions — specifically, that it gives us reason to trust majority verdicts in jury trials and in referendums on propositions where there is a fact of the matter, even once we account for the theorem's restrictive assumptions.

The audience I'm worried about is roughly thirty students, mostly juniors and seniors, with a few distinct factions. About a third are Math Club regulars who know probability and will jump on any technical sloppiness; they've done combinatorics and basic probability theory but not formal social choice theory. Another third are debaters from the Policy Debate team who are steeped in arguments about voting paradoxes, agenda-setting, and strategic behavior — they've read enough about Arrow's theorem and the impossibility results to be reflexively skeptical of any formal argument that makes democracy look good. The remainder are general members who've read a bit of political philosophy (Rousseau, Mill, the Federalist Papers) and are sympathetic to democracy but haven't seen the technical case against it. Our faculty advisor is a European history teacher who thinks political institutions are mostly path-dependent historical accidents and will push back on any claim that a theorem can justify a real institution. I expect a Q&A session of about fifteen minutes.

I know my own argument well enough, but I'm drafting a Q&A prep sheet and I need to anticipate what this particular audience will throw at me. I want the objections ordered by how much damage they could do to my thesis — not just the ones that are technically correct, but the ones that would actually land with this room. Please phrase each objection the way a member of this audience would actually raise it, give me a two-minute reply, and flag the weakest point in that reply. I also want to know the question I should be praying nobody asks — the one where my reply would sound evasive or rely on a move this audience won't buy. I can supply a sketch of my argument if that helps, but I'd rather first see what objections arise just from the thesis itself, since that's the version most of the audience will be reacting to.
