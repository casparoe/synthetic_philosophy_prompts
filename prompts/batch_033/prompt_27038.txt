Here's roughly the exchange as I remember it.

Mina said the whole framework is broken because it makes every agent logically omniscient. If you know the axioms of Peano arithmetic, then in the semantics you automatically know every theorem that follows from them, even ones nobody has ever proved. She said that's not a theory of knowledge, it's a theory of a god.

Sam replied that this is just an idealization. The logic is supposed to capture the structure of knowledge for a perfectly rational agent, not the psychology of actual humans. Every model idealizes, he said, so pointing out that real people aren't logically omniscient isn't an objection.

Mina came back that idealizations are supposed to be harmless simplifications you can relax and still learn something about the real target. Here, she said, the idealization removes the very thing that makes knowledge interesting: that reasoning takes time, can fail, and that working something out is an achievement. If no finite agent could even in principle satisfy the model, then it's not an ideal we can approach; it's a fantasy.

Sam said finite agents can't be perfectly consistent either, but we still use consistency as a normative ideal. And closure under known entailment, he said, is a real normative requirement: if you know P and you know that P entails Q, then you're committed to Q even if you havent noticed it yet.

Mina answered that being committed to Q isn't the same as knowing Q. And the model can't even represent the difference between someone who has the premises and is still working through the inference; it collapses having the premises into having the conclusion. So a logic of commitment might be fine, but it shouldn't call itself a logic of knowledge.

Sam replied that the bare model is just a static snapshot, not a theory of reasoning over time, and anyway the framework can be extended with awareness of sentences or impossible worlds to represent agents who haven't put things together. So the framework isn't refuted just because the simplest version is too strong.

Mina said those patches just move the problem around. If you add awareness, the agent becomes omniscient about everything they're aware of, and what determines awareness is left unexplained. If you add impossible worlds, you get baroque machinery with no principled answer about which closure principles actually hold. She said it felt like epicycles.

Sam said ad hoc isn't automatically bad; you patch a model where it leaks. But maybe the deeper point is that we should read the K operator normatively, as something like the agent is in a position to know, which is useful for epistemology even if it doesn't describe occurrent psychology.

Mina said that just relocates the problem: a real agent isn't in a position to know every theorem either, unless you give an account of being in a position to know that doesn't itself smuggle in omniscience.
