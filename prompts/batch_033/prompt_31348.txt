I have been trying to work out what I actually believe about Berkeley's thesis that nothing can exist unperceived, and my case-by-case reactions have settled into a pattern that I cannot yet state as a principle. I find it useful to keep two questions separate for each case: whether the thing exists when unperceived, and whether I can conceive of it as existing unperceived. Here are my verdicts.

A tree in a forest with no perceiver anywhere near it: it exists, and I can conceive of it existing unperceived. A mountain on the far side of the Moon in, say, 1700, when no human being had ever observed it: it exists, and I can conceive of it unperceived. A pain that no one is feeling: it does not exist, and I cannot conceive of an unfelt pain. A sound that no one hears: it does not exist, and I cannot conceive of an unheard sound, though I do think the compression waves in the air that produce the sound exist unperceived. A dream that no one is dreaming: it does not exist, and I cannot conceive of an undreamt dream. An electron in an unobserved region of a cloud chamber: it exists, and I can conceive of it unperceived. The redness of a rose in a dark and empty room: the seen redness does not exist, and I cannot conceive of it unperceived, while the surface structure of the rose that causes that redness does exist, and I can conceive of it unperceived. A number that no one has ever thought of: it exists, and I do not think it needs a mind, though 'perceived' seems the wrong word for numbers.

I am also drawn to the master-argument line of thought I associate with Berkeley, which I would put roughly this way: I can never genuinely conceive of a thing as existing wholly unperceived, because the act of conceiving it brings it before my mind; the most I ever manage is to conceive of a thing as unperceived by others but perceived by me. That thought seems to flatly contradict my verdicts about the tree, the mountain, and the electron, and it makes me suspect that my verdicts are not a coherent set at all.

I would like you to do three things. First, state the principle, or small set of principles, that best captures my verdicts, and identify which of my verdicts, if any, it forces me to abandon. Second, say whether the master-argument thought actually commits me to the strong conclusion that the tree and the electron exist only when perceived, or whether there is a defensible reading on which it shows something weaker and leaves my verdicts standing. Third, apply the position you settle on to a case I have not listed: a bird's alarm call in an empty forest, taking the acoustic waves and the heard call separately, and then to a universe with no minds at all in which a tree grows.
