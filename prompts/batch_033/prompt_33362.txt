"The three leading accounts of the norm of assertion are the knowledge account, the truth account, and the belief account. The knowledge account, most prominently defended by Timothy Williamson, holds that one should assert p only if one knows p. The truth account, associated with Matthew Weiner, holds that one should assert p only if p is true. The belief account holds that one should assert p only if one believes p.

Moore-paradoxical sentences come in two varieties: the omissive form ('p, but I don't believe that p') and the commissive form ('p, but I believe that not-p'). These sentences are not logically contradictory—both conjuncts can be true—yet they are clearly defective to assert. A good theory of assertion should explain why.

Williamson argues that the knowledge account best explains the data. Consider the sentence 'p, but I don't know that p.' If the knowledge account is correct, this sentence is always improper to assert: the first conjunct's assertion is governed by the knowledge norm, so in asserting p you represent yourself as knowing p, but the second conjunct explicitly denies that you know p. This is a pragmatic contradiction. The truth account cannot explain why 'p, but I don't know that p' is defective, because both conjuncts could be true. The belief account fares no better: you can believe p without knowing p, so the belief account doesn't rule out asserting 'p, but I don't know that p.'

Despite this advantage, the knowledge account faces a serious problem: it is too demanding. If the norm requires knowledge, then many ordinary assertions turn out to be improper. Suppose I assert that my keys are on the kitchen table because I clearly remember leaving them there, but unbeknownst to me someone has moved them. My assertion is false, so I don't know it, and the knowledge account deems it improper. But it seems like a perfectly reasonable assertion to make. The truth account handles this case better, since it imposes no epistemic requirement beyond truth. The belief account also does better, since I did believe my keys were on the table.

The truth account, however, has its own problem: it is too weak. It permits lucky guesses. If I guess that a fair coin will land heads, and it does, the truth account says my assertion was proper, but that seems wrong. The belief account rules this out, since I didn't believe the coin would land heads—I was merely guessing.

I therefore favor a version of the belief account supplemented with a justification requirement: one should assert p only if one has a justified belief that p. This explains the Moore-paradoxical data (asserting 'p, but I don't believe that p' is improper because the assertion expresses belief in p, contradicting the second conjunct), avoids the demandingness objection to the knowledge account, and rules out lucky guesses. On balance, this is the strongest position."
