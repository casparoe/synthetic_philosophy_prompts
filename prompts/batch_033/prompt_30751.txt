I host a small philosophy podcast and next weeks guest is a philosopher known for defending the view that moral luck is an illusion: he thinks luck of any kind should make no difference to how much we blame or praise people, and that our ordinary practice of blaming people more when things turn out badly is a mistake to be revised, not something to be explained away. I dont want eight to ten questions that just let him restate the view. I want questions that press on where it strains. Specifically, at least one question on each of the following: (1) whether resultant luck can be salvaged as merely epistemic — a bad outcome is just evidence of a bad character — and whether that actually saves the practice or changes the subject; (2) whether he can keep agent-regret and apology while denying resultant luck, and if not, what those practices become for him; (3) constitutive luck, and whether his demand for control would end up eliminating moral evaluation of persons altogether; (4) circumstantial luck, and whether there is any real moral difference between what someone did and what they would have done. For each question, give a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give. Structure the response in three sections: "Opening questions" (two questions, 1–2 sentences each), "Pressure questions" (five questions, each 2–4 sentences, each with a follow-up), and "What he has to give up" (two or three questions, each with a follow-up, asking which practices and reactive attitudes he is willing to abandon).
