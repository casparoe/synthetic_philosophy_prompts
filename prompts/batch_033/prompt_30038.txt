I'm a doctoral student in political philosophy preparing a dissertation prospectus, and I want to pitch a chapter — or possibly the whole dissertation — on the analogy between Ulysses contracts and constitutional precommitment. I got into this after a long argument with my advisor, who thinks the Ulysses analogy is basically sound and that my worries about it are just the standard countermajoritarian difficulty in new packaging. But I think there's something genuinely wrong with the analogy that the literature hasn't fully pinned down. The standard move, going back to Jon Elster and running through the constitutional theory debates of the last few decades, treats the individual case — Ulysses binding himself to the mast to resist the Sirens — as the normative template: just as Ulysses protects his own considered preferences against foreseeable weakness of will, a constitution protects a polity's considered commitments against foreseeable majoritarian panic or myopia. But the more I read, the more I suspect the analogy does rhetorical work that outruns its argumentative force. In the individual case, there is a single agent whose identity persists across time, whose "deep" and "surface" preferences can be distinguished without begging any political questions, and who can authorize the binding in a reasonably unproblematic way. In the collective case, you have multiple agents with conflicting preferences, no uncontroversial way to distinguish deep from surface collective preferences (is the "deep" preference what a supermajority wants? what the framers wanted? what rational deliberation would yield?), and a serious problem about whether a past majority has the standing to bind a present majority at all. And even in the individual case, the justification for precommitment depends on the agent's own prior authorization — Ulysses chooses the ropes — whereas constitutional framers are not authorized by the future majorities they constrain in anything like the same way.

I'd like you to turn this half-formed worry into a tight, one-page research proposal suitable for a dissertation fellowship application. The proposal should state the question precisely: something like "Does the Ulysses-contract analogy justify constitutional entrenchment, or does it merely redescribe the phenomenon it purports to justify, and if the latter, what normative work remains for it?" It should explain why existing treatments leave the question open — I know there's a large literature spanning Elster, Holmes, Sunstein, Waldron, and others, but I need the proposal to identify the specific gap my project would fill rather than just summarizing the debate. It should lay out the method: what kind of philosophical work I'd actually be doing (conceptual analysis of the precommitment concept? case-based argument drawing out disanalogies? normative reconstruction of what a defensible version of the analogy would require?), and why that method rather than, say, a historical or empirical approach. It should give a realistic timeline for a one-year project, with milestones. And crucially, it should specify what would count as the project failing — not just "I didn't finish," but what finding or impasse would show that the question was badly posed or the approach was wrong. Don't worry about citations; I'll add the references myself. What I need is the conceptual architecture — the framing that makes a committee say "this question is worth a year of someone's life."
