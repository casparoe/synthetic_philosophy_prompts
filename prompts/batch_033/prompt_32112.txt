I work on a backend team, and one of the senior engineers, Dana, sort of runs our incident postmortems. She's become the de facto judge of what 'caused' an outage, and she's wierdly consistent about it. I've been keeping a list of her calls, because I can't figure out what rule she's actually running. Here's what she's said over the past year:

- In March a config change set a timeout to 10ms instead of 10s, and the API fell over. Nothing else was weird that day. Dana said the config change caused the outage.

- In May a new query parser shipped right around the same time as a huge traffic spike, and search latency blew past our SLO. The postmortem showed the old parser would also have blown the SLO under that traffic. Dana said the traffic spike caused it, and the parser didn't.

- In July a bad deploy took down checkout, and it turned out our alerting was misconfigured so nobody got paged for 40 minutes. Dana said the bad deploy caused the outage; the alerting misconfiguration caused the slow response but not the outage itself.

- In August a feature flag got flipped on and error rates went up, but the investigation showed the errors were actually from a background job whose TLS certificate had expired around the same time. Dana said the cert expiry caused the errors, and the feature flag didn't.

Now we've got a new one to postmortem. Two independant changes went out in the same deploy: a database migration with a bad schema, and a caching config that pointed at the wrong cluster. Either one on its own would have taken down the API; they both shipped together. I want to know what Dana's going to say. Would she call both of them causes, neither of them (since rolling back either one alone wouldn't have saved us), or something else? Honestly I care more about the rule than about this one outage. What rule is she running, what other rule fits her past calls just as well, and is there a case I could bring up that would settle which one it is?
