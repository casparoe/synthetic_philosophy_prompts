I work in a field in which null-hypothesis significance testing is the default language of evidence, and I have watched the frequentist/Bayesian dispute run for decades without resolution. What frustrates me is not that the two sides disagree, but that each side appears to know the other's arguments and no one moves. The frequentist side defends significance testing as a set of procedures with controlled long-run error rates and treats the replication crisis as a problem of misuse: p-hacking, optional stopping, HARKing, low power, and a publication culture that treats p < .05 as a discovery. The Bayesian side replies that the crisis is the predictable consequence of quantifying evidence with p-values, which condition on outcomes never observed, depend on the stopping rule, and cannot directly weigh a null against a substantive alternative. Yet Bayesian practice has its own familiar embarrassments: priors can be chosen to produce a desired posterior, models are misspecified, and the result is only as good as the prior and likelihood. Both sides know all of this, and neither moves.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is at bottom about the meaning of probability — long-run frequency versus degree of rational credence — and that the two camps are largely answering different questions: one about error control across hypothetical repetitions, the other about how a rational agent should update beliefs given the data actually seen. But I am not confident: it might instead be a substantive disagreement about which risks matter more, or about whether inference should be a decision procedure or a representation of evidence.

Please diagnose the disagreement rather than adjudicate it. Is it verbal, substantive, a hidden empirical or formal question, two different questions under one heading, or a sign that the question is ill-posed? Anchor the diagnosis in what each side actually argues, and then say what would count as progress given that diagnosis — a theorem, a distinction, a new kind of data, a change of question — or whether I should stop expecting progress and why. I want a committed diagnosis: take a definite position on what kind of disagreement this is and defend it, rather than laying out the possibilities and leaving me to choose.
