I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, but I got talked into TAing a philosophy of language course because the professor liked the dialogue in my last book and figures I have an ear for subtext. The midterm had this question:

"Using Grice's distinction between sentence meaning and speaker meaning, explain what a conversational implicature is, and walk through one example showing how a hearer would calculate it."

One student wrote:

"Sentence meaning is what the words themselves mean by the conventions of the language, while speaker meaning is what the speaker actually intends to get across. Grice uses 'conversational implicature' to name the gap between the two. For example, if A asks 'Did you like the play?' and B replies 'The costumes were stunning,' the sentence meaning is just about the costumes. But B's speaker meaning includes that she did not like the play. The hearer calculates this because he assumes B is following the Cooperative Principle and the maxim of Relation; since B only praises the costumes, the hearer infers that B is avoiding a negative verdict, so the implicature is that she didn't like it. This is a conversational implicature rather than a conventional one because it can be cancelled: B could have added 'but I actually loved the play.'"

I have to give it a mark out of 10 plus writen comments. I'd rather not grade on vibe. What grade would you give it, and what specific comments would you write to justify that grade?
