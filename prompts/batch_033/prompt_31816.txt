I'm trying to work through F.H. Bradley's Appearance and Reality and got stuck on a passage from the chapter on Relation and Quality. He's basically arguing that if you think of an object as just a bunch of qualities held together by a relation, you run into trouble. The bit I'm stuck on goes something like this (I'm paraphrasing from memory):

Take a simple fact like sugar being sweet. We have a quality, sweetness, and we have something else—the sugar, or perhaps another quality like whiteness—that it's supposed to be connected to. But how are they connected? If we say there's a relation R that links them, then we have to ask: how does R itself hook onto the sweetness and the sugar? It seems like we'd need another relation to do that linking, and then another one for that link, and so on forever. Bradley says that if the relation is something distinct from the qualities, it can't actually do the job of relating them without starting an infinite regress. But if the relation is nothing apart from the qualities, then it's not really a relation at all, and we're left with a heap of qualities that aren't unified.

I get the basic structure of the regress, but I'm confused about what exactly Bradley thinks the regress shows. Is he saying that relations are impossible, full stop? Or is he making a narrower point about how we can't explain the unity of a thing by appealing to a relation if we think of that relation as a seperate ingredient? Like, maybe the problem is specifically with treating relations as entities, and a different view of relations could avoid the regress? I'd appreciate it if someone could walk me through what the argument is actually supposed to prove.
