I host a small philosophy podcast, and next week's guest is a moral philosopher who defends a broadly Taurekian view of rescue cases. On his view, if you are the only person who can save either one stranger or five different strangers, the fact that there are five on one side and one on the other does not by itself give you a stronger reason to save the five; numbers are not what matters, and it can be permissible to save the one or to give each person an equal chance. I have already read the familiar exchange in which critics call the view innumerate and defenders reply that the critics are quietly assuming the very kind of aggregation that is in question, and I do not want the interview to replay that exchange. I want eight to ten questions that press the view where the literature actually presses it, and no generic questions about how he came to philosophy. In particular, include at least one question each on: (1) the verdicts the view yields when the numbers become very large, or when the choice is between one serious harm and very many trivial ones, such as preventing one person from losing an arm versus preventing a very large number of people from each suffering a brief headache; (2) the individualist rationale for comparing losses pairwise rather than summing them across persons; (3) whether giving each person an equal chance is required, merely permitted, or something else; and (4) how the view responds to a Scanlonian critic who tries to justify saving the greater number through a principle no one could reasonably reject, without summing the losses. For each question, include a follow-up keyed to the reply he is most likely to give, the kind of follow-up that keeps him from deflecting with a restatement of the view.

If you think any of my framing assumptions misdescribes the Taurekian position, for example if the equal-chances question or the contractualist contrast does not cut the way I have suggested, say so plainly and rewrite the question instead of quietly writing around the problem. I would rather have seven well-aimed questions than ten that assume a straw man. Keep the entire set under 1,200 words, and format it as a numbered list with each question followed by its follow-up.
