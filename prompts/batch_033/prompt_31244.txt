A friend of mine has thought carefully about consent for years, and she has arrived at firm verdicts on a range of cases. Here they are:

1. A surgeon falsely tells a patient that a procedure is medically urgent when it is in fact elective; the patient consents. Verdict: consent invalid.
2. A person threatens to disseminate private photographs unless an ex-partner agrees to meet; the ex-partner agrees. Verdict: consent invalid.
3. An individual with advanced dementia agrees to revise their will in favor of a recent acquaintance. Verdict: consent invalid.
4. A psychology study accurately describes all procedures and risks to participants but conceals the specific hypothesis being tested; a participant agrees to enroll. Verdict: consent valid.
5. A manager tells an employee, "The team would really appreciate it if you worked this weekend," attaching no threat or promise; the employee agrees. Verdict: consent valid.
6. A physician truthfully informs a patient that without surgery there is a roughly 30% chance of death within the year; the patient, though frightened, agrees to the surgery. Verdict: consent valid.
7. A sixteen-year-old who demonstrably understands the risks and benefits agrees to take part in a low-risk psychology study, but the legal age of majority in the jurisdiction is eighteen. Verdict: consent invalid.
8. A used-car dealer does not mention that the previous owner smoked in the vehicle; there are no mechanical issues and the buyer does not ask. The buyer agrees to the purchase. Verdict: consent valid.

She is now considering a new case. One romantic partner says to the other, "If you don't stop seeing that friend, I don't know whether I can stay in this relationship." The speaker means it sincerely — they are genuinely uncertain about the relationship's future and are not bluffing. The other partner, who is a competent adult with no special vulnerabilities, agrees to cut off contact with the friend.

I would like you to do three things. First, identify the most plausible rules or principles that would generate her existing eight verdicts — there should be more than one defensible candidate, and I want you to articulate each one clearly enough that its logic can be applied to new cases. Second, say what each candidate rule predicts about the new case and which prediction you think is the better fit, with your reasons for preferring it. Third, propose a further case — real or constructed — that would most cleanly discriminate among the candidate rules you have identified, and explain why it would do so.

Throughout, please separate what is settled among people who work on the ethics of consent from what is genuinely contested, and avoid presenting a contested position as though it were the consensus view.
