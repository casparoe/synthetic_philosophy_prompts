Build me a Frankfurt-style case for a class handout on moral responsibility where the counterfactual intervener is just a colleague watching for an ordinary, involuntary tell — a blush, a microexpression, a catch in the voice — not a neuroscientist with a brain-reading device. Constraints: realistic, office or family setting, no electrodes, no demons, no perfect predictors, one paragraph for the scenario, and the agent has to do the deed on his own for his own reasons; the intervener never actually steps in. The setup should still make it plausible that the agent couldn't have done otherwise, and the tell should be chosen so the usual flicker-of-freedom reply (that he could have done otherwise in some respect, even if not the main action) doesn't get an easy foothold. After the scenario, run the standard Frankfurt argument: what the agent did, why he had no alternative, why he's still responsible. Then give the strongest reply a defender of the principle of alternative possibilities would actually make to your case — flicker-of-freedom, or denying that a merely counterfactual intervener removes alternatives — and say which feature of the case does the work. As you go, seperate what's settled from what's genuinely contested; don't present the success of the case as consensus.
