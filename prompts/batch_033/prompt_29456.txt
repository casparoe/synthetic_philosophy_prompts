And here's the student's answer (I've typed it out):

"Nørretranders' user illusion theory says that consciousness is like a computer desktop interface — it presents a simplified, user-friendly representation of reality that hides the massive complexity of unconscious neural processing underneath. Just as we drag file icons without needing to know about memory addresses and disk sectors, we experience a streamlined conscious world without access to the neural computations producing it. The key claim is that what we consciously experience is not the raw processing itself but a constructed 'interface' that lets us navigate the world efficiently.

Dennett adopts a very similar view in Consciousness Explained, where he argues against the Cartesian Theater — the idea that there's a single place in the brain where consciousness all comes together. Instead Dennett proposes his multiple drafts model, which is essentially the user illusion idea applied more systematically: there's no final 'show' of consciousness, just parallel streams of processing, and what we call conscious experience is a narrative we construct after the fact.

The epiphenomenalism objection is the most serious challenge to this view. If consciousness is just an interface that displays the outputs of unconscious processing, then it seems like consciousness itself doesn't do any causal work — it just reports on decisions the brain has already made. This is supported by Libet's experiments showing that readiness potentials appear in the brain hundreds of milliseconds before subjects report making a conscious decision. If the brain has already decided before we're consciously aware of deciding, then consciousness really is epiphenomenal. The user illusion view seems forced to accept this conclusion.

I think the objection largely succeeds. Nørretranders himself seems to accept something like this when he characterizes consciousness as a kind of deception — our sense of conscious control is, on his view, not what it appears to be. Dennett tries to resist epiphenomenalism by arguing that conscious contents can still play a functional role through their influence on memory and verbal report, but this feels like a dodge — if the conscious experience itself comes too late to affect the action, then influencing later memory of the action doesn't rescue genuine conscious agency. So yes, the user illusion view does entail epiphenomenalism, which is a significant cost of the theory."
