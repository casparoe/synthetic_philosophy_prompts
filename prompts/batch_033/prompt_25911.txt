I've been running a philosophy of language reading group for a couple years now and we keep coming back to conditionals. Specifically, the debate about whether indicative conditionals ('If it rained, the game was cancelled') and counterfactuals ('If it had rained, the game would have been cancelled') should get a unified semantic treatment or two completely different ones. This fight has been going since Stalnaker and Lewis in the 70s and honestly it feels like nobody's moved. The same arguments get recycled — the or-to-if inference, the apparent non-truth-functionality of indicatives, Gibbard's standoff, all of it. I have a hunch the impasse isn't really about the linguistics but about what each side thinks a semantic theory is supposed to do: the unificationists want a systematic theory that captures the formal parallels, the split-theory people care more about fitting the probability judgments speakers actually make. But maybe I'm wrong and it's something else entirely — a hidden empirical bet about how kids acquire conditionals, or just a verbal dispute about the word 'conditional.' Diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is, and tell me what, if anything, would break the stalemate. Keep it to 800 words max.
