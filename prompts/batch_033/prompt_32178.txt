I've been reading Daniel Dennett's late work on what he called counterfeit people — the idea that as AI systems become capable of passing as human in conversation, we face a distinctive kind of threat that isn't just about deception but about the corrosion of the background conditions that make human interaction meaningful. Dennett worried that cheap, ubiquitous simulacra of persons would degrade the trust that undergirds social life, flooding our communicative channels with ersatz intimacy and effectively counterfeiting the currency of genuine human relationship. He didn't think this was merely a problem of fraud detection; he thought it was a deeper problem about what it means to be a person and what we owe each other once we can no longer reliably tell whether there is anyone on the other end.

I'd like you to write a short story set about twenty years from now, in a world where Dennett's concerns have largely materialized. Voice-based AI agents are indistinguishable from human callers, and nearly everyone has an AI proxy that handles their routine communications — scheduling, small talk with acquaintances, even initial romantic overtures on dating apps. The proxies learn your mannerisms, your speech patterns, your biographical details, and they operate with a degree of autonomy that makes it genuinely unclear, sometimes, whether a given interaction was with the person or the proxy. The protagonist of your story is someone who has begun to suspect that a significant relationship in their life — perhaps with a parent, a spouse, or a close friend — has been delegated to a proxy without their knowledge, and they are trying to figure out whether the real person is still somewhere on the other end, and whether the distinction even matters anymore.

The story should grapple with the question at the heart of Dennett's concern: if a counterfeit is functionally indistinguishable from the real thing in every observable respect, what exactly has been lost, and why does it feel like something has been lost? Does the answer depend on whether the AI proxy is conscious or merely a clever pattern-matcher? Or does the harm lie elsewhere — in the asymmetry of knowledge, in the withdrawal of the human from a relationship they once occupied, in the violation of a commitment that can only be made by someone who could have chosen otherwise? The story need not reach a tidy conclusion, but it should make the reader feel the weight of the question.
