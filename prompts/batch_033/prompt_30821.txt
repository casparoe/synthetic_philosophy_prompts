I'm teaching The Tempest in a course on early modern moral philosophy, and I want to pair it with Montaigne's "Of Cannibals." The play is often read as borrowing from the essay, most obviously in Gonzalo's speech imagining a commonwealth without sovereignty, wealth, or labor, and in the way Caliban is used to test European ideas about savagery. But the fit seems uneasy: Montaigne's cannibals are presented as having their own virtues and a frankness that puts European cruelty to shame, while Caliban is treated by Prospero as fit only for servitude, and his attempt on Miranda is used to justify his subjection. I'd like a careful reading of the play through Montaigne's claim that custom governs what we call barbarous and that the supposedly civilized often outdo the savages in cruelty. Which scenes genuinely dramatize that claim, and which scenes pull against it? Does the play ultimately endorse Prospero's civilizing project, satirize it, or leave the two unreconciled? And what does the play add to Montaigne's argument by turning the essay into a drama of power, language, and dependence? I'd rather have a reading that honestly marks where the lens fails than one that forces the play to be an allegory of the essay.
