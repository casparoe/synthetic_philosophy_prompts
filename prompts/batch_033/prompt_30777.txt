You are a philosopher of mathematics with expertise in Gödel's theorems and their philosophical implications. Think through the following argument carefully — it may help to begin by identifying which steps are uncontroversial and which carry hidden weight.

I've been turning over an argument that's meant to show Gödel's incompleteness theorems refute computationalism about the mind, and I've become convinced it proves too much — but I can't locate the exact slip. Here's the argument:

(1) For any consistent, recursively axiomatizable formal system F that contains enough arithmetic, Gödel's first incompleteness theorem yields a sentence G_F such that F can neither prove nor disprove G_F, yet G_F is true.

(2) A human mathematician who understands Gödel's proof can, when presented with any such F, see that G_F is true — provided she can recognize that F is consistent.

(3) Therefore, for any such formal system F, the human mind can recognize an arithmetical truth that lies beyond F's reach.

(4) If the human mind were itself a formal system (or computable by a Turing machine), then there would be some F_M that exactly captures its arithmetical output.

(5) By (3), the mind can recognize the truth of G_{F_M}, which F_M cannot prove. But by (4), whatever the mind recognizes, F_M proves. Contradiction. So the mind is no formal system; computationalism is false.

(6) But now apply the same reasoning to two people, Alice and Bob. If Alice knows Gödel's proof, then for any formal system F_B that supposedly captures Bob's arithmetical competence, Alice can see that G_{F_B} is true while F_B cannot prove it. So Alice can outstrip Bob. Symmetrically, Bob can outstrip Alice. And worse: Alice can apply the reasoning to her own F_A, yielding truths she herself cannot prove — yet she just did. The argument seems to grant anyone who knows Gödel's proof the power to transcend any finitely specifiable system, including their own.

(7) That conclusion is absurd. Merely knowing a proof does not make me capable of outreasoning every other human, nor of stepping outside my own cognitive bounds. So the argument must fail somewhere. But where? I want a step-by-step diagnosis: which premise or inference is the culprit, why it looked plausible, and what giving it up costs for the broader debate about minds and machines.
