A friend of mine who just finished a metaphysics course tried to explain Black's two-spheres argument to me, and I wrote down what she said as best I could. Here is my reconstruction of her summary:

"Leibniz's Law says that if two things are identical they share all their properties, and also the converse — if they share all properties, they're identical. Black came up with a counterexample to the converse. He imagined a universe containing nothing but two iron spheres, each exactly alike in size, shape, composition, temperature, and so on. Since there's nothing else in the universe, they also share all their relational properties — each is the same distance from the other, each has the same gravitational relation to the other, and so on. So by Leibniz's Law they'd have to be the same sphere, but obviously there are two of them. The argument works because in a universe that simple we couldn't possibly tell the spheres apart even in principle, so any claim that they differ in some hidden property is meaningless. That's why Black's argument also shows that verificationism — the idea that a claim is meaningful only if it's verifiable — is basically correct, at least for this kind of case."

I'm suspicious of a few things here. I know Leibniz's Law and the identity of indiscernibles are distinct principles but I'm fuzzy on exactly how, and the appeal to verificationism feels like it's importing something external to the argument. Could you go through her summary and tell me what's accurate, what's subtly wrong, and whether the conclusion she draws about verificationism actually follows from anything Black established?
