I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind, currently preparing my dissertation prospectus on perception and the argument from illusion. My committee has asked me to submit a concise statement of my core commitments before the prospectus meeting, and I want to make sure the set I'm bringing to them is internally consistent — or at least that I know where the fault lines are before they do. I've been working through the disjunctivist literature over the past year (Martin, McDowell, Snowdon, the early Hinton papers, the Byrne and Logue collection, Fish's book), and I've settled on the following five commitments, which I take to hang together as a broadly disjunctivist package:

(1) Metaphysical disjunctivism: veridical perceptions and hallucinations are fundamentally different kinds of mental states. They share no common mental core — no "highest common factor" that is present in both cases.

(2) Naive realism about veridical perception: when I veridically perceive an object, the mind-independent object itself is a constituent of my perceptual experience, and the phenomenal character of the experience is partly constituted by that object and its properties.

(3) The argument from illusion — and its stronger variant, the argument from hallucination — is a genuine philosophical challenge that the disjunctivist must answer. I do not think it can simply be dismissed as question-begging, even if it ultimately fails.

(4) Hallucinations can be subjectively indistinguishable from veridical perceptions. A perfect hallucination — one that matches a veridical perception in its subjective character — is possible in principle, whether or not it occurs in practice.

(5) Epistemic disjunctivism: veridical perception provides a distinctive kind of rational support that hallucination cannot provide. Even when a hallucination is subjectively indistinguishable from a veridical perception, the epistemic standing of the subject differs in the two cases.

I'd like you to audit this set for consistency. Go through the commitments pairwise and identify which pairs are genuinely in tension, which pairs only appear to be in tension, and rank the tensions by severity. For each genuine tension, tell me what the least costly revision would be and what it would cost — what I would lose by giving up one or the other commitment. I am not signaling which one I would be willing to sacrifice; I want an honest assessment of where the problems are, not a nudge toward a particular resolution. If there are pairs that look bad at first glance but turn out to be fine when you think them through, flag those too — I'd rather know what I do not need to worry about than walk into the meeting blindsided.
