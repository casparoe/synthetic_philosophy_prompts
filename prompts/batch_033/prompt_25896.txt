Next week I have to lead the discussion — essentially, I need to teach the core idea to the rest of the seminar in about forty minutes. The other students are philosophy majors who've done the standard epistemology sequence (Gettier cases, internalism versus externalism, Cartesian skepticism, foundationalism versus coherentism), but most of them haven't read Wittgenstein before, and I can already tell from the first session that several are filing hinge propositions under "beliefs we're just really, really sure about," which I'm fairly certain misses the point entirely.

I don't need you to explain hinge epistemology to me — I've done the reading. What I need is a teaching plan. Specifically: what has to come first in the explanation and why, in terms of conceptual dependencies (what idea won't make sense until what other idea is in place?); what single example or case should carry the whole thing, and whether I should use Moore's hands or whether there's a better one that avoids students getting stuck on the skeptical framing; what misconceptions I should expect and in what order they'll arise, and exactly where in the sequence I should head each one off before it calcifies; and one question I can pose near the end that would tell me whether they've actually understood what a hinge is, as opposed to just being able to repeat the definition. I need to make clear how Wittgensteinian hinges differ from merely central or entrenched beliefs in a Quinean web, and I'm not sure at what point in the sequence that contrast should land — too early and they won't yet see the distinction, too late and the Quineans will have spent the whole session mapping everything onto their own framework. Finally, if there's some live debate in the literature that would derail us if we opened it — the relativism charge, say, or whether hinges are a kind of knowledge after all — I'd rather you tell me what to flag and explicitly set aside than pretend the terrain is all settled.
