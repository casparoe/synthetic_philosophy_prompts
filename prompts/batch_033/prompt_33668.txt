Question: "Describe Thomson's lamp thought experiment and explain what Thomson took it to demonstrate about supertasks. Then evaluate whether the argument succeeds."

Student's answer:

Thomson's lamp is a thought experiment where a lamp can be switched on and off instantaneously. The lamp is on at t=0. After half a minute it's switched off, after a quarter minute more it's switched on, after an eighth of a minute off, and so on — each interval is half the previous one. After exactly one minute, infinitely many switchings have been performed. The puzzle is: at t=1, is the lamp on or off?

The state of the lamp doesn't converge to anything — it just oscillates on, off, on, off forever. Since the sequence has no limit, there's no fact of the matter about what state the lamp ends up in. Thomson took this to show that supertasks are logically impossible: you can't coherently complete an infinite sequence of actions in a finite time, because doing so would leave you with an undetermined, contradictory result.

I don't think the argument works. Benacerraf responded that the lamp description is simply incomplete — it specifies the state of the lamp at every moment before t=1 but says nothing about t=1 itself. That's not a contradiction, it's just a failure to specify the boundary condition. The supertask itself is perfectly possible; we just didn't say what happens at the end. In fact, Zeno's dichotomy paradox has basically the same structure — Achilles has to complete infinitely many sub-journeys to catch the tortoise — and since we know motion happens, supertasks must be possible. Thomson's lamp doesn't prove anything except that you have to define your functions all the way to the limit point.
