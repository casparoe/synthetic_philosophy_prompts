The debate over whether you survive gradual neural replacement — replacing each neuron with a functionally identical artificial substitute, one at a time — has been running since at least the 1970s. Both sides accept the same stipulated facts: the replacements preserve input-output behavior, the process is behaviorally undetectable, and the resulting system is functionally isomorphic to the original brain. Yet one side concludes that personal identity is preserved throughout, while the other concludes that the original person is destroyed and replaced by a duplicate. The arguments on both sides are well-rehearsed and nobody's position shifts. I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this really is — verbal, metaphysical, empirical, or something else — and what, if anything, would count as progress. Give me exactly five diagnostic points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. Separate what is settled among philosophers working on personal identity from what remains genuinely contested, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
