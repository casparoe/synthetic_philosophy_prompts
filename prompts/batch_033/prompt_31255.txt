A couple of people in my reading group got into it last week over Arrow's impossibility theorem, and neither of them would let it go, so I said I'd write up the exchange and get an outside ruling. I couldnt follow all of it, honestly. Here's the back-and-forth as best I remember it.

(1) Dana started it by saying ranked-choice voting is pointless, because Arrow proved that no fair voting method can turn individual rankings into a coherent social ranking unless it's basically a dictatorship, so every real voting method is going to be broken anyway.

(2) Marcus pushed back that she was over-applying the theorem. Arrow's result is about social welfare functions: rules that take everyone's full preference order and spit out a full social preference order. Ranked-choice voting doesn't do that; it just picks one winner. So the theorem doesn't directly say anything about it, and in any case it doesn't license the conclusion that all methods are equally bad. It just says you can't have all four conditions at once.

(3) Dana said sure, but the same shape of problem shows up for single-winner methods too. Plurality voting has spoiler effects, which is basically an IIA failure, and ranked choice can fail to elect the Condorcet winner even when one exists. There are also impossibility results like Gibbard-Satterthwaite for single-winner rules, so it isn't an artifact of demanding a full social ranking.

(4) Marcus came back with: okay, but once you move to single-winner rules, "fair" splinters into different conditions and there's no theorem that ranks which violations are worse. Also, IIA is the weak link in the first place. It says the social ranking of x and y can't depend on anything about z, but why accept that? People's preferences over x and y can legitimately shift depending on what the alternative set is, and agenda effects are part of politics, not automatically a defect.

(5) Dana said: fine, but then you've granted the main point, which is that any workable method violates something, and "IIA is too strong" is just picking which axiom to throw away. You still haven't shown ranked choice's violations are less bad than plurality's, so invoking Arrow to say ranked choice is no magic fix still stands.

(6) Marcus replied: no, the original claim was that ranked choice is pointless because Arrow says all methods are broken. If the theorem is silent on which method is better, then it can't support that original claim. All it gives you is a checklist of tradeoffs, and you have to argue about the tradeoffs directly.
