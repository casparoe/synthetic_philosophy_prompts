So I'm a novelist working on a book where one of the main characters is a philosophy professor who's a modern Confucian role ethicist — she genuinely tries to live by the idea that who you are is constituted by your relationships and that ethical reasoning is about fulfilling the demands of your roles (daughter, teacher, friend, citizen, etc.) rather than applying universal rules. I've already written several scenes where she gives advice or makes judgments about ethical dilemmas, and I want to make sure she's consistant. I'm stuck on a new scene and could use help figuring out what she'd say.

Here are her verdicts so far in the novel (I've written all of these):

1. A father discovers his teenage son shoplifting. The father is also a police officer. She says: the father absolutely should not arrest his son. He should handle it within the family — return the goods, make amends, discipline at home. The father-son role swallows the police-officer role here.

2. A woman's elderly mother asks her to skip an important work presentation to come to a routine doctor's appointment. The mother is anxious but medically fine. She says: go with the mother. The daughter role is more fundamental than the employee role. Reschedule the presentation.

3. A minister discovers the ruler is about to launch a war based on faulty intelligence. The minister's brother is a general who'd benefit professionally from the war. She says: the minister must present the corrected intelligence to the ruler privately. The minister-ruler role demands honest counsel, and the brother relationship shouldn't distort that. But don't publicly shame the ruler — that would violate the role too.

4. A teacher catches a student cheating. The student is the child of the teacher's close friend and neighbor. She says: apply the same penalty you'd give any student. The teacher-student role requires fairness. But then, seperately, go talk to your friend as a friend — explain what happened, don't let them find out from the school.

5. A husband's wife asks him to lie to his parents about a financial decision the couple made, to avoid a fight. She says: don't lie to your parents. The son-parent role requires honesty. But work with your wife to find a way to present the decision that's truthful but minimizes conflict. You owe both roles something.

6. A person's close friend confesses to a minor crime from years ago — vandalism, no one hurt, statute of limitations long passed — and asks them to keep it secret. She says: keep the secret. The friendship role requires confidentiality, and no other role is harmed by keeping quiet.

Now here's the scene I'm stuck on. The character herself is in the situation this time. Years ago, her childhood best friend donated a kidney that saved her life. They've stayed close. Now she's a mid-level government official with discretion over hiring, and this friend comes to her asking her to give his son a government job. The son meets most of the qualifications but is slightly underqualified — he's missing one of the listed requirements, but she has the authority to make exceptions, and other candidates are stronger on paper. The friend says he'll never ask for anything again, and she knows he means it.

What does she do? I'm less interested in the answer itself than in what rule or principle she's implicitly running across all these cases. I can see at least a couple different rules that fit her first six verdicts equally well but give opposite answers on case seven. Can you lay out the candidate rules, say what each would predict for the hiring case, give me your best guess about which rule she's actually using (and why that one fits better than the alternatives), and then describe one more case I could write that would definitively settle which rule she's running?

Oh, and think through this like you're a professional philosopher who specializes in Confucian ethics — really get into the weeds of how role ethics cashes out in hard cases. Maybe start by brainstorming the different ways someone could operationalize Confucian role ethics into actual decision procedures, then map the cases onto them.
