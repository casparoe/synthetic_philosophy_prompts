I teach a small seminar on collective decision-making and social epistemology — about 15 students, mostly philosophy majors with some logic background but no social choice theory. We have a 75-minute session coming up on the discursive dilemma, and I want them to feel it viscerally before they see the formalism. The idea is that they should be genuinely surprised when the group-level outcome contradicts what most of them individually believe, and they should see that the contradiction isn't a gimmick — it follows from perfectly reasonable aggregation rules that nobody would want to give up.

Here's my rough plan: I'll give them a case where a conclusion depends on two or three premises. They vote on each premise individually, we aggregate by majority, and then we compare the premise-driven conclusion to what the majority actually thinks about the conclusion itself. But I want the case to feel real and the stakes to matter enough that they don't treat it as a logic puzzle where they can see the trick coming. I also want the voting to be genuinely independent — not a situation where people can coordinate or fudge their answers to avoid the paradox.

Can you design the full activity? I need:

- A case text (a paragraph or two) that presents a real-feeling decision — maybe a tenure case, a grant proposal, a disciplinary hearing, something where multiple conditions all have to be met for a yes. The premises should be things reasonable people can disagree about on the merits.
- Voting rules and procedure. Should they vote in small groups first and then aggregate across groups, or vote as individuals and then we aggregate? What's the best structure for 15 people?
- A timeline for the 75 minutes, including setup, voting rounds, the reveal, and the debrief.
- A debrief that maps what happened onto the formal structure: why the paradox arises, what premise-based versus conclusion-based aggregation would look like, and why you can't have both intuitive constraints (responsiveness to individual views on each issue, and collective rationality) without running into trouble.
- What's going to go wrong when I run it: what moves will students make that break the activity or let them slip out of the paradox too easily, and how should I handle those?
- And please be honest: what feature of the discursive dilemma does the activity necessarily distort or fail to capture, that I'll have to supply in discussion afterwards?

The goal is that they walk out understanding why this isn't just a quirky voting artifact but a deep tension in the idea of group rationality itself. No lecture up front — they should run into the problem before I name it.
