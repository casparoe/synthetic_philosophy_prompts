I run a monthly salon for novelists and narrative nonfiction writers — about sixteen people, most of them regulars, meeting for two hours. We are spending the season on the theme of individual agency inside large systems, and I want to stage an experience rather than lead a discussion. The idea I want them to feel in their bones is the inefficacy objection: the thought that one person's contribution to a collective harm — or a collective good — is so small that it makes no perceptible difference, so there is no reason to act, even though, if everyone reasoned that way, the outcome would be catastrophic. This pattern shows up in arguments about climate, about voting, about eating meat, about boycotts, and our discussions keep circling it without landing inside it.

I am looking for a structured game or simulation that takes about forty-five minutes of our two-hour block, leaves the rest for debrief, and requires no advance reading. The activity should work without real money. Here is what matters most: the structure of the game itself should generate the inefficacy intuition as a live, first-person experience. Participants should find themselves thinking something like "what I do here doesn't matter" before anyone names that thought. The debrief should then map specific moments in the game onto the structural features of real-world collective action problems — the relationship between individual contribution size and the threshold for harm, the difference between cases where contributions are aggregated (climate) and cases where they are counted (voting), and the question of whether the inefficacy objection is a mistake or a truth we have to live with.

A few constraints from how my group works. These are people who think fluently in terms of character motivation, dramatic irony, and narrative structure, so the debrief can and should draw on those concepts — but the activity itself should work through doing, not through framing. They are also people who will resist a game that feels like a party trick; the rules need to be clean enough that the dilemma itself does the work, not the facilitator's cleverness. If the activity calls for roles, make them thin — a single choice that repeats, or a single constraint — so that the group's attention stays on the structure, not on inhabiting a character.

I also want an honest note on what the activity cannot reproduce. The real-world cases involve vast numbers of strangers and actions dispersed across decades. A room of sixteen people who know each other, making simultaneous choices under a shared clock, is a different thing. Tell me what gets distorted by that translation and how to address it in the debrief, and tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — the move a clever participant will make that short-circuits the point, the misunderstanding that will need to be caught and redirected — and how to handle it without breaking the experience.
