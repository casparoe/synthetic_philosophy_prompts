A friend in my political philosophy seminar pushed this argument on me and now I can't shake it. It feels like it proves too much but I can't spot the sleight of hand.

(1) Any decent way of aggregating individual preferences into social choices has to satisfy two minimal conditions. First, the Pareto principle: if everyone in the society prefers X to Y, the social ranking should put X above Y. Second, minimal liberalism: for each person, there's at least one pair of alternatives that are purely that person's business — like whether they themselves read a particular book — and society has to defer to their preference on that pair.

(2) Now imagine two people, Prude and Lewd, and one copy of a scandalous book. There are three options: P reads it, L reads it, or no one reads it. Prude's ranking: no one reads it > P reads it > L reads it. (He hates the idea of L being corrupted by it, but if someone must read it he'd rather fall on the sword himself.) Lewd's ranking: P reads it > L reads it > no one reads it. (He'd love to see Prude scandalized, but he'd also enjoy reading it himself, and he'd hate the book going unread.)

(3) By minimal liberalism, P's own reading decision is his private business: so whether P reads it or not should be up to P. Since P prefers not reading it to reading it, the social ranking should put "no one reads it" above "P reads it." Similarly, L's reading decision is his private business: L prefers reading it to not reading it, so the social ranking should put "L reads it" above "no one reads it."

(4) But by Pareto, since both prefer "P reads it" to "L reads it," the social ranking should put "P reads it" above "L reads it."

(5) So we get a cycle: no one reads it > P reads it > L reads it > no one reads it. Thats not a coherent social ordering.

(6) Something has to give. Pareto seems non-negotiable — if literally everyone prefers X to Y, what could justify society picking Y? Minimal liberalism seems equally non-negotiable — if we can't carve out even a tiny sphere of personal autonomy, we're saying collective preference always trumps individual choice.

(7) But if both are non-negotiable, the conclusion is that individual rights and rational social choice are incompatible. And that can't be right — liberal democracies exist and make collective decisions all the time.

So where does the argument go wrong? If you have to give up one of the two principles, which one and what's the cost?
