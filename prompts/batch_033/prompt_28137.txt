I'm a PhD student in animal welfare science and I've kinda backed myself into a corner. The aquaculture certification body I'm collaborating with wants to roll out welfare standards for farmed decapods — shrimp, prawns, maybe crabs down the line — and they've asked me to help design the assessment framework their auditors will use on site visits. The problem is, all the standard welfare frameworks we reach for (Five Freedoms, the Brambell stuff, etc.) were built for terrestrial vertebrates and they just assume the animal has a kind of subjective life that we genuinely aren't sure crustaceans have. So you try to apply "freedom from fear" to a shrimp and it's not clear the concept even makes sense, or it makes sense but for reasons you can't defend to a skeptical reviewer.

What I need is a decision procedure — something step-by-step that an auditor can actually work through — that takes a proposed welfare requirement or indicator and tells you whether it's justified to include in the standard, given the state of the evidence (and the state of the uncertainty) about crustacean sentience. The procedure has to steer between two failure modes I keep seeing in these discussions: (1) just assuming shrimp are tiny chickens and importing every terrestrial welfare concept uncritically, and (2) throwing up your hands and saying "well we don't KNOW they feel pain" and using that to justify doing nothing at all.

Some constraints: the auditors are marine biologists or aquaculture people, not philosophers or neuroscientists, so the procedure can't ask them to resolve the debate about invertebrate consciousness. It should distinguish between requirements that are justified even under fairly skeptical views about crustacean sentience (like basic water quality parameters — if nothing else, poor water quality kills them, which is at least an economic loss) and requirements that only make sense if you assume a richer subjective experience (like "environmental enrichment to prevent boredom"). And I need it to flag where the procedure itself is making a normative call rather than a scientific one — I don't want to dress up my values as science and call it objective.

If you could run the procedure on two candidate requirements — say, a maximum stocking density versus a requirement for electrical stunning before boiling — that would help me see how it handles indicators that rest on very different assumptions about what's going on inside the animal.
