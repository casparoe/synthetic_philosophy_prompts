I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy of biology, and I've spent the past semester in a reading group devoted to the functions debate — specifically, the standoff between etiological accounts and causal-role accounts of what it means for a biological trait to have a function. On the etiological side, a trait's function is the effect for which it was selected: the heart's function is to pump blood because pumping blood explains why hearts were preserved by natural selection. On the causal-role side, a trait's function is its contribution to some system-level capacity, full stop — the heart's function is to pump blood because that's the role it plays in the circulatory system, regardless of how it got there. We've worked through the canonical papers, the counterexample volleys (swampman duplicates, vestigial organs, exapted traits), and the various attempts at hybrid or pluralist resolutions. And I've come away with a conviction I can't shake: this debate has been stuck for roughly forty years, and I don't think anyone in my group could articulate what kind of disagreement it actually is.

The frustrating thing is that both sides seem to agree on all the empirical facts about any given trait — its selective history, its current causal contribution to organism-level capacities, the counterfactuals about what would happen if it were removed. Both sides agree that function ascriptions do real explanatory work in biology and that they license normative claims (malfunction, proper function) in ways that mere effect descriptions don't. And yet the disagreement persists. When an etiological theorist insists that a vestigial organ has no function because it wasn't selected for its current effect, and a causal-role theorist replies that it has a function if it now contributes to some capacity, they seem to be disagreeing about something — but I can't tell whether it's a substantive metaphysical disagreement about what functions really are, a verbal dispute about how to use the word "function," or two research programs answering different questions (one about trait origins, one about trait roles) under a single heading. My working hypothesis, which I don't fully trust, is that the two sides are divided by bedrock intuitions about whether the point of a function concept is to explain the presence of a trait or to explain the operation of a system, and that neither intuition is argumentatively reachable from the other.

I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself, not a verdict on who is right. What kind of disagreement is this? Is there a hidden empirical or formal question doing the work? Are the two sides divided by different views about which cases count as data for a theory of function, or about what an explanation in biology must deliver? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a new kind of biological case, a conceptual distinction that dissolves the apparent conflict, a formal result, or is the persistence of the impasse itself evidence that the question is ill-posed?
