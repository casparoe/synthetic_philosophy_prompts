I'm a retired physicist — spent my career in condensed matter, where the math just works — and I've recently got into philosophy. I keep coming back to the view that mathematical objects, numbers and groups and Hilbert spaces and the rest, are real and mind-independent: we discover them, we don't invent them. I gather philosophers call this platonism and also that it's considered a bit embarrasing in polite company. I'm not asking you to defend it; I want the opposite. What would count against it?

I'd like a map of the position's exposure, not a debating-society list. Which arguments, possible developments in mathematics or physics, formal results, or commitments that turn out unacceptable should actually move me away from platonism — sorted from fatal to merely awkward? I know the standard hits (the Benacerraf-style access problem, the indispensability argument being weaker than it looks, nominalist reconstructions of science), but I want to know which of these a committed platonist would themselves concede counts against them, and which they'd shrug off. And tell me straight: has platonism been qualified enough that nothing could in principle count against it? If so, is that a problem for a metaphysical view of this kind, or just what metaphysics is like?

One thing that would personally move me: if the reality talk turned out to be doing no explanatory work — if every time a platonist says mathematics works because it's about real objects, a nominalist could tell the same story without loss. So weigh that too, and be honest if the upshot is that platonism is pretty well insulated and that's fine.
