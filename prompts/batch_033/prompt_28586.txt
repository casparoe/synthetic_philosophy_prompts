Question: Explain what heritability (h²) measures and what it does not measure. Discuss at least two common misinterpretations of heritability estimates, explaining why each is mistaken.

Student answer:

Heritability (h²) is the proportion of phenotypic variance in a population that is accounted for by genetic variance. It is calculated as the ratio of genetic variance to total phenotypic variance: h² = V_G / V_P. In twin studies, heritability is typically estimated by comparing monozygotic and dizygotic twin correlations; if MZ twins are more similar than DZ twins, this suggests genetic influence.

A crucial thing heritability does not measure is the extent to which a trait is genetically determined or fixed. High heritability does not imply immutability. For instance, phenylketonuria (PKU) is highly heritable—it is caused by a single-gene mutation—yet its most serious effects can be entirely prevented by a dietary intervention. So heritability and malleability are independent dimensions.

Second, heritability does not tell us about the causes of a trait in an individual. Saying that the heritability of height is 0.8 does not mean that 80% of my height is due to my genes and 20% to my environment. Heritability partitions variance, not individual causation.

Third, heritability estimates are population- and environment-specific. If you measure the heritability of a trait in a population where the environment is very uniform, heritability will be high because environmental variance is low—not because genes are especially powerful. Conversely, in a population with highly variable environments, heritability may be lower even if the same genes are at work.

A fourth common mistake is to think that high within-group heritability implies that differences between groups are genetic. This does not follow. As Richard Lewontin illustrated with his thought experiment about seeds planted in different soils, the same genetic stock can produce very different outcomes in different environments, and the difference between environments is not genetic even though variation within each environment might be largely genetic.

Finally, heritability is not a fixed property of a trait. It can change over time, across populations, and even across the lifespan. For example, the heritability of general cognitive ability appears to increase from childhood to adulthood.
