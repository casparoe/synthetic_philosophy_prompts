I am a novelist at work on a campus novel set at a small liberal-arts college. One of my central characters, Professor Miriam Adeyemi, teaches an upper-level seminar on the ethics of consent, and I have drafted the scenes in which she rules on a series of hypotheticals. She is the sort of character who prides herself on consistency, and in the novel's climax she is asked to apply her view to a case involving the protagonist; I therefore need to know precisely which principle she has been running, not merely what she has said. Her rulings so far, spread across cases of deception, coercion, and impaired competence, are these:

1. A man pretends to be his identical twin brother and has sex with the brother's girlfriend in a darkened room; she believes he is her boyfriend. Ruling: no valid consent.
2. A man tells a woman he has recently tested negative for a serious sexually transmitted infection when he has not been tested, and she consents to sex without a condom only because of that assurance. Ruling: no valid consent.
3. A man agrees to use a condom and then removes it during intercourse without his partner noticing. Ruling: no valid consent.
4. A manager tells an employee that she will be fired unless she has sex with him, and she agrees. Ruling: no valid consent.
5. A person who is stumbling drunk and barely coherent agrees to sex with a sober acquaintance. Ruling: no valid consent.
6. A person who has had two glasses of wine and remains articulate and clear-headed agrees to sex. Ruling: valid consent.
7. A man tells a woman he is thirty-four when he is in fact thirty-eight; she later says the lie made no difference to her decision. Ruling: valid consent.

The new case, which matters to the plot, is this: a graduate student tells a woman he meets at a conference that he is a wealthy gallery owner who can advance her career, when he is in fact an adjunct with no money or connections. She says she would not have slept with him had she known the truth. He is honest about his name, uses a condom as agreed, and misrepresents nothing about the physical encounter itself.

Please predict how Professor Adeyemi would rule on the new case if she is being consistent with her earlier verdicts. In doing so, identify the candidate rules that fit her track record (including any that fit just as well as the one you think she holds), say what each rule predicts about the new case, give your best guess with your reasons for preferring it, and name a further hypothetical that would cleanly distinguish the leading candidate rules. Keep your answer to no more than 500 words.
