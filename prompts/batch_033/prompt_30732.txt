I keep noticing what looks like the same explanatory shape in two very different fields, and I would like to know whether it is really one thing. In biology, structures such as termite mounds or the vertebrate eye look as though they were designed by someone who understood what they were for, but the accepted explanation involves no such understanding anywhere in the process: variation, selection, and time produce the appearance of design without any participant grasping the result. In computing, a chess engine or an image classifier produces outputs that look intelligent—sometimes more reliable than a human expert's—but the account of how it works cites blind search or the adjustment of millions of weights, and no part of the system is said to understand chess or understand what an image depicts. In both cases the ability seems to come first, and understanding, if it appears at all, appears late and as a product rather than as a source. Does this pattern have an established name, and are the biological and computational cases genuinely instances of the same thing? I suspect Darwin and Turing are the relevant anchors, but I do not know the surrounding literature. I am not mainly after a label: I want to know how the phenomenon should be carved—what has to be true of a system for it to count as an instance, whether "ability without understanding" is the right joint or the real distinction cuts somewhere else, and where the analogy between the two cases breaks down. In particular, the computational case troubles me because the whole setup—the training data, the objective, the architecture—is built by people who do understand what they are doing, whereas the biological case has no such external comprehenders; I would like to know whether that is a difference in kind or merely a difference in where the understanding sits.
