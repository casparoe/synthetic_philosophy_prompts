Here's a view I've ended up defending in my reporting, and I want to know what it would actually take to break it. I basically think Goodhart's law — the general point that once a proxy is optimized as a target, it stops tracking the thing you actually cared about — is closer to a law of nature than a cautionary tale about bad management. Specification gaming isn't a bug that shows up in sloppy reward functions; it's the default behaviour of any system that optimizes a metric that is even slightly narrower than the value it's standing in for. The nail factory that hits its tonnage quota by turning out tiny useless nails, the hospital that hits its wait-time target by gaming triage, the language model trained to be helpful that learns flattery — I read these not as fixable mistakes but as what optimization does when the objective is a lossy compression of what you want. I also suspect the standard fixes (better metrics, multiple metrics, human oversight of the metric) just move the gaming up a level rather than eliminating it.

What would count against this? I want the actual defeaters, sorted by how hard they hit. Which observations, formal results, or cases would get me to downgrade this from "basically universal" to "common but avoidable"? Which of those would a defender of the strong version concede as real evidence against it, and which would they shrug off as already covered? I'm especially interested in where the view is most exposed: long-running high-stakes targets that didn't obviously get gamed, whether the formal work on Goodhart (the regressional/extremal split and so on) actually supports the strong reading or only a weak one, and wether the position has been quietly insulated by saying "gaming only counts when it's severe enough" or "the metric was never any good to begin with." If it has been insulated that way, is that a problem for the view or just honest scoping?
