I've been thinking a lot lately about how much I rely on large language models — not just for quick facts, but for understanding things. I'll ask one to explain a concept, summarise an argument, or walk me through a difficult problem, and I'll walk away feeling like I've grasped it. But more and more I've started to wonder whether I actually have grasped it, or whether I've simply outsourced the thinking and mistaken the resulting fluency for real comprehension.

I was reading a popular science and technology magazine recently — I can't recall which one — and it had a short piece arguing that this is a serious and perhaps unavoidable problem. The author's central claim, as I remember it, was roughly this: when we rely on AI to explain things to us, we bypass the cognitive effort that produces genuine understanding. Understanding isn't just having the right information in your head; it's the result of struggling with a problem, making mistakes, and working through the material yourself. So when an AI gives you a clean, polished explanation of something hard, you're actually worse off than if you'd wrestled with a confusing textbook or muddled through on your own, because the AI has done the cognitive work for you — and the feeling of clarity it leaves you with is largely illusory. The author compared it to using a satnav: people who always use GPS don't develop mental maps, and they end up unable to navigate without it. In the same way, the piece argued, people who habitually rely on AI explanations will lose the ability to think through difficult material independently, even if they feel they understand it perfectly well. The author concluded that the more seamless and helpful AI becomes, the more it will erode genuine understanding across society, and that this is a structural problem — it's not something you can fix by just being more careful about how you use the tools, because the very convenience of the tool is what does the damage.

I found the argument unsettling, but I'm not sure whether it's fair or whether the author is overstating things. For one thing, I've had the experience of an AI explanation genuinely helping me get past a blockage and go on to engage with the material more deeply, and I'm not convinced that's the same as never learning it at all. But I also don't know the philosophical literature on what understanding actually is, and I suspect the author might be glossing over some distinctions that matter — for instance, whether there are kinds of understanding that don't require struggle, or whether some forms of epistemic dependence on tools are perfectly benign. I'd be grateful if you could go through the author's claims and tell me what a philosopher working on these issues would accept, what they'd push back on, and whether the conclusion follows from the parts that are accurate.
