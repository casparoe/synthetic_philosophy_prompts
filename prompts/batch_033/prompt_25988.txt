I hold the view that an action's moral worth depends entirely on the agent's motivating reasons, and specifically on whether those reasons coincide with the features that make the action right. On this view, someone who visits a sick friend out of genuine concern for the friend's wellbeing acts with moral worth; someone who visits the same friend purely to secure a place in the will does not — even if both perform the same helpful action. Crucially, what matters is the actual right-making features, not what the agent believes about rightness: the view thus allows that someone can act with moral worth without believing they are doing anything morally significant (the inverse akrasia cases), and that someone can act dutifully but without moral worth if their conception of duty is mistaken. I want to know what would count against this picture. I'm looking for a map of the view's exposure: which cases, intuitions, and theoretical pressures should make me revise or abandon it, sorted from those that defenders would dismiss as resting on a misunderstanding to those they would have to concede are genuine problems. I also want to know whether the view, as developed in the literature, has been qualified in ways that make it hard to falsify — and whether that matters for a view of this kind.
