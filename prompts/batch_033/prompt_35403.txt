I've been following the manipulation vs. persuasion debate in ethics for a while now, and I'm starting to think it's just spinning its wheels. You know the one: everyone agrees that holding a gun to someone's head isn't persuasion, and everyone agrees that laying out a syllogism with premises they accept is. But the middle is a mess. Is appealing to someone's emotions manipulation? What about framing the same information differently? What about nudges that exploit cognitive biases? What about complimenting someone before making a request?

The thing is, people have been at this for decades and the positions haven't budged. You've got people who think anything that bypasses conscious deliberation is manipulative, people who think only deception and coercion count, and people who think it all comes down to whether the influence is reasons-responsive in the right way. But nobody seems able to convince anyone else, and I'm starting to suspect the problem isn't that we're missing a clever distinction — its that the disagreement is about something else entirely.

So here's what I want: a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it a verbal dispute where different camps are using "manipulation" to mean different things and talking past each other? Is it a substantive disagreement about the nature of autonomy thats just hard to resolve? Is it a clash of bedrock normative intuitions that no argument can settle? Or is there some hidden empirical question — about how actual persuasion works psychologically, say — that, if answered, would dissolve the whole thing? Give me a committed answer, not a survey of options. Tell me what you think is really going on and what, if anything, would count as progress.
