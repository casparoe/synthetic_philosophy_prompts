At a philosophy discussion group I attend, two regulars got into an extended argument about self-deception, and I have been turning it over ever since. I shall reconstruct it as best I can.

Eleanor opened by saying she had been reading around the topic and had concluded that the very idea of self-deception is incoherent. Her argument was this: deceiving someone requires that the deceiver knows the truth and deliberately leads the deceived to believe otherwise. But in self-deception, the deceiver and the deceived are the same person. So the self-deceiver would have to simultaneously know that P is false (in order to carry out the deception) and believe that P is true (in order to be deceived). That is a flat contradiction. Whatever we are describing when we say someone is deceiving themselves, it cannot literally be deception.

James replied that this is one of those philosopher's arguments that proves too much. We all know people who manage to believe what they want to believe despite evidence to the contrary — the husband who cannot see his wife's infidelity, the politician who convinces himself his motives are pure, the parent who refuses to accept that her child is bullying others. If Eleanor's argument says these are not real phenomena, so much the worse for the argument. The phenomenon is robust; the question is how to account for it, not whether it exists.

Eleanor pushed back: she was not denying that people hold false beliefs that happen to serve their interests. She was denying that they deceive themselves into those beliefs. There are plenty of other mechanisms — wishful thinking, confirmation bias, selective attention, motivated reasoning — that produce motivated false beliefs without requiring the paradox. Calling it self-deception smuggles in the idea of intentional self-manipulation, which is precisely what is in question. If we can explain the data with ordinary cognitive biases, the paradox is avoidable and the concept of self-deception is redundant.

James said this relabels the phenomenon rather than explaining it. The reason we reach for self-deception rather than confirmation bias plus wishful thinking is that the person seems to be doing something more active and directed. The husband does not just happen to miss the signs; he arranges not to see them — he changes the subject when certain topics arise, he interprets suspicious behaviour in innocuous ways with a creativity that looks purposeful, he avoids situations where the truth might become undeniable. That purposiveness needs accounting for, and bias does not capture it. Bias is a passive drift; self-deception is an active project.

Eleanor granted the appearance of purposiveness but said it points toward a divided-mind account: one subsystem knows the truth and keeps it from another subsystem. But that, she argued, is not self-deception either — it is one part deceiving another part, which is just ordinary interpersonal deception relocated inside a single skull. The self as a whole is not doing anything paradoxical; it is hosting a conflict between subpersonal parts. So the paradox dissolves, but so does the phenomenon as usually understood, because what we are left with is not a self deceiving itself but a mind containing a deceiver and a deceived that happen to share a body.

James replied that this was a distinction without a difference. If the divided-mind account is correct, then self-deception is a perfectly good term for what happens when one part of a person's mind deceives another part, and Eleanor has simply provided a theory of how it works rather than showing it does not exist. The ordinary concept does not require that the self be an indivisible Cartesian ego. We say a country deceives itself when its leadership hides inconvenient facts from its populace; nobody objects that a country lacks a unitary consciousness.

Eleanor's final move was to say that even on the divided-mind account, a residual puzzle remains. For one subsystem to deceive another, the deceiving subsystem must know the truth. But if it knows the truth, why does that knowledge not simply propagate to the rest of the mind? What keeps the two subsystems sealed off from each other in a way that allows deception but not ordinary communication? And if the answer is something like motivated inattention or active suppression, we are back to describing a process that looks less like deception and more like strategic avoidance — which, she noted, was her original alternative. The divided-mind account either collapses back into the paradox or collapses forward into the bias account it was meant to replace.

At this point the discussion moved on, but I have been unable to resolve it. Could you adjudicate their exchange move by move? I would like to know who has the better of each step, whether either of them misses a decisive consideration, and what the most defensible overall position is on whether self-deception is genuinely possible.
