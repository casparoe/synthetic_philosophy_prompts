ok so I've ended up having to defend a paper I wrote after a mess at my university last spring, where I got put on a panel about wether a student group should be allowed to bring in a speaker with a history of doxxing trans students. I realized I had actual views about Mill I'd never fully worked out, and now I have to present them to people who've read On Liberty properly, not just the famous harm-principle paragraph.

The thesis, roughly: Mill's harm principle, read in context, is neither a general license to restrict speech that offends or distresses people, nor is it the claim that speech can only be restricted when it directly causes physical injury. I argue Mill has the resources to restrict what I call 'constitutive' harms — speech that, in context, is itself intimidation or incitement against determinate people (credible threats, doxxing, coordinated harassment) — while protecting speech that merely expresses hateful or offensive opinions. The controversial bit is that some hate speech comes out regulable, but not because it's hateful or because it causes diffuse harm to dignity or equality; only because in particular contexts it functions as an act of intimidation or incitement. And I deny that 'indirect' harms — speech changing public attitudes in ways that eventually lead to harm — ever justify legal restriction. The argument has three steps: (1) for Mill, harm means a wrong to an assignable individual, not mere dislike or offense; (2) some speech acts are themselves that kind of wrong, because they threaten or incite against identifiable people; (3) once you let diffuse or cumulative effects count as harms, the principle can't draw a line anywhere, so it can't be doing the work people want it to do.

The audience: a legal philosophy workshop, mostly First Amendment doctrine people and political philosophers, plus two Mill scholars, one person who works on hate speech and subordination, and a retired judge. The First Amendment people are going to want content-neutrality and clear doctrinal tests; the Mill scholars will accuse me of reading a harm principle into a liberty principle and ignoring the text; the hate speech person thinks Mill is too thin to handle subordinating speech; the judge will care about administrability.
