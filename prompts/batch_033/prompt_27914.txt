I spent a long evening arguing with a friend about whether DNA is really a code, and the disagreement kept circling around a pattern I don't have a word for. Act as a professional philosopher of biology who works on the concept of information; think carefully, and before you commit to an answer, briefly lay out the distinctions you think are doing the work. Here is what I noticed. My friend kept pointing out that molecular biologists call nucleotide triplets codons, say that DNA is transcribed into RNA and translated into protein, and speak of proofreading and editing, and from this he concluded that DNA is literally a code. But when I pressed him, he conceded that DNA has no sender with intentions, that a codon does not stand for an amino acid in the way a word stands for a thing, and that a cell does not read a sequence the way a person reads a sentence. Then, minutes later, he was back to saying that DNA contains information in just the way a written text does. I have seen the same move elsewhere: people use a thin sense of information, in which one thing simply determines another by physical template the way a key determines the shape of a lock, to make the vocabulary of a richer sense available, the sense in which a message has content that can be correct or mistaken, interpreted or misread. They then slide back and forth between the two, borrowing the authority of the rich sense while retreating to the thin sense whenever challenged. I do not think this is plain equivocation, because the two senses are not unrelated, and code talk really did seem to guide the scientists who worked out how sequence determines sequence. My questions: is there an established concept in philosophy of biology for exactly this pattern, the borrowing of semantic vocabulary for causal molecular processes and the slide between the thin and rich senses? If there are several nearby concepts, which fits best and where does it break down? And if nothing established fits, what should the phenomenon be called, and how should it be defined so that it excludes plain equivocation on one side and harmless, self-aware metaphor on the other? A bare name will not satisfy me; I want the boundaries drawn.
