I'm a backend engineer who spends most of my time reasoning about references versus values, dependency injection, environment variables, and the difference between an interface and its implementations. A colleague recently described a thought experiment from philosophy that I haven't been able to stop thinking about: "Twin Earth." As I understand it, the setup is that there's a planet identical to Earth in every observable respect, except that the clear, potable liquid in its lakes and rivers isn't H₂O but a different chemical compound — call it XYZ. On both planets, people use the word "water" for the local stuff, and their subjective experiences are indistinguishable. The philosophical move, apparently, is to claim that when an Earthling says "water" and when a Twin Earthling says "water," they mean different things — because what a term means depends partly on what's actually out there in the world, not solely on what's in the speaker's head. This is supposed to support a broader position called semantic externalism.

To me, this sounds immediately like a story about late binding and environments: the symbol "water" resolves to different referents depending on which environment it's evaluated in, the way two pointers with the same variable name can point to different objects in different address spaces, or the way an interface reference resolves to whatever implementation was injected at runtime. But I'm told the philosophers draw conclusions from Twin Earth that go well beyond "names are context-dependent" — something about meanings not being mental states, about a "division of linguistic labor" where experts in a community anchor what terms refer to, and about reference being fixed by causal-historical chains rather than by descriptions speakers hold in their heads. I don't have a clean mapping for those parts, and I suspect that's where the interesting friction is.

I'd like you to restate the Twin Earth argument and the semantic externalism it motivates in the vocabulary I actually use: references and values, pointers and the objects they point to, environments and scope, dependency injection and inversion of control, interface versus implementation, configuration management, and whatever else from software engineering feels like a natural fit. Be as faithful as you can, but don't force it — if a concept has to be bent out of shape to fit, say so. Then give me the residue report: which claims translate cleanly, which ones you had to fudge, and which ones have no counterpart at all in how we think about names and references in software. I want to know whether my intuition that this is "just" about environments and late binding is right, or whether the philosophers are pointing at something my vocabulary genuinely cannot express — and if so, whether that's a limitation of my vocabulary or a sign that their claim is less precise than it looks.
