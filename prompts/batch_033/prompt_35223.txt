ok so I'm teaching a grad seminar on moral worth next term and I want to start the first real session by putting the cart before the horse — before we read any of the usual suspects (Kant, Arpaly, Markovits, etc.), I want us to figure out what we're actually asking of a theory here. So: write me the desiderata. What does a theory of moral worth — of what it takes for an action to be not just right but done for the right reasons, such that the agent deserves credit — have to get right to count as adequate?

For each requirement, give me the case or consideration that motivates it. Then flag which ones you think are non-negotiable (any theory that fails here is just not talking about the same thing) and which are more like theoretical preferences or demands from one camp that another camp might reasonably reject. If any pair of desiderata can't be jointly satisfied, I especially want to know that — that's the part I most want to see, actually.

And then, because I'm going to ask this in class anyway: which single requirement does the most work in knocking out the most popular views? Don't do a full scorecard — just tell me which one is the real bottleneck and why.

Some things I definitely want on the list: the Huck Finn / inverse akrasia problem, the "right kind of reasons" problem (not just any ol' reason that happens to cause the right act counts), and the overdetermination / mixed motives problem. But don't just list those three — I want the full set of genuine constraints, and I want you to tell me if some of the things people treat as constraints are actually just baggage from one particular view that the rest of us don't have to accept.
