I'm in a third-year political theory course on tradition and institutional change, and the phrase "Chesterton's fence" gets thrown around in nearly every seminar as if it's a single, settled argument. Last week someone invoked it to shut down a proposal to restructure the departmental tenure process, and yesterday someone else used it while defending zoning regulations that almost everyone in the room agrees are exclusionary. I can't tell whether these are the same argument or entirely different ones wearing the same name.

I want to map out the distinct claims that "Chesterton's fence" — or the associated imperative "don't take a fence down until you know why it was put up" — could actually be expressing. I can already see a spectrum from a trivial epistemic platitude ("understand a thing before you change it") to a strong Burkean deference to inherited institutions that would paralyze almost all reform. But that spectrum is too coarse; I need the intermediate positions laid out clearly.

For each candidate reading, I'd like to know: (a) whether it's defensible on its own terms, (b) whether it's the kind of claim that could actually do argumentative work or is just a dressed-up intuition, and (c) whether it's the reading that the slogan's typical users — including my classmates — seem to be reaching for when they invoke it. Ideally, I'd also like to know which readings would actually license the two specific uses I encountered (blocking tenure reform; defending exclusionary zoning), and which readings, if any, would rule one of those uses out while permitting the other.
