I work as a software engineer, and after years of building products on top of machine translation APIs I still cannot say what I think these systems are doing. I have read the essays arguing that statistical translation is shallow and that no quantity of training data amounts to understanding, and they feel right to me, but I cannot tell which of my own reactions they actually support. I would like you to extract a principled position from my gut verdicts on a batch of concrete cases. I have no formal philosophy background, so please keep the answer precise but as plain as you can, and be explicit about which of my verdicts cannot all be kept.

My verdicts, roughly as they come to me:

1. Two systems produce identical translations on the 500-sentence technical-manual test set I use for regression testing, including the same three errors. One is a rule-based system built from a hand-written grammar and a lexicon of a few thousand words; the other is a transformer with hundreds of billions of parameters. My gut says the rule-based system understands the manual's language in a thin but real way, and the large model does not. I dislike that this makes understanding depend on what is inside the box rather than on behavior, but the reaction is stable.

2. The large model translates "The painting wouldn't fit through the doorway because it was too wide" correctly, resolving "it" to the painting. Given "The painting wouldn't fit through the doorway because it was too narrow," it again resolves "it" to the painting, producing a sentence that says the painting was too narrow to fit through the doorway. The second error bothers me more than the first success reassures me: the first was probably a high-probability pattern from training, and the second shows the system has no grip on how sizes work.

3. A system trained only on parliamentary transcripts and newspaper text is asked to translate a menu item and a sentence of slang. It produces fluent, confident translations that are completely wrong. I read that as strong evidence against understanding: a system that understood would either get it or flag uncertainty. The confidence is what bothers me most.

4. A system translates a long German compound it cannot have seen — a word meaning the badge on a river-shipping captain's cap — correctly, by decomposing it into parts. I want to credit this as understanding, because it did what a human with morphological knowledge would do. But I know the subword tokenizer is doing pattern matching below the word level, so I suspect I am being taken in.

5. The same large model as in (1) and (2) translates a sentence correctly but, when I then ask it who did what to whom in that sentence, it cannot answer. My verdict: correct translation without the ability to answer a simple comprehension question is not understanding; it is a transformation between surface forms.

6. Given a grammatically well-formed but semantically defective sentence — the familiar example about ideas that are both colorless and green sleeping furiously — the model produces a confident, fluent translation. I treat this as a failure of understanding even though there is arguably no correct translation: a system that understood would recognize that the input is semantically defective. But a human translator asked to translate it might also just produce a fluent version, so I worry this verdict is unfair.

7. A person who has studied a language for a week, knows roughly five hundred words and a grammar book, translates a simple sentence, with errors. I unhesitatingly credit them with understanding. So my criterion cannot be "makes errors" or "has a small vocabulary." Yet I denied understanding to a system with vastly more knowledge. I need to know whether that asymmetry can be defended or whether it is just chauvinism about biology.

Is there a principle — or a small set of candidate principles — that captures all or most of these verdicts? Please state it precisely, tie each clause to the cases that motivate it, and apply it to a case I have not listed: a system that, after I point out a translation error, replies that I am right and that it misread the subject, and then corrects itself. Then tell me which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and which I should overturn. My working hypothesis is something like "understanding means producing the right output for the right reason, where the right reason rules out mere statistical association," but I suspect that is too vague or too strict; please repair it or replace it.
