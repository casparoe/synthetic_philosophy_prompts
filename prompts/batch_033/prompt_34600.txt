I'm a journalist covering the science-and-ideas beat, and I've been trying to write about a standoff in AI safety that has felt frozen for years now. One side says the only safety guarantees worth having are worst-case ones: a system is "safe" only if you can prove it won't do the bad thing on any input or in any deployment, the way you'd want a guarantee for a cryptographic protocol. The other side says that's a category mistake for learned systems: real deployments are average-case, you care about behavior on the distribution you actually see, and insisting on adversarial guarantees either rules out every useful system or gives you proofs about toy models that don't transfer. Both sides seem to agree on the math and on the fact that current systems fail in surprising ways, and yet neither camp move. My working theory is that it's mostly a disagreement about the word "guarantee," and that the worst-case people are smuggling in a standard of safety the average-case people never accepted. Diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is — verbal, factual, a difference about threat models, two subfields answering different questions under one label — and say what would actually count as progress given the diagnosis. Don't survey the two camps; give me a structural account of why the argument is stuck.
