I've been thinking about a tension in AI safety that I can't seem to resolve on my own, and I'd like to see it explored through a dialogue.

The issue is this: when we talk about aligning AI systems, should we demand worst-case guarantees — proofs or strong assurances that a system won't cause catastrophic harm even under unusual, adversarial, or distributionally shifted conditions — or is that standard unreasonably high, such that we should instead aim for average-case reliability, the way we do with most engineered systems and indeed with the humans we entrust with power? The worst-case camp seems to say: if the stakes are existential, anything short of a guarantee is irresponsible. The average-case camp seems to say: demanding worst-case guarantees is paralyzing and doesn't reflect how safety actually works in practice; we manage nuclear weapons, pandemic risks, and other existential threats without formal worst-case proofs, so why should AI be different?

Please write a dialogue between two people who have thought deeply about this. One argues that alignment work should be organized around worst-case guarantees; the other argues that this is a mistake and that average-case reliability is the more appropriate standard. Both should be equally competent and well-informed — neither should be a strawman. The dialogue should involve real back-and-forth: each should respond to the other's actual points, and their exchange should push past slogans into concrete considerations about what it would mean in practice to adopt one standard or the other.
