So I'm in this philosophy of language seminar and the professor has been going on about norms of assertion all semester — like, what rule governs when you're allowed to say something. She's given us her verdicts on a bunch of cases and I'm trying to figure out what norm she's actually running before the final. Here are the cases she's commented on so far:

1. **The clock case**: Speaker says "it's 3:00" after checking a working clock on the wall. It is in fact 3:00. Verdict: proper.

2. **The hunch case**: Speaker tells a friend "it'll rain tomorrow" based on nothing — no forecast, no clouds, just a feeling. It does rain. Verdict: improper.

3. **The lie case**: Speaker says "the package arrived" knowing full well it didn't, just to end an awkward conversation. Verdict: improper.

4. **The goldfinch case**: Speaker says "that's a goldfinch" after getting a clear look at a bird with all the right markings in good light. It is a goldfinch. But here's the thing: a prankster has filled the area with lifelike taxidermied goldfinches, and the speaker just happened to spot the one real bird — she would have said the same thing if she'd been looking at any of the fakes. Verdict: proper.

5. **The store hours case**: Speaker says "the store is open" after checking the store's official website, which lists hours as 9–9 daily. It's 2pm on a Tuesday. Unbeknownst to the speaker, the store closed early that day for a private event, so it's actually closed. Verdict: proper.

Now for the new case she hasn't ruled on yet:

**The wishful thinking case**: Speaker tells a friend "the restaurant takes reservations." She really wants this to be true and has convinced herself it is, but she has no actual evidence — she hasn't called, hasn't checked online, nothing. She does genuinely believe it though. As it happens, the restaurant does take reservations.

What do you think the professor will say about the wishful thinking case, and why? I'm especially interested in:

- **The candidate norms** — What are the two (or maybe three) rules of assertion that could still fit all five of her existing verdicts? Give each a name and state it clearly.
- **What each norm predicts** for the wishful thinking case — proper, improper, or something else.
- **Your best guess** — which norm you think she's actually running, and what makes you lean that way over the alternatives (maybe 100–150 words).
- **The tiebreaker** — describe one further case (doesn't have to be from real life, can be totally made up) whose verdict would definitively tell the candidate norms apart, and explain what each norm would say about it.
