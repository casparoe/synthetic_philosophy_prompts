I accept a version of the hedonistic imperative: the abolition of suffering in all sentient life is both achievable in principle through biotechnology — recalibrating hedonic set-points and abolishing the capacity for suffering at its neurobiological root, rather than merely palliating it — and morally urgent, such that any retained capacity for suffering should be treated as a design flaw rather than a feature. I want you to map what would count against this position, treating it at full strength rather than as a caricature. Distinguish, first, the feasibility defeaters from the evaluative ones and the practical ones. Among the candidate defeaters I want assessed: whether the pain/suffering distinction (nociceptive signaling versus negative valence) can be drawn cleanly enough to make the engineering target well-defined; whether the view overreaches in claiming that the goods we care about — motivation, learning, moral development, achievement, deep attachment — survive the elimination of negative valence, or whether some of them constitutively require the possibility of suffering, in which case the view predicts the wrong thing about value; the charge that a sufficiently flat bliss gradient would amount to wireheading rather than genuine well-being, and whether the view can answer that without ad hoc qualification; the ecological scope problem, including predation, the food chain, and whether the imperative extends coherently to nonhuman animals and to beings whose sentience is uncertain; the coercion problem, including whether anyone may retain a right to suffer and whether the imperative licenses overriding that right; and the suspicion that the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability — by defining suffering so narrowly, pushing the endpoint so far out, or redescribing every apparent counterexample as a failure of implementation rather than of the view itself — that no conceivable outcome could count against it. For each, say whether it is decisive, merely awkward, or absorbable by refinement, and be explicit about which of these a committed defender would themselves concede as counting against the position. Conclude with an honest assessment of how exposed the position actually is, and whether its insulation — if it is insulated — is a problem for a view of this kind.
