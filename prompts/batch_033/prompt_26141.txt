I have been thinking about a set of epistemological cases and find myself with verdicts that seem hard to reconcile. I would like you to identify the principle, if any, that captures the pattern, test it against a further case, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts I may need to abandon.

Here are the cases and what I am inclined to say about each.

1. The fuel gauge. I buy a used car whose fuel gauge I have never checked against any independent measure. I fill the tank, note that the gauge reads F, drive a while, and note that the gauge reads half-full. I do this over many weeks, each time recording what the gauge says and using the gauge itself—and nothing else—to confirm that the tank level matches what the gauge would predict. Can I come to know, by this procedure, that the gauge is reliable? I say: no.

2. The thermometer. I acquire a thermometer whose accuracy I have no independent reason to trust. I take it outdoors, read the temperature, come indoors, note the reading drops, and so on. Over months I compile a record: the thermometer rises when placed in sunlight, falls when placed in the fridge, and so forth. I never check its readings against another thermometer, nor against any non-thermometric indicator of temperature. Can I come to know that the thermometer is reliable? I say: no.

3. The chicken sexer. A novice chicken sexer begins sorting hatchlings into male and female. He receives no external feedback on his judgments—no one ever tells him whether he was right, and the chicks are taken away before any traits become visible. After a decade, he says to himself: I have sorted fifty thousand chicks by my own lights, and by my count my accuracy is very high, so I must be reliable. Does he know that he is reliable at sexing chicks? I say: no.

4. Ordinary vision. I look across the room and see a blue coffee mug on my desk. I have not performed any independent calibration of my visual system. Do I know that there is a blue mug on my desk? I say: yes, obviously.

5. Memory. I recall that I had toast for breakfast this morning. I have not independently verified that my memory is functioning reliably today. Do I know that I had toast? I say: yes.

6. The colour checker. A man suspects his colour vision may be deteriorating. He buys a set of Ishihara plates and, without looking at the answer key, looks at each plate and writes down the number he sees. He then checks his answers against the key—but the key itself is a printed card whose numbers he must, of course, read with the very colour vision he is testing. He finds that his answers match the key on thirty-eight out of forty plates. Does he know, on this basis, that his colour vision is in good working order? I say: I am genuinely torn. It feels better than the fuel gauge case—he is at least comparing his judgments to something external to him, and the plates were designed by others—but the verification step seems to lean on the very faculty under test. If forced, I would say he does not know, but with less confidence than in the first three cases.

The trouble is plain. Cases 1 through3 feel like clear failures of knowledge: circular track-record reasoning cannot pull itself up by its own bootstraps. But case4 and case5 feel like clear successes: I do know about the mug, and I do know about my breakfast. Yet in both perception and memory, any attempt to justify the reliability of the faculty seems to fall into the same circularity. I cannot check vision without using vision; I cannot check memory without using memory. So either my verdicts in1–3 are too strict, or my verdicts in4–5 are too permissive, or there is a principle that distinguishes them.

What I want from you. First, state as precisely as you can the principle or principles that might reconcile these verdicts—or, if no single principle can, lay out the leading candidates and explain which verdict each one must reject. Second, apply your preferred account to a further case: a juror hears a witness testify that the defendant was at the scene. The juror has no independent way to check the witness's reliability; she judges the witness to be credible because his story is internally consistent and fits what she already believes—beliefs that are themselves partly based on the testimony of other witnesses, whom she assessed by the same method. Does the juror know, on this basis, that the defendant was at the scene? Third, tell me which of my six verdicts I would have to give up under your preferred account, and whether that is a price worth paying.
