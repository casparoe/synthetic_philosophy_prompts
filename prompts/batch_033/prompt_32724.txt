I've been thinking about a puzzle in the philosophy of consciousness that I first encountered in Dennett's *Consciousness Explained* and that keeps surfacing in discussions on LessWrong and related forums: the Orwellian versus Stalinesque distinction, motivated by the color phi phenomenon. The experimental setup is straightforward enough. Two differently colored dots are flashed in rapid succession at different locations, and subjects report seeing a single dot that moves smoothly from the first location to the second, changing color roughly midway along the trajectory. The puzzle is that the second dot's color seems to be somehow anticipated by the visual system before the second dot appears, in order to construct the coherent percept of a single moving-and-color-changing dot. Dennett uses this to draw a distinction between two kinds of editing the brain might be doing. Under the "Orwellian" account, the brain initially represents the sequence more or less veridically and then retrospectively revises the memory of what was experienced, inserting a fictional moving dot after the fact — like the Ministry of Truth rewriting the historical record. Under the "Stalinesque" account, the brain delays the onset of conscious experience by a couple hundred milliseconds, waiting for the second dot to appear before staging a single coherent narrative — like a show trial where the verdict is fixed in advance but the proceedings are made to look legitimate. Dennett's provocative claim is that there is no fact of the matter about which account is correct. No empirical test could distinguish them, he argues, because any evidence we could gather would itself be subject to the same ambiguity: any report or behavioral response could be the product of an experience that was later edited, or an experience that was delayed and then staged. The distinction, he concludes, collapses once we abandon the idea of a Cartesian Theater where experiences arrive in a definite temporal order for a central audience.

I'm writing a term paper for a philosophy of mind seminar — around five thousand words, due at the end of the semester — and I want to take a serious look at whether Dennett is right that the Orwellian/Stalinesque distinction is genuinely undecidable. I'm not convinced the case is closed. For one thing, there's been pushback from philosophers and neuroscientists who suspect there might be ways to get empirical traction: for example, by examining whether later stimuli can influence the perceived timing of earlier stimuli in ways that systematically favor one model, or by looking at the neural correlates of the relevant temporal windows and asking whether the dynamics of early visual cortex look more like editing-in-progress or more like a delayed broadcast. For another, I'm not sure the conceptual argument that the distinction collapses is as tight as Dennett makes it sound. It seems to rely on a verificationist premise — that if two hypotheses make no difference to any possible observation, they are not genuinely distinct hypotheses — and that premise itself needs defending. There might also be indirect ways of breaking the tie: for instance, by asking whether the two accounts make different commitments about the contents of unconscious processing during the relevant window, or whether one account fits more naturally with what we know about the architecture of working memory and its relationship to conscious access.

Could you help me turn this into a proper proposal? I need a precise statement of the core question, a clear explanation of why Dennett's original argument doesn't fully settle it, a sketch of the approach I'd take — what kinds of considerations I'd bring to bear, how I'd structure the argument, what the main dialectical moves would be — and an honest assessment of what would count as the project succeeding versus failing. In particular, I'd like to know what I should do if I conclude partway through that Dennett is basically right and there isn't much more to say: is that a failed project, or is there a worthwhile paper to be written about why the apparent alternatives aren't genuinely distinct?
