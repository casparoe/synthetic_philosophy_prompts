I spent last night arguing with a friend about the following case — a variant of the standard perfect-forgery puzzle. A painting is discovered in an attic and attributed to Vermeer; it is displayed in a major museum, admired by millions, written about by critics, and insured for eighty million dollars. Then new evidence conclusively shows it was painted in 1992 by a skilled copyist. The visual surface is identical to what it was before the discovery — the same photons hit the retina. My friend insists that the painting's value has collapsed, and that this collapse is rationally justified: the object is now a forgery, and forgeries are worth less because they are not the achievements they appeared to be, and our appreciation of art is appreciation of achievement, not of surface patterns. I argued that if the experience of looking at the painting is unchanged, and if the object's intrinsic properties are unchanged, then the shift in valuation is pure snobbery — a fetish for provenance that aesthetics has no business endorsing. Three hours in, neither of us had moved.

What bothers me is that this argument felt like a miniature of the whole debate in philosophical aesthetics about the value of originals: the same moves get made, the same intuitions get traded, the same thought experiments — perfect fakes, van Meegeren, the indistinguishable recording — get cited, and yet the debate seems frozen. I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is, not another round of arguments for one side. My working hypothesis is that the two sides are answering different questions under one heading: one side is asking about the object's intrinsic aesthetic properties, the other is asking about the fittingness of our attitudes toward objects given their histories, and the impasse persists because neither side will grant that the other's question is the real one. But I'm not sure that's right. It could instead be a disagreement about what counts as an aesthetic property, or a clash of bedrock intuitions about whether history can be perceptually relevant, or even a covert empirical disagreement about how human psychology actually processes knowledge of provenance — if we could show that learning an object is a forgery alters perceptual experience itself rather than just post-perceptual judgment, would that settle the debate, or would it just relocate the disagreement to a new question about whether that alteration is rational?

I want you to diagnose the structure of this debate: what exactly the disagreement is about, whether the sides are genuinely contradicting each other or talking past each other, and whether there is any move — a conceptual distinction, an empirical finding, a reframing of the question — that could actually break the stalemate. If the impasse is permanent, say why and what that tells us about the question itself. Engage the actual positions as they appear in the literature, not a caricature of them, and make your diagnosis precise enough that someone who holds one of these positions could recognize themselves in it and feel the force of the challenge.
