For years I've covered debates in metaethics and the philosophy of normativity, and one that reliably stalls out at conferences is the wrong kind of reasons problem. The setup: if a demon threatens to punish you unless you admire a saucer of mud, you have a reason to admire it, but intuitively that reason doesn't make the mud admirable. The project is to say what distinguishes the right kind of reasons — those that bear on an object's value — from the wrong kind, which are merely pragmatic or strategic. After more than two decades of proposals — object-given versus state-given, constitutive accounts of attitudes, fittingness-first approaches, attempts to dissolve the distinction — every move has been made, counterexampled, and refined, and the field seems no closer to consensus. My working hypothesis is that the impasse reflects a deeper fragmentation: some participants are trying to analyze the concept of value, others are giving a theory of normative reasons for attitudes full stop, and still others are doing something closer to conceptual engineering, and these projects pull in different directions. Is that diagnosis right, or is there a different structural feature of the debate that explains the stall? And given your diagnosis, what — if anything — would break the logjam?
