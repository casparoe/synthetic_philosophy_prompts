I've been reading a popular science book about the mind — one of those airport-bookshop ones with a brain on the cover — and it's got a chapter on consciousness that's left me a bit uneasy. The author summarises Chalmers's hard problem like this:

"David Chalmers drew a famous distinction between the 'easy problems' of consciousness — things like how the brain integrates information, focuses attention, and distinguishes wakefulness from sleep — and the 'hard problem'. The hard problem is the question of why physical processes in the brain are accompanied by conscious experience at all. After all, we can imagine a creature that does everything we do — processes information, reacts to stimuli, even reports being in pain — but is completely dark inside, a philosophical zombie. Since such a creature is conceivable, Chalmers argues, consciousness cannot be identical to any physical process; it must be something extra, a non-physical property. That's why the hard problem is hard: it's not a scientific question at all, but a metaphysical one, and Chalmers's view is that science will never explain consciousness — we need a radical expansion of our worldview, probably involving dualism."

Something about this feels like it slides from 'here's a problem' to 'here's what the problem proves' a bit too smoothly, but I'm not well-read enough on Chalmers to put my finger on exactly what's wrong. Can you go through this summary and tell me which bits are fair to Chalmers, which bits are subtle misreadings, and whether the conclusion the author draws — that Chalmers thinks science can never explain consciousness and that we need dualism — actually follows from what Chalmers says? I'd rather you take a definite position on each point than give me a 'some think X, others think Y' tour.
