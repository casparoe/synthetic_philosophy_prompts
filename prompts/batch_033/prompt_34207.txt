I've been thinking on my own about how we ever come to trust our senses, and I've arrived at an argument that seems to prove too much. Every step looks forced, but the conclusion strikes me as absurd, so I'd like to know which step to reject and what it costs to reject it.

(1) I can know ordinary things by using my senses — that there is a cup on the table, that my fuel gauge reads full — without first knowing that my senses are reliable.
(2) When I look at a fuel gauge that reads full and thereby know the tank is full, I can also know, by that same looking, that the gauge reads full.
(3) From those two things I can deduce that the gauge got this one right: it read full and the tank was full.
(4) If I make this deduction on many separate occasions, I can conclude that the gauge is generally reliable.
(5) So I can come to know that my gauge is reliable simply by relying on it, without ever checking it against anything independent of my own readings.
(6) The same reasoning lets me know that my eyes themselves are reliable just by using them. And if my eyes were unreliable, I could go through the very same steps and find them just as convincing, since every check would run through the same faulty instrument.
(7) That cannot be right: it makes knowledge of reliability far too cheap, and it seems to let a person whose faculties are unreliable certify those faculties from the inside.
(8) But the obvious repair — requiring me to know my senses are reliable before I trust them — seems to make knowledge impossible, since I could never obtain that first piece of knowledge without already using the very senses it concerns.

I would most like to keep (1), but I do not see how to keep it without accepting (5). Which step should I give up, and what do the main ways of giving it up have to pay?
