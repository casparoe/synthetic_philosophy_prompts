**Question:** Explain the paradox of deontology. What exactly is the paradox, why is it thought to pose a distinctive problem for deontological theories (as opposed to a general objection to non-consequentialism), and what are the main strategies for resolving or dissolving it?

**Student Answer:**

The paradox of deontology is a problem for deontological theories that hold there are agent-relative constraints against certain types of actions, like killing or lying. The paradox arises because these constraints seem to require agents to refrain from violating them even when doing so would prevent a greater number of violations of the very same constraint by others. For example, a deontologist would say you cannot kill one innocent person even if that would prevent five other people from being killed by someone else. This looks paradoxical because if the whole point of having a constraint against killing is that killing is bad, then you should want there to be less killing overall. But the constraint forces you to allow more killing to happen rather than do one killing yourself. So the constraint appears to undermine its own rationale.

What makes this a distinctive problem for deontology, rather than just a general objection to non-consequentialism, is that the paradox specifically targets the agent-relativity of deontological constraints. A consequentialist can say that you should minimize killings overall, so they face no analogous problem. The paradox shows that once you make reasons agent-relative—once you say that each agent has a special reason not to do the killing themselves, rather than a reason to minimize killings in the world—you end up committed to courses of action that produce worse outcomes by your own lights.

The main strategies for resolving the paradox are threefold. First, one can appeal to the doing/allowing distinction: killing is something you do, whereas failing to prevent killings is something you merely allow, and what you do matters more for your moral record than what you allow. Second, one can appeal to the doctrine of double effect: there is a moral difference between intending harm and merely foreseeing it, and when you violate a constraint you intend the harm, whereas when you refuse to violate a constraint and others kill as a result, you merely foresee their killings. Third, some deontologists simply bite the bullet and accept that morality sometimes requires us to bring about worse overall states of affairs; they argue that this is not a paradox but simply a feature of deontological thinking that consequentialists find hard to accept because they have already assumed that the right action must always produce the best consequences.

In my view, none of these strategies fully works. The doing/allowing distinction cannot bear the weight placed on it because in many cases allowing a death seems just as bad as causing one—for instance, a parent who lets their child starve is no better than one who poisons them. The doctrine of double effect faces similar counterexamples. And biting the bullet just restates the problem without solving it. So the paradox of deontology remains a serious unresolved difficulty for deontological ethics, and it shows that deontology is ultimately an unstable position.
