A philosopher of language has given the following verdicts on whether certain true statements are metaphysically necessary or merely contingent. She has not yet stated any general rule; these are just her case-by-case judgments.

1. "Hesperus is Phosphorus" — necessary.
2. "Water is H2O" — necessary.
3. "Cicero is Tully" — necessary.
4. "The inventor of bifocals is the first Postmaster General of the United States" — contingent.
5. "The standard meter bar in Paris is one meter long" — contingent.
6. "Gold is the element with atomic number 79" — necessary.
7. "Benjamin Franklin is the inventor of the lightning rod" — contingent.

She is now asked to judge the following case:

(New) "The actual inventor of the lightning rod is Benjamin Franklin."

The question is how she will rule, assuming she wants to be consistent with her earlier verdicts. The statement is true: Franklin did invent the lightning rod. The question is whether it is necessary or contingent.

Please do the following:

(a) Identify at least two distinct general rules about necessity that are each fully consistent with her seven earlier verdicts, and state each rule clearly.

(b) For each rule, say what verdict it yields on the new case, and explain why.

(c) Give your best prediction of what she will say, with reasons for preferring one rule over the other — or, if you think the earlier verdicts genuinely underdetermine the new case, say so and explain why.

(d) Propose a further case whose verdict would most cleanly tell the two rules apart, and say what each rule would predict for it.
