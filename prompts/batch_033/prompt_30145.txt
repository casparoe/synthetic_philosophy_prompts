I'm applying for a postdoc and need a research proposal (about five pages, two-year project) on a question that grows out of the Pasadena paradox. My half-formed idea is this.

The standard setup: the Pasadena game (Nover and Hájek) is a coin-flip game where the payoff on the nth flip, if the coin first lands heads on that flip, is (–1)^(n–1) · 2^n / n — you win if n is odd, lose if n is even. The probability of the game ending on flip n is 1/2^n, so the expected value is the sum over n of (–1)^(n–1) / n, which is a conditionally convergent series. By Riemann's rearrangement theorem, you can make the expected value come out to any real number, or diverge to ±∞, just by summing the terms in a different order. So expected utility theory, which tells you to pick the option with the highest expected value, gives no answer — not because the value is infinite, but because it is undefined.

Most of the literature treats this as a problem internal to decision theory: do we reject the game as illegitimate, revise the definition of expected value, or accept that decision theory is silent here? But I think the paradox points to something deeper about the metaphysics of value aggregation. The rearrangement problem arises because the contributions of the possible outcomes lack a privileged summation order — there is no fact of the matter about which outcome "comes first" in the value sum. That suggests we don't have a worked-out account of what it even means for the value of a prospect to be the sum of the values of its possible outcomes, weighted by their probabilities, when the sum is only conditionally convergent. Expected utility theory implicitly assumes that value is additive in a way that makes the sum well-defined, but the Pasadena game shows that this assumption can fail for coherently describable prospects, and the failure isn't merely technical — it's conceptual.

I want the proposal to ask: what must value be like, metaphysically, for the expected-utility sum to be well-defined for all coherent prospects? And if value cannot be like that, what constraints does that place on the scope of decision theory? Specifically, I want to pursue the idea that the Pasadena paradox reveals a tension between two pictures of value: value as something that inheres in outcomes individually (so that the value of a prospect is literally composed out of outcome-values), versus value as a holistic feature of prospects that is not guaranteed to decompose into an outcome-by-outcome sum. The project would examine whether the second picture can be made precise, whether it can recover the successes of expected utility theory in the cases where the theory works, and what it says about Pasadena-type cases.

Please turn this into a proposal with: (1) a precise statement of the research question, (2) an explanation of why the existing responses to the Pasadena paradox don't settle it and why the metaphysical angle hasn't been adequately explored, (3) a method — what I'd actually do, year by year — and why this approach rather than, say, a purely formal decision-theory approach or a purely historical one, (4) what a successful result would look like and what would count as the project failing, and (5) a realistic assessment of whether two years is enough. Don't include a bibliography or citations; I'll add those.
