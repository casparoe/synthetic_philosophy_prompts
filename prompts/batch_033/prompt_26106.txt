I'm a science and ideas journalist preparing a piece on how game-theoretic models of irrational escalation map onto literary depictions of the same phenomenon. I want to read Macbeth through the lens of the dollar auction and the broader theory of escalation of commitment.

The dollar auction, as formalized by Martin Shubik, is a two-player game where participants bid for a dollar, but the second-highest bidder also pays their losing bid — a structure that rationally traps both players into bidding far beyond the dollar's value. The concept has been generalized into the psychological literature on escalation of commitment: sunk costs, loss aversion, and the narrowing of perceived options once a threshold of investment is crossed.

Macbeth has long been read as a study in the logic of escalation, and the play's third act offers what looks like a direct articulation of the trap. After the banquet and the ghost of Banquo, Macbeth says: "I am in blood / Stepp'd in so far, that, should I wade no more, / Returning were as tedious as go o'er." I'd like you to read the whole arc of the play — from the murder of Duncan through the killing of Banquo and Macduff's family — against the formal structure of the dollar auction and escalation theory.

Some specific questions to explore: Where does Macbeth's behavior genuinely conform to the rational-choice logic of the dollar auction (each step being locally optimal given prior investments), and where does the play invoke something the model misses — for instance, the role of prophecy, guilt, or the erosion of Macbeth's own preferences? At what point in the play, if any, does Macbeth cross from a calculated actor into someone whose decision-making is no longer captured by the model? Does the play suggest that the only exit from the escalation trap is the destruction of the player, or does it offer a counter-model (in Malcolm, in Macduff) of what it looks like to refuse to get into the bidding in the first place?

I'm not looking for a summary of the play or a standard character analysis. I want a reading that takes the game theory seriously and uses it to illuminate the play's structure, while also being honest about where the play complicates or resists the model. As a starting point, you might consider brainstorming what the "dollar" is in Macbeth's auction — the crown, security, the fulfillment of the prophecy — and whether the prize changes as the bidding escalates. Please think through the logic step by step, and treat this as a piece of serious criticism that could run in a magazine like Nautilus or Aeon.
