Analyze the US-Soviet nuclear arms race (roughly 1949 through the SALT I treaty in 1972) through the lens of superrationality. For context, superrationality is the idea that in perfectly symmetric games, each player should choose the action they'd want the other player to choose, because identical reasoning in identical situations will produce identical decisions — so you can't outsmart a copy of yourself.

I want you to do two things. First, map the arms race as a game: who the players are, what the options were (build more nukes? disarm? pursue arms control?), what the payoff structure looked like, and what counts as cooperation vs defection. Second, use the episode to test superrationality as a concept. Where does it get traction — for instance, does it explain why mutual deterrence was stable even though both sides would have preferred a world with no nukes? And where does it break down — why didn't superrational reasoning lead to unilateral disarmament, and why did verification and inspection regimes matter so much if the logic is supposed to be purely a priori? End with your assessment of whether the arms race is a counterexample to superrationality, or whether it just shows that the symmetry conditions superrationality needs rarely hold in practice.
