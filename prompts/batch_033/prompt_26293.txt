The debate over the conditional analysis of dispositions has been running for decades, and from where I sit it looks stuck. The simple conditional analysis says that an object is disposed to manifest M under stimulus conditions C just in case, if C were to obtain, the object would M. But we have known at least since C.B. Martin that finks—processes that destroy the disposition precisely when C occurs—can make the conditional true when the disposition is absent. And we have known at least since Mark Johnston that masks (or antidotes) can make the conditional false when the disposition is present, by blocking the manifestation without removing the disposition. The literature has cycled through an array of repairs: David Lewis's reformed conditional analysis, which appeals to the object's retaining an intrinsic causal basis; analyses that build in causal independence clauses; normality-based and comparative-similarity approaches; and various increasingly sophisticated counterfactual refinements. Each repair is met with a new round of ingenious counterexamples, and the dialectic has settled into a rhythm that feels less like convergence and more like trench warfare. Meanwhile, another camp has given up on the conditional analysis entirely: dispositional essentialists take dispositions as fundamental features of properties, and primitivists treat them as unanalyzable. These groups have not been persuaded by the analysts, and the analysts have not been persuaded by them.

I am not asking you to resolve this debate or to propose yet another analysis. I am asking you to diagnose it. What kind of disagreement is this, really? Is it a substantive metaphysical disagreement about the nature of causal powers—about whether dispositions are reducible to categorical properties plus laws, or whether they are themselves fundamental features of reality? Or has it become, at least in part, a verbal dispute about what the word "disposition" means and what standards an "analysis" must meet? Could it be that the two camps are tacitly answering different questions—one a semantic question about the truth conditions of disposition ascriptions, the other a metaphysical question about the grounding of causal powers—and have been mistaking them for a single dispute? Or is the impasse best explained in some other way? Ground your diagnosis in what each side actually argues, not in a caricature. Then tell me: given your diagnosis, what (if anything) would count as progress? A new conceptual distinction, a formal result, a reframing of the question, or perhaps the honest conclusion that the question as posed is ill-posed and should be retired.
