The Knobe effect refers to a robust experimental finding: when people evaluate whether a side effect was brought about intentionally, their judgments shift depending on the moral valence of the outcome. In the best-known vignette, a CEO adopts a profit-maximizing policy that also harms the environment. Participants overwhelmingly say the CEO harmed the environment intentionally. But when the same structure is used with a beneficial side effect — the policy helps the environment — participants deny that the CEO helped the environment intentionally. The only thing that changes is whether the outcome is morally bad or morally good. The asymmetry has been replicated across numerous variations, cultures, and populations.

The controversy is over what this asymmetry reveals. One family of views, associated with Joshua Knobe, holds that the effect is genuinely conceptual: the folk concept of intentional action itself incorporates a normative dimension, such that whether an outcome counts as "intentional" depends in part on whether the agent can be faulted for it. A second family of views treats the effect as a performance error. On such accounts, people's underlying concept of intentional action is purely descriptive, but the experimental task triggers pragmatic confounds — for instance, participants may interpret "intentionally" as a way of expressing blame, or they may confuse the question of whether the CEO did something intentionally with the question of whether he deserves punishment. A third approach grants that the effect is conceptual but argues that it tracks something narrower than moral valence per se — for example, whether the agent's attitude toward the side effect violates a norm, or whether the agent foresaw the outcome with a mental state that is itself morally objectionable.
