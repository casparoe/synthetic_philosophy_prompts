Read Steven Spielberg's Minority Report through the lens of Newcomb's problem. The Precrime system puts the precogs in the role of a nearly infallible predictor: a prediction about what a person will do is in place before the person acts, and the state intervenes on the strength of that prediction. Treat the would-be perpetrator as facing something structurally like a Newcomb choice. In Newcomb's problem, evidential and causal decision theory split over whether to take one box or both; in the film, the analogous split concerns whether a person who learns of a reliable prediction of their future criminal act should reason as if their present choice is evidence about what was predicted, or should treat the prediction as fixed and choose, from the present forward, the action that minimizes harm. Develop the analogy in detail: identify which characters reason in an evidentialist way and which reason causally; work through how the film's picture of prediction, intervention, and moral responsibility maps onto the two-box structure; and be precise about where the analogy fails—for instance, Newcomb's predictor predicts the very choice at issue, whereas Precrime predicts an act rather than a choice, and the film introduces third-party enforcement, legal guilt, and the possibility of a minority report that have no counterpart in the original puzzle. Then assess what the film adds to the philosophical debate that the bare puzzle does not: does it dramatize a live possibility that decision theory leaves abstract, and does it in the end vindicate one of the two styles of reasoning, or show the dispute to be miscast? The reading should be faithful in both directions—neither reducing the film to an illustration of the puzzle nor distorting the puzzle to fit the film—and it should close by saying where the film genuinely resists the Newcomb reading.
