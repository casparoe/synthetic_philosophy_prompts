I serve on my university's Research Ethics and External Impact Committee. Over the past several years we have built up a set of precedents on cases where a researcher proposed to act unilaterally — publishing, releasing, or deploying something without waiting for institutional or broader consensus — in situations where the action could affect people outside the university. A difficult case is coming up next month, and I want to understand what our own precedents commit us to. Here are the five clearest precedents we have:

1. A computer security researcher discovered a vulnerability in a widely used open-source authentication library that underpins thousands of websites. She emailed the maintainer, waited the standard ninety days with no response, and then published the vulnerability along with proof-of-concept code on her personal blog. We approved.

2. A synthetic biology lab developed a novel method for assembling viral vectors that reduced the cost of gene therapy research by an order of magnitude. The principal investigator wanted to post the full protocol on a preprint server immediately, arguing that any delay would slow medical research. The method also substantially lowered the barrier to synthesizing pathogens. We rejected; we required prior consultation with the institutional biosafety committee and the funding agency.

3. A team of social scientists trained a classifier that could predict, from public Twitter posts alone, whether a user was likely to be diagnosed with depression within the next year. They had validated the model against survey data collected with participants' consent. They wanted to release the model publicly as a tool for early-intervention research. We approved, but only on the condition that the model carry a license prohibiting commercial use and that the release be accompanied by a public explanation of its error rates and known limitations.

4. A doctoral student wrote a script that would automatically submit public-records requests to every municipality in the state for police misconduct records, compile the responses, and publish them in a searchable database. The student argued that the information was already public by law and that compiling it served accountability. Several committee members raised concerns that the volume of simultaneous requests would be received as harassment and that the database would include unadjudicated allegations. We rejected.

5. An environmental scientist collected water quality data from rivers downstream of industrial sites, some of which she accessed by crossing private land without permission. The data showed contamination exceeding legal limits at several sites. She wanted to publish the findings and the full dataset immediately, citing public health. We approved the publication of the findings and aggregate data but required her to withhold the exact coordinates and raw data until the trespassing issue could be resolved with the university's legal office.

The new case: A research group in our computer science department has trained a large language model on a corpus that includes copyrighted books, academic papers behind paywalls, and personal blog posts — all scraped from the web without licensing or permission. The model is, by their account, the best-performing system on several biomedical reasoning benchmarks, and they argue that releasing it publicly could accelerate drug discovery and improve clinical decision support. They want to release the model weights and a paper describing the training methodology. They have not consulted the university's legal office, the publishers whose works were used, or the authors of the scraped blog posts. The training is already complete; the question before us is only about release.

I want to predict how the committee will rule if we are being consistent with our own precedents. Specifically: reconstruct the rule or rules that best fit our five earlier decisions — there should be more than one plausible candidate. For each candidate rule, state what it predicts about the new case. Give your best guess about which rule we are actually running, with the reasons for preferring it, and name a further case — real or hypothetical — whose outcome would cleanly tell the remaining candidate rules apart. If our precedents genuinely underdetermine the answer, say what principle we would be adopting with each possible decision on the new case.
