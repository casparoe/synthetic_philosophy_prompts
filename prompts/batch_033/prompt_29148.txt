On the morning of 1 November 1755, an earthquake estimated at around magnitude 8.5 to 9 struck the seafloor off Lisbon, followed by a tsunami and then a fire that together destroyed most of the city; tens of thousands died, many of them in churches during All Saints' Day Mass. The disaster became the central test case for the philosophical optimism associated with Leibniz — the view that an omnipotent, omniscient, and perfectly good God actualizes the best of all possible worlds. Voltaire attacked the view in a poem on the disaster and in Candide; Rousseau replied, arguing that much of the destruction was humanly amplified and that providence is consistent with a lawful natural order.

Analyze this episode through the lens of Leibniz's best-of-all-possible-worlds theodicy. I know the broad historical outline; what I want is a careful, two-direction analysis. First, reconstruct what the theodicy actually commits Leibniz to about an event like this, working through the machinery he had available: the principle of the best; evil as privation; the distinction between God's antecedent and consequent will; the preference for simple, general laws over constant miraculous correction; and the claim that a local evil can be a necessary condition of a whole that only God can survey. For each, say what it would have to establish to cover Lisbon, and what it implies God should have done — permit the disaster as part of the best whole, or intervene at the price of a less orderly world. Keep separate the strong claim that this is the best possible world from the weaker claim that no particular evil can be shown to be gratuitous. Second, identify where the lens breaks: the features of the case — the scale, the distribution of suffering, worshippers killed in church on a holy day, the apparent pointlessness — that the machinery cannot obviously accommodate, and whether Voltaire's and Rousseau's arguments engage the actual Leibnizian position or only a vulgarized slogan. Then run the analysis in the other direction: does Lisbon refute the best-of-all-possible-worlds view, shift the burden of proof onto the theodicist, expose the position as unfalsifiable, or reveal that the critics were demanding something the theory never promised? Conclude with an honest assessment of which nearby position, if any, fares better — a free-will defense, a soul-making theodicy, skeptical theism, or abandoning theodicy altogether — and what the episode ultimately shows about whether the best-of-all-possible-worlds view remains a live option.
