I spent most of yesterday evening arguing with a friend about the repugnant conclusion. We covered the standard territory — Parfit's mere addition paradox, the move from total utilitarianism to the repugnant conclusion, the various escape routes (averagism, the critical level view, person-affecting restrictions, lexical views) — and by the end I realized we were stuck: we couldn't tell whether we were disagreeing about which axiology is correct or about whether our intuitions at the margin are trustworthy. So I'd like you to give me a credence, decomposed.

The proposition: the repugnant conclusion is true. That is, for any population of lives of very high quality, there exists some sufficiently larger population of lives that are merely worth living such that the existence of the latter would be impersonally better than the existence of the former.

Please decompose your credence into at least the following sub-questions. For each, give a probability and a short justification:

1. What is the probability that an impersonal totalist axiology is correct — that the value of an outcome is an increasing function of total well-being, with no discount for additional lives, no averaging, no lexical priority for higher-quality lives, and no person-affecting restriction?

2. Conditional on totalism being correct, what is the probability that the repugnant conclusion actually follows? (Some argue that the iteration from "barely worth living" to "vast numbers of barely worth living lives" requires a continuity or separability assumption that may fail, or that at sufficiently large numbers the lives would cease to be worth living. Others argue that totalism straightforwardly entails the conclusion.)

3. Conditional on totalism being false, what is the probability that the repugnant conclusion is nevertheless true under the correct axiology? (Arrhenius and others have argued that very weak and widely accepted conditions — like avoidance of the sadistic conclusion and some form of dominance — still force something like the repugnant conclusion for any theory.)

4. What is the probability that our intuitive revulsion at the repugnant conclusion is tracking a genuine axiological fact, as opposed to arising from scope insensitivity, a numeracy heuristic, status quo bias, or some other epistemically distorting factor?

Then combine these into your final credence. After that, I want you to tell me which sub-question your final number is most sensitive to: if you could learn the answer to exactly one of them with certainty, which would shift your credence the most?

My friend ended the evening at around 0.2; I'm at roughly 0.55. Given your decomposition, where do you think our disagreement most likely lives — that is, which sub-question are we probably disagreeing about?
