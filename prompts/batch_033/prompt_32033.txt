I've been reading about the discursive dilemma in judgment aggregation, and I find myself returning to an analogy that seems to do a great deal of work in legal and political philosophy — and that the dilemma, if sound, appears to undermine. The analogy is that a deliberative body (a jury, a parliament, a committee, a board of directors) is, or should be treatable as, a single rational agent. An individual collects evidence, weighs it, reasons from premises to conclusions, and is expected to maintain consistency across her beliefs. A group, on this picture, does the same: it takes in testimony, deliberates, and produces a verdict or a policy, and we expect that verdict to be the coherent output of a rational process rather than a mere voting tally. The analogy licenses some heavy conclusions: that a corporation can be held criminally liable as an entity, that a court's ruling expresses a coherent legal doctrine rather than a voting accident, that "the will of the people" is a meaningful concept in democratic theory.

But the discursive dilemma — the fact that majority voting on logically connected premises can produce an inconsistent set of group-level judgments even when every individual voter is perfectly consistent — seems to drive a wedge straight through this analogy. A group of impeccably rational individuals can, by the perfectly reasonable procedure of premise-by-premise majority voting, yield a group output that no rational individual would ever hold.

I spent my career in the physical sciences, where we are trained to be suspicious of analogies that treat a composite system as though it were a simple one. A pile of sand is not a single grain; a gas is not a molecule. The group-as-agent analogy seems to be doing precisely this kind of work: it treats the group as a unified system whose properties are straightforwardly inherited from the rationality of its members. The discursive dilemma suggests that the inheritance fails in a systematic and interesting way, and I suspect the analogy has been quietly importing features of individual agency that groups simply do not possess.

I'd like a committed analysis — one that takes a definite position rather than surveying the options. How far does the analogy between a group and a single rational agent actually hold? What maps onto what (individual beliefs map to member votes? individual reasoning to deliberation procedures? individual consistency requirements to...?), and where does the mapping break down irreparably? Which features of individual agency does the analogy silently import that groups demonstrably lack? And does the conclusion — that groups can be treated as unified agents for purposes of liability, doctrinal coherence, or democratic will — survive once the discursive dilemma is fully on the table, or does the dilemma force us to abandon the analogy and with it the conclusions it was carrying?
