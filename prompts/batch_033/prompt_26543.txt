I'm a PhD student in evolutionary biology working on signaling theory, and I've gotten tangled up in an argument about the handicap principle that I can't find my way out of. I'd be grateful for a careful diagnosis.

The handicap principle, as Zahavi originally proposed it and as Grafen later formalized, says roughly this: for a signal to be honest — for it to carry reliable information about some unobservable quality of the sender — the signal must be costly to produce. The cost is what keeps low-quality senders from faking it; they cannot afford the investment, so only high-quality senders produce the signal, and receivers can trust it.

Here is the argument that is bothering me:

(1) A signal is honest only if it is costly to produce, such that low-quality senders cannot profitably send it. (Handicap principle)

(2) For a receiver to treat a signal as honest, the receiver must have some basis for believing that the cost was in fact incurred — otherwise the receiver would be taking the signal's honesty on faith.

(3) The mere production of the signal does not, by itself, guarantee that the cost was incurred. A low-quality sender might, through some developmental accident or environmental fluke, manage to produce the signal without paying the full cost (imagine a sick peacock that happens upon an abundant food patch and grows a large tail one season despite poor genes).

(4) Therefore, the receiver needs evidence, beyond the signal itself, that the cost was paid. (From 2 and 3)

(5) This evidence constitutes a further signal — call it a cost-certification signal.

(6) By the handicap principle (1), this further signal must itself be costly to produce if it is to be honest.

(7) This generates an infinite regress: every honest signal requires a prior honest signal certifying its costliness, which requires another, and so on.

(8) An infinite regress of costly signals is biologically impossible — organisms have finite energy budgets and finite time.

(9) Yet honest signaling plainly exists in nature (alarm calls, mate-choice displays, begging nestlings, and so on).

(10) Therefore, the handicap principle cannot be the correct account of honest signaling.

I find the conclusion absurd — the handicap principle is one of the most successful ideas in behavioral ecology — but I cannot locate the misstep. The regress feels like it gets its grip from something about the epistemology of the receiver: the receiver needs to know the signal is costly, and that knowledge seems to require a further signal. But perhaps this is the wrong way to think about what the receiver is doing. I am not sure.
