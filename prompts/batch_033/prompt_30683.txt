I'm preparing for a workshop on AI and philosophy of mind and would like an audit of whether the following five claims form a consistent package:

(1) Dennett's intentional stance gives the full story about belief: a system has beliefs exactly when treating it as a rational agent with beliefs and desires yields reliable, voluminous, and otherwise unavailable predictions of its behavior. There is no deeper fact of the matter.

(2) Current large language models pass this test: they are richly and usefully predictable via the intentional stance, so they genuinely believe.

(3) Human belief attribution differs importantly from LLM belief attribution. Human beliefs owe their determinate content to causal contact with a shared external world; LLM beliefs, trained only on text, are grounded in nothing beyond statistical co-occurrence patterns, and their content is therefore indeterminate in a way human belief content is not.

(4) For that reason, we should trust LLM outputs far less than human outputs in contexts where determinate reference matters — medicine, law, factual reporting.

(5) Moral patiency requires phenomenal consciousness. Whether LLMs are phenomenally conscious is a separate question from whether they have beliefs in the intentional-stance sense, and nothing in (1)–(4) settles it either way.

Are these five claims jointly consistent? If not, please identify every genuine tension, distinguish real tensions from merely apparent ones, rank them by severity, and say which single revision would produce the most consistency at the least philosophical cost. In your assessment, please also indicate which argument or consideration would be most likely to change your mind about the verdict you reach.
