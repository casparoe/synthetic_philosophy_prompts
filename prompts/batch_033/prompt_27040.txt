I'm teaching Middlemarch in a course on Victorian literature and philosophy, and I want to do a session on George Eliot's religious views as they play out in the novel. Eliot translated Feuerbach's The Essence of Christianity and was deeply influenced by his idea that theology is really anthropology — that God is a projection of human qualities and the real object of religious feeling should be humanity itself. I want to read the novel, especially Dorothea Brooke's arc, as a dramatization of this "religion of humanity." But I'm worried the novel is more ambivalent than that: Dorothea's religious longings don't just get neatly redirected into humanism, and characters like Bulstrode show the ugly side of religious self-deception in a way that might complicate the Feuerbachian picture. Can you give me a reading that takes both Eliot's philosophy and the novel seriously, saying where the novel vindicates the religion of humanity, where it resists it, and what the novel shows that the philosophical position on its own can't capture? I'm especially interested in whether the novel's ending — Dorothea's "unhistoric acts" and her marriage to Ladislaw — is supposed to be a triumph of the religion of humanity or a quiet defeat.
