Student's answer:

"Lipton distinguishes between the loveliness of an explanation and its likeliness. An explanation is lovely to the extent that it possesses explanatory virtues such as simplicity, scope, precision, and unification — roughly, it would provide deep understanding if it were true. An explanation is likely to the extent that it is probably true given the available evidence. Lipton's key claim is that these two properties can come apart: a lovely explanation need not be likely, and a likely explanation need not be lovely.

This distinction matters for defending inference to the best explanation (IBE) against the Bayesian challenge. The Bayesian challenge holds that IBE is merely Bayesian updating in disguise — that when we 'infer the best explanation,' we are really just selecting the hypothesis with the highest posterior probability, which is fully determined by priors and likelihoods within the Bayesian calculus. If this were correct, IBE would be redundant as a distinct form of inference.

Lipton's response turns on the claim that loveliness is a reliable guide to likeliness — this is sometimes called the 'Voltaire maneuver.' Because we cannot directly perceive which explanation is most probably true, we rely on explanatory loveliness as our epistemic criterion. So when we practice IBE, we are in fact inferring the likeliest explanation; we simply use loveliness as our fallible heuristic for identifying it. The distinction between loveliness and likeliness therefore shows that IBE and Bayesianism converge on the same target — both aim at the hypothesis most probably true given the evidence. The difference is merely methodological: IBE uses explanatory considerations as a heuristic shortcut, while Bayesianism employs explicit probability calculations. Lipton thus preserves IBE as a practically indispensable form of inference that is fully compatible with Bayesian confirmation theory, and the charge of collapse is defused."
