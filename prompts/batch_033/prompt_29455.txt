Write a villanelle about Hume's account of the standard of taste. The two refrains should be the poem's own formulations of the two claims whose tension drives the essay: that beauty is not a feature of the object itself but something felt by the mind that beholds it; and that some judgments of taste are nonetheless better than others. Let the alternation of the refrains enact the way these two claims keep circling one another, and let the final quatrain bring them together as Hume does — not by surrendering either claim, but through the idea that the standard of taste is the joint verdict of ideal critics: people of strong sense and delicacy of sentiment, improved by practice, perfected by comparison, and freed from prejudice. In the middle stanzas, work in at least three of the following: the test of time and the survival of some works across ages and nations; the circularity worry that ideal critics are partly identified by the very taste they are supposed to ground; the way practice and comparison refine perception so that finer qualities become noticeable; and the point that even a real standard leaves room for legitimate differences of temperament and culture. The poem should be accurate enough that someone who knows the essay would recognize Hume's position and its genuine tensions, not a caricature of subjectivism or a dogmatic objectivism. Then add a section titled Commentary, about 200 to 300 words, explaining the formal choices and where the poem follows Hume, and flagging any point where the poem compresses or takes a side on a disputed reading.
