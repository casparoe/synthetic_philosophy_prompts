I've been re-reading the Pensées for a discussion group, and I keep getting stuck on the internal logic of the famous wager fragment—specifically, the move Pascal makes when his imagined interlocutor concedes the force of the expected-value argument but then protests that he simply cannot bring himself to believe. The passage (in the Trotter numbering, it's §233, the "Infinite—nothing" pensée) runs roughly as follows: Pascal first argues that we are incapable of knowing by reason alone whether God exists, but that we are nevertheless forced to wager, because to abstain from choosing is effectively to bet against God. He then frames the choice as a decision problem with infinite stakes: if you bet on God and God exists, you gain an infinite reward; if you bet on God and God does not exist, you lose at most some finite worldly pleasures; if you bet against God, you stand to lose either an infinite reward or nothing. The expected-value logic seems to point unambiguously toward wagering for God. So far, so familiar from the decision-theoretic discussions on LessWrong and in the older Yudkowsky sequences.

But then the interlocutor says, in effect, "I agree that the wager compels me, but I am so made that I cannot believe—what would you have me do?" And Pascal's reply is not to offer additional evidence or to revisit the structure of the decision matrix. Instead, he tells the interlocutor to act as if he already believed: to take holy water, to have masses said, to follow the rituals, and—here is the phrase that trips me up—that this will naturally bring about belief and will "make you stupid" (cela vous fera croire et vous abêtira). I want to understand what kind of argument this is supposed to be. On one reading, it is straightforwardly pragmatic: Pascal thinks that belief is at least partly under the control of habituation, so he is prescribing a behavioral regimen that will causally produce the state of belief that the wager has shown to be rationally required. But calling it a regimen that will "make you stupid" seems to concede that the belief itself is not reached by rational means—that the whole apparatus of expected-value reasoning is being used to justify a process that bypasses the very faculty that was doing the reasoning. Is this a performative contradiction, or is it a psychologically sophisticated admission that the faculty of reason is insufficient to motivate assent on its own, and that the will must be enlisted through non-rational channels? And if the latter, does the wager argument collapse into a kind of indirect consequentialism, where the argument's job is not to justify belief directly but to justify the decision to take steps that will cause belief, steps that themselves lie outside the scope of epistemic rationality?

I've seen decision-theoretic reconstructions of the wager that treat it as a straightforward dominance or expected-utility argument, and I've seen discussions of Pascal's mugging and infinities that treat the wager as a cautionary tale about unbounded utility functions. But I haven't seen much that grapples seriously with this pivot—the move from "the numbers say you should believe" to "here is a technology for manufacturing belief, and it works by making you less rational." Can you walk me through what is actually happening at this step in the text, and whether it undercuts the wager or reveals something deeper about Pascal's conception of the relationship between reason, habit, and faith? I'm particularly interested in whether this move can be defended without abandoning the premise that the wager is an argument aimed at a reasoning agent, and what someone who accepts the wager's decisional logic but is skeptical of the habituation step should conclude about Pascal's overall project.
