I'm a retired chemist — spent forty years doing what Hume would call induction, though in the lab we called it calibration and curve-fitting — and I've recently gotten into philosophy, mostly reading on my own. Lately I've been working through Hume's Enquiry and then moved on to Goodman's new riddle of induction with the grue predicate (roughly: a thing is grue if it's examined before some future time t and found green, or not so examined and blue). The thing that keeps nagging me is weather Goodman is really adding something to Hume or just restating him in fancier vocabulary. Hume says our expectation that the future will resemble the past is a matter of custom or habit, not reason. Goodman comes along and says the real problem is not whether the future will resemble the past but which of the many possible regularities we project — since someone raised on grue and bleen would find their inductions just as natural as ours. The part I keep tripping on is that Goodman seems to undercut the very idea of a natural or projectible regularity, which Hume more or less takes for granted when he talks about constant conjunction. So I can't tell whether grue is a problem Hume's account already has the resources to shrug off, or whether it exposes a hole he never noticed.

So here's my question, and it's deliberately counterfactual: if Hume had somehow seen the grue paradox, what do you think he would of made of it? Would he have treated it as a refutation of his account, a harmless redescription, or something his own view actually predicts? I'm not after what Hume scholars have actually written; I want a careful, best-guess reconstruction of how his actual commitments would have pushed him.

Think carefully, and start by brainstorming the distinct positions Hume could have taken before settling on the one you find most defensible.
