A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about consciousness. She has read a lot of Dennett and keeps returning to the idea that the Cartesian theater is a deep mistake — that there is no central place in the brain where experiences "come together" and no single timeline along which conscious events are ordered. I pushed her on a series of cases to see where she draws the lines. Here are her verdicts:

1. **Color phi.** Two dots are flashed in quick succession at different locations, the first red and the second green. Subjects report seeing a single moving dot that changes from red to green midway. She says there is no fact about when the color change is consciously experienced. The brain constructs the narrative after both flashes have occurred, and asking "but when did you actually experience the color change?" presupposes a theater.

2. **Cutaneous rabbit.** Taps are delivered to the wrist, then near the elbow, then near the shoulder in rapid succession. Subjects feel taps hopping evenly up the arm. She says the conscious experience of the second tap is not located at the actual stimulation site. The brain retrospectively constructs the intermediate sensations. There is no "real" conscious experience at the wrist that gets overwritten by a later one.

3. **Libet timing.** Subjects watch a rapidly spinning clock and report the moment they first became aware of the urge to move. The readiness potential in the brain precedes the reported time by several hundred milliseconds. She says "when did the conscious urge occur?" has no determinate answer. Consciousness is not a sequence of datable events with a single clock.

4. **Change blindness.** Two versions of a photograph alternate with a brief blank between them, and one contains a large change. Subjects often fail to notice it despite insisting they have a rich, detailed visual experience of the scene. She says they were not conscious of the pre-change detail. The feeling of having a detailed conscious experience of the whole scene is itself part of the narrative — and it is false.

5. **Backward masking.** A target shape is flashed briefly and immediately followed by a mask pattern. Subjects report seeing only the mask. She says the target never becomes conscious. There is no brief conscious experience of the target that gets erased by the mask; the mask prevents the target from entering the narrative at all.

6. **Inattentional blindness.** Subjects count basketball passes in a video and miss a person in a gorilla suit walking through the scene. She says they were not conscious of the gorilla. It never made it into the narrative, not even for a moment.

I think I understand the negative project: she rejects any view on which there is a definite place and time where a conscious experience occurs, and she rejects the idea that we are authoritative about the contents of our own consciousness. But I am less clear on the positive view. What does she think consciousness is, such that these verdicts follow?

Here is the case I wish I had asked her about. A person with blindsight is trained over many sessions to guess the location of a stimulus in their blind field. Initially they perform at chance and report no conscious experience. After training, their accuracy improves to well above chance, but they still report no conscious experience. The experimenters then add a twist: after each guess, the person must also press a button indicating their confidence. At first, confidence ratings do not correlate with accuracy. But after hundreds more trials, the confidence ratings begin to track accuracy — when the person is right, they tend to be more confident — even though they continue to insist they do not consciously see anything.

What would she say about this person? Are they conscious of the stimuli at any point in the training? If she says yes, what changed, and why does verbal report not settle the matter? If she says no, what does she make of the metacognitive access? If she says the question has no determinate answer, what distinguishes this case from the color phi, where she was willing to say there is no fact about when the experience occurred but did not deny there was an experience at all?

I am less interested in the bare verdict than in the rule she seems to be running. Lay out the candidate rules that fit her first six verdicts, say what each predicts about the blindsight case, give your best guess about which rule she holds and why, and tell me what further case would cleanly separate them.
