I'm a retired physical chemist who's recently gotten into philosophy, and I've been working through social choice theory. I'm fine with the formal side, but I keep tripping over the way people talk about the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem. The usual gloss is that every nontrivial voting method is manipulable, so voters are always just strategically misrepresenting their preferences and democracy is kind of a sham. But the theorem itself, as I understand it, is narrower: with at least three alternatives and an unrestricted domain of preference orderings, any strategy-proof social choice function is either dictatorial or not onto — or something like that. So here's what I'd like: give me an example of a conceptual issue that comes up when you move from the theorem to that sweeping conclusion that all voting is strategic. I'm especially interested in a concrete scenario where a voting rule is manipulable in the technical sense but the manipulation isnt the kind of lying-about-your-preferences we normally worry about, or the reverse, where a rule avoids the technical problem only by quietly giving up something else that matters. And please tell me what would change your mind: which argument or counterexample would make you revise or abandon the example, and where you think your reasoning is most likely to fail.
