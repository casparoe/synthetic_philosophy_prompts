I don't have any formal training in philosophy, but I have spent a good deal of my own time thinking about how a rational person ought to relate to their own future beliefs. The principle I keep coming back to is van Fraassen's Reflection principle. As I understand it, the principle says that my current confidence in a claim, conditional on the supposition that at some later time I will assign credence x to that claim, should itself be x: if I knew now that I would later be 80% confident in something, I should now be 80% confident in it. The intuitive idea is that my future self is a kind of forecaster about my own evidence, and, barring special reasons for distrust, I should defer to that future self the way I would defer to someone I consider well-informed. The usual caveat is that this is meant to hold only when I have no reason to expect my future self to be irrational, misinformed, cognitively impaired, or otherwise unreliable; it is not supposed to tell me to adopt beliefs I would hold after being brainwashed or after taking a pill that scrambles my reasoning.

I have gone back and forth about whether this is a genuine requirement of rationality or only a useful rule of thumb that happens to work in ordinary cases. On one side, there is something appealing in the thought that I should not regard my own future, better-informed self as systematically mistaken; on the other, I can think of cases where I would want to refuse to defer, and I am unsure whether those cases merely fall outside the principle's intended scope or whether they show it is not a real norm at all. I would like your actual credence that the principle, understood with the usual idealizing caveats, is a correct requirement of rationality — not a hedge, but a number and the reasoning behind it.
