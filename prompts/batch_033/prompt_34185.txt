I keep noticing a move in moral arguments that I do not have a name for, and I would like to know whether it has one. In a debate about whether we owe more to our families than to strangers, one side will dismiss the other's partiality as in-group favoritism — a bias — while describing their own impartiality as a considered moral preference. In a debate about whether an existing inequality is unjust, one side will dismiss the other's resistance to change as status quo bias, while treating their own support for change as a principled commitment rather than as a bias in favor of novelty. In each case, the choice between the labels 'bias' and 'preference' seems to track which side the judgment supports rather than any feature of the judgment itself, and the labels do the work of an argument even though no criterion is offered for what distinguishes a bias from a preference. I am not asking whether either side in these examples is right. I am asking about the argumentative move itself. I know it is somewhere near the bias blind spot and naive realism, and it functions rather like a debunking argument, but none of those seems to name exactly the asymmetric application of 'bias' and 'preference' to disqualify one moral judgment while protecting its rival. Does this move have an established name? If it does, how exactly is it distinguished from the bias blind spot, from naive realism, and from an ordinary debunking argument? If it does not, what should it be called, and what are its boundaries — for example, must the two judgments issue from the same kind of psychological process, or is the asymmetry in labeling alone enough? A bare name would not satisfy me; I am after the precise carving of the concept.
