I'm a software engineer running a monthly reading-and-discussion group at my company — about 14 people, mostly engineers and designers, meeting for 90 minutes over lunch. We've been working through parts of Nudge and some essays on dark patterns and choice architecture, and for the next session I want to get people out of discussion mode and into something experiential.

Design an in-class activity that makes participants feel the force of paternalism and nudging — specifically, the slide from helpful default-setting into something that starts to feel like manipulation or disrespect for autonomy. I want them to experience being on the receiving end of a nudge they didn't ask for, and also to experience designing a nudge for someone else and noticing the pull toward overreach. The activity should make the conceptual tension show up in what happens, not in what I say afterwards.

Constraints:

- 45–50 minutes for the activity itself, leaving the rest of the session for debrief.
- No lecture up front; rules should be explainable in under five minutes.
- Materials should be things I can print or bring from home — cards, handouts, a whiteboard — nothing that requires coding or screens.
- If there are stakes or payoffs, keep them symbolic (points, rankings, bragging rights) rather than real money.

For the debrief, I'd like:

- A mapping from specific moments in the activity to concepts like libertarian paternalism, default effects, transparency, the difference between nudges that enlist System 1 versus those that engage System 2, and the objection that nudging treats adults like children.
- A note on what predictably goes wrong the first time someone runs this and how to handle it.
- An honest account of which feature of the philosophical debate the activity cannot reproduce and what I'll need to supply myself in the discussion afterward.

Finally, I'd like the response to indicate how confident it is in its claims and to single out the point it's least sure of.
