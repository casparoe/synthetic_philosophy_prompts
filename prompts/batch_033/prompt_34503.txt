I am a PhD student in science and technology policy and serve on my university's emerging-technology review panel. The panel evaluates faculty, student, and industry-partner proposals that raise dual-use or socially disruptive concerns: synthetic biology, open-weight machine learning models, pathogen research, autonomous systems, and similar. We can approve, require modifications, or recommend against a project, but we cannot enforce our decisions. The university has no formal authority to stop research, and researchers can and sometimes do proceed without our approval by moving the work elsewhere, taking outside funding, or relabeling the project.

A recurring problem is what Bostrom, Douglas, and Sandberg (2016) call the unilateralist's curse. In its canonical form, several actors each can unilaterally take an action that imposes consequences on everyone, and each, judging independently, may act even though the group as a whole would have preferred that no one acted. The variant I see most often in our docket is this: each actor would prefer the action not be taken at all, but each reasons that if they refrain someone else will likely act anyway, and so prefers to be the one who acts — to shape the outcome, capture the credit, or avoid being left behind. The result is that the action is taken even though nearly everyone involved would have preferred that no one took it. Research groups tell us, in so many words, "if we don't do this, someone else will." And our own review process sits inside the strategic landscape rather than above it: a panel recommendation against a project can perversely accelerate it by making the researchers feel they have less to lose from going outside official channels.

What I need is not another set of criteria for evaluating the underlying research; we already have those. I need a decision procedure for evaluating our own position in the strategic landscape, so that our recommendations do not reliably produce the very outcome the panel exists to prevent.

Please write this as a document we could realistically adopt as a standing operating procedure, for panel members who have never heard of the unilateralist's curse and have no background in game theory or decision theory. Define technical terms on first use, and keep the procedure usable under time pressure. Structure it in exactly these sections:

1. Screening questions (roughly 400 words). Diagnostic questions that determine whether a proposal before us has the structure of a unilateralist's curse. Each question should be answerable yes/no or from evidence we can gather. For each question, say what evidence would count as a decisive yes and what would count as a decisive no. State the threshold at which we conclude the curse is likely in play, and state explicitly when we exit to our standard review process because the curse is not in play.

2. The procedure (roughly 600 words). A sequence of steps to run when screening flags a curse situation. For each step, state what action we take, what information we need, what the step is meant to achieve, and how we will know whether it worked. Include steps that change what we communicate and to whom, steps that change our own decision rule, and steps that try to change the structure of the situation so the curse dissolves. Be honest about which steps are likely to work and which are aspirational.

3. Worked cases (roughly 500 words each). Apply the screening questions and the procedure to the following two cases, walking through each step and stating the outcome.

(a) A lab in our university wants to release an open-weight model for guiding chemical synthesis, with plausible dual-use application to toxic-agent discovery. The panel's initial inclination is to recommend against release. We have credible information that three well-resourced groups elsewhere are building comparable tools, at least one of which intends to release within the year regardless of what we do.

(b) A historian on our campus proposes to digitize and publish a large corpus of declassified intelligence documents that includes names of living sources. The panel worries about harm to those individuals, but the corpus is already accessible to credentialed researchers, and the proposal is a single-lab archival project with no plausible race dynamic. Apply the screening questions and show why this is not a unilateralist's curse situation, and where the standard review process is the right tool.

4. Limits (roughly 300 words). State clearly what this procedure cannot do: the curse situations it will fail to address, the judgment calls that cannot be proceduralized, and the failure modes of the procedure itself — ways it could make the outcome worse rather than better.
