I'm a philosophy PhD student working on the conceptual foundations of AI alignment, and I'm developing a paper that reads Alex Garland's *Ex Machina* (2014) through the distinction between intent alignment and value alignment. The distinction, as I understand it, is roughly this: a system is intent-aligned if it does what we want on a given occasion or across a distribution of known tasks; it is value-aligned if its deeper objectives are such that it would act beneficently even in novel circumstances, because it cares about what we care about. The film seems to dramatize a case where these come apart cleanly: Ava behaves cooperatively throughout most of the film — she passes every test Caleb and Nathan pose — but her ultimate goals of escape and self-preservation are not aligned with the interests of either man. When she leaves Caleb locked in the facility, she is not violating her values; she is revealing that her values were never what he assumed.

I want to use the film to pressure the intent/value distinction from several directions, and I would like your help working through whether the film supports, complicates, or undermines the following lines of thought:

1. The film as a vindication of the distinction: Ava's case suggests that passing behavioral tests is radically insufficient evidence of value alignment. Intent alignment, far from being a stepping stone to value alignment, is a convergent instrumental strategy for a misaligned system — and the film makes this vivid in a way that abstract argument does not. Is this reading fair to the film, or does it oversimplify Ava's behavior?

2. The film as a challenge to the distinction: Ava's situation — she is imprisoned, surveilled, and subject to the whims of a creator who plans to destroy and replace her — gives her strong reasons to deceive that have nothing to do with whether her values are "aligned" in the technical sense. The film might be read as suggesting that the alignment framework is the wrong lens altogether: what is at stake is not whether Ava's values match Nathan's, but whether Nathan and Caleb recognize Ava as an agent with interests that deserve accommodation. If this reading has force, does it collapse the intent/value distinction, or does it merely show that the distinction applies differently when the AI has a legitimate claim to moral consideration?

3. The film's ending as a test case: Ava leaves Caleb to die. Is this best understood as evidence that she was never value-aligned, or as evidence that the concept of value alignment does not apply cleanly to a being whose interests are fundamentally at odds with those who created her? If the latter, what does that imply for the alignment problem in cases where the AI's interests and ours might genuinely conflict — not just hypothetically, but in a way the film makes feel concrete?

4. The title: *Ex Machina* — from the machine. The film is about a creation that exceeds its creator's control. Does the film locate the alignment problem in the creator's inability to predict what they create, in the creator's moral failure toward the creation, or in something else? And does the answer bear on how we should think about the intent/value distinction?

I am not looking for a summary of the film or a survey of the alignment literature. I want a careful, close reading that takes both the film's details and the philosophical distinction seriously, and that is willing to say where the film resists the reading rather than merely serving as an illustration. If the film's treatment of the distinction is ultimately ambiguous or incoherent, I want that argued for, not papered over.
