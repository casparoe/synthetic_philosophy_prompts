I facilitate dialogues across deep divides for a living — last month it was Palestinian and Israeli students, next month it's a mining company and the Indigenous community downstream. I've been reading about the Jain concepts of anekāntavāda and syādvāda, and I want to operationalize them: not just "be open to other perspectives," but the actual structure — the idea that any claim is true only from some naya (standpoint), that the full truth is irreducibly many-sided, and that the syāt ("in some respect") conditional should be recoverable even for statements that feel absolute.

Give me a reusable procedure I can follow in session prep and during the dialogue itself. I need it to be concrete: steps I can run, questions I can ask, moves I can make when someone is digging in. For each step, tell me the exit condition (how I know I've done it) and the stopping condition (where I hand it to judgment). Be honest about anekāntavāda's own commitments — it's not relativism, it's not "all views are equally valid," and it's not a technique for getting people to compromise. Flag where the procedure itself takes a stand that one of the parties might reasonably reject.

Then run it on two cases: (a) a dispute over closing a rural maternity ward, hospital admin vs. the community, and (b) a dispute over using macaques in neuroscience research, animal rights groups vs. the university researchers. In at least one of them, I want the procedure to surface something I as the facilitator would rather not have to deal with.
