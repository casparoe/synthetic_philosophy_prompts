So last night I got into a long argument with a friend about induction, specifically about Goodman's grue, and now I'm not even sure what either of us was really claiming. His whole line was that induction rests on the assumption that the future will resemble the past, that assumption is just a prejudice, so induction is in trouble. I kept pushing back that the grue example doesn't show the future won't resemble the past — it shows that "resembles" is doing a ton of work. Every emerald observed so far has been grue as well as green (where "grue" means examined before time t and green, or not examined before t and blue), so the past "resembles" the future under both predicates. The real issue is which predicates we project, not whether the future resembles the past. He said I was playing word games, and we went around in circles for an hour.

I want to actually untangle this instead of just winning the argument. The sentence "the future will resemble the past" (or "induction assumes the future will resemble the past") seems like it can mean a bunch of quite different things. Can you seperate out the distinct precise principles that the slogan could be expressing, from the trivial to the substantive to the outright false, and say for each whether it's plausible? Then the part I actually care about: which of those readings, if any, is the one the grue problem really needs to attack — and does my move about predicate-projection block the strongest version of his point, or does the strongest version still leave induction with a genuine problem?
