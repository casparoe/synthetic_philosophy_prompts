I'm serving on a working group that advises a national medical association on revising its guidelines for the determination of death. The clinical criteria—apnea testing, brainstem reflexes, confirmatory imaging—are relatively settled, at least among the neurologists in the room. But the conceptual foundations keep generating friction, and the friction has practical consequences. Families contest brain-death determinations in court, sometimes successfully; several religious communities have pushed for statutory conscientious objection to neurological criteria; intensivists disagree about whether certain patients with preserved hypothalamic function meet the standard; and the dead donor rule ties the entire organ procurement system to a concept that turns out, on inspection, to be surprisingly hard to pin down. The working group has asked me, as the philosopher in the room, to produce a short memo identifying the three or four open conceptual questions about the definition of death that most urgently need philosophical attention. I'm not being asked for a survey of the literature or a reading list; they want a prioritization: which questions, if answered or at least clarified, would do the most to resolve downstream disputes in law, clinical practice, and policy?

For each question, I need to explain what hangs on it—which practical disagreements it underlies, which other theoretical questions depend on settling it first, and how tractable it is for philosophers working with the conceptual tools we currently have. I'd also like to know which questions you'd deprioritize and why: which debates in the literature generate heat without light, which turn on empirical facts philosophy can't supply, and which are philosophically interesting but practically inert. The audience is clinicians and policy people, not metaphysicians, so the reasoning should connect the conceptual work to concrete stakes: organ allocation protocols, family counseling, religious accommodation, and the need for legal bright lines. What would your list look like, and how would you defend the ordering?
