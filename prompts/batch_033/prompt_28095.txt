I'm presenting next month to my firm's internal quantitative research seminar. The audience is about thirty people: mostly financial mathematicians and quantitative analysts, with a couple of senior risk managers who came up through the quant track. They are sophisticated about measure-theoretic probability, stochastic calculus, and the standard machinery of mathematical finance, but they have little patience for what they would call philosophical concerns — they tend to think that if a problem does not arise in a well-specified continuous-time model with standard regularity conditions, it is not a real problem.

My thesis is that the Pasadena paradox — and the broader class of decision problems with conditionally convergent or otherwise undefined expected values — is not just a mathematical curiosity but exposes a genuine crack in the foundations of expected utility theory that has practical bite. Specifically, I want to argue that the standard move of saying "real-world payoffs are always bounded, so this never matters" is too quick: there are structurally analogous situations in finance where the same issue arises in a form that cannot be dismissed by bounding the state space. My positive proposal is modest — I am not offering a full alternative to expected utility theory — but I want to convince them that the problem deserves attention from practitioners, not just philosophers.

The core of my argument runs as follows. I first walk through the Pasadena game itself: flip a fair coin until it lands heads; if the first heads occurs on the nth toss, the payoff is (−1)^(n−1) · 2^n / n. The expected value is the sum over n of (1/2^n) · (−1)^(n−1) · 2^n / n, which simplifies to the alternating harmonic series. That series converges conditionally to ln 2 — but by Riemann's rearrangement theorem, its terms can be reordered to yield any real number, so the expectation is not well-defined in the Lebesgue sense (the positive and negative parts both diverge). I then argue that this is not a quirk of one contrived game: any time an expected value involves a conditionally convergent sum or integral, the same ambiguity arises, and the choice of ordering or limiting procedure is doing substantive normative work that expected utility theory provides no guidance about. I then present three finance-adjacent examples: (1) a dynamic hedging strategy for a binary option where the P&L, under a natural discrete-time approximation, has a conditionally convergent expectation; (2) a reinsurance contract whose payoff structure, under a heavy-tailed loss distribution, yields an undefined expectation in the same sense; and (3) a sequential investment rule where the order of taking limits — number of periods going to infinity versus position size going to zero — determines the sign of the expected return. I close by suggesting that these cases are not exotic edge cases but arise naturally once you take model uncertainty seriously, and that the right response is not simply to retreat to bounded utility functions but to develop decision rules that handle undefined expectations directly — though I acknowledge this is an open research program.
