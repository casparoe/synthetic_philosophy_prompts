I've been following the debate about the moral equality of combatants in just war theory for a while now, and I'm starting to think it's never going to resolve. On one side you've got the Walzerian view: soldiers on both sides of a war are morally symmetric — they can permissibly kill each other — because they're caught up in something bigger than themselves, they don't choose the war, and the real moral wrong lies with the political leaders who start unjust wars. On the other side you've got the revisionists (McMahan, Rodin, et al.) who say: no, if you're fighting for an unjust cause, you're engaged in wrongdoing when you kill just combatants, because the permission to kill in war has to track individual liability, and you can't be liable to be killed just because someone put a uniform on you.

The debate has been going since the 70s, and lately it feels like both sides have fully developed their arguments and are just talking past each other. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is really about whether moral evaluation should take the institutional perspective (the "rules of the game" that apply to soldiers qua soldiers) or the individual perspective (what any given person is morally permitted to do, full stop), and that these are just different starting points that neither side can argue for without begging the question.

But maybe I'm wrong. Maybe there's a hidden empirical question in there — about what soldiers can actually know on the ground, or about what kind of rules would actually minimize suffering in war. Or maybe it's a verbal dispute about the word "permissible" (all-things-considered vs. pro tanto). Or maybe the whole question is ill-posed because it confuses the morality of war with the law of war.

Diagnose this. What kind of disagreement is this, really? And given your diagnosis, what — if anything — would count as progress?
