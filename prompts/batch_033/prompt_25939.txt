I cover science and medicine for a national magazine, and I'm working on a piece about how to interpret hospital outcome data. I've constructed a simplified comparison from real figures, and it's left me genuinely unsure what conclusion to draw.

Here is the situation. Two hospitals in the same state perform the same complex surgery. I've broken their patients down by preoperative risk — high and low, as scored by a standard instrument both hospitals use.

Hospital A: 900 high-risk patients, 82% survival; 100 low-risk patients, 97% survival.
Hospital B: 100 high-risk patients, 78% survival; 900 low-risk patients, 96% survival.

Hospital A does better among high-risk patients. Hospital A also does better among low-risk patients. But when I pool all patients, Hospital A's overall survival is about 83.5%, and Hospital B's is about 94.2%.

I understand, mathematically, how this happens: Hospital A treats far more high-risk patients, so its overall number is dragged down by the mix. What I don't know is what to say to my readers. Does Hospital A provide better surgical care or doesn't it? The subgroup comparison says yes; the aggregate says no. I can write a piece that explains the arithmetic, but my editor wants the piece to reach a bottom-line judgment about which hospital a patient should prefer, assuming the patient doesn't yet know their own risk level. What's the right answer, and what's the principle behind it?
