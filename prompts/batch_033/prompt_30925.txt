I help administer a small forecasting tournament among a few dozen friends and colleagues, and I have begun to suspect that our scoring system quietly commits us to a picture of what an opinion is, though I cannot tell whether it is a genuine philosophical commitment or just a harmless bookkeeping convention.

Every quarter, each participant must assign a number from 0 to 1 to each of roughly forty future events — election results, product announcements, court rulings, and the like — where 0 means the event certainly will not happen and 1 means it certainly will. At the end of the quarter we score everyone with a fixed rule: for each event, you lose the square of the difference between your number and the outcome, counting the outcome as 1 if the event occurred and 0 if it did not. A forecast of 0.9 on an event that occurs therefore costs (1 − 0.9)², or 0.01, while a forecast of 0.9 on an event that fails to occur costs (0 − 0.9)², or 0.81, and the person with the smallest total loss across all events wins. The organizers defend the rule on the grounds that it gives everyone an incentive to report their honest opinion rather than a safe or self-promoting one. Yet the same organizers require every participant to put a number on every event — no one may skip an item or mark themselves as having no view — and they openly mock the member who answers 0.5 to everything as refusing to say anything, even though 0.5 is precisely the number that caps your worst-case loss on any single event at 0.25 no matter what happens.

I would like to understand what this practice presupposes. It plainly treats an opinion as a graded thing that can be closer to or farther from how things turn out, and it treats being right as a matter of degree rather than all-or-nothing. Is that merely a scoring convention adopted for convenience, or does it commit us to a substantive view of what belief is and of what makes a belief a good one? Does it rule out the ordinary picture on which you either believe a proposition or you do not, or can the two pictures coexist? Is the contempt for the always-0.5 forecaster consistent with the scoring rule's own logic, or does it smuggle in a further assumption about what a worthwhile forecast must do beyond being accurate? And what would the tournament have to look like if we took the all-or-nothing picture of belief seriously instead?
