I write about evolutionary biology for a general-interest magazine of science and ideas, and I have noticed a recurring failure in my own drafts and those of my colleagues: a trait will be described as having evolved "for the good of the species" in the headline, "for the good of the group" in the lede, and "because it benefits the individual" in the body, with the gene-level account appearing only inside a quotation from a researcher. My editor, who has to sign off on the final copy, favors the elevated, cooperative framing and pushes back when I try to make the language more austere, so I would like to stop settling these questions by feel and on deadline.

Please write me a reusable decision procedure for deciding which level or levels of selection a sentence about an evolved trait is actually claiming, and whether that claim is defensible given the distinctions standard in the units-of-selection debate: replicators versus vehicles or interactors, individual selection versus group selection versus species selection, kin selection and inclusive fitness, and the conditions under which group-level or species-level talk is more than metaphor. The procedure has to be runnable by a nonspecialist journalist in about ten minutes, so give me the questions in order, say what counts as a sufficient answer to each, say where the procedure stops with "rewrite this," and mark the points where it hands the decision to a person because neither the biology nor the philosophy settles the wording. Then run it on three cases: a claim that lemmings disperse to avoid overgrazing "for the good of the species"; a claim that prairie dog alarm-callers are self-sacrificing sentinels acting "for the good of the group"; and a claim that a honeybee worker's sterility is a sacrifice "for the good of the colony." Arrange at least one worked example so the procedure returns an answer my editor would not like, and conclude by saying what the procedure cannot decide.
