I am trying to work out what principle, if any, captures my judgments about when the criminal law should prohibit conduct between consenting adults. I think it should prohibit: a stranger beating someone in an alley; a broker selling a terminally ill patient a sham cure; two adults who agree to a duel to the death; one adult killing and eating another with the victim's documented, freely given consent; and a person contracting to sell himself into chattel slavery for life. I think it should not prohibit: two adult siblings having private, contraceptive sex; someone burning the national flag in protest; a person selling one of his kidneys to a willing buyer; professional boxers; and members of a private club who pay to receive severe but non-permanent beatings for sexual gratification. This mostly looks like a harm principle, but the dueling and cannibalism cases make me doubt whether consent can ever be a defense to death, and I cannot see what distinguishes the private club beatings, which I would allow, from the duel, which I would punish, except the severity of the harm — a line I am not sure I can defend. I also cannot tell whether these exceptions commit me to legal moralism after all. Is there one principle that yields all or nearly all of these verdicts? If so, state it precisely and say which clause is motivated by which case. What does it imply about cases I have not settled — for example, a physician who gives a lethal dose at a competent, terminally ill patient's repeated request, or a consensual gladiatorial match to the death? Which of my verdicts, if any, does the principle force me to abandon? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. Use American English.
