I'm a high school student writing an independent-study paper that connects my literature and philosophy courses. This year we read Arthur Miller's The Crucible, and I have been reading Rousseau's Letter to d'Alembert on the Theatre on my own. I am particularly struck by his argument that theater is not a school of morals: a play, he claims, can succeed only by flattering the passions its audience already brings; the pity or indignation it arouses is discharged in the theater rather than carried into action; and even a virtuous character on stage is compromised by being served up for applause. He develops this last point through Molière's The Misanthrope, where he thinks the audience is taught to laugh at the one honest character. I would like to test these claims against The Crucible. Read the play through Rousseau's critique: where does his account genuinely illuminate what the play is doing—say, the way it works on the audience's indignation about the trials—and where does the play resist the reading? Does Miller's play show that moral outrage in the theater can be something more than self-congratulation, or does it inadvertently confirm Rousseau's worry that an audience that has been moved has thereby been let off the hook? I want a reading that is faithful in both directions, that does not reduce the play to an illustration of Rousseau, and that ends by saying what the play adds to the philosophical argument that argument alone cannot capture.
