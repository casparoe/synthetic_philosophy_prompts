In my graduate seminar on the philosophy of artificial intelligence, a student has now several times invoked the slogan "syntax is not sufficient for semantics" — or the variant "syntax does not suffice for semantics" — as though stating it settled the matter against any form of computationalism about the mind. When pressed, he treats the phrase as obvious and self-explanatory. I suspect that different people who repeat this slogan mean quite different things by it, that some of those things are near-trivial, and that only a narrow subset of them actually do the work required to sustain the Chinese Room argument against strong AI.

I would like you to disambiguate this slogan by laying out the distinct, precise claims it could be taken to express. For each proposed precisification, state the claim clearly and offer a brief assessment: Is it (a) trivial or near-trivial, (b) substantive and plausibly true, or (c) substantive and likely false? Where a reading is ambiguous between more than one interpretation of its key terms — "syntax," "sufficient for," "semantics" — distinguish those sub-readings.

Then address the dialectical question: Which precisification is actually needed to make the Chinese Room argument go through — that is, to block the inference from a system's running the right program to that system's genuinely understanding — and does that precisification survive the standard replies, particularly the Systems Reply and the Robot Reply? I am less interested in a summary of the debate than in a precise mapping between the different readings of the slogan and the different moves available to each side.
