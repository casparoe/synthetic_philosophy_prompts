I'm writing an essay on Du Bois's claim that all art is propaganda—the argument he makes in "Criteria of Negro Art" (1926) that the Black artist cannot and should not stand outside the struggle for liberation, and that art that refuses to serve that struggle is an unaffordable luxury. I want to test this claim against Spike Lee's *Do the Right Thing* (1989), which is undeniably a work of political art about race in America but which famously refuses to hand the audience a clear moral or political resolution. The film ends with Radio Raheem's death, the riot, and the dual quotations from Malcolm X and Martin Luther King on violence—leaving the question of what "the right thing" actually is genuinely unresolved.

I'd like a reading that takes both Du Bois's essay and the film seriously. Where does *Do the Right Thing* function as propaganda in Du Bois's sense—what is it arguing for, and how does its artistic form advance that argument? Where does the film resist or complicate Du Bois's framework, perhaps by prioritizing aesthetic ambiguity over a clear political message, or by suggesting that the artist's job is to pose questions rather than to advocate answers? And does Du Bois's framework have the resources to handle this kind of work, or does the film expose a limitation in his view of what political art can be? I'm not looking for a verdict on whether Du Bois was right—I want to know what the confrontation between the essay and the film reveals about both.
