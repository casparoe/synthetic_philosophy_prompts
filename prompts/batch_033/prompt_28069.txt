I'm a software engineer, so I'm coming at this with no formal philosophy background — I got pulled in through the longtermism/EA side of the internet. The thing I can't get past is the standoff over pure time preference. By that I mean the question of whether a year of suffering or happiness two hundred years from now should count for less than a year today, just because it's later. Not the finance version where you discount future cash flows because you could have invested the money now; I get that. The version I mean shows up in climate economics and in arguments about the far future. On one side, people treat pure time preference as impatience, basically a bias, and say there's no principled reason a future person's welfare matters less than ours, so the pure rate should be zero or near zero. On the other side, people say a zero rate makes the math blow up — tiny probabilities of huge future payoffs end up dominating every decision, and policy becomes hostage to guesses about the far future — and that actual people do discount, so a zero rate is a philosophers fiction, not a usable policy input. Both sides seem to accept the same facts about how markets behave and what the standard decomposition says (the social discount rate is pure time preference plus a term that depends on growth and inequality aversion), and yet nobody moves.

My working theory is that they're not actually disagreeing about the same thing: one side is doing ethics (how we should value welfare across time), the other is doing something closer to political economy (how a government should set a rate given real preferences, uncertainty, and the need to actually decide), and the phrase "discount rate" is doing too much work. But I could be wrong.
