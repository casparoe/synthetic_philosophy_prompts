I'm a software engineer working on the safety and refusal layer of a general-purpose AI assistant, and I've been thinking through the logic of when the system should push back on user requests. I've ended up with an argument that seems to prove we should either build something useless or something unethical, and I can't find the exit. I'd like you to approach this as a professional philosopher. Before settling on an answer, brainstorm at least two or three different places the argument might break down, then evaluate which diagnosis is strongest and explain what rejecting that step costs.

Here's the argument:

(1) An AI assistant should refuse a user request when fulfilling it would foreseeably cause the user net harm, unless the user has given informed consent that specifically addresses that harm.

(2) Informed consent to a harm requires that the user actually understands the harm they are consenting to — not merely that they were warned in generic terms or that they clicked through a disclaimer.

(3) In practice, users rarely understand the specific downstream harms of AI-assisted actions in complex domains — financial, medical, legal, psychological. They may know abstractly that "investing is risky" or "sending this message could backfire," but they do not grasp the particular ways things could go wrong in their situation, nor the rough magnitudes involved.

(4) Therefore, by (1)–(3), AI assistants should refuse most user requests in complex domains — not just the obvious cases like requests for instructions on self-harm, but also helping a user draft an angry message to their boss, giving investment suggestions, offering relationship advice, and so on.

(5) But an AI assistant that refuses most requests in exactly the domains where users most want help is not useful. And if it is not useful, there is no point building it — or rather, building it and then deploying it with these refusal policies would be a kind of bait-and-switch.

(6) So we face a dilemma: either we must reject one of (1)–(3), or we must accept that a useful AI assistant cannot be built and deployed ethically.

Now, (1) seems non-negotiable — an AI that knowingly helps users harm themselves without consent is clearly wrong. (3) seems like a factual claim that is hard to dispute — most users really do not understand the specific risks of, say, following AI-generated legal or medical advice. So the pressure falls on (2). But if we reject (2), we are saying that informed consent does not require actual understanding — that a generic "I accept the risks" clickthrough is enough. And that seems to gut the whole concept of informed consent, not just for AI but across the board.

So: where does this argument go wrong? If you had to reject one premise, which would it be, and what exactly do you give up by rejecting it? Alternatively, if you think the argument is sound and we really should accept the conclusion, explain how we should think about building AI assistants given that constraint.
