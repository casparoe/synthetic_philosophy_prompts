I've been going down this rabbit hole about self-reference and strange loops — you know, like Gödel and Escher and that whole thing. I was talking to a friend about it and he brought up Escher's Drawing Hands as this big analogy. You know the one: two hands emerging from a page, each drawing the other into existence. His point was that the drawing shows there always has to be an "inviolate level" outside any self-referential system. Like, the hands can't actually draw themselves — Escher had to draw them. And he said this applies everywhere: consciousness can't explain itself because the explaining mind is part of what needs explaining, a legal system can't ground its own authority because someone had to write the constitution, that kind of thing. He was basically using it to argue that any attempt at a fully self-contained explanation of something — like trying to explain consciousness purely in terms of brain states, or trying to ground ethics without any external foundation — is doomed because you always need that Escher outside the drawing.

I've been turning this over and something feels off but I can't put my finger on it. Like, the drawing is obviously an artifact — of course a real person drew it. But does that really map onto things like consciousness or the foundations of law? What corresponds to the hands, what corresponds to Escher, what corresponds to the page? And is the "inviolate level" actually the same kind of thing in each case, or does the analogy sneak in assumptions that don't hold when you move from lithographs to, say, the mind trying to understand itself? Also, I keep thinking: in the drawing, the hands are literally incomplete without Escher, but is that the right way to think about, say, a legal system? Maybe the constitution isn't "outside" the system in the same way an artist is outside a drawing — it was written by people who were already inside the legal tradition they were shaping. I guess what I'm asking is: audit this analogy for me. What maps onto what, where does it break, and does his conclusion — that you always need an external ground — actually survive once you look at the disanalogies?
