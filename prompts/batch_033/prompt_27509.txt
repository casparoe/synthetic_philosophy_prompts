I am working through the account of exchange and money in Book V, Chapter 5 of Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics, and I need help with what looks like a contradiction within the space of a few lines. The passage reads:

"Money, then, acting as a measure, makes goods commensurate and equates them; for neither would there have been association if there were not exchange, nor exchange if there were not equality, nor equality if there were not commensurability. Now in truth it is impossible that things differing so much should become commensurate, but with reference to demand they may become so sufficiently."

My difficulty is this. Aristotle first treats commensurability as a necessary condition of exchange — no commensurability, no equality, no exchange — and says that money, acting as a measure, brings it about. Then, almost immediately, he says that "in truth" it is impossible for things differing so much to become commensurate, and that they do so only "with reference to demand" and only "sufficiently." I would like to understand precisely how these two claims are meant to fit together. In particular: Does Aristotle think commensurability is something real that money produces, or is it a practical accommodation that is adequate only for the purposes of exchange? What is the "demand" that he identifies as the true unit, and what exactly is money a conventional representative of, if the goods themselves are not commensurable in truth? Finally, does the passage commit Aristotle to a substantive thesis about the commensurability of values, or should it be read as deflationary — the claim being only that exchange requires a conventional measure, not a genuine common value?

I would prefer an answer that stays close to the internal logic of the passage rather than surveying secondary literature.
