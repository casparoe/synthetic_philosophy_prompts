I keep going in circles on this argument from my epistemology seminar and I'd like a diagnosis.

(1) Suppose I'm badly sleep-deprived and I have strong evidence that sleep deprivation makes people overconfident in exactly the kind of probability estimate I'm about to make.
(2) That evidence is higher-order: it doesn't bear on the content of my estimate, only on the reliability of the process that produces it.
(3) If I should lower my confidence in the estimate, then evidence about my own mental state is moving my belief about the math. But the math is settled by the numbers, not by how tired I am.
(4) If I shouldn't lower my confidence, then I'm ignoring evidence about my own unreliability; learning I was impaired would make no epistemic difference.
(5) And whichever policy I adopt, the fact that I adopted it while impaired is itself higher-order evidence against that policy, so the dilemma just reappears one level up.
(6) So there's no stable rational response to higher-order evidence: either it turns out to be ordinary first-order evidence after all, or any policy for handling it undercuts itself.
(7) But that cant be right — a tired pilot should recheck the fuel math, and a drunk person should distrust their judgment that they're fine to drive.

Which step goes wrong, and what does rejecting it cost? Id most like to keep (3), because I really do think facts about my head aren't facts about arithmetic; but then I don't see how to stop the regress.
