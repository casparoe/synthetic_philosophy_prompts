I teach an advanced undergraduate seminar called "Strategic Concepts in Philosophy," about 18 students, mostly philosophy and political science majors, one 80-minute session. We've covered Schelling on commitment, first-mover advantages, and the difference between threats and promises. Next session I want them to grasp commitment races — situations where each player's best move is to commit before the other does, even though both would be better off if neither could commit at all. They've read the abstract description, but it hasn't landed: they treat it as a curiosity rather than a structural feature of bargaining, arms races, standards wars, and preemptive moves.

Design an in-class game that makes them feel the logic of a commitment race in their bones. The game should have the following properties:

- Players face a genuine incentive to commit early, and committing early should sometimes be a mistake (because you lock in before you know enough) and sometimes be essential (because waiting means the other player locks you out). Both possibilities should arise naturally from play, not from me scripting them.
- The game needs at least two rounds so students can adjust their behavior after seeing what happened in the first round. The shift in behavior between rounds is part of the point.
- Payoffs should be in points, not money. The points should matter enough to focus attention — I'm willing to award a small course-credit bonus to the top scorer in each section, so design the scoring so that someone can win decisively without the game turning into a lottery.
- Materials should be minimal: index cards, a whiteboard, maybe a stopwatch. Nothing that requires software or pre-printed decks.

Give me: the rules stated so I can read them aloud in under three minutes; the exact sequence of what I do and what students do, minute by minute; the payoff structure and why it creates a commitment race rather than a coordination problem or a pure waiting game; and a debrief plan that maps specific moments in the game onto the theoretical distinction between commitment races, wars of attrition, and ordinary first-mover advantages. The debrief should also surface the normative question: when, if ever, is it rational to regret winning a commitment race?

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — students who refuse to commit, a round where everyone waits and nothing happens, confusion about the rules — and how I should handle each failure mode without breaking the pedagogical point. And be honest: what feature of real commitment races does the game distort or leave out, and how should I compensate for that in the debrief?
