I'm preparing a handout for an interdisciplinary graduate seminar on comparative cognition and AI architectures, and I want to give students a rigorous classification tool for Daniel Dennett's four kinds of minds — Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures — as developed in his 1996 book *Kinds of Minds*. The students come from philosophy, computer science, and evolutionary biology, and I've noticed a recurring problem: they can recite the four labels and give a canonical example for each (the tick, the pigeon, the chimpanzee, the human), but they struggle to articulate what exactly separates adjacent categories or to classify a novel case — say, a reinforcement-learning agent with a world model, or a corvid that bends a wire into a hook — without falling back on vague intuitions about "complexity."

I'd like you to write a decision tree (described in text, since it will become a handout) that takes any cognitive system — a biological species, an AI architecture, or a hypothetical agent — and walks the user to one of the four named categories, plus an "ambiguous" or "mixed" leaf where the boundaries genuinely blur. Each branch node should be a yes/no diagnostic question about a specific capability: whether the system can modify its behavior within its own lifetime on the basis of feedback, whether it can test candidate actions internally before executing them in the world, whether it recruits external symbolic structures (language, notation, diagrams) to perform cognitive operations it could not perform without them, and whatever further distinctions you judge necessary to carve the space cleanly. The questions must be phrased so that someone can answer them for a concrete case without already knowing which category the case belongs to — the tree should sort by what the system does, not by which label feels right.

Please build the tree from the logical space of capabilities first, and only then locate the four named positions within it. I want you to flag any regions of the logical space that are unoccupied — combinations of answers that no known creature or system instantiates — and briefly diagnose why: is the combination incoherent (e.g., a system that uses external symbols but cannot learn at all), evolutionarily inaccessible, or merely unexplored? Also flag any pair of categories the tree cannot cleanly separate and explain where the boundary gets fuzzy. For instance, the Skinnerian/Popperian distinction turns on internal modeling, but one could argue that operant conditioning already involves a minimal kind of internal anticipation; similarly, the Popperian/Gregorian distinction turns on whether the cognitive tools are external and symbolic, but some nonhuman animals use physical tools in ways that seem to offload cognition. I'd like the tree to acknowledge these tensions rather than paper over them. Each leaf should carry the category name, a one-sentence summary of the defining capability, and a short paragraph of commentary that notes the hardest case for that leaf and what it reveals about the framework's limits.
