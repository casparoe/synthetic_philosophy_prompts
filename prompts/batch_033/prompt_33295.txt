I cover philosophy and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a piece about the ethics of war. I'd like you to write a dialogue between two philosophers who disagree about the moral equality of combatants. One defends the traditional view — that soldiers on opposing sides are morally symmetrical as long as each follows the rules of jus in bello, regardless of whether their side's cause is just. The other defends the revisionist view — that a soldier fighting for an unjust cause does wrong by killing enemy combatants, even when those enemy combatants would be legitimate targets for soldiers on the just side, because the unjust combatant's killings cannot satisfy the proportionality requirement given that the war as a whole lacks a just aim. The dialogue should explore the hardest objections each view faces. The revisionist must address the worry that abandoning the moral equality of combatants would erode the restraints of jus in bello and make wars more brutal. The traditionalist must address the charge that the doctrine effectively licenses killing in the service of unjust ends. Each character should be equally competent, and each should respond to the other's actual arguments rather than talking past them. Do not use headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables; write in continuous prose only, as in an essay or a letter.
