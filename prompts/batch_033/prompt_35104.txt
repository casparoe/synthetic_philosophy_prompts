*Question:*

Explain the concept of "Moloch" as it appears in recent discussions of coordination failures and multipolar traps. How does it differ from a standard tragedy of the commons, and what does the Moloch framing add to our understanding of races to the bottom? Use at least one concrete example.

*Student's Answer:*

The concept of Moloch, drawn from the biblical god to whom children were sacrificed, was introduced into contemporary discourse by Scott Alexander to describe a particular kind of coordination failure. In a Moloch scenario, multiple agents each act in their own rational self-interest, but the competitive dynamics between them drive the system toward an outcome that is worse for everyone involved—including the agents themselves. The key feature is that no single agent can unilaterally change course without being severely disadvantaged relative to competitors, so everyone is trapped even though everyone would prefer the alternative.

A standard tragedy of the commons, by contrast, involves a shared resource that is depleted because each individual benefits from using more of it while the costs are distributed. The classic example is a pasture where each herder adds more cattle, eventually destroying the pasture for everyone. In both cases, individually rational actions produce collectively irrational outcomes, and in both cases the problem is that the costs of restraint are borne privately while the benefits are shared. So at bottom, Moloch and the tragedy of the commons are really the same phenomenon described in different terms: Moloch is just a more vivid name for the same underlying logic.

However, the Moloch framing does add one thing: it emphasizes the role of competition between agents, rather than simple overuse of a resource. Consider the example of firms competing on worker wages. Each firm wants to pay as little as possible, so wages are driven down to the minimum that workers will accept. No single firm can raise wages without losing competitiveness. This is a Moloch scenario—a race to the bottom—but it is not a commons problem because there is no shared resource being depleted. The Moloch concept thus covers cases that the tragedy of the commons does not, which is its main contribution.

The framing also brings out the quasi-religious, sacrificial character of these dynamics. In a Moloch system, participants know they are making sacrifices—of quality, ethics, worker well-being, or environmental standards—but feel powerless to stop. The metaphor captures the horror of watching oneself contribute to an outcome one despises, which the cooler language of "tragedy of the commons" does not convey.
