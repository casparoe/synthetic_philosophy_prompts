I sit on the admissions committee of a selective one-year master's program. We must decide whether to retain a requirement that every applicant sit, in person and at their own expense, for a proctored four-hour quantitative screening exam. The exam is not used for placement, and our own data show that its scores do not predict first-year grades or completion. The exam's defenders do not dispute the data; they argue that the burden is the point. On their view, completing the exam is an honest signal of commitment: a low-commitment applicant would not pay the cost, so the requirement separates serious applicants from dilettantes in a way no free online form could. The critics reply that this gets the handicap principle backwards: for a costly signal to be reliable, the cost must fall more heavily on the lower-quality signalers the committee is trying to screen out, not uniformly on everyone, and a pure sunk-cost hurdle mostly selects for wealth, free time, and tolerance for pointless hoops. The dean wants a decision this cycle.

Draft the short written opinion I will circulate before our vote. It should recommend a concrete course of action — keep, drop, or replace the requirement — with a specific rule and a timeline, and it should engage the strongest version of the losing side's argument rather than a caricature of it. Several committee members work on signaling theory, so use terms such as "honest signal," "handicap," and "differential cost" precisely. Begin with the memo itself, with no preface or commentary, and make the memo's first sentence state the recommendation.
