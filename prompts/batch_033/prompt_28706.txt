Two colleagues of mine, both in a philosophy and economics reading group, got into an argument about the centipede game that I still can't resolve. Here's roughly how it went.

(1) Maria said backward induction in the centipede game is a reductio of the common-knowledge-of-rationality assumption, not a prescription. If the only way to get to the cooperative outcome is for players to violate at least one assumption of the standard model, then so much the worse for the standard model. Real people routinely pass in the lab, and they're not obviously making a mistake.

(2) David replied that this conflates two questions: what people do and what they should do. The fact that subjects pass in experiments doesn't show passing is rational — it might just show that people are bad at reasoning through sequential games, or that they import norms from everyday reciprocity that don't apply in a one-shot anonymous setting. The backward-induction argument doesn't need the assumption that people are actually rational; it only needs the claim that if you were rational and knew the other player was rational, you'd take at the first node. That's a normative claim, and lab behavior doesn't refute it.

(3) Maria came back with: but the normative claim is only as strong as its premises. One premise is that at each node, each player believes the other would play rationally at subsequent nodes. But once you're inside the game and the other player has already passed — which is the only way you'd ever face a later choice — you have evidence that they're not playing the backward-induction strategy. So the belief that they'd take at the next node is no longer rationally required. The backward-induction argument assumes you maintain a belief in the other's rationality even after they've done something the theory says a rational player wouldn't do. That's not common knowledge of rationality; that's dogmatism.

(4) David said that move is too cheap, because it licenses defection in any finite repeated prisoner's dilemma too: as soon as someone cooperates once, you update and throw out the unraveling argument. But the unraveling argument in the prisoner's dilemma is supposed to work even if you know the other player has cooperated in the past — it's about what they'll do at the remaining nodes, given their rationality. If updating on past play defeats backward induction, you've thrown out a huge amount of game theory, not just the centipede result.

(5) Maria said that might be right, and maybe we should throw out that part of game theory — or at least restrict it to cases where the players have no opportunity to signal or build a reputation. The centipede game, with its passing moves, is precisely a setting where actions can function as signals that alter what it's rational to believe about the other player. So backward induction might be correct for some games but question-begging in the centipede game specifically.

They went back and forth for another twenty minutes and never settled it. I'd like you to adjudicate this move by move. For each numbered move, say whether it's clearly right, clearly wrong, or somewhere in between, and explain why. Then say who has the better overall position, and identify anything important both of them are missing.
