"Sen basically proved that individual rights and the Pareto principle can't coexist in any social welfare function. The idea is that if you give people even minimal rights over their own private sphere—like the right to decide what books they themselves read—you can end up with social outcomes where everyone agrees things could be better, but the rights structure prevents you from getting there. The famous example is a prude and a lewd deciding who reads Lady Chatterley's Lover. The prude would rather nobody read it, but if someone has to, he'd rather it be him; the lewd wants the prude to read it to loosen him up, but if that's not happening, he'd rather read it himself than have nobody read it. Sen shows that if each guy gets to decide for himself whether he reads the book, the outcome is that the lewd reads it and the prude doesn't. But both of them actually prefer the outcome where the prude reads it! So respecting individual rights forces the group into an outcome that's Pareto-inferior. The takeaway is that any society that respects individual rights will sometimes violate the Pareto principle, meaning you have to pick between liberty and efficiency—you can't have both."
