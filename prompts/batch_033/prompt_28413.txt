I'm a journalist covering the life sciences and conceptual foundations of biology, and I've been following a long-running tension in how researchers define "life." I'd like to compare two frameworks that seem to pull in opposite directions, and I'd welcome a careful analysis of whether the tension is real or merely apparent.

The first framework is the evolutionary or Darwinian definition. Its canonical formulation is NASA's working definition: "Life is a self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution." The core idea is that what distinguishes living from non-living systems is the capacity to participate in natural selection — to replicate with heritable variation, such that lineages can adapt and complexify over time. On this view, life is fundamentally a historical, population-level phenomenon.

The second framework is the autopoietic or organizational definition, developed by Humberto Maturana and Francisco Varela. Here, life is identified not by its evolutionary potential but by a specific kind of present-tense organization: a living system is a network of processes that continuously produces its own components and thereby maintains the boundary that distinguishes it from its environment. It is self-producing, self-maintaining, and operationally closed. On this view, life is a kind of dynamic organizational pattern, and the question of whether it can evolve is secondary.

These frameworks appear to diverge on a number of borderline cases. A sterile hybrid like a mule is alive by the autopoietic standard — it metabolizes, maintains homeostasis, and repairs itself — but it cannot reproduce and therefore cannot undergo Darwinian evolution. Conversely, certain computational or chemical systems (computer viruses, prions, some autocatalytic sets) might arguably satisfy a minimal reading of the Darwinian criterion while lacking the integrated self-production that autopoiesis demands. Viruses are the classic puzzle: they evolve, but they rely on host machinery for metabolism and self-maintenance.

I'd like you to compare these two frameworks in depth. In particular: are they genuinely competing definitions of the same thing, or are they answering different questions — one about what life is as an ongoing process, the other about how life originates and diversifies across generations? If they are in genuine tension, which framework handles the borderline cases more coherently, and what does that tell us about the project of defining life? And if you think the entire enterprise of seeking a single definition of life is misguided — that "life" is a family-resemblance concept, or that definitions should be domain-specific rather than universal — please say so plainly. I'd rather have the premise challenged than have a forced comparison that papers over deeper conceptual problems.
