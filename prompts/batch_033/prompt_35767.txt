Otto Neurath's Isotype system (International System of Typographic Picture Education) was a design practice governed by precise rules. Greater quantities were shown by repeating a fixed-size pictogram, never by enlarging it — because enlargement, Neurath argued, leaves it ambiguous whether height, area, or length is being compared. Perspective was almost entirely avoided: the pictograms were flat, standardized, and stripped of anything decorative or individuating. Color served only to distinguish categories. Neurath described the system as a language-like technique, and the pictograms were meant to be combined into ordered visual statements readable across linguistic boundaries. Yet he also insisted that Isotype was never a fully developed language; it was always to be accompanied by words. And in practice, producing an Isotype chart required a trained transformer to translate raw data into visual form — the pictograms were not simply self-evident. What philosophy of meaning, representation, and communication does this design practice presuppose? For example, does it presuppose that pictures carry meaning by resemblance, by convention, or by some third relation that is neither? Does it presuppose that a visual code can be genuinely language-neutral, or that all communication, verbal and pictorial alike, rests on learned conventions? Reconstruct the implicit theory of pictorial meaning behind Isotype, distinguishing what the design rules strictly presuppose from what they merely fit well with. Identify any tensions in the view — between, say, the ambition of international transparency and the fact that the system required trained transformation and verbal supplementation — and say what would have to change in the practice if one adopted a pure resemblance theory of depiction, or a pure convention theory, instead.
