You are a professional philosopher specializing in moral psychology and the theory of responsibility. Take your time, and before you commit to an answer, brainstorm several candidate characterizations of the phenomenon I describe below, identify the closest concepts in the literature, and say explicitly where each fits and where it falls short.

I keep noticing an asymmetry in ordinary moral judgment for which I have no name. Consider two drivers who both run a red light. The first does so because a child in the back seat begins choking and he panics; we tend to say the situation got the better of him, that almost anyone might have done the same, and we soften our blame. The second does so because he is a habitually impatient man who finds waiting intolerable; we tend to blame him fully, and perhaps blame him more than we would blame someone else for the identical act, because this is simply the kind of person he is. The impatient man did not choose his temperament any more than the first driver chose to have a choking child in his back seat. Yet an unchosen feature of the situation seems to excuse, while an unchosen feature of the person's make-up seems to aggravate, or at least does nothing to mitigate.

The pattern generalizes. A soldier who breaks under a genuinely terrifying ambush is treated as having an excuse; a soldier who breaks because he is timid by unchosen disposition is treated as a coward. Someone who embezzles while under severe financial pressure he did not create receives some sympathy; someone who embezzles because he is grasping by unchosen disposition receives none, and may receive extra blame for being grasping.

I want to know whether this asymmetry is a recognized phenomenon with a name of its own, and how it relates to the broader idea that people are blamed for things beyond their control. I am not satisfied with a bare label; I want the carving. Is the operative distinction really between situation and make-up, or is it between something else, such as how external the factor feels to the person, or how easily we can imagine ourselves acting the same way? Are there unchosen dispositions that do mitigate, and unchosen situations that do not? Where are the edges of the phenomenon, and if no standard name fits exactly, what would you call it and how would you define it so that it stays distinct from the neighboring concepts?
