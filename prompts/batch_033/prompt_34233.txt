I've been reading rationalist and philosophy-of-science blogs for years, and every few months someone rediscovers Hempel's raven paradox and the comment section fragments into the same two or three positions without ever converging. I'd like to see what would happen if the debate were treated as an adversarial collaboration.

The paradox, briefly: Nicod's criterion says that a universal generalization of the form "All F are G" is confirmed by observing an F that is G. But "All ravens are black" is logically equivalent to "All non-black things are non-ravens." So observing a green apple — a non-black non-raven — apparently confirms that all ravens are black. The conclusion feels absurd, but it's not obvious which step to reject.

The two camps I see recurring:

Camp A holds that the paradox is genuine but dissolves once you quantify confirmation. In a Bayesian framework, a green apple does confirm "All ravens are black," but by a minuscule amount — roughly proportional to the prior probability that a randomly sampled object is a raven. The intuition that this is absurd is just a failure to distinguish tiny confirmation from no confirmation at all. The real lesson is that Nicod's criterion and purely qualitative confirmation are bankrupt, and any adequate theory of confirmation must assign degrees of support.

Camp B holds that no, a green apple tells you precisely nothing about ravens, and any framework that says otherwise has made a mistake. The paradox is resolved by rejecting the unrestricted application of Nicod's criterion — for instance, by requiring that confirmation only operates through the predicates one actually used to formulate the hypothesis, or by noting that the logical equivalence of two universal generalizations does not entail that they are confirmed by the same instances when the background classes differ vastly in size, or by insisting that confirmation requires a sampling procedure directed at the subject class, not a random draw from the universe.

Draft the joint statement these two camps could sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. I want:

(1) A numbered list of agreed claims — what both camps grant about the formal structure of the paradox, about what the paradox definitely does not show, and about what each camp concedes to the other.

(2) A disagreements table: for each point of residual disagreement, state Camp A's view in wording Camp A would accept, and Camp B's view in wording Camp B would accept. Avoid strawmen.

(3) For each disagreement, identify the crux — the sub-question that, if settled, would move at least one camp. Where possible, specify a formal condition or test case both camps agree would bear on the crux, and record each camp's prediction about it.

(4) An assessment of whether any part of the dispute is merely verbal. If they are using "confirms" to mean different things, say so explicitly and propose a disambiguation both would accept.

I'm less interested in which camp is right than in whether a stable joint formulation exists, and whether the remaining disagreement is one the camps can productively reduce to a further empirical or formal question. If the camps turn out to agree on every prediction about what an ideal reasoner would believe after a given observation and disagree only about whether the word "confirms" should be reserved for one of two compatible patterns, I want that stated clearly.
