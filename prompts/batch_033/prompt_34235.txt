ok so im in this philosophy of language class and we just finished austin's how to do things with words — all twelve lectures — and i cannot stop thinking about how the book's own structure is basically the argument. it opens with this clean, satisfying split: constatives on one side (describing the world, true or false), performatives on the other (doing something in the world, happy or unhappy). and then lecture by lecture austin picks at his own distinction until by the end it's collapsed and we're left with the general theory of illocutionary acts, where all saying is doing and the real question is what kind of act you're pulling off. i want a sonnet that does this arc. the octave should give us the crisp early-lecture confidence — "i promise" and "i do" and "i name this ship" over here, "the cat is on the mat" over there, two different kinds of thing, case closed. the volta should be the moment the reader feels the ground shift: maybe when they realize that "i state that the cat is on the mat" is itself a performative, or when the constative starts looking like it has its own felicity conditions (what do you have to get right for stating to come off successfully?). the sestet should trace the unravelling — explicit performatives, the discovery that stating is an act like any other, the move to locutionary/illocutionary/perlocutionary — and the closing couplet should land on the picture where the real work is classifying illocutionary force, not sorting utterances into two bins. i want it to be accurate enough that someone who knows the book recognizes the argumentative moves and not just the vocabulary. blank verse is fine, shakespearean is fine, whatever makes the volta do its job. just make the turn do the philosophical work.
