I teach a course that includes a unit on Buddhist ethics, and every time I teach it I realize I dont actually know what I think about whether Buddhist ethics is a form of consequentialism. My gut verdicts on cases are all over the place, and I'd like you to help me reverse-engineer whatever principle I seem to be tracking, if there is one.

Here are the cases. (1) I lie to a would-be murderer about where my friend is hiding. I think that's not just allowed but required, and a Buddhist who refused would be fetishizing the precept. (2) I tell a dying woman that her estranged son called to say he forgives her, when he didn't. I think that's wrong, even though it would give her peace. (3) In a standard trolley case I can flip a switch to kill one stranger and save five. I think I should flip it, though I feel the weight of the one. (4) A doctor gives a terminal patient a lethal dose at her request to end her suffering. I think that's wrong, though the doctor's motive is plainly compassion. (5) In the story about the bodhisattva sea captain who kills the would-be mass murderer to save the five hundred merchants on board, I think the captain does something admirable, not merely excusable. (6) I tell a student her paper is good when it isn't, just to keep her confidence up. I think that's wrong, but small, and I'd do it again tomorrow.

I can't find a single principle that gets all six. The pair (3) and (4) already looks hard to reconcile — both kill one innocent person to prevent suffering, but I approve of one and not the other — and then (5) seems like it should group with (4), because the killing is the means rather than a side effect, yet I want to call it admirable. Maybe the principle is something like "the precepts are training rules that give way to compassion, except when the act uses someone as a mere means," but I'm not sure that survives the cases either. So here's what I want. Try to state, precisely, the principle or small set of principles that best fits my verdicts, and be honest about which of them it can't accommodate — I'd rather overturn a verdict than keep a gerrymandered rule. And then apply it to the case I keep vacillating on: I've promised a colleague I'll attend her talk, an old friend shows up in crisis, and I skip the talk and lie about why.
