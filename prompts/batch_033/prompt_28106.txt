I just read a chapter that summarized Grice's account of meaning and implicature, and I want to know how much of it to trust. Here's the gist, as I understood it:

Grice's big idea is that what a sentence means is really just what speakers standardly intend to get across by it, so meaning bottoms out in speaker intentions. On his view, conversational implicature is the extra meaning an utterance carries beyond its literal content, and it's generated because speakers follow a Cooperative Principle with maxims like "be truthful" and "be informative." Whenever an utterance seems to violate one of these maxims on the surface, the hearer assumes the speaker is still being cooperative and infers that they meant something else. So if I say "There's a gas station around the corner" to someone who's out of gas, I implicate that the station is open; and irony works the same way — you say something false, I notice the violation of the maxim of quality, and I infer you meant the opposite. Grice also used this machinery to argue that logic and ordinary language don't really come apart: things like "and" suggesting temporal order are just implicatures, not part of the word's meaning.
