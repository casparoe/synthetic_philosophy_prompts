I'm leading a two-hour seminar for third-year medical students on their clinical ethics rotation. The topic is the philosophical problem of advance directives: when a formerly competent patient has written an advance directive refusing certain treatments, but now lacks capacity and seems to express preferences or exhibit interests that conflict with that directive, what authority should the earlier self's instructions carry? These students already know the legal framework—they have seen advance directives on the wards—but they have not examined the underlying normative question. Plan how I should structure the session. What conceptual pieces must be in place before the problem even bites, and in what order must they be introduced? What one case or scenario should carry the whole discussion, and what features must it have (and lack) to keep the philosophical tension alive rather than collapsing it prematurely? What misconceptions will these students predictably bring from clinical experience—for instance, that the earlier self obviously prevails because the document is legally binding, or that the later self's apparent preferences do not count because the patient lacks capacity, or that the whole problem reduces to poor drafting of the directive—and at exactly which points in the sequence should I head each one off? What question can I pose near the end that would tell me whether they have grasped the genuine philosophical tension rather than just learned to recite a position? And what should I explicitly flag as unresolved at this level?
