I'm a high school junior taking a philosophy and economics elective, and we recently spent several sessions on expected utility theory and the Allais paradox. The way my teacher presented it, the paradox is basically a knockout punch to the descriptive claim that people obey the independence axiom. For those unfamiliar: in the classic setup, you first choose between a sure $1 million and a gamble with an 89% chance of $1 million, a 10% chance of $5 million, and a 1% chance of nothing; then you choose between an 11% chance of $1 million and a 10% chance of $5 million. Most people take the sure thing in the first choice and the slightly higher upside in the second, even though, if you factor out the common 89% chance of $1 million from the first pair, the two choice problems are structurally identical. The standard interpretation is that this exposes a systematic violation of the independence axiom — people do not in fact reason in accordance with it, and the finding is robust across stakes, cultures, and framings. I found this argument compelling, and I have been defending it in class discussions against a couple of classmates who think the independence axiom is still salvageable as a descriptive principle.

But I am aware that I am new to this material and that I might be missing something important. I would like to understand what would count against the view that the Allais paradox constitutes a genuine refutation of the independence axiom as a descriptive claim about human choice. I am not asking for the standard rebuttals that simply redescribe the paradox — I already know about the certainty effect and nonlinear probability weighting. What I want is a map of the position's exposure: what evidence, arguments, or conceptual considerations would, if true, weaken or overturn the view? I am particularly interested in (a) defenses of the independence axiom that take the paradox seriously rather than dismissing it as an error, and that explain why the paradox does not refute the axiom on its own terms; (b) empirical findings that complicate the standard reading of the Allais results — for instance, evidence that the effect disappears under certain conditions, or that people revise their choices when the structure of the problem is made transparent; and (c) the question of whether the view has been gradually insulated by qualifications — for example, by restricting it to certain domains, by explaining away contrary findings as noise, or by shifting the claim from "people violate independence" to "people violate independence in the specific pattern the paradox picks out." If such insulation has occurred, I would like to know whether that is a problem for a view of this kind (a descriptive claim about human psychology) or simply good theory-building. Please rank the defeaters from those that would be fatal to those that would be merely awkward, and give an honest assessment of what a defender of my position should concede and what they can reasonably dismiss.
