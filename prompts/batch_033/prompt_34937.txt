You're a professional philosopher of probability. A friend who works on the philosophy of probability gave me this argument and I can't fine the step to reject. It seems to show there are no objective single-case probabilities, which can't be right — or maybe it is right and I'm just resisting it.

(1) A probability claim is objective only if it can in principle be checked against long-run frequencies: if no sequence of trials has a limiting frequency that would bear on whether the claim is true, the claim isn't about the world at all.
(2) Any single event falls under indefinitely many reference classes, and the frequency of the outcome can differ across them. This coin's landing heads is in "coin tosses," "tosses of this coin," "tosses of this coin from this height with this spin," and so on.
(3) If the value of a quantity depends on which reference class you choose, and the choice isn't dictated by the world itself, then that quantity isn't an objective feature of the world.
(4) So the chance that this coin lands heads on this very toss — or that this patient recovers, or that this atom decays in the next minute — is not an objective feature of the world.
(5) But science is full of single-case probabilities like these, and we treat at least some of them as true or false independently of anyone's beliefs.
(6) So either a lot of scientific talk is non-objective, or one of the steps above is false.

I'd like to keep (5); I'd rather give up something earlier. But every way out I try seems to force me into either "probabilities are just degrees of belief" (which feels like giving up on objective chance) or "probability is not one thing" (which feels like a dodge). Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If you have to choose, which interpretation — frequentist, subjectivist, or propensity — survives this argument most comfortably, and which is in the most trouble?
