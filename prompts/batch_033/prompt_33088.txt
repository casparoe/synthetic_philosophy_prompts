Last night a friend and I spent the whole evening arguing about whether the features that mechanistic interpretability claims to find inside trained neural networks are really there. He took the deflationary line: a trained network is, for explanatory purposes, just a function from inputs to outputs, and talk of hidden units representing curves, syntax, or truth is a gloss we impose on it, because the same function can be implemented in endlessly many ways and an activation space can be carved into features in endlessly many ways. He pressed the usual points — a linear probe can recover a label from activations even when the network is not using it, sparse autoencoders choose a dictionary rather than reading one off the weights, and since nearly any direction in activation space has some causal effect when you intervene, causal manipulability cannot by itself separate a real feature from a convenient fiction. I defended the opposite view, but by the end of the evening I felt I had not given the strongest version of it.

I would like you to argue for a moderate realism about neural network representations: the claim that at least some of the structures uncovered by interpretability methods are genuine features of the trained network rather than artifacts of the method. Engage the deflationist's strongest form of the argument, not a strawman. In particular, explain why underdetermination of description and the limitations of probes do not by themselves defeat realism; propose the criterion or criteria you think actually distinguish real features from artifacts, such as convergence across independently derived methods, the specificity of causal effects under controlled interventions, or predictive power on novel inputs; and address the worry that any such criterion is question-begging or would also rule out the unobservable posits of other sciences. I am less interested in a survey of interpretability methods than in a single, well-structured argument.
