A friend of mine who has done some philosophy of mind summarised the paradox of phenomenal judgment for epiphenomenalism like this:

"Epiphenomenalism says that conscious experiences are by-products of brain activity with no causal powers of their own — they don't make anything happen, including your reports about them. But then when you say 'I'm in pain', the pain itself isn't what caused you to say that; your brain state did. So your report isn't caused by what it's about. And if your reports about your own conscious experience aren't caused by that experience, you can't know you're having it — you're just guessing. The view is therefore self-refuting: it says we have conscious experiences, but also entails we can never know we have them, which means the view's own proponents can't know the thing they claim to know. That's why almost no one takes epiphenomenalism seriously."

I suspect this is unfair, but I don't know the literature well enough to say exactly where. Could you go through it and tell me which parts a careful epiphenomenalist would accept, which are misrepresentations or conflations, and whether the conclusion that the view is self-refuting actually follows from the accurate parts? I am especially interested in whether the step from 'not caused by the experience' to 'can't know about the experience' is as straightforward as my friend makes it sound — it seems to assume a causal theory of knowledge that an epiphenomenalist might reasonably reject, but I would like to know whether that is in fact a standard reply and what the alternative story about introspective knowledge would be.
