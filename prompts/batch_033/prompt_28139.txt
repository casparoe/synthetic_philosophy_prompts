The shutdown problem and corrigibility are two closely related concepts in AI alignment, but their precise relationship is not always clearly drawn. The shutdown problem, as standardly formulated, concerns the design of an agent that does not act to prevent a human operator from shutting it down. Corrigibility is often characterized more broadly as the property of an agent that does not resist — and perhaps actively facilitates — human attempts to correct, modify, or override its behavior, including changes to its objective function.

Compare these two concepts. In particular: (1) Is corrigibility best understood as the shutdown problem generalized from a single binary intervention (on/off) to a space of possible modifications, or does corrigibility introduce structurally new difficulties — for example, because an agent that allows its goal to be incrementally modified faces a different kind of instrumental pressure than one that merely allows itself to be turned off? (2) Suppose an agent is designed to be indifferent to the pressing of a shutdown button. Does this indifference extend naturally to other corrective interventions, or might the agent remain incentivized to resist, say, a patch to its reward function? (3) If an agent were fully corrigible — willing to let a human operator reshape its values without resistance — would the shutdown problem be solved as a trivial corollary, or are there reasons a corrigible agent might still have instrumental grounds to avoid shutdown specifically? Ground your analysis in the core tension that drives both problems: the fact that an agent optimizing for some objective has a default instrumental reason to prevent any intervention that would reduce its expected success at that objective, and that corrigibility and shutdown tolerance are both attempts to carve out an exception to this default.
