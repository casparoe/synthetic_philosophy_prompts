Ok so I've been reading about the forgery debate in aesthetics — you know, whether a perfect forgery of a painting has less aesthetic value than the original even if no one can tell them apart — and I've noticed something wierd. This argument has been going on at least since Goodman's *Languages of Art* in 1968 and arguably since the van Meegeren Vermeer forgeries in the 40s, and it feels totally stuck. You've got one camp saying that if two objects are perceptually indistinguishable then any preference for one over the other is just snobbery or investment psychology, not aesthetics proper. And you've got the other camp saying that the history of production *is* an aesthetic property — that knowing something was painted by Vermeer rather than van Meegeren changes what you see in it, and that's a legitimate aesthetic difference, not a category mistake.

What's strange to me is that both sides seem to grant each other's factual claims. Nobody disputes that the two paintings look identical. Nobody disputes that people feel differently about them once they know the provenance. So what's left to argue about? My working theory is that this isn't really one debate — it's two different projects sharing a vocabulary. One side is doing a kind of descriptive psychology of aesthetic experience (what do people actually value and why?), while the other is doing normative aesthetics (what *should* they value?). If that's right, they've been talking past each other for fifty years. But I'm not sure — maybe I'm missing a substantive disagreement that's still on the table, or maybe there's a third diagnosis that makes better sense of why neither side budges.

Diagnose this for me: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? And given your diagnosis, is there anything that could move it, or is this one of those disputes where the best we can do is clarify what each side is really after?
