I'm teaching an upper-division epistemology seminar in the fall and I want to build a "which norm of assertion do you actually hold?" sorting quiz for the first day. You know the debate: some people think the rule of assertion is knowledge, others truth, others belief, others reasonable belief or justification, and a few think there's no special epistemic norm at all — just ordinary conversational rules. I want the quiz to sort students by how they actually answer cases, not by which position they like the sound of. So no question can mention the labels, and nothing like "do you need to know to assert?" — every item has to be a concrete little scenario with two or three answer options.

The quiz should have eight to ten questions, and it has to use at least these cases somewhere: (1) a fair lottery where the draw has happened but the speaker hasn't checked, and they assert "my ticket lost"; (2) a teacher who doesn't personally believe evolution but teaches it because the curriculum requires it, and asserts the standard evolutionary claims; (3) a Moore-paradox-style case where someone asserts "it's raining but I don't know that it's raining"; (4) a lucky guess — a groundless hunch that happens to be true; (5) a stopped-clock case where the speaker has a justified true belief that isn't knowledge; and (6) a case where the claim is true and known but irrelevant or actively misleading in context. You can add one or two more of your own if they help seperate positions.

For each question: a short scenario, then ask whether the assertion is proper or permissible, with options like yes / no / it depends — and if you use "it depends," spell out what it depends on. Then provide a scoring key that maps answer patterns to these five positions: knowledge norm, truth norm, belief norm, reasonable-belief norm, and conversational/no-special-norm. The key should be built from the combinations of answers each position would give, and it should explicitly flag (a) any pairs of positions the questions can't actually tell apart, and (b) any answer pattern no position predicts, with a one-line diagnosis of whether that pattern is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. On ties or ambiguous patterns, say so rather than silently assigning the pattern to a position.

I'd like the whole deliverable as a JSON object, since I'll turn it into a little web quiz. Use these fields: "title" (string); "audience" (one sentence); "questions" (array of objects with "id", "scenario", "question", "options" as an array of strings, and "separates", a short note on which positions the item mainly pulls apart); "positions" (array of objects with "name", "one_sentence_gloss", and "expected_pattern", a list like ["q1_no","q2_yes"]); "scoring_key" (object mapping patterns to positions, plus a "tie_breaking" note); "inseparable_pairs" (array of objects with "pair" and "why"); and "unoccupied_patterns" (array of objects with "pattern" and "diagnosis"). Keep the scenarios to a couple sentances each, and write them so a student can answer without having read any of the literature — no question should tip off which position it's fishing for.
