I sit on the programme committee for a workshop on Bayesian epistemology, and we have been running a two-year adversarial collaboration between a defender of Bayesian confirmation theory and a philosopher who holds that the problem of old evidence exposes a structural defect in Bayesianism that cannot be repaired without abandoning the framework. The collaboration is concluding, and the pair have been asked to produce a joint statement. I need you to draft that statement.

The problem of old evidence, in brief: if an agent already knows E, then P(E) = 1, so P(H|E) = P(H) for any H — old evidence cannot confirm. Yet in scientific practice, a theory is often held to be confirmed by evidence that was known before the theory was formulated. The defender maintains that the problem can be resolved within the Bayesian framework, whether by counterfactual degrees of belief, by enriching the hypothesis space, or by some other move that preserves the core Bayesian machinery. The critic holds that the problem reveals a principled limitation: Bayesian confirmation theory constitutively cannot capture the confirmatory role of evidence that was already in hand, and the attempted fixes either collapse into something non-Bayesian or fail on their own terms.

Draft the document they should sign. It must contain:

1. A numbered list of claims both parties accept, about the formal structure of the problem, about what a successful account must do, and about which attempted resolutions they agree have failed and why. These should be substantive — not truisms — and should reflect genuine convergence after two years of work.

2. A table of residual disagreements. Each row should state the precise point at issue, the defender's formulation of her own position, and the critic's formulation of his, each in wording its own proponent would endorse. The table should also identify, for each disagreement, whether it is substantive or partly verbal, and if verbal, specify which term or concept is being used differently.

3. A crux list: the sub-questions that, if settled, would move at least one party on at least one residual disagreement. For each crux, state what each party predicts about how it would be resolved, and describe a concrete test, argument, or formal result that both agree would bear on it — even if they disagree about what it would show.

4. A brief closing note on what the collaboration changed: which views each party entered with and which they modified, and what each now regards as the strongest consideration on the other side.

The statement should be in British English throughout. The tone should be collegial and precise, suitable for circulation to a philosophical audience that includes both Bayesians and their critics.
