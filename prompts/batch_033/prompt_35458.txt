I'm on my university's academic grievance committee and we've got a weird one this week. A sophmore is appealing a failing grade from a philosophy of logic course, and the whole thing turns on the surprise exam paradox. Here's the deal: the professor's syllabus said, quote, "There will be exactly one in-class quiz this semester. It will be a surprise — meaning you will not be able to deduce the date of the quiz from the syllabus before the day it happens." The quiz was given on the 8th class meeting (out of 14), the student bombed it, and now they're arguing the entire quiz policy was logically impossible. They've submitted a three-page proof walking through the standard backwards-induction argument: the quiz can't be on the last day because by then it wouldn't be a surprise, so it can't be on the second-to-last day either, and so on all the way back to day one. Their conclusion is that the syllabus contained an incoherent requirement, so the quiz was illegitimate and the grade should be struck.

The professor's response is basically: look, the quiz happened on day 8, the student was genuinely surprised, so the policy worked exactly as stated. He also points out that the student didn't object to the syllabus at the start of the semester — they only came up with the logical argument after they failed.

Our committee is split. A couple members think this is just a clever student trying to logic their way out of a bad grade and we should deny it summarily. Others think the student has identified a genuine problem: if a policy is logically impossible to satisfy, can you really penalize someone under it? The chair wants a short written opinion from me before we vote on Thursday. I need to recommend whether to grant or deny the appeal, with reasoning that takes the student's logical argument seriously — not just hand-waving it away as sophistry. The committee actually need to understand why the paradox does or doesn't apply here, and whatever we decide is gonna set a precedent for syllabi across the college.

Give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no introduction or conclusion. And write it in American English.
