I am a novelist. For the past decade I have run a residential writing workshop — sixteen participants, all experienced writers but not academics, living together for a week and working on book-length projects. One evening session (three hours, after dinner) is always set aside for something oblique: an exercise that unsettles a habit of thought they didn't know they had. This year I want to build that session around the extended mind thesis.

I want them to feel the thesis before they hear it named. The core claim — that cognitive processes can extend beyond the skull into the external environment, that a notebook or a set of index cards can be *part of* one's mind rather than merely a tool one uses — should surface as a discovery about their own writing practice, not as a proposition delivered from the front of the room. No lecture. They should not hear the phrase "extended mind" until the debrief.

Here is what I need you to design. Please approach this as a professional philosopher of mind who also understands the dynamics of a workshop setting. Begin by brainstorming — what is the core experience you want the participants to undergo, and what sequence of tasks would make that experience unavoidable? Then build the rules, materials, timeline, and debrief around that experience.

The broad shape I have in mind, though you should refine or replace it:

- Early in the session, each participant is given a writing task that forces them to build up an external cognitive system — notes, sketches, timelines, character maps, whatever — that they come to rely on heavily. The task should be genuinely engaging as writing work, not a sterile puzzle.
- At a carefully chosen moment, that external system is disrupted or removed (not merely "put away"; the disruption should be surprising and structurally revealing), and they are given a second task that makes visible how much of their thinking was located in the artifacts they had assembled.
- The whole arc should make the parity principle intuitive: if an external resource plays the same functional role as an internal cognitive process, there is no principled reason to deny it cognitive status.

The debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto the philosophical architecture: the parity principle itself, the Otto-and-Inga thought experiment, the worry about cognitive bloat, and the objection that the external stuff is not genuinely cognitive because it is not conscious. Be honest about which feature of the thesis the activity cannot reproduce — I suspect it is the "glue and trust" conditions Andy Clark later added, or perhaps the deeper question of the mark of the cognitive. The debrief should feel like the group collectively unpacking what they just lived through, not like a mini-lecture.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this. Which participants will resist the intended takeaway, what exactly will they say, and how should I handle it without turning the debrief into a philosophy seminar? If the activity risks producing a different lesson than the one I want — say, a lesson about the fragility of memory rather than about the location of cognition — flag that and tell me how to steer the debrief back.

Finally, the activity should feel like a writing exercise throughout. The participants are novelists; they should leave with something useful for their craft, even if the philosophical reframing is the real payload.
