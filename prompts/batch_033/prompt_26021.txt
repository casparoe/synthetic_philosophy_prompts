I'm working on a novel where my protagonist has just found out her husband is cheating — she's seen the texts, the credit card receipts, everything. But she can't face it yet. In a scene with her sister, she sort of blurts out: "He's having an affair, but I don't believe it."

My editor flagged the line as nonsense. She says you can't assert something and then say you don't believe it — it's like saying "the cat's on the mat but I think it isn't." But I've heard people say things like "I know it's true but I can't bring myself to believe it" in real life, and it felt psychologically real to me when I wrote it.

So here's what I'm after: is there a way to read my character's line that makes it coherent rather than just a contradiction? What's actually going on, philosophically, when someone says something like this? Is she describing two different parts of her mind, or is there something about the logic of belief and assertion that makes the statement paradoxical in an interesting way rather than just dumb?

The whole novel turns on how people can know something and not-know it at the same time, so I want to get the underlying structure right. And please — seperate what's basically settled among philosophers who work on this stuff from what's genuinely contested. I don't want to build my character's psychology on some fringe view and then get demolished by a reviewer with a philosophy degree.
