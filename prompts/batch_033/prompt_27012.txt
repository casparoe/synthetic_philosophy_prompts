So I've been going back and forth with some people on a forum about Maimonides on animal sacrifice, and I keep hitting the same wall. The basic fact everyone agrees on is that in the Guide of the Perplexed he says the sacrificial system wasn't the ideal form of worship: it was a concession to the Israelites because they'd been soaked in idolatry and couldn't have handled a religion with no sacrifices at all. He leans on the verse about God not taking Israel the short way out of Egypt because they'd lose heart and go back, and he spends a lot of ink explaining specific sacrificial laws as anti-idolatry moves—rules that deliberately tweak the surrounding pagan cults. So far, uncontroversial.

The fight is over what this means. One camp reads him as basically saying sacrifices are a second-best, a piece of divine accomodation that in a better world would never have been commanded, and that intellectual worship—or at least prayer—is the real thing. The other camp says no: Maimonides still held that sacrifices were genuinely commanded and binding, and that giving a historical rationale for them—weaning people off idolatry—doesn't make them worthless, any more than explaining why a medicine is prescribed to a sick patient makes the medicine fake. Underneath that there's the Ramban-style objection that a God who commands sacrifices only as damage control for bad times is making a pretty big concession, and that sacrifices are about drawing near (korban—the root is about nearness, and all that), not just anti-pagan damage control.

I've watched this argument go in circles for a while, and it feels like one of those disputes where the two sides aren't actually disagreeing about the same question but nobody can say exactly which two questions. This is the kind of thing where tabooing a word is supposed to dissolve the argument, but I can't figure out which word to taboo. So here's what I want: diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it a textual question (what did Maimonides literally say), a philosophical question (what follows from his broader theory of the commandments about the status of a commandment whose rationale is historical accommodation), a theological one (whether a rationalist account of sacrifice can coexist with sacrifices still being commanded), or something else entirely? My working theory is that it's secretly a disagreement about whether explaining a commandment's origin can undercut its ongoing normative force, and the Maimonides text is just where that disagreement happens to get staged. But I could be wrong. And given whatever diagnosis you land on, tell me what would actually count as progress—a distinction, an argument, a change of question—or whether I should just expect the circle to keep going.
