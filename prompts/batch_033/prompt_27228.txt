I'm in a vagueness seminar this term and I've come out as an epistemicist: I think borderline sentences are still either true or false, the boundary is just unknowable, and supervaluationism's way of keeping classical logic while saying neither side is true feels like it's trying to have it both ways. But I've noticed I mostly defend my view by poking holes in supervaluationism rather than by having positive evidence for sharp boundaries, so I want to know what would actually count against epistemicism.

I'd like the defeaters sorted from fatal to merely awkward. Some of the things I keep going back and forth on: higher-order vagueness, the fact that competent speakers reject both the sentence that the patch is red and the sentence that the patch is not red while accepting the disjunction either the patch is red or it isn't, the behavior of modifiers like very and comparatives like redder than, and the weirdness of saying there's a last red patch in a sorites series. Which of these do epistemicists themselves treat as real costs, and which do they think they can absorb? Also be straight with me about whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability. If the boundary is unknowable in principle, is there any conceivable result, linguistic or psychological, that could tell against epistemicism? If not, is that a worse problem for a semantic theory then it would be for a metaphysical one?
