After a long argument with a friend, I've come to hold a hinge-epistemology view drawn from Wittgenstein's On Certainty: certain commitments — that there is an external world, that the earth has existed for a very long time, that my memory is broadly trustworthy — are not ordinary empirical beliefs held on evidence, but the ungrounded, taken-for-granted scaffolding that makes evidence, doubt, and inquiry possible at all; wholesale doubt of them is not a deeper intellectual option but a failure of doubt to get a grip. I want a genuine map of the view's exposure rather than a defense of it. Which observations, cases, arguments, or results in neighboring fields would show that these hinges are really just very general empirical beliefs, or that they can be meaningfully doubted, or that the view has been qualified so far that it risks nothing? Distinguish what would be decisive from what would merely be awkward, say which defeaters a careful hinge epistemologist would themselves concede as counting against the position rather than dismiss, and assess whether the position has been insulated into unfalsifiability — and, if so, whether that is a problem for a view of this kind. I am especially interested in whether it can distinguish genuine hinges from merely deep-seated but revisable convictions, and whether variation across communities in what is treated as beyond question turns it into a kind of relativism.
