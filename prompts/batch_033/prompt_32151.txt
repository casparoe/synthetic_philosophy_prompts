Kant draws a sharp distinction between acting from duty and acting merely in accordance with duty. An action has moral worth, for Kant, only when the agent performs it out of respect for the moral law — that is, because they recognize the action as morally required and this recognition is what moves them to act. When an agent performs the same outward action but is motivated by something else, like self-interest or natural inclination, the action is merely in accordance with duty and lacks moral worth.

Kant's main example is the shopkeeper. A shopkeeper who gives correct change to customers because it's good for business acts merely in accordance with duty — his honesty serves his self-interest. By contrast, a shopkeeper who gives correct change because he recognizes honesty as a duty acts from duty, and his action has genuine moral worth. Kant also discusses the "friend of humanity" who helps others because he finds joy in spreading happiness. This person acts in accordance with duty, but because his motive is sympathy rather than duty, his action lacks moral worth.

Now, the parent case raises the overdetermination problem: the parent is motivated both by love for their child and by the recognition that caring for their child is a duty. If we read Kant strictly, it seems the parent's action lacks moral worth because an inclination-based motive is present alongside duty. But this seems too harsh — surely the parent's action is morally worthy.

Kantians have several responses. The first, associated with Barbara Herman, says that an action can have moral worth as long as the motive of duty is present and sufficient to produce the action on its own. The fact that the agent also has inclinations pointing in the same direction doesn't undermine moral worth. The second response is that we should distinguish moral worth from moral permissibility. Even if the parent's action lacks moral worth, it's still permissible and even praiseworthy in some sense — it just doesn't have the special kind of worth Kant reserves for purely duty-motivated actions. The objection is therefore less damaging than it seems. A third response draws on Kant's distinction between perfect and imperfect duties. The parent's duty to care for their child is an imperfect duty, and Kant's standards for moral worth may be less strict for imperfect duties than for perfect duties like the duty not to lie.
