I'm taking a philosophy of science course on causal inference and I've basically came around to the view that treating factors as independent until you have specific positive evidence of a common cause or interaction is not just a modeling convenience but a truth-tracking default. Like, if I'm looking at a system and I don't have any particular reason to think X and Y are connected, the right move is to factorize the joint and not go hunting for hidden confounders unless something flags one. I want to know what would actually count against this as a general epistemic principle, not just as a rule of thumb for keeping models small. Wich cases, formal results, or experimental setups should make me think the presumption of independence systematically misleads rather than just occasionally misses? I'd like the defeaters ranked: which are fatal to the strong version, which only force a weaker version where independence is the default until a specific cue gives you reason to look further, and which are just local failures the view can absorb. Also, does the view risk being unfalsifiable because any apparent counterexample can be redescribed after the fact as a case where you did have information linking them? If it is unfalsifiable, is that a problem for a default like this, or is that just what it is to be a default? One thing that would personally move me is a clean case where assuming independence doesn't just make you wrong but flips the conclusion you'd otherwise draw.
