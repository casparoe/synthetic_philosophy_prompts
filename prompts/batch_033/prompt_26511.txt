Dr. Chen and Dr. Navarro are theoretical physicists who have just spent the day at a workshop on the foundations of quantum mechanics. They are now sitting in a quiet corner of a hotel bar, still deep in conversation.

DR. CHEN: I want to return to something you said during the panel this afternoon. You claimed that treating the wavefunction as epistemic — as a compact summary of an agent's knowledge or beliefs about a system — dissolves the measurement problem rather than merely restating it. I've been turning that over in my head, and I don't see how the move actually works. Could you walk me through it?

DR. NAVARRO: Gladly. The measurement problem, at bottom, is the problem of reconciling the deterministic, unitary evolution of the wavefunction with the apparently discontinuous, probabilistic change that occurs upon measurement. But notice that this is only a problem if you take the wavefunction to be a physical entity undergoing real physical change. If the wavefunction is instead a representation of an agent's information — a credal state, if you like — then the so-called collapse is nothing more than Bayesian updating. You acquire new data, you revise your probability assignments. No one thinks Bayesian updating is a physical process that needs a dynamical explanation. The measurement problem evaporates.

DR. CHEN: That's a clean story, but I worry it explains too little rather than too much. The wavefunction doesn't merely assign probabilities to outcomes in the way a Bayesian prior does. It encodes phase relationships, interference terms, the full algebraic structure of a vector in Hilbert space. If the wavefunction is purely epistemic, why does the world respect the Born rule? Why does the structure of our knowledge take this particular, highly constrained mathematical form? In a psi-ontic picture, the answer is direct: the wavefunction corresponds to something physically real, and the Born rule reflects the dynamics of that real thing.

DR. NAVARRO: I'd answer that the structure comes from the operational constraints that define what it means to acquire information about a system that admits complementary observables. There's a whole research program — quantum reconstructions — that derives the Hilbert space formalism and the Born rule from simple information-theoretic principles. The mathematical structure isn't a mystery that cries out for an ontological ground; it's the natural consequence of formalizing measurement and information-acquisition in a world where not all questions can be asked simultaneously. The structure is imposed by the nature of inquiry, not by the nature of reality.

DR. CHEN: I'm familiar with the reconstruction program, and I'm sympathetic to parts of it. But reconstructions are, at best, neutral on the ontology. You can run the same derivation and still be a realist about the quantum state — the derivation tells you what formal constraints any successful physical theory must satisfy, not whether the resulting objects are epistemic or ontic. And in any case, the PBR theorem seems to put real pressure on the epistemic view. If you want distinct quantum states to correspond to overlapping probability distributions over some underlying ontic state space, you have to give up preparation independence — or accept that the overlap is vanishingly small. That's a significant cost.

DR. NAVARRO: The PBR theorem is important, but its scope is narrower than it's sometimes taken to be. It rules out maximally psi-epistemic models — models where the epistemic states for distinct quantum states have substantial overlap — under the assumption that independently prepared systems have independent ontic states. But a thoroughgoing epistemic view has independent reasons to question preparation independence. If the ontic state of a system is relational, or if it depends on the full experimental context, then the independence assumption doesn't hold in the way the theorem requires. The theorem doesn't rule out epistemic views; it tells us what epistemic views must look like.

DR. CHEN: That's a fair point about the assumptions, but notice what you're committing yourself to. To preserve the epistemic character of the wavefunction, you're now positing contextual ontic states, relational properties, and a failure of preparation independence. At some point I have to ask which view is really more parsimonious. You're adding structure to avoid reifying the wavefunction, when reifying it — as Everett did, or as de Broglie and Bohm did — gives you a clear, if counterintuitive, picture of what the world is like.

DR. NAVARRO: Parsimony cuts both ways, and I think you're underestimating the costs on your side. The Everettian pays for a real wavefunction with a staggering multiplicity of worlds, the vast majority of which are empirically inaccessible. The Bohmian pays for it with a guidance equation and a preferred foliation of spacetime that breaks Lorentz invariance at the fundamental level. My supposedly baroque metaphysics — relational ontic states, contextuality — may actually be leaner than either of those. And it has the virtue of taking seriously the idea that physics is ultimately about what agents can predict and observe, not about describing a reality that floats free of our epistemic situation.

DR. CHEN: I think we may have reached the bedrock here. You're willing to treat the predictive and observational success of quantum mechanics as the end of the explanatory story. I want there to be a further story about what the world is like such that those predictions come out right. I don't think either of us can claim a decisive argument at this stage.

DR. NAVARRO: Agreed. But I'll leave you with this thought. The history of physics is littered with entities we once took to be physically real — caloric, the luminiferous ether, absolute simultaneity — that turned out to be features of our representational frameworks rather than features of the world. I suspect the wavefunction will join that list.

DR. CHEN: Or it will join the list of things we initially dismissed as mere calculational devices — atoms, fields, spacetime curvature — that turned out to be real after all. The historical track record cuts both ways, and I suspect we'll be arguing about this one for a long time.
