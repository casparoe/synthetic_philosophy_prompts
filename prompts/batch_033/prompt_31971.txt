I've been thinking about something and I wonder if there's a name for it, or at least if philosophers have mapped out the territory.

People often say things like "if I'd been born in the 18th century I'd have been a revolutionary" or "if I'd grown up in a different country I'd be a totally different person." These feel like they're still about *me* — same person, different circumstances.

But then there are counterfactuals that seem to cross a line. "If my parents had never met, I wouldn't exist." That already feels different — less like me-in-another-life and more like the absence of me. And then: "If a different sperm had fertilised the egg, I wouldn't be here." That one feels even stronger — it's not just that I wouldn't exist, but that someone else would exist instead. And yet weirdly, "if I'd been born to different parents" seems to split people: some say "that wouldn't be me," others say "no, I can imagine being born to different parents."

So there seems to be a gradient — or maybe a sharp line — between counterfactuals that preserve identity and those that don't. But where is it exactly? Is it the particular sperm and egg? Is it some psychological-continuity thing? Do identical twins break the sperm-and-egg criterion — same sperm, same egg, different people?

I've also noticed this comes up in arguments about luck and privilege. Someone says "you were just lucky to be born into a wealthy family," and the response is sometimes "if I'd been born into a poor family I wouldn't be me." That feels like a weird deflection — like it's confusing two different senses of "could have been me." Is there a name for that move, and for the underlying distinction?

I'm not just after a label — I want to know how the whole cluster is carved up, what the main positions are, and whether the sperm-and-egg criterion is actually the standard view or just one option among several. And if the argumentative move has it's own name, even better.

Oh, and please write the answer in British English.
