I am a retired physical scientist who has recently taken up epistemology, and I have been recording a philosopher friend's judgments about when a speaker is entitled to assert something. Her verdicts so far are these. (1) In good light, a speaker looks at a red wall and says, "That wall is red"; she judges this proper. (2) With no meteorological evidence, and without actually believing it, a speaker says, "It will rain tomorrow"; it does not rain; she judges this improper. (3) With no evidence and no belief either way, a speaker says, "My old friend is in town this week"; this happens to be true; she judges this improper. (4) On a pure hunch, which she sincerely believes but for which she has no evidence, a speaker says, "My old friend is in town this week"; it is false; she judges this improper.

She will next be asked about this case: a speaker has excellent evidence that his colleague Smith owns a Ford—he has seen Smith drive a Ford to work for years, and Smith has shown him the registration—and he asserts, "Someone in my department owns a Ford." In fact, Smith sold the car yesterday and no longer owns it, but another colleague, Jones, bought a Ford this morning.

Predict her verdict. State which of the standard candidate norms—the knowledge norm, the truth norm, the belief norm, the justified-belief norm—her earlier verdicts already exclude, which remain live, and what each live norm predicts for the Ford case. If her record underdetermines the prediction, say so, and specify the further case that would most cleanly settle the matter.
