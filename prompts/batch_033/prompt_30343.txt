I work as a software engineer and do a fair amount of probabilistic modeling, so I'm comfortable with conditional probability, Bayes' theorem, and the idea that a coherent set of degrees of belief should satisfy the probability axioms. Lately I've been reading Bayesian epistemology in my spare time, and I keep running into van Fraassen's Reflection Principle, which says something like: my current credence in a proposition, conditional on my future credence in that proposition being r, should itself be r. I understand the formal statement, but I don't understand what sort of principle it is supposed to be. The probability axioms are synchronic constraints on a single credence function; the Principal Principle is a deference principle that ties my credences to objective chances; conditionalization tells me how to change my credences when I get evidence. Reflection, by contrast, seems to tell me to treat my own future self as an expert, and I can't see why I should do that, especially since I know my future self might be tired, forgetful, or misinformed. I have no formal training in philosophy, so I suspect I'm missing the underlying conceptual structure rather than the math.

Could you explain the conceptual issue in a way that keeps the formal content precise? In particular: (1) What exactly does Reflection add over and above conditionalization and the probability axioms — is it meant to be a consequence of them, a further normative constraint, or a pragmatic constraint backed by a Dutch-book-style argument? (2) How are the standard apparent counterexamples supposed to be handled — cases where I anticipate my future credence will be irrational or degraded (sleep deprivation, a drug, memory loss, a known cognitive bias), and cases where I anticipate that my future self will have evidence I now lack? (3) Does Reflection properly apply to my future self simply because it is my future self, or is it really a disguised claim about my future self's expected reliability? I'd like the answer to make the distinctions sharp enough that I could explain to a colleague why some people think Reflection is trivial and others think it's false.
