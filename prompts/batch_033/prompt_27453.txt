I'm preparing a seminar on Sartre's concepts of radical freedom and bad faith, and I want to use Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day as the central literary text. The butler Stevens seems to me to be a nearly perfect case study in bad faith, but I want to make sure I'm not just cherry-picking scenes that fit the theory while ignoring what the novel actually does with them.

Here is what I think I see. Stevens treats his role as a butler not as a contingent social position he has chosen and could in principle abandon, but as what he is — he identifies himself entirely with his function, which is exactly Sartre's description of bad faith: the attempt to become a being-in-itself, a thing with a fixed essence, rather than accepting the burden of being a being-for-itself who must continually choose. His ideal of "dignity" — the butler who never drops character, who inhabits his role so completely that there is no gap between the man and the performance — is the ideal of bad faith made into a virtue. His refusal to acknowledge his feelings for Miss Kenton, his inability to question Lord Darlington's political judgments even when they involve Nazi appeasement and the dismissal of Jewish staff, his framing of his own passivity as professional excellence — all of this looks like the flight from freedom that Sartre describes.

But the novel complicates this. Stevens is not a free-floating consciousness who simply chooses bad faith out of weakness; he is embedded in a class structure that genuinely constrains his options, and his father before him was a butler who modeled the same ideal of self-erasure. The novel also makes Stevens deeply sympathetic — his self-deception is bound up with real virtues of loyalty, discipline, and care, and the reader feels the loss he has inflicted on himself as something genuinely tragic rather than merely culpable. And then there is the question of whether Stevens, by the end of the novel, achieves anything like an authentic recognition of his situation, or whether his final reflections are just another layer of bad faith.

I would like a reading that does three things. First, walk through three or four specific episodes from the novel and show exactly how Sartre's framework illuminates Stevens's psychology in ways that ordinary notions of self-deception or repression do not capture — what does calling it "bad faith" rather than "denial" or "rationalization" buy us? Second, identify the points where the novel pushes back against a Sartrean reading: where does Ishiguro seem to be saying that Stevens's situation is not adequately described as a free choice of bad faith, and that Sartre's picture of radical freedom misses something about how social structures shape the self? Third, say what the novel contributes to the philosophical discussion that Sartre's theoretical apparatus does not — does Ishiguro dramatize some feature of bad faith that the abstract account in Being and Nothingness misses or flattens? I am less interested in a verdict on whether Stevens is "guilty" than in whether the novel reveals something about the structure and the cost of bad faith that philosophy alone cannot.
