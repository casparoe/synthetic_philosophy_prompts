I keep noticing something in how public discourse works, especially online but also in the older media, and I can't find the right word for it.

The press reports on some political scandal or controversy, and suddenly everyone has an opinion. People who have no stake in the outcome, no relevant expertise, and no intention of doing anything about it, all feel entitled to a view and, more than that, feel they've done something worthwhile just by having that view and maybe posting about it. But here's the thing: "the public" gets invoked as this serious moral force — "the public demands answers," "public opinion has turned" — and yet there's no actual person whose considered judgment it is, and no one who would take responsibility if the collective reaction turned out to be wrong or destructive. It's an aggregate of private individuals consuming news in their living rooms or scrolling on their phones, but it gets treated as a kind of agent with a will of its own.

Then there's the other side: the press presents itself as merely reflecting what the public thinks, but it also decides what's news and how to frame it, so it's shaping the very opinions it then reports on. There's this loop: the press creates a public, then claims to speak for it, and the public consumes the press's products and feels like it's participating in civic life. But nobody is actually doing anything except reading and talking.

I've seen this pattern in everything from newspaper coverage of a distant war to twitter pile-ons, and it always feels like the same underlying thing. I know there's a lot written about the public sphere, the fourth estate, the tyranny of the majority, and so on. But what I'm describing feels more specific: it's about the way the press and the public co-produce each other into a kind of phantom entity that has opinions without responsibility, and how this lets people feel morally serious without ever having to commit or act. Is there a specific concept for this exact configuration, and if not, can you name it and distinguish it carefully from the usual public-sphere ideas?
