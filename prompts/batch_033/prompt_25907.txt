So my friend Dave and I just spent like two hours going back and forth on Keynes and the good life — specifically, what he actually thought it consisted of once the economic problem was solved — and we couldn't settle it. We were both working from the same two texts, basically, "Economic Possibilities for Our Grandchildren" and "My Early Beliefs," but we walked away with completely different pictures. I'll lay out the argument as it happened and then ask you to sort through it.

Dave's position, roughly, was that Keynes had a pretty clear, positive vision of the good life and it was basically the Bloomsbury ideal: once we're freed from the need to work, the good life is about cultivating states of mind, aesthetic experience, and personal relationships. He pointed to the famous passage where Keynes says the love of money as a possession — as distinct from the love of money as a means to enjoyments — will be recognized for what it is, a somewhat disgusting morbidity, one of those semi-criminal, semi-pathological propensities. Dave said that's the key: the whole point of the essay is that we've been stuck in a morality of accumulation and striving that's actually a kind of collective mental illness, and the good life is what's on the other side of that — art, friendship, contemplation, the "art of life itself" that Keynes gestures toward.

I pushed back that this reading makes Keynes sound like he's just a Bloomsbury aesthete, and that doesn't square with the whole essay. I said Keynes is actually a lot more ambivalent than that. He calls the money-making drive a morbidity, sure, but he also says that for the time being we have to keep pretending that fair is foul and foul is fair — that avarice and usury have to be our gods for a while longer. And the way he writes about the "purposeful money-makers" has a weird kind of admiration in it, even if it's grudging. He says they're the ones who carry the rest of us on their backs. That's not just contempt.

Dave came back with: sure, but that's instrumental. The money-makers are useful because they're solving the economic problem, not because their way of life is actually good. They're a necessary evil for a transitional period. The telos, the endpoint, is the life of leisure and cultivation. The instrumental value of striving doesn't make striving part of the good life.

I said that's too neat, because in "My Early Beliefs" Keynes actually criticizes the Bloomsbury worldview he'd been part of. He says they were too dismissive of the "inner need" that drives people to strive and achieve, and that they misunderstood human nature by ignoring the value of purposeful activity. He says they gave too much weight to states of mind and not enough to the kinds of commitments and projects that structure a life over time. So it's not just that striving is instrumentally useful for reaching a future where no one strives — it's that Keynes, on reflection, thought striving and purposeful activity might be part of a well-lived human life in a way the early Bloomsbury view didn't acknowledge.

Dave's response was that I'm conflating two different things. He said the critique in "My Early Beliefs" is about their theory of human nature and their naivete about how social change works — it's not a revision of the ideal of the good life itself. Keynes still thought the endpoint was a world where people enjoy the "art of life itself," and he still thought that art consists in the cultivation of relationships, beauty, and states of mind. The fact that he came to think people have a psychological need for purposeful activity doesn't mean he elevated that need into an ideal — it just means he realized the transition to the ideal would be psychologically harder than he'd thought in his twenties.

I said that's a distinction without a difference. If you come to believe that human beings have a deep-seated need for striving and purposeful activity, then a vision of the good life that consists entirely of contemplative leisure is incomplete — it's not a good life for the kind of creatures we are. Keynes in the "Economic Possibilities" essay even worries about this explicitly: he says there's a problem of what people will do with their freedom when they no longer have the pressure of economic necessity, and he notes that the wealthy classes who have already reached this state have mostly failed at it — they're bored and directionless. That's not a side note; it's the central difficulty. And he doesn't really solve it.

Dave said that's exactly his point — Keynes is identifying a cultural and psychological problem of adjustment, not revising his view of what the good life would be. The good life is still the life of cultivation. The problem is that people have been so warped by capitalism that they've lost the capacity for it. The therapy is to recover that capacity, not to redefine the good life to include the pathology.
