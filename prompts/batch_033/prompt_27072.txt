I keep running into the sentence 'a nation has the right to control its borders' in debates about immigration, most recently when I suggested that restrictions on the free movement of people are morally analogous to restrictions on the free movement of goods. My interlocutor deployed it as if it were a self-evident axiom that simply ends the discussion. I suspect the sentence is doing a lot of work by being ambiguous between several quite different claims. Please lay out the distinct precise theses it could be expressing — distinguishing readings that differ on what kind of right is being invoked (moral, legal, or something else), who or what the right-holder is (the state, the citizenry, the national community, the territory itself), what the right is grounded in (self-determination, property, association, security, convention), what 'control' amounts to (full discretion, a presumption with exceptions, something weaker), and whose claims the right is supposed to override. For each reading, say whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, or probably false, with a brief justification. Then tell me which of these readings, if any, would actually support dismissing the analogy between immigration restrictions and trade restrictions, and which would not survive that analogy. Please write in British English and keep the response to no more than 1,200 words.
