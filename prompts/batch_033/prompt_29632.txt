I'm a high school student writing a paper for an independent study on the philosophy of mathematics, and I have worked out an argument that I now find suspicious because its conclusion seems to leave too much of ordinary probabilistic reasoning unexplained. I would like to know which step goes wrong, and what I would be committed to if I rejected it.

(1) The principle of indifference says that if I have no reason to regard any one of several mutually exclusive possibilities as more likely than another, I should assign them equal probability.

(2) In Bertrand's paradox, I ask for the probability that a chord chosen at random from a circle is longer than a side of the equilateral triangle inscribed in it. The phrase chosen at random seems to mean that I have no information favoring any chord, or any method of selecting one, over another.

(3) If I choose a radius and a point on it uniformly, and draw the chord perpendicular to the radius through that point, the probability is one half; if I choose the two endpoints uniformly on the circumference, it is one third; if I choose the chord's midpoint uniformly inside the circle, it is one fourth.

(4) Each of these three assignments is an application of the principle of indifference to the same problem, merely described in a different way.

(5) Therefore the principle of indifference yields incompatible answers to a single well-posed question, so it is not a valid rule of probabilistic reasoning.

(6) But the same principle seems to be what justifies our usual probability assignments for fair coins, dice, and card draws. If (5) is right, those assignments lose their standard justification.

I would most like to keep the principle of indifference in some form, because abandoning it entirely seems to make too much of our ordinary reasoning about chance mysterious. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
