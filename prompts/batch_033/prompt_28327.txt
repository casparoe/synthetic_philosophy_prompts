I'm TAing a philosophy of literature course and grading this midterm. heres the question I set and a student's answer. I want you to grade the answer out of 10 and, more importantly, give me the specific comments I should write on it — which claims earn credit, which lose it, and why. Dont just give an overall impression; go point by point.

Question: "A critic says a poem is about the death of the poet's father. The poet later says: 'I meant it to be about the end of a close friendship.' Does the poet's statement settle what the poem means? Answer by explaining what an anti-intentionalist about literary meaning can say here, including what, if anything, the poet's statement does change."

Student's answer: "No, the poet's statement doesn't settle the poem's meaning. An anti-intentionalist holds that a text's meaning is fixed by the public conventions of the language and the features of the text itself, not by whatever private mental states the author happened to have while writing. This is the point of the 'intentional fallacy': it's a mistake to confuse what an author was trying to do with what the text actually says. If meanings were private intentions, communication would be impossible, since we could never check anyone's words against anything public. So the critic could be wrong too, and the poet's later gloss isn't authoritative.

That doesn't mean the poet's statement changes nothing. Here we can use Hirsch's distinction between meaning and significance: the meaning of the poem stays fixed, but its significance — how it relates to readers, to the poet's biography, to other texts — changes once we learn what the poet says he meant. That's also how an anti-intentionalist can explain ordinary talk about what an author 'meant': we track the author's intention as one piece of evidence among others, but it's never decisive, because the text's public meaning can override it."
