I'm organizing a workshop on the cognitive penetration of perception and I want to produce a document that both a committed defender of cognitive penetrability and a committed defender of cognitive impenetrability could sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. You are an expert philosopher of mind and cognitive science who has followed this debate closely, including the methodological critiques from both sides. Think carefully about the strongest versions of each position and where the genuine disagreements lie, and begin by brainstorming what each side would need to concede to the other before a joint statement could even get off the ground.

Draft the joint statement. It should include: the claims about perception, cognition, attention, and experimental methodology that both sides can accept; the precise formulations of their remaining disagreements, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording; the cruxes—the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side; and, for each crux, the experimental paradigm or argument both sides would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Pay special attention to whether any part of the dispute turns out to be verbal—about what counts as "perception" or "cognitive," or about where to draw the line between perception and perceptual judgment—and if so, say exactly which part and why. Take a committed approach: don't just list possibilities; make definite choices about what the strongest formulations are and which disagreements survive clarification. Write in continuous prose only, as you would in a letter or an email—no headings, no bullet points, no tables.
