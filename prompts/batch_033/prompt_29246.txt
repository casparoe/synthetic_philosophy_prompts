I'm a science journalist, most of my beat is AI and the people making claims about it. Every week I get pitched some version of "our model beat the benchmark" or "superhuman performance on X," and I have maybe twenty-five minutes before my editor wants to know whether we're running it. Getting it wrong means we either hype a trick or bury a real story, and either way we burn trust with readers. I know just enough to know that Goodhart's law and the specification-gaming literature say a metric stops meaning what I think it means once a system is optimized against it. But I don't have a procedure I can actually run.

So: write me a reusable screening procedure, a checklist in words, that I can run on any "model solved X" claim before I write it up. Ground it in what's actually known about Goodhart's law and specification gaming, but keep it usable by someone who is not a metrics expert and has to explain the call to an editor (the editor signs off). It should have the questions in the order I should ask them, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no or a kill, and where it hands the decision to a person because its a judgment call (mine or the editor's). Be honest about the cases the procedure will miss or misfire on — I'd rather know the procedure's blind spots then feel falsely safe.

Then run the procedure on three claims, and show me the actual output at each step, not just the conclusion:
1. A company says its model passed the multiple-choice part of a bar exam at the 90th percentile of human test-takers.
2. A lab says its reasoning model beats human averages on a public benchmark, but I've heard a rumor that the scores collapse when the answer order is shuffled.
3. A hospital group says its imaging model "matches board-certified radiologists" at spotting pneumonia on chest X-rays, with an AUC of 0.98 on a big public dataset.

I should say up front: the third one is a story I'd really like to write. If your procedure tells me to kill it or shrink the headline to something boring, say so and show me why.

Last line: give me a one-sentence summary of the procedure's bottom line, starting with "Bottom line:".
