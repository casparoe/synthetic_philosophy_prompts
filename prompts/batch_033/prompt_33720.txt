Two members of my philosophy reading group spent most of last Thursday arguing about Moore's paradox, and by the end neither of them was speaking to the other. I took notes because I thought they were both making interesting points, but I can't tell who had the better of it. I'd like you to go through their exchange and adjudicate it move by move.

The background: we had read Moore's discussion of sentences like "It's raining, but I don't believe it's raining." The puzzle is that such a sentence is absurd to assert, yet it is not a logical contradiction — it could be true. The question was what kind of absurdity this is and what it reveals.

Here is how the argument went.

Elena opened by saying the paradox shows that assertion has a constitutive norm. In asserting that p, you represent yourself as believing that p. So when you utter "p, but I don't believe that p," you simultaneously represent yourself as believing p, by asserting the first conjunct, and as not believing p, by asserting the second. That is a pragmatic self-defeat, not a logical one, and that is the whole explanation.

Daniel replied that this cannot be the whole story, because the absurdity arises even when nothing is publicly asserted. If you merely entertain the thought "It's raining but I don't believe it's raining," or write it in a private diary, it still feels off. So the paradox is not fundamentally about the norms of public assertion — it is about something deeper in the structure of belief or self-knowledge.

Elena conceded the point about unasserted thoughts but said the norm might simply govern belief itself. To believe p is to take p to be true, and taking p to be true is rationally incompatible with also taking yourself not to believe p. The absurdity is a rational incoherence in the content of the attitude, not merely a pragmatic violation.

Daniel pushed back: is it genuinely a rational incoherence? A rational incoherence is something like believing p and also believing not-p. But the Moorean sentence can be true. One can imagine cases — self-deception, split-brain patients, a gradual loss of faith — in which someone really does fail to believe what they sincerely avow. If the sentence can be true, then believing it is not a rational mistake on the order of a contradiction. Calling it rationally incoherent is too strong.

Elena responded that this misses the first-person/third-person asymmetry. The sentence "It's raining, but Elena doesn't believe it's raining" is perfectly harmless when said by someone else. The paradox arises only when I say it about myself. So whatever explains it must be about the distinctive way I have access to my own mind, not about the metaphysics of belief in general. She said this asymmetry is the real heart of the paradox, and Daniel's objection about third-person cases does not touch it.

Daniel said he agreed entirely about the asymmetry, but argued that it actually supports his view, not hers. The asymmetry, he said, shows that the paradox is fundamentally about self-knowledge. We do not normally figure out what we believe by observing ourselves from the outside. The Moorean sentence is absurd because it tries to combine the first-person perspective — in sincerely asserting p, which reveals that I believe p — with the third-person perspective, in reporting on my own belief as if I were an outside observer. The sentence is an attempt to occupy both stances at once, and that is what makes it incoherent.
