Last night a friend and I argued for hours about whether a source can certify its own reliability, and she left me with an argument I cannot shake. I want to know which step fails and what it would cost to reject it.

Her argument ran as follows:

(1) A belief amounts to knowledge if it is produced by a reliable process, even if the believer cannot say what makes the process reliable.
(2) My gas gauge is in fact reliable, though I have never checked it against any independent source.
(3) When the gauge reads "full," my belief that the tank is full is produced by a reliable process — the gauge's reliability together with my reading it correctly — so by (1) I know that the tank is full.
(4) I also know, by ordinary perception, that the gauge reads "full."
(5) Deduction from known premises yields knowledge, so from (3) and (4) I can come to know that the gauge read correctly on this occasion.
(6) By repeating this many times, I can assemble a long track record of the gauge's reading correctly.
(7) A long track record of correct readings is good inductive evidence that the gauge is reliable.
(8) Therefore I can come to know, by induction over that track record, that the gauge is reliable — without ever checking it against anything independent.

She takes this as a reductio of (1): no source should be able to pull itself up by its own bootstraps. I am reluctant to give up (1), because it seems to be the only view on which perception and memory can justify beliefs without our already knowing that they work, and I also want to keep the deduction principle in (5). Which step or premise should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? What would a reliabilist have to give up or add to block the conclusion, and does that fix avoid requiring independent checks for every source we ever rely on?
