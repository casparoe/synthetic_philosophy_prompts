I am a novelist at work on a book set in a society rather like our own, and I have discovered that I cannot write convincingly about what my characters find corrupting, or merely transactional, or perfectly natural, until I have some clarity about my own moral geography on these questions. So I have been inventorying my reactions to a range of exchanges—some legal, some criminal, some merely distasteful—and I find I cannot state the principle I am applying, if indeed there is a single one. 

Here are my verdicts, as honestly as I can give them.

- A healthy adult selling a kidney to a stranger for $35,000: I find this repugnant. It is not merely that it is illegal; it strikes me as a kind of category mistake, as though the body is not the sort of thing one puts a price on. I feel this even when I imagine the sale being safe, informed, and genuinely voluntary.

- A person on an overbooked flight paying a fellow passenger $300 to surrender their seat: I find this entirely unobjectionable. It is a mutually beneficial exchange of a place in a queue for money, and no one is worse off.

- A vendor after a hurricane selling bottled water for $40 a case when the usual price is $6: I find this predatory. Even if the high price draws fresh supply to the stricken area—which I know it might—I cannot shake the sense that exploiting someone's desperation for profit is wrong in a way ordinary markups are not.

- A couple paying a surrogate to carry a pregnancy, covering medical costs plus a $30,000 fee: I am tentatively comfortable with this, though I want to add conditions—informed consent, fair compensation, protection against exploitation. The unease I feel is real, but it does not rise to an objection, and it feels categorically different from my reaction to the kidney sale.

- Someone offering $500 for another person's vote in a national election: I find this profoundly objectionable, far out of proportion to the sum involved. It feels like a corruption not just of the election but of citizenship itself—as though a vote is the kind of thing that is degraded by being bought and sold, regardless of consent.

- A theme park that sells a "fast pass" allowing purchasers to skip the regular queue: I dislike this. It feels as though the wealthy are buying their way out of a shared inconvenience, and it makes the experience slightly worse for everyone else. But I would not make it illegal, and I am not sure my objection is a moral one rather than an aesthetic one.

- A parent paying a university admissions consultant $20,000 to help craft essays and polish a résumé: I am uneasy but do not think this should be prohibited. The line between this and ordinary tutoring or test preparation is hard to draw, and I suspect my unease is about inequality in background opportunities, not about the transaction itself.

- A parent bribing a university athletics official $500,000 to have their child designated as a recruited athlete: I find this straightforwardly corrupt and properly criminal. It is fraud on the institution and a theft of an opportunity from a more deserving applicant. No ambivalence.

- A healthy adult selling blood plasma twice a week for $50 per session: I find this acceptable. It does not trigger the revulsion I feel about kidney sales, even though both involve removing something from the body for payment. The difference in my reaction is stark, and I do not know what to make of it.

- A firm that buys life insurance policies from elderly policyholders, paying a lump sum now in exchange for the death benefit later: I am unsettled by this in a way that feels almost superstitious. It is legal, and the policyholder consents, but there is something ghoulish about a stranger holding a financial interest in another person's death. I cannot decide whether this is a real moral problem or merely an ick reaction.

I keep returning to a case that does not appear on this list, and I suspect it will be central to the novel. A person under severe financial pressure sells to a corporation the right to name their firstborn child—something like "Pepsi Cohen" or "Tesla Williams"—in exchange for a substantial payment that would transform the family's circumstances. My gut says this is wrong, but I cannot tell whether the wrongness is different in kind from what happens in the surrogacy case, where money and the creation of a child are already entangled, or whether I am merely reacting to the case's unfamiliarity. 

Can you identify the principle, or the small set of competing principles, that best captures the pattern of my verdicts? For each candidate, I would like to know which of my verdicts it forces me to revise, what it says about the naming-rights case, and what further case—one I have not listed—would most sharply distinguish the candidates. If my verdicts genuinely underdetermine the principle, I would rather know that than be given a false resolution.
