Two colleagues in my department got into an argument at lunch about whether pure time preference is rationally defensible, and neither would concede. I took notes because it seemed like both were making real points and I couldn't tell who was winning.

(1) Mara said pure time preference — discounting future well-being solely because it lies further in the future — is rationally indefensible. If you wouldn't discount a person's suffering because it happens in another country, you shouldn't discount it because it happens in another century. Temporal distance is no more morally relevant than spatial distance.

(2) David replied that the spatial analogy is misleading. We don't occupy a "view from nowhere." We are temporally situated agents, and practical reasoning is inherently perspectival — we care more about what happens to us tomorrow than what happens to us in thirty years, and this isn't a mistake, it's a basic structural feature of agency. The spatial analogy fails because we aren't spatially located in the same constitutive sense.

(3) Mara answered that this confuses two distinct things. Of course we have agent-relative reasons that privilege our own future selves over strangers — but that's agent-relativity, not temporal discounting. The question is whether, having already accounted for the fact that the future beneficiary is me or my child or my community, I should then discount further just because the benefit arrives later. David hasn't given a reason for that second, additional discount.

(4) David said the reason is that a normative framework without pure time preference generates absurd demands. If we discount the future only for uncertainty and catastrophe risk, then a tiny increase in the well-being of an astronomically large number of potential future people would swamp any finite benefit to people alive today. We'd be obligated to invest nearly everything for the sake of the far future. Since that can't be the right verdict, pure time preference must be doing necessary normative work.

(5) Mara said that's a different argument entirely — it's a worry about fanaticism in population ethics, not a defense of pure time preference. You could block the fanatical conclusion by rejecting total utilitarianism, or by adopting a bounded utility function, or by discounting for the probability that future people won't exist. None of those moves requires saying that a future person's suffering matters less simply because of when she lives.

(6) David said that even if you can block the fanatical conclusion in theory, the practical upshot is the same: any ethical view that refuses to discount the future at all will be radically revisionary of how we actually live and allocate resources. And if a normative theory is that revisionary, that's itself a reason to doubt it.
