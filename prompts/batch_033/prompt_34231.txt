So in my philosophy of AI seminar we've been doing this unit on embedded agency — the idea that decision-makers are part of the world they're modeling, which creates all these weird self-reference problems for standard decision theory. Two people in my class got into this argument after seminar that I can't stop thinking about.

One of them (let's call him David) was arguing that embedded agency is a genuinely deep problem for expected utility theory. His point was basically: if you're part of the world, you can't treat your own actions as ordinary events to conditionalize on. Like, the whole "if I choose X, then the probability of Y is P" move breaks down, because you can't simultaneously be the one deciding and model your decision as an exogenous event. He said this isn't some niche thing — every real agent is embedded, so the whole framework is built on a fiction.

The other (call her Maya) pushed back hard. She said this is a problem for formalizing decision theory, not for actual decision-making. When you're deciding whether to bring an umbrella, you don't need to solve the self-reference problem. You just think about what happens if you bring it vs. if you don't, and you pick. The embedding problem is a model-building issue, not a rationality issue. She said the paradoxes people point to — Newcomb's problem, the smoking lesion — are basically manufactured and don't tell us anything about real choices.

David came back with: but the whole point of decision theory is to give a normative account of what you *should* do. If it breaks down under its own assumptions in edge cases that are actually universal (since all agents are embedded), that's not a minor modeling glitch — it means the theory is incomplete at the level of its foundations. He said Maya's "just do the thing" response is basically giving up on having a theory at all.

Maya said that's too strong. She admitted the embedding problem is real for certain kinds of formal models, but she said the practical upshot is basically zero. You can acknowledge the self-reference issue and still use expected utility reasoning for everything you'll ever face. The fact that philosophers can build toy cases where it gets weird doesn't mean the whole project is in trouble.

They went back and forth a couple more times and neither budged. I was sort of taking notes cause I have to write a response paper for this unit anyway, and I'm honestly not sure who's right about what. Can you go through their exchange and adjudicate it move by move? Like, who's right about which specific points, and is there anything important both of them are missing?
