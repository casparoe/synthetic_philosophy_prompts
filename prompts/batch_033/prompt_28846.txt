I've been turning this over after a bunch of late-night arguments with a friend who's way more Marxist than I am, and I can't tell if there's a name for the pattern we keep falling into or if I'm just tying myself in knots. Here's the pattern. Someone holds a moral view — say, that we have stronger obligations to our family than to strangers, or that certain sexual practices are wrong, or that we should give much more to global poverty relief. Someone else says: "you only think that because of how you were raised" or "that's just an evolutionary instinct dressed up as morality" or "you're rationalizing your class interests." And the first person says: "no, that's my considered moral judgment — you're the one biased by your subculture." And then they're stuck. Neither can point to anything independent to settle it, because any criterion you propose for distinguishing a bias from a genuine moral reason will itself be contestable on moral grounds. It's not just that people disagree; it's that the very distinction between "this is my bias" and "this is my moral insight" seems to collpase under pressure, because what counts as a bias depends on what the correct morality is, which is exactly what's in dispute. I've seen this in debates about meat-eating (is my revulsion at factory farming moral clarity or a culturally specific disgust response?), about monogamy, about filial piety — the structure is always the same. It feels like there's a deep epistemic problem here about the reflexivity of moral judgment: you need a moral framework to identify biases, but your moral framework is exactly what the bias accusation is targeting.

Is there an established name for this specific problem — not just "moral disagreement" or "debunking arguments" generally, but the difficulty of non-circularly distinguishing moral preferences from moral biases? How have philosophers carved this up? I want to know the main positions on whether the distinction can be drawn at all, what the boundary cases are (when does a cultural influence become a bias?), and whether anyone has proposed a satisfying way out that doesn't just beg the question.
