In my country's public healthcare system, a government agency decides which treatments to fund by calculating how many "quality-adjusted life years" (QALYs) each treatment would produce. A year of life in full health counts as 1 QALY; a year with a serious condition counts as a fraction of that. The agency then funds the treatments that produce the most QALYs per dollar. This seems to involve two different kinds of comparison. On the one hand, the system treats a QALY as interchangeable across people — a QALY gained by one patient counts the same as a QALY gained by another, which looks like an interpersonal comparison of well-being. On the other hand, the system assigns QALY weights by asking samples of the general public how they would rate hypothetical health states, rather than by looking at how actual patients experience those states — which looks like an attempt to avoid relying on the subjective experiences of particular individuals.

What theory of welfare, and of interpersonal comparability, does this practice presuppose? Does it coherently combine the claim that we can compare well-being across people with the claim that we should not ask those people directly? And what would have to change in the agency's methodology if we adopted a view that interpersonal utility comparisons are impossible, or a view that only the individual's own report of their well-being is authoritative?
