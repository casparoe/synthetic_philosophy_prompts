A friend of mine has thought a lot about the moral difference between lying and merely misleading, and over the years I've collected her verdicts on a series of cases. Here they are:

1. **The murderer at the door.** Asked whether your friend is hiding in your house, you say "I haven't seen him all day" (true — you saw him an hour ago but he entered through the back and you genuinely haven't seen him since). She says this is no worse than saying "He's not here" (a lie), and both are equally permissible given the stakes.

2. **The reference letter.** A professor writes "Her penmanship is excellent" for a weak philosophy student, knowing the reader will infer she's a poor philosopher. She says this is less blameworthy than writing "She's a talented philosopher" (a lie), because the reader shares responsibility for drawing the wrong inference from a literally true statement.

3. **The used car.** A seller says "I've never had any trouble with it" (true — he's owned it for two days and it's been in the shop the whole time). She says this is just as bad as saying "It runs perfectly" (a lie). In a commercial transaction, she thinks, the distinction collapses.

4. **The poker game.** A player says "I've got nothing" (a lie about his strong hand) and another player sighs heavily and shakes his head (misleading). She says neither is wrong at all — deception is part of the game and everyone consents to it by sitting down at the table.

5. **The spouse.** Asked "Did you remember our anniversary?" one answers "I've been thinking about it all week" (true but misleading — they remembered it's this month but forgot the exact day). She says this is just as bad as saying "Of course I remembered" (a lie). In marriage, she holds, you owe your partner more than literal truth.

6. **The doctor.** A physician tells a patient "This procedure is very safe" (true statistically, but the doctor knows this particular patient has a condition that makes the risk much higher). She says this is actually worse than if the doctor had simply lied about the risk percentage — patients are epistemically vulnerable and entitled to full disclosure.

I'm trying to figure out what principle she's running. At least two different rules seem to fit these six verdicts. One possibility is that she holds a "reasonable expectations" view: the moral difference between lying and misleading depends entirely on what the listener is entitled to expect in that context. When the listener should be on guard (commerce), the distinction evaporates; when the listener is entitled to take statements at face value (marriage, medicine), misleading can be as bad or worse; when deception is consented to (poker), neither is wrong. Another possibility is that she holds a "warranty of truth" view: lying is worse than misleading only when the speaker explicitly vouches for the truth of the asserted content, but this baseline difference can be outweighed by other factors like the heightened duty of candor in fiduciary relationships.

I'm about to ask her about a seventh case: **The courtroom witness.** A witness is asked on cross-examination, "Did you see the defendant at the scene?" The witness answers "I was at home that night" (true — but the witness's apartment window directly overlooks the scene, and they did in fact see the defendant there). The alternative would be answering "I didn't see the defendant at the scene" (a lie).

Before I ask her, I'd like to work out what each of the two candidate principles would predict about this case, which prediction seems more consistent with the overall pattern of her first six verdicts, and whether there's a third principle I haven't considered that fits all six verdicts even better. And once I have her answer on this seventh case, what eighth case should I put to her — one where the two leading principles would yield opposite verdicts — to settle conclusively which rule she's actually running?
