I retired a few years ago after a career in atmospheric science, mostly building and running numerical weather models, and I've recently started reading philosophy of science. Its made me see my old field differently, and there's one practice I used to take for granted that now seems philosophically loaded in ways I can't sort out.

The practice is ensemble forecasting. For decades now, operational centers haven't just run one forecast; they run dozens or hundreds of versions of the model, each started from a tiny perturbation of the initial conditions, and then present the spread of the resulting trajectories as a probability distribution — a 70 percent chance of rain, a plume of possible hurricane tracks. The working assumption is that the atmosphere is a chaotic deterministic system: there's one true trajectory, but we can't know the initial state exactly, so the spread across perturbed starts is meant to represent our uncertainty, not any real branching in nature. Verification then treats the probabilities as calibrated if it rains on 70 percent of the occasions when the forecast said 70 percent.

What I can't get straight is what this practice actually commits us to. Does it presuppose determinism — that there is a single future trajectory we're imperfectly sampling? Or is it compatible with the atmosphere being genuinely stochastic at some level, or even with the idea that the future weather isn't a determinate fact until it happens? I can see arguments both ways, and I'd like a reconstruction of the implicit theory: what the practice strictly presupposes, what it merely fits well with, and where it's philosophically underdetermined. I'm also wondering whether it smuggles in a particular view of what probability means — objective chance versus degree of belief — and whether the way we verify forecasts commits us to one of those too. And if we dropped the determinism assumption, what would actually have to change in how we run and verify ensembles, or is the whole edifice fine either way?
