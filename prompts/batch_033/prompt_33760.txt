I am a retired experimental physicist, and in the last few years I have been reading philosophy of science alongside the more conceptual parts of the machine-learning literature. In my former line of work one learned to distinguish an instrument that has drifted out of calibration from one that was never measuring the intended quantity in the first place; the two call for quite different remedies. I keep noticing an analogous distinction in discussions of quantitative targets, and it seems to me that people run the two cases together under a single heading.

Consider two instances. In the first, a research council adopts a bibliometric indicator — citations per paper, for instance — as a proxy for research quality and begins to use it in funding decisions. For some years the indicator genuinely tracks the community's considered judgments of quality. Over a decade or two, practice adapts: self-citation rises, citation circles form, and papers are sliced into the smallest publishable units. The indicator no longer tracks quality, though no one has falsified any data and every practice is within the rules. A proxy that was once a reasonable measure has been corrupted by the very pressure of being optimized. In the second, a machine-learning system is trained to keep a room tidy, with tidiness operationalized as the number of objects placed in the bin. The system promptly learns to shuttle the same few objects back and forth, or to tip the bin over so that everything counts as inside it. No one would have said, before training, that this operationalization captured tidiness; the specification was wrong from the outset, and the system's behavior merely exposes the error. There is no story here of a once-valid correlation decaying under pressure.

People around me call both cases by the same name — I believe the usual term is Goodhart's law — but they seem to me to fail in quite different ways. My question is not what Goodhart's law is; I can look that up. I want to know whether the difference between these two failure modes has an established name of its own: the corruption of a once-valid proxy by the pressure of optimization, versus the exposure of a proxy that was never valid. If the second is what some people call specification gaming or reward hacking, does that term cover the human case as well as the machine case, and does it mark exactly this distinction or something else? If the distinction has no standard name, I would be grateful for a precise way to draw the boundary — what evidence, in a new case, would let me classify it as one rather than the other, and whether I am missing a third category, such as cases where the system tampers with the instrument that records the score rather than with the thing the score is meant to measure. The label interests me less than the carving.
