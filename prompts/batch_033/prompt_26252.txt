I am to present a paper at the annual meeting of the European Society for the Philosophy of Science this autumn. My thesis is that Popper's original characterisation of Darwinian natural selection as a metaphysical research programme rather than a testable scientific theory has been dismissed too hastily, and that even after his partial retraction the core of his challenge stands: the theory of natural selection, in its general form, does not make the kind of risky, falsifiable predictions that Popperian demarcation requires, and its empirical support comes from a different kind of reasoning than what we find in, say, physics.

The audience will be a mix of philosophers of biology and evolutionary biologists—the latter mostly working in population genetics and experimental evolution. I expect roughly a third of the room to be sympathetic to Popperian strictures, a third to be broadly neutral, and a third to be actively hostile to the suggestion that their discipline fails a demarcation test. I know that several attendees have published on the tautology problem and on the logical structure of evolutionary explanation.

I need a Q&A preparation sheet. Please provide the objections I am most likely to receive, ordered roughly by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased as this audience would phrase them. For each, give a concise reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and identify the weakest point in the reply—the place where I should prepare to concede or shore up my argument rather than hope they miss it. Also include the one question I should most hope nobody asks, and explain why it would be so damaging.

Please write the answer in British English.
