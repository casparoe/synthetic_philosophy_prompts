A colleague has been arguing that the standard statistical-mechanical account of the second law of thermodynamics, conjoined with the time-reversal invariance of the underlying dynamics, refutes itself. He lays the argument out as follows, and I cannot find the exit.

(1) The fundamental dynamical laws — classical mechanics, and quantum mechanics in the respects relevant here — are time-reversal invariant: if a sequence of microstates is nomologically possible, then the temporally reversed sequence is also nomologically possible.

(2) Therefore, reasoning that uses only the dynamical laws can attach no asymmetry between the past and future directions; any such asymmetry must be supplied by something additional, such as a boundary condition.

(3) The statistical-mechanical account of the second law rests on a counting claim: conditional on a system's being in a low-entropy macrostate at a time t, the overwhelming majority of microstates compatible with that macrostate are ones that evolve under the dynamics to higher-entropy macrostates at later times. Low-entropy macrostates are atypical, and that atypicality is supposed to explain why entropy rises.

(4) But by (1), the time-reverse of every trajectory counted in (3) is also dynamically possible, and the counting measure is invariant under time reversal. So exactly the same argument applies with the temporal order reversed: conditional on the same low-entropy macrostate at t, the overwhelming majority of compatible microstates are also ones that would have evolved from higher-entropy macrostates at earlier times.

(5) Thus the account, taken at face value, predicts that a presently low-entropy system is overwhelmingly likely to have come from a higher-entropy past: a glass containing an ice cube should be a momentary fluctuation between a high-entropy past and a high-entropy future, and the reliable low-entropy records we use to reconstruct the past should be systematically misleading.

(6) That prediction is false. The past is not high-entropy; the whole point of the second law is that entropy increases toward the future but not toward the past, and our records of a low-entropy past are among the most robust data we have.

(7) So either the time-reversal invariance of the fundamental laws must be rejected, or the statistical-mechanical counting argument cannot explain the thermodynamic arrow, or the second law as an asymmetric generalization is not something physics can explain at all. The colleague concludes that the statistical-mechanical account is self-undermining.

I would most like to keep (1) and the idea behind (3), since the counting explanation of entropy increase seems indispensable. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, if the right response is to qualify (3) or to add an extra premise, state the qualification precisely and say whether it rescues the explanation or merely relocates the mystery.
