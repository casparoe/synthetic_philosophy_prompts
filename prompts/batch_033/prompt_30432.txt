In my graduate seminar on the philosophy of physics, students repeatedly deflect the Born rule problem in the many-worlds interpretation — the difficulty of making sense of the Born rule's probabilities once every term in the superposition is realized — with some version of the one-liner that if everything that can happen does happen, then there is nothing for probability to be about. I want to slow that move down and give my students a menu of readings to argue over.

Take that slogan, in its common forms everything that can happen does happen, every possible outcome occurs, and all branches are actual, and separate the precise claims it could be making. For each candidate reading, specify what can happen would have to range over (every term with nonzero amplitude, every history decoherence selects, everything kinematically consistent with the initial state, every macroscopic outcome an observer could report, and so on), what does happen would have to mean (exists as a branch, is experienced by some successor of a given observer, is actual simpliciter, is equiprobable with the others), and whether that reading is a platitude, a substantive but defensible commitment of the interpretation, or false. Then turn to the use the slogan is being put to in the seminar: which of these readings, if any, actually dissolve or transform the Born rule problem; which leave it fully intact; and which merely rename it as a problem about branch weights, self-locating uncertainty, or a measure over branches. I want a disambiguation with verdicts, not a single interpretation defended, and I want the closing section to identify which reading a student is tacitly relying on when they use the slogan to dismiss probability talk, and what precise objection I should hand back to them.
