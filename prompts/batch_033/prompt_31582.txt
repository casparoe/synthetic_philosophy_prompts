I spent my working life in the natural sciences, and one of the things you learn there is that an instrument has to be calibrated against something outside itself before you trust its readings. Since I retired I've been reading experimental philosophy, and I've ended up with a view I'd like stress-tested. The view is this: the intuitions that analytic philosophers lean on as evidence are, to a worrying extent, artefacts of a particular cultural and linguistic setting -- roughly WEIRD undergraduates and the academics who came from them. When a roomful of people in one place share an intuition, we are mostly learning about a local cognitive style, not about reference or knowledge or causation themselves. I know there are cross-cultural studies of the Gödel case and of things like the Knobe effect, and to my eye they suggest the variation is real and large enough to matter for how philosophy should be done.

But I've been wrong about readings before, so I want to know what would count against this view. Not the stock reply that intuitions are still evidence even when they vary; I want the specific findings, methodological arguments, replications, and philosophical considerations that should genuinely move me, ranked from decisive to merely awkward. Which of these would a committed defender of my view themselves conceed as counting against it, and which would they dismiss as beside the point? And have I insulated the view so thoroughly -- for instance, by saying any cross-cultural invariance is superficial, or that any invariance is itself an artefact of translation or experimental design -- that it can no longer be falsified? If so, is that a problem for a view of this kind, or is the view more like a research programme than an empirical claim?
