I'm a novelist, and I've been wresting with something that I think is actually a philosophy problem, but I need it for my writing. Here's the deal.

I've got a character — call her Mira — who's unusually honest about her own messed-up psychology. She says things out loud that most people only think, or won't even admit to themselves. And I keep running into sentences that feel wrong in her mouth, and I can't figure out the rule for when it works and when it doesn't.

Here are my gut reactions to different things she might say:

1. "It's raining, but I don't believe it is." — This just sounds like word salad. No real person says this. It's not that it's false, it's that the act of saying it undermines itself somehow.

2. "It was raining, but I didn't believe it was." — This one's fine. She's reporting a past failure of belief. I can imagine her saying this about a time she was in denial.

3. "He's cheating on me, but I don't believe he is." — Also fine, actually. This feels like a perfectly good description of a hunch you can't bring yourself to credit.

4. "I'm an alcoholic, but I don't believe I am." — This one's weird. It's not as broken as the rain one, but it's not comfortable either. It feels like something someone might actually say in a moment of painful clarity, but it also feels like they're contradicting themselves in a way that the cheating one doesn't.

5. "I believe it's raining, but it's not." — This one's fine. No problem at all. She's just saying her belief is wrong.

6. "God exists, but I don't believe He does." — This feels broken in the same way as the rain one. But here's the thing: I can imagine a character saying this in a novel, and it would work. It would read as someone acknowledging a truth their whole psyche is rebelling against. So maybe it's not broken in fiction the way it is in life?

7. "You're going to leave me, but I don't believe you will." — This one works. It's like the cheating one. It's a fear you have that you can't fully accept.

8. Here's the one that's really bugging me. I want Mira to say: "I know I'm going to die, but I don't believe it." That feels like it captures something real — the difference between knowing something abstractly and really believing it in your bones. But is this the same kind of sentence as the others, or is it a different thing entirely? And if it works, why does it work when "it's raining but I don't believe it" doesn't?

So here's what I'm after. Is there a principle that sorts these cases — that tells me which of these a character can say without sounding like she's lost her grip on language, and which ones are genuinely incoherent? And if you give me a principle, I want to know: does it say case 8 works or doesn't? And if the principle forces me to give up one of my verdicts, which one should I let go of?

Also, if it matters, I'm not asking about what's logically possible. I know all of these could be true at the same time. I'm asking about what a person can *say* — what comes out of a character's mouth and lands on the reader as psychologically real rather than as authorial incompetence.

Last line: give me a one-sentence summary of the principle, formatted as \answer{your summary here}.
