I'm grading a midterm for an intro Modern Philosophy course, and I keep going back and forth on this one answer. The question was:

"Reconstruct Berkeley's master argument for idealism (the one from the Principles of Human Knowledge). Then explain what you take to be the strongest objection to it, and say whether you think the objection succeeds."

The student wrote:

"Berkeley's master argument goes like this. Try to conceive of a sensible object—say, a tree—existing entirely unconceived and unperceived by any mind. But as soon as you try, you are conceiving it. So the tree you are conceiving is being conceived by you. Therefore, you cannot conceive of an unconceived tree. But if it's impossible even to conceive of an object existing unconceived, then it's impossible for an object to exist unconceived. So the very idea of a mind-independent object is incoherent. For sensible objects, to be is to be perceived (esse est percipi).

The strongest objection is that Berkeley confuses the act of conceiving with the object of conception. When I conceive of a tree existing unconceived, the act of conception is something I do—it requires a conceiver. But the content of my conception is the tree existing unconceived, and that content doesn't include me. The fact that I have to perform an act of conceiving to think about the tree doesn't mean the tree itself has the property of being-conceived. It's like saying: when I think about a fire, my brain is doing the thinking, so the fire must have brain activity. That's obviously a fallacy, and Berkeley's argument commits the same one. So the objection is decisive—the master argument fails completely."
