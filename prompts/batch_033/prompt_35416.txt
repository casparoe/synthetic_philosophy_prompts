Alright, here's the assignment. I want you to argue for the following claim: Lewis's theory of convention, whatever its virtues, is fundamentally incapable of explaining how norms change. And this isn't just a gap in the theory — it's a sign that the theory is built on the wrong picture.

Lewis models conventions as equilibria in coordination games — everyone's playing their part because everyone expects everyone else to play their part, and there's common knowledge backing it all up. But norm change — like the shift in conventions around citation practices, or the emergence of new norms about email sign-offs, or the way norms around what counts as "too forward" in dating have shifted — doesn't look like a population jumping from one equilibrium to another. It looks like a messy process where for a long time nobody's quite sure what the convention is, some people are following the old one, some the new one, and many are strategically ambigious. During that transition, there's no common knowledge, no stable regularity, and people aren't in a coordination problem with a shared set of expectations. So Lewis's account can't even describe the transition, let alone explain it.

But a theory of convention that can't handle how conventions actually behave — how they come into existence, how they shift, how they die — isn't really a theory of convention at all. It's a theory of something else: frozen equilibria. And if that's what it is, then it's not doing the work philosophers have wanted it to do when they reach for Lewis to explain language, or law, or social norms.

Make the strongest case for this position you can, and be sure to anticipate and respond to the obvious objections. The best papers will engage seriously with what Lewis actually says about precedent and the role of past conformity in his account, and with the question of whether the problem is with Lewis's theory specifically or with any equilibrium-based account of social norms.
