I serve on a grant review panel in my field. Each proposal is assigned to four reviewers, who evaluate it independently before convening to discuss. I recently reviewed a proposal and, after careful study, concluded that it contained a methodological error serious enough to warrant rejection. I wrote a detailed critique laying out the mistake and its implications for the proposed work.

When the panel met, I learned that the other three reviewers had all recommended funding, and none of them had identified the error I saw. We discussed my objection at some length. They understood the point I was making but were not persuaded that it was in fact an error, or that it was fatal if it was one. I understood their responses but remained convinced that the error was real and disqualifying. The discussion was collegial and substantive — no one was grandstanding or digging in.

I have no reason to think my fellow panelists are less competent than I am; if anything, one of them has considerably more experience. I was not tired, rushed, or distracted when I reviewed the proposal. Re-reading my notes and the proposal, my original reasoning still strikes me as sound. Yet I also know, as a general statistical fact about my track record, that when three qualified peers independently disagree with my judgment on a technical matter of this kind, I am usually the one who is mistaken.

What should I believe about the proposal's merits, and what should I do with my vote? Is there a principled way to resolve the tension between the fact that my own reasoning still seems correct to me and the fact that peer disagreement is normally reliable evidence that I have gone wrong somewhere?
