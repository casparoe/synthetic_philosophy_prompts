I help run a small prediction-market platform where the traders are a mix of employees of our parent company, a few outside quantitative types, and some hobbyists, and where we make markets in quarterly revenue, product launch dates, and a handful of public events. Several times a week I have to decide, on an incoming order, whether to move our quotes against it, hold them, or widen the spread, and I usually have about ten minutes before the market itself moves. I can move quotes modestly on my own; anything bigger has to be justified in writing to our risk committee. The recurring question is whether the person on the other side is telling me something I should believe, or is just different from me in a way that carries no information about the proposition. I know the rough shape of the no-trade theorem — that if two rational traders really shared a prior, one of them would update on the other's very willingness to trade until the disagreement collapsed, so purely informational differences should not by themselves produce a trade, and that real trade has to be explained by hedging, liquidity, risk appetite, different priors, or someone being irrational — but I don't know how to turn that into something I can actually run on a live order without a model.

Write me a decision procedure for a single incoming order, grounded in what the no-trade result and the epistemology of markets actually imply. It should be a checklist I can execute in my ten-minute window: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each before I move on, where the procedure should stop and say this is just a hedge or a liquidity need and I should not update, where it should say update and roughly how much when I cannot get a number, and where it has to hand the decision to a human because the question is about whose prior or model is better rather than about evidence. Then run the procedure on three orders: a corporate employee selling a revenue market to hedge an equity award; a hobbyist taking the other side of a public-events market with no apparent hedging or liquidity motive; and a sophisticated outside trader repeatedly buying a market we think is fairly priced after a long stretch in which our pricing has been right. I want the procedure to be honest about the case where it forces me to do something I will not like — treating a hobbyist's order as information, or refusing to let me dismiss an annoying counterparty as simply irrational — and about what it cannot decide for me. Write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way you would write a memo or an email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
