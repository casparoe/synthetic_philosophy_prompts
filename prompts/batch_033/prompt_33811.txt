I am defending a paper at a philosophy of religion colloquium next month. The paper defends J.L. Schellenberg's argument from divine hiddenness: roughly, that if a perfectly loving God existed, God would ensure that every person capable of a personal relationship with God and not resisting it would be in a position to enter that relationship; but there exist people who are not resisting and yet find no evidence of God's presence; therefore no such God exists. My version adds a move I think is underappreciated: I argue that the standard theistic replies either concede too much about God's love (by making it conditional on something arbitrary) or shift the problem to a different divine attribute without noticing they have done so.

The audience is a mixed philosophy department — fifteen or so faculty, plus graduate students. The department has four philosophers of religion: a Reformed epistemologist who thinks belief in God is properly basic and doesn't need evidential support, a Thomist who will resist any premise that treats God as one agent among others, a Molinist who works on middle knowledge and theodicy, and a skeptical theist who thinks we are in no position to assess God's reasons. There are also three epistemologists who work on higher-order evidence and disagreement, two of whom are agnostics and one an atheist. The rest of the department has no stake in theism but will jump on any argument that looks too tidy. The chair is a Hume scholar who will be watching for any slide from "no evidence of God" to "evidence of no God."

Prepare my Q&A prep sheet. Give me the objections I am likely to face from this audience, ordered by how damaging they are (not how likely), phrased the way these specific people would phrase them, with a two-minute reply for each. For each objection, note where my reply is weakest and whether I should concede anything. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks, and say why I should fear it.
