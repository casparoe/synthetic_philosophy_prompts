Two people in my moral philosophy seminar got into a debate about Bernard Williams's "one thought too many" objection to utilitarianism, and I've been unable to settle it in my own head since. I'll reconstruct the exchange as faithfully as I can.

She opened by saying that Williams's objection is more radical than most people in the class were treating it. Take the case Williams gives: a man sees two people drowning, one of whom is his wife. For Williams, she said, the husband who saves his wife should not be thinking "this is my wife, and in rescue situations it is permissible to favor one's spouse, so I will save her." That would be one thought too many. The husband should simply save her because she is his wife — the motivating thought should be immediate and particular, not mediated by a moral principle that licenses partiality. She argued that this reveals a structural problem for utilitarianism: it requires the agent to stand outside his own deepest commitments and view them as factors to be weighed impartially, and that stance is incompatible with genuinely having those commitments in the first place.

He pushed back. He said Williams is confusing the psychology of deliberation with the justificatory structure of the action. A husband can save his wife without any conscious deliberation at all — just reach out and grab her — and yet the action can still be justified on utilitarian grounds. The fact that utilitarianism would endorse the action does not require the husband to run through a calculation, either at the moment or afterward. So where is the alienation?

She replied: the alienation is not about what flashes through your mind in the moment. It is about what you would say if asked to justify yourself. The husband who says "I saved her because she is my wife" is giving a reason of the right kind — it arises from within the relationship. The husband who says "I saved her because, after weighing the relevant considerations, saving her was the right thing to do" is giving a reason of the wrong kind, even if the action is identical. The justificatory structure matters, not just the behavioral output.

He came back: but that just pushes the question back a step. Why is "because she is my wife" a better reason than "because saving her maximizes utility"? If the relationship is genuinely valuable, presumably it is valuable for reasons — and those reasons might well be utilitarian ones. Perhaps the reason spousal relationships matter is that they are among the things that make human lives go well. If so, then "because she is my wife" is just shorthand for a deeper justificatory story, and Williams is mistaking a surface-level reason for a fundamental one.

She responded: that move is exactly what Williams is warning against. You are taking a reason that is supposed to be immediate and non-derivative and treating it as if it is merely a placeholder for an impartial calculation. But that transforms the nature of the commitment. If I am committed to my wife only because I have concluded, at some level, that spousal commitments tend to maximize utility, then I am not really committed to her — I am committed to utility-maximization, and she happens to be the current instantiation of it. That is the alienation.

He said: I think that is too strong. I can believe both that my relationship is justified by deeper moral principles and that I am genuinely committed to this particular person. Those are not in tension. In fact, I would be worried about a relationship that could not survive the question "why do you love me?" — and any good answer to that question will refer to features of the person that make loving them worthwhile. That is not alienation; that is just having reasons for one's attachments. If Williams thinks that having reasons for an attachment undermines it, then Williams is committed to the view that the best relationships are arational — and that seems false.

She replied: you are conflating two different kinds of reasons. The question "why do you love me?" asks for the particular features of the person that elicit your love — her sense of humor, her kindness, the way she listens. Those are reasons internal to the relationship. The utilitarian's reason is external to the relationship: it is a reason to have the relationship at all, a reason that would apply equally to any relationship with the same utility profile. The difference is between loving someone for who she is and loving her because loving people like her is optimific. Williams's point is that utilitarianism can only give you the second kind of reason, and that is not the kind of reason that actual human commitments run on.

The seminar ended there, and I have been going back and forth ever since. Could you adjudicate this exchange move by move? I am especially interested in whether her final distinction between internal and external reasons for a relationship successfully answers his point about love and reasons, and whether there is a way for a utilitarian to accept Williams's phenomenology of commitment without granting his normative conclusion.
