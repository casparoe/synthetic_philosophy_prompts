You are the editor of a volume on anthropic reasoning, and you have persuaded two philosophers — one a committed advocate of the Self-Sampling Assumption (SSA), the other a committed advocate of the Self-Indication Assumption (SIA) — to engage in a structured adversarial collaboration. Their task is to produce a joint document that clarifies where they genuinely disagree, separates those disagreements from merely verbal ones, and identifies the cruxes on which their dispute turns. The document should also record, for each crux, what concrete consideration or thought experiment both sides agree bears on it, together with each side's prediction about how that consideration should come out if their own view is correct.

Please draft this adversarial-collaboration document. It should include the following sections, in this order:

1. **Agreed Claims** (roughly 400–600 words): a numbered list of claims that both the SSA advocate and the SIA advocate can sign without reservation. These should cover the formal structure they share (e.g., Bayesian updating under indexical uncertainty), the thought experiments whose setups both accept, and any methodological commitments they hold in common. Avoid vacuous truisms; the claims should be substantive enough that someone outside the debate would learn something by reading them.

2. **Disagreements Table** (roughly 300–500 words of table content, plus a brief introductory sentence): a table with three columns — (a) the issue, (b) the SSA advocate's position, and (c) the SIA advocate's position. Each position must be phrased so that its own proponent would endorse the wording as fair and accurate. The table should cover at least: how each treats the Doomsday argument, how each handles the Presumptuous Philosopher thought experiment, and whether "I exist" itself constitutes evidence about the number of observers. If any row turns out to be a merely verbal dispute once the positions are stated precisely, note that in the table.

3. **Cruxes and Tests** (roughly 400–600 words): identify the sub-questions such that, if they were settled, at least one side would revise its view. For each crux, describe a concrete case, argument, or piece of evidence that both sides agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side predicts about that case. Where possible, specify what kind of result would cause each side to reduce its confidence. The Presumptuous Philosopher and the Doomsday argument should each appear as at least one crux, but you may identify additional cruxes if the logic of the disagreement demands them.
