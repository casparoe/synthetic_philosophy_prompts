You are a philosopher of science who works on dispositions, finks, and masks. I am a senior reviewer at a chemical safety agency. My team handles dozens of contested assay results a quarter, and we must decide whether a substance is itself hazardous — corrosive, sensitizing, acutely toxic — on the basis of standardized tests, where a wrong call either leaves workers exposed or triggers costly restrictions. We keep hitting a known confound. Sometimes an assay is negative even though the substance is hazardous, because the assay conditions remove the disposition before it can manifest (a fink) or because something in the test system blocks the manifestation while leaving the disposition in place (a mask, like an antidote). Sometimes the reverse happens: the assay creates a hazard that no realistic exposure would produce.

Write a reusable decision procedure that a reviewer without philosophical training can run on any contested assay result, and that will be defensible to a risk-management committee. State the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure concludes that a test result is informative about the substance itself, where it concludes that the result is an artifact of the assay and further testing is required, and where it hands the decision to a human because the question is one of policy rather than fact. Then apply the procedure to three cases: (1) a corrosive that tests negative because the test medium neutralizes it on contact; (2) an acutely toxic compound that tests negative in a standard assay that includes an antidote; (3) a high-volume industrial chemical that tests negative only because the assay masks its sensitizing effect — a case where the procedure should force a classification the agency's industry stakeholders will not like. Begin by brainstorming the distinctions that must survive in the procedure, and be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure and about what the procedure cannot settle.
