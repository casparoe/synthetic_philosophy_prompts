I've been working through the KK principle — the thesis that if a subject knows that P, then they know that they know that P — by testing it against a range of cases. My verdicts on the cases are pulling me in different directions, and I'd like to know whether there's a single principle behind them, or which ones I should give up.

Here are the cases and my verdicts:

1. **The nervous examinee.** A student has studied thoroughly and knows the material. On the exam, she correctly writes that the Tennis Court Oath was in 1789. But she suffers from severe test anxiety and throughout the exam she feels as though she's merely guessing. If asked, she'd say she isn't sure whether she knows the answer or is confusing it with something else. My verdict: she knows the date, but she does not know that she knows it.

2. **The chicken sexer.** A poultry worker with decades of experience sorts male from female chicks by sight with near-perfect accuracy. The skill is entirely perceptual and subpersonal — they cannot articulate what cues they're using, and they report that it feels like guessing despite their track record. My verdict: when they correctly identify a chick as male, they know it's male, but they do not know that they know it.

3. **The forgotten language.** A heritage speaker of Polish was fluent as a child but hasn't spoken the language in thirty years. They sincerely believe they remember no Polish at all. Yet when tested, they reliably distinguish grammatical from ungrammatical sentences and translate simple phrases above chance, all while insisting they're guessing. My verdict: they know the grammatical rules (in some sense), but they do not know that they know them — indeed they believe they don't.

4. **The careful mathematician.** A mathematician has proved a theorem, checked the proof repeatedly, presented it at seminars, and had it pass peer review. The proof is correct. My verdict: she knows the theorem, and she knows that she knows it.

5. **The colour spectrum.** You're looking at a sequence of 1,000 colour patches shading imperceptibly from red to orange. Patch 1 is clearly red; patch 1,000 is clearly not red; adjacent patches are indiscriminable. At patch 200, the patch is red and you confidently judge it to be red. But you also know that there's a first non-red patch somewhere in the sequence and that, for any patch you look at, you couldn't tell it from its neighbours. Reflecting on this, you lose confidence: for all you can tell, you might already be past the cutoff. My verdict: at patch 200, you know it's red, but you do not know that you know it — the margin-of-error consideration defeats your higher-order knowledge even though your first-order judgement is correct and reliable.

6. **The chess master.** A grandmaster glances at a middlegame position and immediately sees that White has a forced win. She cannot articulate the full calculation — the recognition is pattern-based — but her judgement in such positions is highly reliable, and she is confident in it. My verdict: she knows White is winning, and she knows that she knows it.

The tentative principle I've been toying with is this: a subject knows that they know P just in case (i) they know P, and (ii) they can reliably discriminate situations in which they know P from situations in which they merely believe P truly. This would explain why the nervous examinee, the chicken sexer, and the heritage speaker fail the higher-order condition — they lack a reliable capacity to tell that their own cognitive state is knowledge rather than guesswork — and why the mathematician and the chess master satisfy it. But I'm not sure it handles the colour spectrum case cleanly: there, the subject seems to have a perfectly reliable capacity to discriminate red from non-red at the first-order level, and the failure only emerges when they reflect on the sorites structure. And I'm also unsure about the forgotten-language case: is the problem really that they can't discriminate knowledge from guesswork, or is it that they've lost access to the grounds of their knowledge?

What I'd like to know is: is there a single principle that captures all these verdicts, or at least most of them? Which of my verdicts would a clean principle force me to revise, and what would the principle say about cases I haven't considered — for instance, someone with blindsight who correctly 'guesses' the location of a light in their blind field, or someone who knows P purely by trusting an expert's testimony but has no independent grasp of the evidence?
