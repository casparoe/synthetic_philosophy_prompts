A political theorist I follow has written, over the years, about whether various political outcomes genuinely count as expressions of "the will of the people." I have collected her verdicts on the following cases. She has said that a referendum passing with sixty-three percent on seventy-one percent turnout does express the will of the people. She has said that a referendum passing with fifty-one and a half percent on forty-two percent turnout does not. She has said that a statute enacted by a legislature whose members were collectively elected by a majority of the popular vote does express the will of the people, even when the statute itself was opposed by a narrow majority in contemporaneous polling. She has said that a constitutional amendment ratified by supermajorities in three-quarters of the states does express the will of the people. She has said that a policy that has enjoyed steady supermajority support in independent polls for over a decade but that the legislature has never enacted does not express the will of the people, because the people's will must be procedurally realized. She has said that a mass protest movement whose demands are never adopted through formal processes does not express the will of the people, though she grants it may express something else important. And she has said that a judicial ruling striking down a popular but unconstitutional statute does not express the will of the people, while insisting that this does not make the ruling illegitimate.

Now a new case has arisen that she has not yet addressed. A citizens' initiative qualifies for the ballot with the support of roughly fifty-seven percent of the electorate in independent polling. Before it can go to a vote, the legislature preempts it by passing a substantially similar but watered-down version that the initiative's backers denounce as a betrayal. The governor signs it, and the original initiative never appears on the ballot.

I want to know what she would likely say about whether this watered-down statute expresses the will of the people. But I am less interested in the answer itself than in the rule or rules that are implicit in her earlier verdicts, which of those rules would yield which answer about the new case, which rule she is most likely running given everything she has said, and what further case would most cleanly discriminate among the candidate rules. Please write your response in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables—the way you would write an email or a letter.
