I have been reading through Whitehead's essays on education, and I am trying to fit together two strands of his thought that seem to pull in opposite directions. On the one hand, he makes the inert idea — knowledge that is merely received and stored without ever being used, tested, or combined into something new — the central vice of schooling, and he treats an education built on such ideas as actively harmful rather than merely useless. On the other hand, his account of the rhythm of education moves from romance through precision to generalization, and the stage of precision sounds very much like the disciplined acquisition of exact detail: technique, classification, and the patient mastery of what has already been worked out.

I find myself wanting to read that precision stage as a reluctant concession. On this reading, Whitehead's real enthusiasm is for exploration and for putting ideas to work, and he admits that a certain amount of drill and exactness has to be tolerated along the way; the overall position would then be an early version of the case for discovery learning, with a bow to necessity rather than a principled defense of discipline. But that reading makes the precision stage sit awkwardly with the rest of the view, and I suspect I am flattening him. Is the attack on inert ideas best understood as an argument that instruction and memorization are the problem, and that the classroom should be rebuilt around active use — or is there a way to give the precision stage real weight without abandoning the polemic against inert knowledge?
