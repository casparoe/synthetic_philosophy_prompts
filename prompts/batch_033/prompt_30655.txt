For a graduate seminar I teach on population ethics and the long-term future, I would like a decision tree that takes a student from an initial stance on the simulation argument to a named position on whether that argument dampens fanaticism about the far future. Please write the instrument itself — the branch nodes, the diagnostic questions, and the leaves — not a prose essay comparing positions.

Use the following stipulations. By "fanaticism about the far future" I mean the view that, because the expected number of future beings is astronomical, the overwhelming share of what matters about our actions is their long-run consequences, so that near-term effects are negligible by comparison. By "the simulation argument" I mean the trilemma that at least one of the following holds: (i) civilizations at roughly our stage almost never survive to a posthuman stage capable of running large numbers of ancestor simulations; (ii) such posthuman civilizations almost never choose to run large numbers of ancestor simulations; or (iii) we are almost certainly living in a simulation. The tree should be able to place students who reject the trilemma as well as those who accept it.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of labels: first the diagnostic questions and the combinations of answers they allow, then the actual positions located in that space. Each branch node should be a concrete case or a claim that a student can answer without already knowing which position it leads to. At least two nodes should be concrete cases — for example, a foundation choosing between a global health program with large near-term benefits and an asteroid-deflection program that lowers extinction risk, or a scenario in which the simulators can be expected to terminate the simulation at the onset of space colonization. At a minimum, the tree must route students through these distinctions: (a) whether the probability that we are in a simulation should be treated as a distinct truncation scenario, over and above ordinary extinction risk; (b) whether simulated minds and their future lives have the same moral significance as base-level minds and lives; (c) whether our actions inside a simulation can influence the far future of our own world, the far future of base reality, both, or neither; (d) whether the relevant far-future population is larger, smaller, or the same under the simulation hypothesis; and (e) whether the simulation argument changes how we should handle uncertainty about the far future, as opposed to merely adding one more scenario.

The leaves must include at least these five positions, each with a one-sentence statement of its most awkward commitment: (1) simulation dampening — the argument substantially lowers the expected value of far-future-oriented interventions; (2) fanaticism survives — the argument is one more existential-risk scenario and leaves the structure of far-future reasoning intact; (3) redirected fanaticism — the argument relocates the dominant source of value to the far future of base reality, to which our access is at best acausal; (4) simulation-strengthened fanaticism — the argument enlarges the relevant far future, making fanatical concern more urgent; and (5) near-term moderation — the argument reveals far-future calculations to be too fragile to license fanaticism, favoring robust near-term or person-affecting priorities. Add any further leaves the logic demands. Mark any unoccupied combinations of answers as such, with a one-sentence diagnosis of whether the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot genuinely separate, and say in one sentence why not.

Keep the whole instrument to roughly six to eight branch nodes and no more than eight leaves. The audience is advanced undergraduates who know the simulation argument and longtermism in broad outline but do not know the named positions in this debate.
