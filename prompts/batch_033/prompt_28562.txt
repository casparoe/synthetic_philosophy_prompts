I spent most of last night arguing with a close friend about a question that seems empirically tractable but turned out to be maddeningly slippery: from a utilitarian perspective that takes all sentient beings into account, does eating chicken cause more or less expected suffering than eating beef, per calorie delivered? We both accept the welfarist framework — we're not arguing about whether animal suffering matters, or whether factory farming is wrong in some deontological sense. The disagreement is narrower and, in a way, more frustrating because it feels like it should be resolvable. On the chicken side: broiler chickens are substantially more feed-efficient than cattle, which means less cropland per calorie of meat, which means fewer wild animals — mice, voles, ground-nesting birds, insects — killed by combines, pesticides, and habitat conversion for feed production. On the beef side: the number of individual lives taken per calorie is orders of magnitude lower for beef, and the lives of feedlot cattle, while far from good, may involve less day-to-day physical misery than the ammonia-burned lungs, leg deformities, and extreme crowding of industrial broiler operations. We went back and forth on these considerations for two hours, and by the end she was at roughly 0.7 that chicken is worse, I was at roughly 0.6 that beef is worse, and neither of us could pinpoint the exact sub-question where our assessments diverged.

I want your own credence on this question, stated as a precise probability, but the real deliverable is the decomposition. Break the question into the sub-questions your credence depends on — I expect at least the following: the relative direct suffering per animal (broiler chicken vs. feedlot steer, accounting for duration and intensity), the relative number of animals killed per calorie, the magnitude of wild animal suffering caused by feed-crop cultivation per unit of cropland, and the sign and magnitude of indirect effects (whether crop cultivation on net increases or decreases wild animal populations and their welfare, which could flip the crop-suffering term). Assign a rough probability to each sub-question, show how they combine to your overall number, and identify the term to which your final credence is most sensitive — the one where new evidence would shift your number the most. Finally, tell me which of the two positions I described is closer to your own, and which sub-question most likely explains the gap between her 0.7 and my 0.6.
