I've been working through the Frege–Geach problem for expressivism and I've arrived at an argument that seems to show that if expressivism solves the problem at all, it does so in a way that makes it a theory of all language, not just moral language. That can't be right, but I can't find the misstep.

(1) Expressivism says moral sentences like "Lying is wrong" express non-cognitive attitudes — say, disapproval of lying — rather than beliefs with truth conditions.

(2) The Frege–Geach problem: "Lying is wrong" occurs unasserted in "If lying is wrong, then getting your little brother to lie is wrong." The speaker of the conditional needn't disapprove of lying, yet the sentence is meaningful. So the meaning of a moral sentence is not exhausted by the attitude it expresses when asserted.

(3) The standard expressivist fix is compositional: the conditional expresses a higher-order attitude — disapproval of simultaneously disapproving of lying while failing to disapprove of getting one's brother to lie. Logical connectives map attitudes to attitudes.

(4) But now apply the same compositional machinery to descriptive language. "If the cat is on the mat, then the cat is not in the kitchen" can be analyzed as expressing a higher-order attitude — say, disapproval of simultaneously believing the cat is on the mat while failing to believe the cat is not in the kitchen. And "The cat is on the mat" expresses the attitude of believing that the cat is on the mat.

(5) So descriptive language, too, is a system for expressing attitudes — beliefs being one kind of attitude — and logical connectives operate on attitudes in exactly the same way.

(6) But then the distinction between cognitive and non-cognitive discourse, which expressivism needs in order to state what makes moral language special, is just a distinction between two species of attitude-expression. The view has become a global expressivism, and the original contrast with cognitivism evaporates.

(7) If the expressivist resists (4) by insisting that descriptive language gets a different, truth-conditional semantics, then the challenge is to explain why the compositional machinery that saved moral language from the Frege–Geach problem cannot be applied to descriptive language. What principled difference between the two domains blocks the move?

My study group is split. Half say (4) is illegitimate because belief contents compose truth-functionally in a way that non-cognitive attitudes don't, so the parallel fails. The other half say that if attitudes compose via their contents at all — disapproval of lying is keyed to the content "lying" — then the expressivist is already smuggling in propositional content at step (3) and the view collapses into cognitivism before we even reach step (4). I can't tell which objection is stronger, or whether there's a third diagnosis I'm missing.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost the expressivist?
