I'm a PhD student in history and philosophy of science, and I've been reading a pile of anonymized grant-review reports for a side project. There's a pattern I cant quite make sense of, and I'd like help working out what it commits the reviewers (or the funding body) to.

In the ordinary funding stream, reviewers keep penalizing proposals for things like "doesn't engage enough with the current literature," "uses non-standard terminology," "doesn't build on established findings," or "the methods don't fit what this field currently does." They basically treat fit with the field's existing framework as a mark of rigor. But the same agency runs a separate high-risk, high-reward program whose whole point is to fund work that challenges core assumptions, and there the same features get described as bold, potentially paradigm-shifting, and so on. So a proposal that tries to overturn the current framework gets penalized in one stream and praised in the other for what looks like the same property.

What picture of scientific change and theory choice does the ordinary stream presuppose? Is it basically Kuhnian normal science — the idea that good science is puzzle-solving within a shared paradigm and that evaluators can only judge work against standards they already share? If so, is the special stream a tacit admission that normal peer review is structurally unable to recognize revolutionary science? And are the two streams inconsistent, or do they actually presuppose the same underlying view (revolutions happen, but they can't be evaluated by ordinary standards)? More generally, if you take incommensurability seriously, could a reviewer ever fairly assess a proposal that doesn't share their standards, and what would a review process designed on that assumption even look like?
