You are one of the trustees of a newly endowed institute for the study of work and automation. The institute will fund three four-year projects, each pairing a philosopher with a small team of labor economists and sociologists, and its stated purpose is to clear up the conceptual confusions that currently distort empirical research and public argument about automation. It is not to produce another estimate of displacement risk or another inventory of automatable tasks. The other trustees have assembled a long list of candidate questions, including: whether the concept of work should extend to unpaid care and to forms of contribution that fall outside the wage relation; whether involuntary unemployment harms people mainly through lost income, lost social recognition, or the loss of opportunities for skilled activity; whether meaningful work is a distinctive good that neither income support nor leisure can replace; whether automating meaningful but economically unproductive tasks should be resisted for reasons that do not apply to automating drudgery; whether post-work proposals confuse the value people find in contribution with a mere preference; and whether a large part of the automation debate is really a disagreement about authority and the terms of cooperation rather than about scarcity.

Only a handful of these can be pursued well. Which three should the institute back, and in what order? I want a defended agenda rather than a survey: for each question you choose, show how settling it would change what the empirical teams measure or what policy debate takes for granted; say whether the question is already being handled adequately elsewhere; say why the philosopher on the team, rather than the economists or sociologists working alone, is the one positioned to make progress on it; and name the development over the next four years that would most clearly signal the question had been the wrong investment.
