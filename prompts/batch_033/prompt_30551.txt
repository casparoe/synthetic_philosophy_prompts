I'm a high school student writing an extended essay on how scientific theories get confirmed, and I've been reading about Bayesian confirmation theory. One book I found summarizes the theory and a well-known objection to it roughly as follows. Bayesian confirmation theory says that a piece of evidence E confirms a hypothesis H exactly when learning E raises the probability of H, so that P(H|E) > P(H). But the theory cannot handle old evidence. If E was already known before H was proposed, then P(E) is already 1, so conditioning on E cannot change any probabilities, and P(H|E) must equal P(H). Yet the history of science shows that old evidence does confirm new theories: the classic example is the anomalous precession of Mercury's perihelion, which had been known for decades before Einstein proposed general relativity and was then taken as strong confirmation of the theory. The book concludes that since Bayesianism cannot represent this case, the Bayesian account of confirmation is refuted and a non-probabilistic theory of evidence is needed. I would like to rely on this summary in my essay, but I suspect it overstates things. Could you go through it and tell me which parts a careful Bayesian would accept, which parts are misstated or conflated, and whether the conclusion actually follows from the accurate parts?
