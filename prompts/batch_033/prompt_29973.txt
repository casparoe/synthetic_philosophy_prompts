I've been reading around in Daniel Dennett's work on consciousness and the self, and I keep coming back to his analogy between the self and a center of gravity. The way the analogy is usually put, by Dennett and by the people who lean on it, goes something like this. A center of gravity is not a material thing you could point to inside an object; it is an abstract point whose location is fixed by the distribution of mass, and it is perfectly real as an abstraction even though there is no special particle sitting at that point. Likewise, the self is said to be a center of narrative gravity: an abstractum posited over the stories we tell about ourselves, useful for prediction and explanation, but not an inner substance, a Cartesian theater, or a little person in the brain. A friend of mine has been using this analogy to argue that questions like "what is the self, really?" and "where is the self located?" are as confused as asking what a center of gravity is made of. On his view, the analogy shows that the self is a useful fiction, and we should stop looking for anything more substantive behind it.

I am not sure the analogy can carry that much weight. I would like you to audit it the way you would audit an argumentative analogy. First, spell out the correspondences in prose: what plays the role of the mass distribution, the physical object, the abstract point, the gravitational field, and the usefulness of the center of gravity for calculation? Then identify where the mapping strains or breaks. For instance, centers of gravity have no point of view, do not care about their own persistence, are not the objects of moral attitudes, and do not author anything. Which of these disanalogies are merely superficial, and which ones matter for the conclusion my friend wants to draw? Finally, tell me whether his conclusion, that the self is only a useful abstraction and that questions about what it really is should be set aside, survives the audit, or whether the analogy was quietly doing the argumentative work for him. If there is a better analogy that captures what is right and wrong in Dennett's picture, say what it is and where it improves on the original.
