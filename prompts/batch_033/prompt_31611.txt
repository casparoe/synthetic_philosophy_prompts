I've been collecting my gut reactions about who is in a position to blame whom, and I want to know what principle, if any, is underneath them.

Dave has been cheating on his wife for years. Last week he found out his brother-in-law had a one-night stand and really laid into him: you made a vow, you broke it, you wrecked your family. My gut says Dave has no business saying any of that. No standing, full stop.

Same setup, but this time Dave opens with "look, I'm the last person to talk, I've done worse, but what you did was still wrong." My gut still says no standing. If anything the disclaimer makes it worse, because now he's using his own record as a shield while he keeps doing the thing.

My aunt cheated exactly once, about twenty-five years ago. She told her husband the next day, they went to counseling, and she spent years rebuilding trust; she's been faithful ever since. Recently she told a younger friend, pretty firmly, that the friend's affair was wrong and she should come clean. My gut says my aunt has standing. I don't find her hypocritical at all.

A coworker of mine has never cheated, but he's told me more than once that the only reason is lack of opportunity — he'd do it in a heartbeat if an attractive woman made it easy and he thought he wouldn't get caught. When he called another coworker a sleaze for cheating, my gut said that's off too. He doesnt get to blame, even though he's never actually done it.

A guy who drops wrappers on the sidewalk every day sees a stranger drop a cup and mutters "people are such pigs." No standing there either.

A stranger in a café overhears a couple letting their kid have unlimited tablet time and tells them they're setting him up for failure. My gut says he shouldn't say that, no standing — but its not that he's a hypocrite, it's just none of his business.

The two verdicts I can't get to sit together are my aunt's and my coworker's: she actually did the thing and I give her standing, he never did and I don't. If you'd told me the rule was "no clean hands, no standing," I'd have it exactly backwards.

The case I keep going back and forth on: my father-in-law smokes a pack a day, and he told my fourteen-year-old not to start smoking. Half of me thinks he's being a hypocrite and should shut up; the other half thinks he's exactly the person who should say it, because he knows first-hand what it costs.

My working theory was something like: you have standing to blame someone for a wrong only if you're not currently treating the norm against that wrong as optional for yourself. But I can feel it straining against my aunt (she violated the norm once) and against the café stranger (he isn't treating any norm as optional).

So: is there one principle — or a small set of candidates — that captures all of this, or nearly all of it? State it as precisely as you can, tell me what it says about the father-in-law case and about a case I haven't listed, and be straight with me about which of my verdicts it forces me to drop.
