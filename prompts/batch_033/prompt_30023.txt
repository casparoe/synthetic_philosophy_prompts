I'd like you to work through a counterfactual question about the Mencius–Xunzi debate over human nature. Mencius held that human nature is good: everyone is born with incipient moral dispositions—the four sprouts of compassion, shame, deference, and the sense of right and wrong—which need only to be nourished. Xunzi held that human nature is bad: people are born with desires and impulses that, left to themselves, produce contention and disorder, and whatever is good in us is the product of deliberate effort (wei), teaching, and ritual. Now suppose Xunzi were confronted with the body of recent developmental-psychology findings according to which preverbal infants already prefer an agent who helps over one who hinders, sometimes comfort a person in distress, and look longer at unequal distributions. How would Xunzi most plausibly respond? Would he have to concede that Mencius was closer to the truth about an inborn moral core, or can his framework absorb these findings without revision? And, on the other side, should Mencius regard these studies as vindication of the four sprouts, or would a faithful Mencian have reason to resist treating infant reactions as evidence of morality at all? I want a careful reconstruction of how each position would actually operate when extended to this evidence, not a generic nature-versus-nurture essay. If you think the question is badly posed—for example, because bringing infant-behavior data to bear on a Warring States debate about xing rests on a confusion—say so plainly and explain how a better-posed version of the question would go.
