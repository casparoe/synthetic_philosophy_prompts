A colleague in our group has been arguing that the low-impact AI research program rests on a distinction it cannot define, and I cannot locate where the argument overreaches. I will set it out as he gave it.

(1) An effect is a side effect only relative to a distinction between what a system is meant to do and the rest of what its action changes.
(2) To build a system that avoids or penalizes side effects, one must operationalize that distinction: fix a baseline state, choose a set of variables whose departure from the baseline counts as impact, and set a penalty that says how much departure matters.
(3) Each of these choices is evaluative. A baseline encodes a view about how things would otherwise have gone; the choice of variables and weights encodes a view about which changes matter and to whom; a change to a bank balance and a change to a distant asteroid are treated differently only because something values the former more.
(4) So a low-impact objective cannot be specified without smuggling in a substantive standard of value.
(5) But a substantive standard of value is exactly what the alignment problem has not delivered; if we had a full specification of value, a separate low-impact constraint would be unnecessary.
(6) Therefore the low-impact program either presupposes a solution to the alignment problem or rests on an arbitrary, value-laden choice presented as value-neutral.

So far I follow him. But then he draws the corollary I resist:

(7) Absent a full specification of value, no one can say of any actual system which of its effects are side effects and which are its task.
(8) So the distinction between task and side effect is empty for every real system, not merely hard to draw.

This seems to prove too much. A thermostat's task is to regulate temperature; the click it makes is a side effect. An industrial robot's task is to place a part; scuffing the floor is a side effect. Ordinary engineering draws these distinctions daily without a full theory of value. If the argument is sound, then limiting a machine's force, heat, or noise is arbitrary in exactly the same way, which nobody believes.

Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? I would most like to keep (6) while rejecting (7) and (8), but I want to know whether that is stable or whether the pressure that gets you to (6) really does force the corollary. Name the step to reject, explain why it looked compulsory, and price the alternatives.
