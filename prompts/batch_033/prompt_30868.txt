I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about longtermism — the idea that the sheer size of the far future means it should dominate how we think about doing good now. Somewhere in the weeds I tripped over infinite ethics, and now my gut calls on the toy cases won't add up. All the cases are worlds with an infinite line of people, one behind the other, each with a welfare number. Here are my verdicts:

(1) Raise everyone: everyone is at 1, and I press a button that moves everyone to 2; obviously the second world is better. (2) One bonus: everyone is at 1, and I press a button that moves exactly one person — say the one at position 17 — from 1 to 2, nobody else changing; I say that's a small improvement, the world is better, not merely different. (3) The swap: everyone is at 1 except positions 1 and 2, who are at 0 and 2; I press a button and they trade, so position 1 is at 2 and position 2 is at 0; I say the two worlds are exactly equally good, same distribution, just different names. (4) The new arrival: everyone is at 1, and I press a button that adds one brand-new person with welfare 1 at the back of the line; I want to say the world is no better, there was already infinitely many people at 1, so one more doesn't increase the total. (5) The spread: one world alternates 0, 2, 0, 2 forever and the other has everyone at 1; I feel the all-1s world is better because nobody is stuck at 0, even though the average is 1 either way.

The pair I can't square is 2 and 5: if lifting one person from 1 to 2 is a real improvement, then a world with infinitely many people lifted to 2 should be better unless the infinitely many people dropped to 0 somehow cancel that out, and I can't say why they would. Is there a single principle that gets all five? If not, which verdicts does the cleanest principle force me to drop, and what does it say about a sixth case I keep going back and forth on: everyone at 1 forever, versus everyone at 0 for the first billion positions and 2 from then on? Give me the answer as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no introduction or conclusion: the principle; how it handles each of my five cases; which of my verdicts it cannot keep; its call on the sixth case; and one further case of your own that would test whether I actually hold it.
