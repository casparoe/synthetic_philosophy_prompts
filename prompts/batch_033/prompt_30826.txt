I've been asked to produce a one-page statement of my views on Moloch-style dynamics, multipolar traps, and races to the bottom for an interdisciplinary working group, and before I circulate anything I want a consistency check on the position I'd be presenting. Here is the set of commitments I currently hold, roughly as I would defend them:

(1) Moloch-type outcomes — arms races, regulatory races to the bottom, AI development races, and similar dynamics — are best modeled as multiparty social dilemmas in which each agent's move is an individually rational response to the incentives created by the others' expected moves. No one in the trap needs to be malicious, irrational, or ignorant for the bad equilibrium to obtain.

(2) The distinguishing mark of a genuine multipolar trap, as opposed to an ordinary conflict of interest, is that the resulting state is bad for every party by the parties' own lights, while each party is nonetheless individually incentivized to keep contributing to it.

(3) Because the trap is a property of the incentive structure rather than of individual character, the remedy has to be a change in the rules — enforceable coordination, commitment devices, mechanisms that alter payoffs. It is unfair, and probably incoherent, to demand self-sacrificing restraint from agents inside the trap as a matter of individual morality, and any account that makes the solution depend on enough individuals voluntarily absorbing costs is mislocating the problem.

(4) I also think participants retain more responsibility than that structural story suggests. The fact that the system made the bad behavior profitable is usually only a partial excuse, and a person of integrity should sometimes accept a real personal cost to exit, refuse to participate, or blow the whistle, even when that will not by itself change the equilibrium.

(5) Other things equal, the more multipolar a decision environment is, the more likely it is to produce a trap and the harder the trap is to escape, because each additional independent actor is another potential defector from any fragile coordination.

(6) I am, however, suspicious of proposals that respond to multipolar traps by concentrating authority. I think a world of many smaller, overlapping, and competing jurisdictions is on the whole more robust than a single global sovereign.

(7) I think many of the things that get called races to the bottom — ordinary market competition, most obviously — are not genuine traps. Competition among firms mostly creates new value rather than dividing a fixed positional prize or producing a public bad, and the label should be reserved for cases involving a fixed positional good, a negative externality, or a public bad.

(8) Finally, I believe these traps are in principle solvable by institutional design. The interesting question is which mechanisms work, not whether some workable mechanism exists.

I'd like an audit rather than a restatement: which of these commitments are in genuine tension, which pairs only appear to be, and how severe is each genuine tension? For each real tension, say what would have to be revised and what the cheapest fix would be, and then tell me which single revision would do the most to make the whole set coherent.
