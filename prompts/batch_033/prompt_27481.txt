I've been reading about how federal agencies decide whether to issue health and safety regulations. The basic method is cost-benefit analysis: the agency estimates how many lives a rule would save, multiplies that by something called the value of a statistical life, adds in other benefits like reduced illness, and compares the total to compliance costs. If the monetized benefits exceed the costs, the rule is treated as justified.

What bothers me is that this method treats harms and benefits as if they can be freely traded off across different people. If a pollution rule saves fifty lives in one community but imposes five hundred million dollars in costs spread across consumers in other states, the analysis calls that a wash — the ledger balances. But the people who die if the rule isn't issued are not the people who pay higher prices if it is. No one compensates the losers. The analysis seems to act as though society is a single person with a single utility account, rather than a collection of separate individuals whose gains and losses cannot simply be netted out.

And then I notice an inconsistency. The same legal system that uses cost-benefit analysis for regulation would never permit that kind of trade-off between identified individuals. If I dump waste on your property, I cannot defend myself by showing that the money I saved exceeds the value of your loss. Tort law requires me to compensate you specifically. Yet the regulatory system effectively authorizes harms to some for the sake of benefits to others, as long as the aggregate numbers work out — and the people harmed get no compensation at all.

So I want to understand: what does this practice presuppose about whether persons are morally "separate" in a fundamental way? Does cost-benefit analysis implicitly deny that there is a deep moral difference between a harm falling on one person and the same harm falling on another, or can the practice be justified without taking a position on that question? And is the practice genuinely consistent with the rest of the legal system's treatment of interpersonal harm, or is there a real philosophical tension here that the law simply papers over?
