So my friend took a philosophy of language class and was explaining speech act theory to me, and her summary went something like this. Austin showed that a whole class of sentences don't describe the world at all — they do something. "I promise," "I bet you five bucks," "I now pronounce you married" are performatives. Unlike ordinary descriptive sentences, they aren't true or false; they're happy or unhappy (felicitous or infelicitous) depending on whether the speaker has the right standing, the circumstances are appropriate, and the right words get said. She also said that because saying "I promise" just is the act of promising, you can't really ask whether the speaker was being sincere — the act happens as soon as the words come out, and whether they intend to keep the promise is a separate ethical question, not a question about the speech act. And the upshot, she said, is that language splits neatly into two kinds: performatives, where saying constitutes the act, and constatives, which describe how things are and are true or false — most of language is constative, and performatives are a special marked-off class.

I vaguely remember reading somewhere that Austin himself later backed off this distinction, or that it breaks down under pressure, but she talks like its settled. Is her summary fair? I don't know enough to tell where the problems are. Go through it and say which parts a careful speech act theorist would accept, which are oversimplifications, and which are just wrong, and wether her "special marked-off class" conclusion survives once the details are fixed.
