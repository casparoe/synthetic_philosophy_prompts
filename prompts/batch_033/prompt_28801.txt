I have spent much of my career working with probabilistic models in the natural sciences, and I have recently turned to formal epistemology with considerable interest. Two principles keep striking me as curiously parallel, and I would like to understand their relationship with more precision than the introductory literature seems to provide.

The first is Lewis's Principal Principle: roughly, that a rational agent's credence in a proposition, conditional on the objective chance of that proposition taking a given value and on any admissible background evidence, should equal that chance value. The second is van Fraassen's Reflection Principle: roughly, that a rational agent's current credence in a proposition, conditional on her future credence in that proposition taking a given value, should equal that future-credence value.

Both have the surface grammar of deference principles — in the one case to the world's chancy structure, in the other to one's own anticipated epistemic trajectory. But the objects of deference are quite different, and I am unsure how deep the formal analogy runs.

Could you compare these two principles systematically? Specifically: (1) Do they share a common formal core, and if so, what exactly is it? (2) Can either be derived from the other under plausible auxiliary assumptions, or are they genuinely independent constraints on rational credence? (3) What are the standard failure cases or counterexamples for each, and do the ways in which each principle breaks down illuminate something about the relationship between chance, time, and rational belief? I am less interested in exegesis of particular texts than in the logical geography of the two principles — where they overlap, where they come apart, and what that tells us about what it means for an agent's beliefs to answer to the world.
