You are a professional philosopher specializing in metaphysics and the theory of causation. I am an undergraduate in an upper-level metaphysics seminar on causation, and we have spent the past several weeks on causation by omission. I have been collecting my own intuitive verdicts on cases, and I have reached the point where I can no longer tell whether they add up to a coherent view. Please work through this with me carefully. Before settling on an answer, brainstorm the candidate principles you might use — at least a pure counterfactual account, an account that restricts genuine causation to physical connections, and an account that builds in expectations, roles, or duties — and say what each candidate would do to my verdicts.

Here are my verdicts. In each case, the question is whether the person's not doing something caused the bad outcome.

1. The paid gardener. I hire a gardener to water my plants while I am away. He forgets, and the plants die. Verdict: his forgetting caused their deaths.

2. The passerby. A stranger walking past my yard sees the plants wilting and could easily turn on the hose, but does not. The plants die. Verdict: I do not think the stranger caused their deaths; at most, he failed to prevent them.

3. The mother. A mother does not feed her infant for a day, and the infant becomes dangerously dehydrated. Verdict: the mother's omission caused the dehydration.

4. The distant donor. Someone in another country receives an email about a famine and could donate enough to save one child's life, but does not; the child dies. Verdict: the non-donor did not cause the child's death.

5. The doctor. A doctor fails to give a routine antibiotic to a patient with a bacterial infection, and the patient dies. Verdict: the doctor's omission caused the death.

6. The lifeguard. A lifeguard is texting and does not notice a swimmer in trouble; the swimmer drowns. Verdict: the lifeguard's inattention caused the drowning.

7. The beachgoer. A stranger on the beach sees the same swimmer in trouble and could throw a life ring, but does not. Verdict: the stranger did not cause the drowning.

8. The friend. A friend promised to feed my cat while I was away, forgot, and the cat became ill from going without food. Verdict: the friend's forgetting caused the cat's illness.

I am struck by two things. First, my verdicts track the difference between the presence and absence of a role, promise, or obligation: gardener versus passerby, mother versus distant donor, lifeguard versus beachgoer. Second, I hold a conviction I do not want to surrender lightly: whether A caused B should be a matter of how the world is, not a matter of what A was obligated to do or what we expected of A. Those two things seem to be in tension, and I cannot see how to resolve it. The closest I have come is a principle like this: an omission causes an outcome just in case the agent could have prevented the outcome and had a special duty or undertaking to do so. That fits most of my verdicts, but it builds obligation into causation, which is exactly what makes me uncomfortable.

I also have two further cases where I genuinely do not know what to say.

A. The useless antibiotic. The doctor fails to give the antibiotic, but the infection was resistant to it; the patient would have died even if the drug had been given. Did the doctor's omission cause the death?

B. The blocked fire escape. A landlord never installs a fire escape that the law requires. On the night of a fire, the fire escape would have been blocked by flames and unusable anyway. Did the landlord's omission cause the deaths?

What I would like from you is this: (a) state the best principle — or a small set of candidate principles — that captures my verdicts, making it precise and saying which case or pair of cases each clause is doing work for; (b) apply the principle to cases A and B, and tell me what I should think about them if I accept it; (c) be explicit about which of my verdicts, if any, the principle cannot accommodate, and which of my verdicts or my standing conviction I should be prepared to revise; and (d) say whether any part of my confusion is verbal rather than substantive — for example, whether the word caused in omission cases might already mean something like causally responsible in a way that packs in norms.
