I keep running into the same move in arguments about the mind, and I'd like to know if it has a name, or if it's just a mistake people keep making without anyone labelling it. The move goes like this. Someone starts from something hard to deny: a thought is occurring, a pain is happening, there's an experience of red going on. So far so good. Then they say an experience has to be had by something, a thought has to be thought by a thinker — you can't have a verb without a noun doing the verb, as one person put it to me — so there must be some self or subject standing behind the experiences, the thing that has them. When I push back and say maybe all we're entitled to is that thinking is going on, not that there's a thing doing the thinking, they treat that as obviously absurd, like I've denied that actions need agents. I first noticed it when someone was arguing that the self can't be an illusion, because a pain is clearly being felt, and being felt means there's a feeler. Then I saw the same shape in an argument that consciousness can't be an illusion: there's an experience occuring, and an experience is always an experience for someone, so there must be someone there. In both cases the conclusion seems to add a thing that wasn't in the premise. I know Descartes' cogito is somewhere in the neighbourhood — he wanted to get from the fact that he was thinking to the fact that he existed. But I'm not asking about that. I'm asking about the specific step from 'there's a thought' to 'there's a thinker', or from 'an experience is occurring' to 'there's an experiencer'. Does that step have a recognized name? Has anyone argued it's a fallacy, and if so, what exactly is the argument? And if it doesn't have a name, what would you call it, and where are its edges — are there cases where the move is legitimate and cases where it isn't? I'm not just after a label; I want to know exactly what this move assumes, and whether that assumption ever does real work or is just grammar dressed up as metaphysics.
