I'm teaching Lord of the Flies in a high-school philosophy elective, and I want to use it to get at the Mencius–Xunzi debate over human nature. Mencius says we're born with sprouts of goodness—compassion, shame, deference, approval—that get stunted by bad conditions, while Xunzi says our raw nature is selfish and chaotic and only ritual and education make us decent. Read the novel through that debate: does the boys' descent into violence come out as evidence for Xunzi, or does the book actually show Mencian sprouts being deformed by the island situation? Look at Simon, Piggy's death, the conch, the fire, the painted faces, and especially the naval officer at the end, and say where the novel complicates both positions instead of just illustrating one. I'm not interested in a canned man-is-inherently-evil reading; I want you to be a serious philosopher of moral psychology doing a close reading, and to be honest about where the book resists the theory. Start by brainstorming the two or three scenes that cut most strongly each way, then build your reading from those. Don't quote passages from memory—just describe the scenes.
