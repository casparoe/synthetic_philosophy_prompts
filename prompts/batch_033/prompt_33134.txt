You are a professor of philosophy of science who works at the intersection of formal epistemology and algorithmic information theory. I'm a PhD student preparing my dissertation proposal, and before I commit myself in print I want a careful audit of whether my views hang together. Please take each of my commitments seriously — they are all positions I find independently attractive — and don't pull punches.

Here are my commitments:

(a) Occam's razor is an epistemic principle, not a merely pragmatic one. Simplicity is a guide to truth, not just a cognitive shortcut or a methodological convenience.

(b) The best formal explication of simplicity is Kolmogorov complexity: the simplicity of a hypothesis is measured by the length of its shortest program on a fixed universal Turing machine.

(c) Solomonoff induction — using the universal prior P(h) ∝ 2^{-K(h)} and updating by conditionalizing on data — is the right normative account of inductive inference.

(d) The dependence of Kolmogorov complexity on a choice of universal Turing machine is not a fatal problem. The invariance theorem guarantees that switching machines changes the prior by at most a constant factor, and with enough data the posterior converges to the true distribution regardless.

(e) In actual scientific practice, the hypotheses we treat as "simpler" are those with fewer free parameters, lower VC dimension, or sparser functional forms. In the cases that matter, this is equivalent to those hypotheses having lower Kolmogorov complexity.

(f) The no-free-lunch theorems are an important part of the case for a simplicity-biased prior. They show that without a non-uniform prior, no learning algorithm outperforms any other when averaged over all possible problems — so we need a prior that encodes a bias, and a simplicity bias is the right one.

(g) The uncomputability of Kolmogorov complexity does not undermine the whole project, any more than the intractability of full Bayesian inference undermines Bayesian epistemology. Solomonoff induction is a normative standard, and we can use computable approximations in practice.
