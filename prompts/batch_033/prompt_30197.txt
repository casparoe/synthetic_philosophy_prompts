I recently had to decide whether to finish an expensive professional qualification I no longer expect to use, and I was struck by how much force the thought carried that I had already put so much into it, even after I had accepted in the abstract that sunk costs should not count. That has made me want to think more carefully about whether honoring sunk costs is always irrational, and I keep returning to the Concorde project as the clearest real-world test of the question. I know the standard story; I am asking for a disciplined case analysis that runs in both directions.

Take the Concorde project as a test of the claim that honoring sunk costs is always irrational. The salient facts, as I understand them: the British and French governments committed themselves in the early 1960s to developing a supersonic airliner jointly; costs rose far beyond initial estimates; by around 1969 or 1970, after the prototypes had flown but before the aircraft had entered service, it was already clear that the program would never recover its development costs and that its commercial prospects were poor; nevertheless, both governments carried it through to completion and then subsidized its operation for years, publicly invoking the binding character of the treaty, national prestige, aerospace employment, and the value of preserving technological capacity.

I want the analysis to do two things. First, the mapping: at a live decision point such as 1970, identify the relevant decision-makers and their options, and separate the genuinely sunk costs from the forward-looking costs and benefits of cancellation versus continuation. Which of the governments' stated reasons were forward-looking — the penalties and diplomatic damage of breaking the treaty, the option value of the aircraft and the industrial base, the reputational cost to the state as a reliable partner — and which, if any, were genuinely backward-looking? Once those forward-looking considerations have been priced in, what exactly does the standard economic model say was irrational about the decision, and what is the residue of pure sunk-cost reasoning that remains?

Second, turn the lens around: what does Concorde show about the theory itself? Does it confirm that honoring sunk costs is a fallacy, or does it reveal that sunk cost is often made to do too much work, because in real continuation decisions there are almost always forward-looking benefits — reputation, commitment, identity, option value — that are easily mistaken for the fallacy? Where does the rational-choice account break down or fall silent, and is there a defensible sense in which continuing was rational? I am less interested in a final verdict than in an honest account of what this episode can and cannot show about whether honoring sunk costs is always irrational.
