A couple of weeks ago I was at a pub with two mates from uni, and things got properly heated between them over something that happened at work. One of them, Callum, works in sales and had just closed a deal by telling a client that a competitor's product had been 'recalled twice in the last three years.' Which is true — but what Callum knew and didn't say is that both recalls were for a trivial labelling issue that posed zero safety risk, and that his own company's product had actually had a more serious (but unpublicised) issue around the same time. The client clearly took 'recalled twice' to mean the product was dangerous, and Callum let them run with it.

When Callum told the story, our other mate Jess basically called him a liar. Callum pushed back hard: every sentance he uttered was true, he never said the product was dangerous, and the client was free to ask follow-ups. Jess said that's exactly what makes it worse — he's using the literal truth as a shield while doing all the same work a lie would do, except now he also gets to feel clever about it. Callum said that can't be right because then basically all of social life would be immoral: we all manage what we reveal, we all let people draw their own conclusions, and drawing a hard line at 'literally false' at least gives us a clear rule. Jess came back with: so if I ask you whether you ate the last biscuit and you say 'I've been in the other room all afternoon' — which is true, but you nipped in and ate it two minutes ago — you haven't lied to me? Callum said that's different because the biscuit case involves a direct question and a reasonable expectation of a straight answer, whereas his client was an adult doing business and should know to be sceptical. Jess said he was just moving the goalposts: first the rule was 'nothing I said was false,' now suddenly context and expectations matter, so the rule wasn't actually doing the work he claimed it was. At that point they both turned to me and I had absolutley nothing useful to say.

It's been rattling around in my head since. They both made points that feel right but I can't hold them all at once. Can you go through their exchange move by move and say who's right about what — not just who wins overall, but which specific claims hold up and which don't? And if there's a sensible middle position that neither of them articulated, I'd like to know what it is. Oh, and please write in British English — I'm sending this on to a couple of people and we're all in the UK.
