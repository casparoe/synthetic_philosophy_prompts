I'm trying to figure out what I actually think about when a thing is the same thing after a change, going by my gut on cases. Here are my verdicts:

- A lump of clay gets shaped into a statue of a horse. I think the lump and the statue are two different things even though they're made of the exact same stuff at the same time. The lump was there before the statue and it'll be there after if you squash it. The statue came into existence when the clay took that shape. Two things, one space. Fine.
- You break the statue's leg off and glue it back on. Same statue. Fine.
- You squash the statue into a ball. Statue's gone, the lump survives. Fine.
- A gold ring is melted down and recast into a ring of a different design. Old ring's gone, new ring exists. But what if it's recast into the exact same design — is it the same ring? My gut says no, but I can't say why, and it bothers me.
- A wooden ship has every plank replaced over thirty years, one at a time. Same ship. I'm firm on this. But if someone collected all the old planks and reassembled them into a ship, I'd want to say that's also the same ship, which can't be right — I can't have two ships both identical to the original.
- A caterpillar becomes a butterfly. Same organism, different life stage. The material turnover is almost total, but I don't care. Yet if I'm fine with the ship, shouldn't I be fine with the reassembled-plank ship too? Something feels different and I can't put my finger on it.
- A person gets gradual, complete memory loss until they remember nothing of their past. Same person. I'm sure of this. But I can't ground it in the body (cells all turn over), and I can't ground it in the memories (they're gone). So what am I tracking?
- My laptop: hard drive and motherboard replaced. Same laptop. But if you replace the screen, casing, keyboard, and battery all at the same time, I want to say it's a different laptop. That cutoff feels arbitrary and I don't like it.

I'm looking for a principle — or maybe a small set of principles that apply to different kinds of things — that captures as many of these verdicts as possible. And I want to know: which of my verdicts does the principle force me to abandon, and why? I'm especially interested in what it says about the ship case and the person case, since those feel the most important and also the most in tension with each other.
