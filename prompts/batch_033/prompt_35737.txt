I'm a retired biochemist who's been reading philosophy of mind and AI for a couple years now, and there's a pattern I keep noticing that I can't find a tidy label for. Maybe you can tell me whether philosophers have already named it and carved it up.

The thing is this. Suppose you want a system — a robot, a program, even a formal model of how humans reason — to figure out the consequences of an action. Say, moving a beaker from one shelf to another. To do that, the system needs to know what *changes* (the beaker's position), but it also somehow needs to know what *doesn't* change: the beaker's mass, the room's temperature, the color of the walls, the fact that the floor is still there, that your colleague hasn't teleported into the room, and so on. The list of non-changes is basically infinite. And yet any competent adult handles this instantly without enumerating anything.

I've now seen this same shape of problem pop up in three places: old-fashioned AI planning, philosophical work on belief revision (when you update one belief, how do you know which other beliefs stay put?), and even in discussions of how scientific models isolate causal factors from a background of everything-else-staying-the-same. Each literature seems to treat it as it's own thing, with its own terminology. Is there a single name in philosophy for this core difficulty — the problem of delimiting what's relevant and what stays unchanged when something happens? And if there is, does that name actually cover all three variants, or are they genuinely distinct problems that just look similar from a distance? I'm less after the label than the carving: is this one thing or several, and where are the joints?
