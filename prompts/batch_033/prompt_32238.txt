Retired spectroscopist here — forty years of trusting that the instrument will behave tomorrow the way it did yesterday, so induction isn't an abstract game to me. I've been reading philosophy of science since I retired, and my reading group asked me to write up a "philosophical profile" of my views on induction and Goodman's grue so the others can poke at it. I'd like a professional philosopher to audit it before I present, because I've got a nagging feeling something doesn't quite hang together but I can't locate it.

Here's where I've landed:

(1) Induction is rationally justified, and the justification is basically its track record: induction has worked so well in the past that we're entitled to expect the future to resemble the past.

(2) Goodman's grue example shows that the same evidence supports the hypothesis that all emeralds are green and the hypothesis that all emeralds are grue equally well, so long as we allow grue-style predicates.

(3) But I don't think the grue hypothesis is a serious rival. Green is a projectible predicate and grue is a gerrymandered, disjunctive mess.

(4) Projectibility isn't just a matter of our habits or our language. Green is projectible because it carves nature at a joint — it picks out a real, mind-independent property — and grue doesn't.

(5) In practice, the way we tell projectible predicates from gerrymandered ones is by track record: we look at which predicates have a long history of successful use in science and everyday induction. Thats how we know green is the right one.
