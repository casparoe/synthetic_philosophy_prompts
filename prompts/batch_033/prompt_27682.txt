You are a philosopher of decision theory with a specialty in diachronic rationality. I run a fiduciary financial-planning practice. Every quarter I review each client's plan, and whenever a client wants to change course I have to decide whether I am looking at a rational revision of preferences — new information or changed life circumstances — or at a pattern of unstable or intransitive preferences of the kind that leaves a client open to a money pump and can also attract churning or exploitative product sales. Please write me a reusable decision procedure I can run in a thirty-minute review and document for compliance. Begin by briefly identifying the distinctions from diachronic rationality and money-pump arguments that the procedure will depend on, then translate them into a checklist of questions in order. For each question, say what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with no action, where it flags a concern, and where it hands the decision to a human because it is a values judgment rather than a fact. My advisors do not know the philosophical theory, so the checklist must be usable without that background, and you should say plainly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Then run the procedure on three cases: a client who repeatedly switches between conservative and aggressive allocations after market news; a client who signs a precommitment to a retirement glide path and then, with no new information, wants to abandon it; and a client whose pairwise preferences among three funds form a cycle. Include at least one case where the procedure tells me something I would rather not do, and end by saying what the procedure cannot settle.
