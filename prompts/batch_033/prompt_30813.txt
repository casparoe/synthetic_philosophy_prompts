I've been trying to get clear on what I think about meaning and interpretation, and I'd like someone to go through my commitments and tell me whether they hang together. This came up because I was in an argument that started about constitutional originalism, veered into literary theory, and then somehow ended up in ordinary language philosophy, and I realized I was making claims at each stop that might not all fit into the same box. I've been reading rationalist-adjacent stuff on philosophy of language for years, but I've never actually lined up my views and checked for consistency. Here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) In ordinary conversation, meaning is a matter of communicative intention. When you say "it's cold in here" and I understand you're requesting I close the window, that's because I've recovered your intention. The Gricean picture — that speaker meaning is prior to sentence meaning, and that understanding is intention-recognition — seems basically right to me for everyday communication. I don't think there's a deeper fact about meaning that floats free of what the speaker meant to convey.

(2) For literary texts, I hold that the author's intentions are irrelevant to what the text means. If I learn from Faulkner's letters that he intended a symbol to mean X, that fact doesn't settle what the symbol means in the novel. The text means what a competent reader, armed with the relevant linguistic and cultural knowledge, would take it to mean. I'm sympathetic to the New Critical rejection of the intentional fallacy and to the Barthes-style claim that authorial intention doesn't constrain interpretation — though I don't go all the way to "anything goes."

(3) In constitutional interpretation, I'm an original-public-meaning originalist. What matters is not what the framers subjectively intended but what the words of the Constitution would have meant to a reasonable, well-informed reader at the time of ratification. I think this is the only approach that treats the Constitution as law rather than as an invitation to judicial policymaking, and it seems to me importantly different from original-intent originalism, which I reject.

(4) When a text is genuinely ambiguous — when the linguistic evidence doesn't decisively favor one reading over another — we should resolve the ambiguity in whatever way produces the morally best outcome, all things considered. This seems to me just what it means to interpret responsibly: you don't pretend the text is unambiguous when it isn't, and you choose the reading that does the most good.

(5) The meaning of a text is fixed at the moment of its composition and does not drift with changing cultural contexts. A seventeenth-century text doesn't acquire new meanings just because words have shifted their extensions or connotations; it means what it meant when it was written.

I can feel that some of these pull against each other, but I'm not sure which tensions are real and which are merely apparent, and I don't have a clear sense of which revision would cost me the least. Please go through these and audit them: identify which pairs are in tension, rank the tensions by severity, say which ones dissolve on closer inspection, and tell me what the cheapest path to consistency looks like. I want a committed answer — don't just lay out the options and leave me to pick; tell me what you think has to give and why.
