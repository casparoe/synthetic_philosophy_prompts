I'm in a philosophy of mind course, and we've just finished the unit on Daniel Dennett's "Real Patterns" and the distinction between the physical stance, the design stance, and the intentional stance. Our professor has given us the option of submitting a creative piece for the final assignment, and I'd like to write a sonnet that makes the paper's core argument — that the patterns picked out by the intentional stance are real, not merely instrumental fictions — legible to someone who hasn't read it.

The sonnet should use its volta to mark the shift from the reductive physical stance to the intentional stance. The octave should build the case for thinking that only the physical stance describes what is really there — atoms, voltages, the microphysical — and that talk of beliefs and desires is just a convenient shorthand we could in principle do without. The turn should introduce a chess-playing computer or a similar system where adopting the intentional stance is plainly indispensable for prediction, and the sestet should show why this indispensability is not a concession but a reason to take the pattern as real. The closing couplet should articulate, in the poem's own terms, the criterion for pattern-realness: that the pattern is compressible and earns its keep by supporting predictions that would be intractable from the physical stance alone. The poem should be accurate enough that someone who knows Dennett's view would recognize it as a rendering of the argument, and it should work as a poem — not merely versified summary.
