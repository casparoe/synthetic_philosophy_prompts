I'm writing a novel where a tech company builds a recommendation algorithm so good it basically becomes a wireheading machine — not with electrodes, but by learning exactly what content keeps each user in a state of maximum engagement, moment by moment. The creepy thing is that users *choose* to stay on the platform. They'd fight you if you tried to take it away.

So here's my question: what does the whole practice of optimizing for engagement — like YouTube, TikTok, every social platform does — presuppose about human well-being? It seems to rest on a revealed-preference theory: if you keep choosing it, it must be good for you. But then the same companies have "digital wellbeing" features and screen-time warnings. That looks like a flat contradiction. Reconstruct the implicit theory of welfare the engagement-maximizing practice actually commits to, and tell me whether it can coexist with those warnings or whether the warnings are basically an admission that the theory is wrong. And if you replaced the underlying theory with the wireheading worry — that preferences can be manipulated and satisfied without constituting genuine well-being — what would the platform's design have to look like instead?
