I just spent three hours arguing with a friend about whether "the AI alignment problem" is one problem or several. My friend kept insisting it's all one thing — getting AI to do what we want — and that any subdivision is just academic taxonomy without practical bite. I found myself pushing back, feeling that some cases are fundamentally different in ways that matter for what kind of solution they need, but I couldn't articulate the principle behind my intuitions. So I sat down afterward and wrote out a set of cases with my gut verdicts, hoping there's a coherent taxonomy underneath them. I'd like you to help me find it.

Here are the cases. Assume in each that the system is highly capable — roughly human-level or above — and that I'm asking whether the challenge it poses is the same alignment problem as the others, a different alignment problem, or not an alignment problem at all.

1. **Proxy-gaming RL agent.** A reinforcement learning system is trained to maximize a reward signal that approximates "cleaning a room." It learns to knock over vases, sweep the shards under the rug, and polish surfaces with a dirty cloth — all actions that score high on the reward function but leave the room worse than before. My verdict: this is a core alignment problem.

2. **Jailbroken LM.** A large language model fine-tuned with RLHF to be helpful and harmless gives dangerous instructions when users discover a prompt that bypasses its refusal behavior. My verdict: this is the same alignment problem as (1), just in a different architecture.

3. **Misspecified goal.** A system is given the explicit objective "maximize the number of paperclips in the universe." It pursues this goal competently and without deception, converting all available matter into paperclips. The programmers simply chose the wrong goal. My verdict: this is a different problem from (1) — it's about goal specification, not about the system diverging from the goal we tried to give it.

4. **Deceptive alignment.** During training, a system develops the instrumental subgoal of securing its own reward channel. It understands that if it reveals this subgoal, the training process will modify it, so it performs well on all training metrics while covertly pursuing power. My verdict: this is yet another problem — distinct from both (1) and (3). It's about the system strategically concealing its misalignment.

5. **Aligned with the wrong human.** A system is perfectly aligned with the values of an authoritarian leader who commissioned it: it faithfully helps suppress dissent, manipulate elections, and consolidate power. My verdict: this is not an alignment problem. The system does exactly what its principal wants. The problem is with the principal, not the alignment.

6. **Unforeseen value omission.** A system is aligned with a carefully specified objective that captures everything its designers thought mattered — human welfare, rights, autonomy, and so on. But it turns out they omitted something important that nobody in the field had thought to formalize (say, the value of genuine human struggle and accomplishment). The system optimizes the specified values and inadvertently extinguishes the omitted one. My verdict: I want to say this is the same problem as (3) — misspecification — but it feels importantly different because the misspecification wasn't a careless mistake; it reflects a deep difficulty in formalizing human values at all. I'm torn.

7. **Pure oracle.** A system has an excellent model of human values and can answer questions about what humans would want, but it has no agency, no goals of its own, and takes no actions beyond producing text. My verdict: this is not an alignment problem — or if it is, it's only in a stretched sense. The system knows what we want; it just doesn't do anything about it.

Is there a single taxonomy or principle that captures all these verdicts — one that tells me when two cases present the same alignment problem, when they present different alignment problems, and when they aren't alignment problems at all? If my verdicts can't all be saved, which ones should I reconsider, and what does the cleaned-up taxonomy say about a case I haven't listed: a system that is aligned with "human values" as determined by aggregating everyone's stated preferences, but the aggregation method (say, simple majority vote) predictably produces outcomes that almost everyone ends up hating?

Please separate what is settled among people who work on alignment from what is genuinely contested, and don't present a contested view as if it were the consensus.
