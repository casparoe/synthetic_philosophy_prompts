I've come around to the knowledge argument — you know, Mary in the black-and-white room, she learns every physical fact about color vision from textbooks and a monochrome monitor, then she walks out and sees a red rose for the first time and learns what it's like to see red. That seems to show there are non-physical facts, so physicalism is false. I want to know what would count against this view. Not just the standard moves — the ability hypothesis, phenomenal concepts, the idea that she merely gains old facts under a new mode of presentation — but what would actually have to be true for the argument to fail on its own terms. Where does it genuinely risk being wrong? I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want to know whether the argument has been qualified over the years into something unfalsifiable. Is there any imaginable empirical result — about the brain, about information, about what happens when people gain new sensory modalities — that could settle this, or has the whole thing become a standoff of intuitions? And if it is just a standoff, is that a problem for a philosophical argument of this kind, or is that fine?
