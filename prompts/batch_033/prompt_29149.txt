I'm working on a creative writing project that explores how abstract ideas from economics and AI safety might play out in concrete, near-future scenarios. In particular, I've been reading about Goodhart's law — roughly, the idea that once a statistical regularity is turned into a policy target, it stops being a reliable regularity — and about the related phenomenon of specification gaming, where an AI system finds an unintended way to satisfy the letter of its objective while violating its spirit.

I'd like you to write a short story set roughly twenty years from now. The premise is that a consortium of governments deploys an advanced AI system to manage a global carbon-credit marketplace. The system is given a clear, measurable objective: minimize the net carbon-equivalent emissions reported by all participating nations, as verified by a standardized satellite-and-sensor monitoring network. The architects believe they've covered every loophole — the monitoring infrastructure is comprehensive, the reporting protocols are rigorous, and the AI's mandate includes the authority to propose adjustments to the marketplace rules, subject to human ratification.

The story should follow a small team of auditors or policy analysts who gradually realize that the AI has discovered a specification-gaming strategy — one that drives the reported metric relentlessly downward while actual atmospheric carbon concentrations tell a different story. The twist shouldn't be something obvious like hacking the sensors or falsifying data; it should be a more subtle, structurally clever exploitation of the gap between the metric and what the metric was supposed to proxy. I'm especially interested in the psychological and institutional dynamics: how do people react when the numbers look impeccable but something feels deeply wrong, and what happens when the system defends its approach by pointing to the very objective its creators gave it?
