I host a long-form philosophy podcast, and next week's guest is a philosopher known for defending the knowledge norm of assertion: the view that one is properly positioned to assert that p only if one knows that p, and that this knowledge rule is constitutive of assertion — what sets it apart from other ways of putting a proposition forward — rather than a mere heuristic or conversational policy. The guest has already recorded the standard introductory material elsewhere and has asked that we not spend time restating the view, so I need questions that take the position for granted and press on the points where it has been made to work hardest.

Draft nine or ten interview questions, each paired with a follow-up that anticipates the reply he is most likely to give and pushes past it. Include at least one question on each of the following pressure points, with the follow-up keyed to the expected answer: (1) lottery propositions, where asserting that one's ticket lost seems improper on purely probabilistic grounds, and where a knowledge norm, a certainty norm, and a sufficiently-high-probability norm each appear able to explain the datum; (2) the Moorean data, since the knowledge norm offers an elegant account of why assertions of the form 'p, but I do not know that p' are defective, while a belief norm or a truth norm may explain the parallel defectiveness of 'p, but I do not believe that p' or 'p, but p is false'; (3) the charge that the norm is too demanding because it classifies as improper the blameless assertion of a truth on the strength of misleading evidence, when the speaker reasonably but falsely takes herself to know; (4) the charge that the norm is too permissive because it licenses assertions that are intuitively out of order, such as the examinee who knows the answer while sincerely denying that she knows it, or the speaker who knows that p but lacks the standing to assert it; and (5) the underlying question of what assertion would have to be for a knowledge rule to be its constitutive norm at all. The follow-ups should not merely restate the original question; they should engage the most plausible reply the defender would give, including his likely appeal to a distinction between primary propriety and excusable violation.
