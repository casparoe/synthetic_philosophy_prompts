I just spent two hours arguing with a friend about the Terri Schiavo case, and I came away convinced that neither of us had a clear grip on what the doing/allowing distinction actually commits us to in a case like that one. You will recall the facts: Schiavo collapsed in 1990 and suffered severe brain damage from oxygen deprivation; she was diagnosed as being in a persistent vegetative state. Her husband, Michael, argued that she would not have wanted to be kept alive by artificial means and sought to have her feeding tube removed. Her parents fought to keep it in. After years of legal battles — including a special law passed by Congress — the feeding tube was removed in 2005 and she died. The public debate was framed almost entirely in terms of killing versus letting die: her parents and their supporters called it murder by starvation; her husband's side called it letting her die naturally.

Analyze the Schiavo case through the lens of the philosophical distinction between doing and allowing harm, and between killing and letting die. I want the careful mapping: what exactly was done, what was allowed, and how do the standard philosophical criteria — withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment, the role of intention, the distinction between an underlying condition and an intervening cause — classify the removal of the feeding tube? Walk through what the major positions in the literature would say about whether this was a killing or a letting-die. Then tell me where the philosophical distinction runs into trouble with the case: does the fact that food and water feel more basic than a ventilator matter philosophically, or is that just psychology? Does the fact that Schiavo was not dying when the tube was removed — she would have lived decades with it — change the analysis? Finally, what does the case reveal about the doing/allowing distinction itself: does it show the distinction is robust but misapplied in public debate, or does it expose a genuine instability in how we draw the line?
