I'm trying to work out what I think about moral worth — when an action deserves moral credit or praise, as opposed to merely being the right thing to do. I've been going by my gut on cases, and I'm not sure my verdicts hang together. Here they are:

(1) A shopkeeper gives a child the correct change. He does it because he knows that shortchanging customers, even children, would damage his reputation and hurt his business. He's honest, but honesty pays. Verdict: his action is right, but it has no moral worth.

(2) A woman visits her elderly mother every week. She does it entirely out of love and affection — it genuinely makes her happy to see her mother, and the thought of not going never crosses her mind. She isn't acting from a sense of duty; she just wants to. Verdict: her action has moral worth, and plenty of it.

(3) A man also visits his elderly mother every week, but he finds it draining and unpleasant. He does it solely because he believes he owes it to her — it's his duty. Verdict: his action has moral worth.

(4) A lawyer takes on a pro bono case. His primary reason is that it will generate good press for his firm. He also genuinely cares about the client's situation, but if there were no press benefit he wouldn't have taken the case. Verdict: his action has little to no moral worth — the self-interested motive seems to crowd out the moral one.

(5) A different lawyer takes on the same kind of pro bono case. Her primary reason is that she cares about the client. She's also aware it will look good for the firm, and she'd be lying if she said that played no role at all. But she would have taken the case even without the press benefit. Verdict: her action has moral worth, maybe slightly diminished by the mixed motive but still substantial.

(6) A man returns a lost wallet to its owner. He does it because he believes there's a reward posted for its return. There is in fact no reward — the poster he saw was for a different wallet. Verdict: no moral worth. He was trying to earn a reward, not to help the owner.

(7) Huck Finn helps Jim escape from slavery. Huck believes, sincerely and painfully, that he is doing something wrong — that he is stealing property and will go to hell for it. He does it anyway because he can't bring himself to betray his friend. Verdict: his action has moral worth. In fact it seems to have more moral worth than if he had helped Jim while confidently believing slavery was wrong — there's something about the cost to his conscience that makes it especially admirable.

(8) A donor gives a large sum to an effective charity. She does it because she wants to help strangers in need. But she also knows herself well enough to admit that if helping strangers didn't give her a warm glow, she probably wouldn't do it. Verdict: I'm genuinely stuck on this one. Part of me says the warm glow doesn't undermine the moral worth — it's not like she's doing it for a tax break or social approval. Another part says that if the feeling is what ultimately motivates her, then her action isn't really about the strangers at all.

Is there a principle — or a small set of candidate principles — that captures these verdicts? Which of my verdicts, if any, does the principle force me to abandon? And what does it say about case (8) and about other cases I haven't thought of?
