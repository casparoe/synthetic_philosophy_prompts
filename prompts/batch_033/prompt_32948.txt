Explain the paradox of phenomenal judgement for epiphenomenalism about qualia. Does the paradox give us a good reason to reject epiphenomenalism?

And here's the student's answer, lightly tidied:

Epiphenomenalism is the view that qualia are real but have no causal powers: physical states cause qualia, but qualia never cause anything physical. The paradox of phenomenal judgement is that if this is right, then our judgements about qualia — for instance my judgement that I'm in pain — are caused entirely by physical processes, not by the pain itself. But then the fact that those judgements happen to be about qualia at all looks like a massive coincidence, and we seem to lose our only reason for thinking we know about our own experiences. In fact the view looks self-contradictory here, because it has to say both that qualia cause our judgements about them and that they don't. I think the paradox is fatal: if physical causes fully explain our reports, then the reports can't be about qualia, so epiphenomenalism can't even state its own position coherently.
