I am a graduate student in a seminar cross-listed between philosophy and Buddhist studies, and our professor—who works on the intersection of Madhyamaka metaphysics and contemporary Anglophone personal identity theory—has spent the last several weeks giving us her verdicts on a series of puzzle cases. She is explicit that she speaks from a broadly Buddhist framework: she holds that there is no substantial, enduring self (anatta), that persons are conventionally designated on the basis of the five aggregates in causal continuity, and that talk of "same person" is always a practical, conventional matter rather than a report of ultimate metaphysical fact. At the same time, she resists simply deflating every question into "there is no self so the question is misguided." She gives specific, discriminating verdicts about when conventional personhood continues and when it does not, and she seems to be running a substantive principle rather than mere quietism.

Here are the verdicts she has stated in class. (1) Destructive teletransportation: a person is scanned, disintegrated, and an exact replica constructed on Mars from new matter, with all memories and dispositions intact. She says the Mars person is not the same person—even conventionally—because the destruction of the original body severs the causal chain that conventional designation tracks. (2) Normal aging from five to eighty-five: she says this is conventionally the same person; the gradual, unbroken causal continuity of the psychophysical aggregates underpins the continued conventional designation. (3) Whole-brain transplant into a new donor body: she says the person goes with the brain; conventionally it is the same person, because the brain carries the relevant causal and psychological continuity and the body was never essential to who the person is. (4) Complete irreversible amnesia with a resulting radical personality change following a brain lesion: she says this is conventionally the same person, though she acknowledges that the case strains the notion. Her reasoning is that the biological organism and its causal processes continue unbroken, and that legal and social practices would still treat this as the same human being. (5) A person falls into dreamless sleep and wakes: trivially the same person; temporary suspension of consciousness does not interrupt the causal continuity any more than a river ceases to be the same river when it flows briefly underground.

The new case she has assigned us to think about for next week is this: a patient undergoes a procedure in which, over the course of eighteen months, each neuron in their brain is replaced one by one with a functionally identical silicon neuron. At every stage of the process the patient is fully conscious, reports feeling like themselves, recognizes loved ones, and retains all memories, preferences, and personality traits. At the end of the procedure the brain is entirely silicon, but the patient's psychology is indistinguishable from before. What should we predict she will say about whether this patient is conventionally the same person at the end of the procedure? I am less interested in a bare yes or no than in identifying the candidate rules or principles that fit all five of her earlier verdicts, explaining what each rule predicts about the new case, giving a best-guess prediction with reasons for preferring one rule over the others, and naming at least one further hypothetical case that would more decisively tell the competing rules apart—especially one that isolates the variable doing the real work here.
