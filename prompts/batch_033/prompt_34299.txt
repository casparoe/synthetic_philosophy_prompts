I am a philosopher who has spent the better part of a decade working on the ethics of consent — what makes it valid, what vitiates it, whether it is best understood as a mental act or a communicative performance, and whether the standards ought to vary across domains like medicine, sex, and contract. Recently I have been collaborating with a colleague in computer security whose research concerns formal verification of authentication and authorization protocols. Our conversations have forced me to restate my claims in his vocabulary, and I have found the exercise more productive — and more destabilizing — than I expected.

I would like you to carry this translation out systematically and more thoroughly than I have managed on my own. Take the following threads from the philosophical literature on consent and restate each in the vocabulary of protocol design and security analysis: authentication, authorization, non-repudiation, coercion resistance, side channels, trusted computing bases, revocation, capabilities versus access control lists, and whatever else from that toolkit proves useful.

The threads are these. First, the deception problem: to what extent does being misled or kept ignorant about the nature, risks, or stakes of an action invalidate consent, and does it matter whether the deception was intentional? Second, the coercion problem: when does pressure, threat, or asymmetric power render consent invalid, and how do we distinguish coercion from mere hard choices? Third, the competence problem: what cognitive and volitional capacities must an agent possess for their consent to count, and how do we handle borderline cases like children, intoxication, or cognitive impairment? Fourth, the ontology of consent: is consent a mental state — a decision or an attitude — or a communicative act that alters normative relations, and why does this matter for cases of unexpressed willingness or expressed but insincere agreement? Fifth, the domain-specificity question: are the validity conditions for consent uniform, or do they shift when we move from a medical context to a sexual one to a contractual one — and if they shift, what explains the variation?

For each thread, I want three things: what translates cleanly into the security vocabulary, what translates only with distortion or loss, and what has no counterpart at all. Then I want the residue report. Does the untranslatable residue reveal genuine normative content that the security framework is constitutively unable to capture — something about moral permissibility that authorization logic simply does not model? Or does it expose places where the philosophical debate has been trading on ambiguities that a formal framework would not tolerate? I am particularly curious about the mental-state versus communicative-act debate: does it map onto any distinction security researchers already draw — say, between an internal authorization policy and a signed message, or between belief and assertion in a protocol — or does it point to something that neither field has adequately theorized?
