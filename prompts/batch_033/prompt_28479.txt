In most democratic legal systems, a private citizen who orders someone to be seized, detained without charge, and subjected to coercive interrogation would face serious criminal charges. Yet when senior executive officials authorize essentially the same acts as part of national security policy, criminal prosecution is rare, and the response to public exposure is typically a legislative inquiry, a truth commission, or a political reckoning — not a trial. This asymmetry is not an oversight; it is embedded in the legal architecture of the modern state.

What theory of moral responsibility does this practice presuppose? In particular: does it presuppose that the same act changes its moral character when performed in a political role, or that political actors are subject to a different kind of accountability altogether? And is the practice consistent with the fact that the same legal systems do prosecute political leaders for corruption and self-dealing — drawing a line between dirty-hands wrongdoing and self-interested wrongdoing? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say whether the line it draws is philosophically defensible.
