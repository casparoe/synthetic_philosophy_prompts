A recent economics blog summarized the socialist calculation debate as follows: "Mises and Hayek argued that socialist planning is impossible in principle, because no central planner could solve the millions of simultaneous equations needed to keep an economy's supply and demand in balance. Lange answered that a Central Planning Board did not need to solve them: it could simply announce provisional prices, adjust them by trial and error—raising prices where goods were in short supply and lowering them where there were surpluses—and in this way reach exactly the equilibrium a competitive market would reach. The economics profession largely took Lange to have settled the debate in favor of market socialism, though the later failure of Soviet central planning is often described as vindicating Mises and Hayek after all." The last sentence puzzles me, since it seems to reverse the earlier verdict, and I do not know the debate well enough to tell where the summary has gone wrong. Which parts of this account are faithful to the historical positions, which conflate distinct arguments or otherwise oversimplify, and does the Soviet experience actually support the Austrian conclusion in the way the blog implies? In particular, I would like to know what a careful defender of Lange would object to and what Hayek would object to in this characterization.
