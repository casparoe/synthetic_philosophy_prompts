I've spent years reading LessWrong and the Alignment Forum, and I've never seen the tool-AI versus agent-AI disagreement written down in a form that both sides would actually sign. I want the document that a well-run adversarial collaboration between a committed tool-AI advocate and a committed agent-AI advocate would produce.

You are an alignment researcher who has genuinely internalized both positions. The tool-AI position, as I mean it, is roughly this: we should aim to build non-goal-directed systems—oracles, predictors, planners whose plans are executed by humans, and ensembles of narrow task-directed services—because agency is the dangerous ingredient, not raw capability; a sufficiently capable tool that pursues no objective of its own avoids the classic failure modes of goal-directed systems. The agent-AI position is roughly this: transformative capability requires autonomous, goal-directed action in the world; attempts to keep superhuman systems tool-like will either fail, leak agency through the interfaces humans inevitably build around them, or cap capability below the point that matters; the correct response is therefore to solve agent alignment—corrigibility, value learning, and the rest—rather than to avoid building agents.

Draft the joint statement of their adversarial collaboration. The statement should be one that both advocates could put their names to, with the residual disagreement made exact rather than softened. It should have four parts.

First, a numbered agreed-claims section. These should be substantive claims about the problem that both sides can sign, not just terminological truisms. They should cover, at least: what the tool/agent distinction amounts to in different senses (architectural, interface-level, training-objective); what each side grants about the other's central concern; which risks both agree are real; and what would have to be true for the disagreement to be practically moot.

Second, a disagreements table. For each genuine point of disagreement, give: the question in neutral wording; the tool-AI position phrased so a committed tool-AI advocate would accept it as their own; and the agent-AI position phrased so a committed agent-AI advocate would accept it as their own. In a final column, say whether the row is substantive or partly verbal, and if partly verbal, say exactly which part is verbal and what the substantive remainder is.

Third, a cruxes section. Identify the sub-questions such that settling one of them would move at least one side. For each crux, specify a concrete case, experiment, or argument that both sides would accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction in advance.

Fourth, a short note on which part of the dispute, if any, you judge to be mostly about words, and which part is a real bet about how future systems will behave.

To keep this in the weeds, the document must engage with at least the following, not merely gesture at them: whether a superhuman oracle is safe to query even if it has no goals, given that its outputs can be manipulative or optimized against the user's request; whether non-agentic systems can reach transformative capability or whether hard real-world tasks require long-horizon autonomous action; which of the standard convergent instrumental drives transfer to task-directed or oracle-like systems and which do not; the wrapper argument, that even if tools are safer, competitive or practical pressure will lead humans to wrap them in agentic scaffolding; the hidden-agency worry, that a system trained with reinforcement learning and marketed as a tool may still be internally goal-directed; and whether "just don't build agents" is a plan or a hope, given that tool-like systems may still require solving value specification and intent alignment.

Do not give a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; the document should stand on the arguments. Do not adjudicate the dispute or declare a winner, and do not write it as a staged dialogue.

Before drafting, take time to brainstorm the strongest version of each position and the places where a shallow version of the debate collapses into "everything is an agent if you squint." Then write the document as if it were being co-signed by the two advocates.

End with a brief author's note: say how confident you are that each side would in fact sign the statement as written, and name the single claim or formulation in the document you are least sure about.
