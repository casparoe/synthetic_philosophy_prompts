Two people in my philsophy of language seminar got into it last week about expressivism and I've been turning it over ever since. They were arguing about whether the Frege-Geach problem is still a live issue.

Dan said it's basically a knockout: if moral claims just express attitudes, then you can't explain what's going on when moral language shows up embedded in conditionals. Take "If lying is wrong, then getting your brother to lie is wrong." The speaker isn't expressing disapproval of lying in the antecedent—they're entertaining a hypothetical. So the meaning of "wrong" seems to shift between asserted and unasserted contexts. That's a compositionality failure, end of story.

Mira said Dan was about thirty years behind. Blackburn showed you can treat the conditional as expressing a higher-order attitude: an attitude of disapproval toward the combination of disapproving of lying and not disapproving of getting your brother to lie. So "wrong" means the same thing throughout—it's just that the whole complex sentence expresses a more complicated attitude. No shift in meaning, no problem.

Dan said okay, but then modus ponens breaks. If "If murder is wrong, then abortion is wrong" and "murder is wrong" are both just attitudes, then what makes the inference to "abortion is wrong" logically valid? You need truth conditions to underwrite logical consequence. Without them, anything goes.

Mira said that's question-begging. Blackburn's whole move is to redefine validity in terms of clashes of attitudes, not truth-preservation. An inference is valid if you can't accept the premises and reject the conclusion without your attitudes clashing. And Blackburn shows there's a clash in the modus ponens case: approving of the conditional, approving of the antecedent, and disapproving of the consequent is an incoherent combination of attitudes. That's the constraint—it's not just "anything goes."

Dan said that's not logic, it's psychology. You're telling me what attitudes I can coherently hold, not what follows from what. The whole point of logic is that it's topic-neutral and attitude-independent.

Mira said that's the point Dan keeps missing: expressivism is a revisionary theory of moral language. Once you give up the idea that moral claims are truth-apt, you should expect the logic to look different. But it still constrains reasoning—incoherent combinations of attitudes are still something you should avoid, and that's normativity enough.

Then Dan shifted ground. He said: fine, but what about mixed inferences? "If the cat is on the mat, then murder is wrong" is a mixed sentence with a descriptive antecedent and a moral consequent. What's the attitude there? You can't just say it's a higher-order attitude about combinations of descriptive beliefs and moral attitudes, because then you've got to explain how logical vocabulary works across the descriptive/normative divide without collapsing the distinction.

Mira admitted mixed sentences are the hardest case but said both Gibbard and later Blackburn have accounts. Gibbard's norm-expressivism treats all normative language as expressing plans, and the logic of plans handles mixed contexts by treating factual-normative worlds as the objects of planning states. She said Dan was cherry-picking the earliest version of the view and acting like nobody's worked on it since 1984.

Dan said Gibbard's solution is basically to build truth conditions back in through the back door—if you're treating normative statements as expressing plans plus factual beliefs with representational content, you're not really an expressivist anymore, you're a hybrid theorist.

That's where they left it. Neither convinced the other. I'm not sure who's right. Can you go through their exchange and adjudicate each move? Who has the better of which specific arguments, and what, if anything, are both of them missing?
