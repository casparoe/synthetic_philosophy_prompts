**Question:**

Explain the Allais paradox. In your explanation, state the independence axiom of expected utility theory precisely, show how the Allais choice pattern violates it, and discuss what implications this has for the normative standing of expected utility theory.

---

**Student's answer:**

The Allais paradox is a famous challenge to expected utility theory introduced by Maurice Allais in 1953. It presents two pairwise choices between lotteries over monetary outcomes. In the first choice, subjects are asked to choose between:

- Option A: A guaranteed $1,000,000.
- Option B: A 10% chance of $5,000,000, an 89% chance of $1,000,000, and a 1% chance of nothing.

In the second choice, subjects choose between:

- Option C: An 11% chance of $1,000,000 and an 89% chance of nothing.
- Option D: A 10% chance of $5,000,000 and a 90% chance of nothing.

Empirically, most people choose A over B and D over C. This pattern violates the independence axiom of expected utility theory. The independence axiom (also known as Savage's sure-thing principle) states that if an agent prefers lottery X to lottery Y, then for any lottery Z and any probability p strictly between 0 and 1, the agent should prefer the compound lottery [pX + (1 − p)Z] to [pY + (1 − p)Z].

To see the violation, notice that both choice problems can be decomposed to reveal a common component. In the first choice, both A and B share an 89% chance of $1,000,000. If we factor this out, the choice between A and B reduces to a choice between a sure $1,000,000 (the remaining 11% of A) and a 10/11 chance of $5,000,000 versus a 1/11 chance of nothing (the remaining 11% of B). In the second choice, both C and D share an 89% chance of nothing, and after factoring this out, the remaining 11% of C is a sure $1,000,000 while the remaining 11% of D is the same 10/11 chance of $5,000,000 and 1/11 chance of nothing. So the two choices are, in their non-common parts, identical. The independence axiom therefore requires that a person who prefers A to B must also prefer C to D, but the Allais pattern has A preferred to B and D preferred to C.

The implications of the Allais paradox are severe for expected utility theory. It demonstrates that expected utility theory is descriptively false — people do not in fact obey its axioms. More importantly, it shows that expected utility theory fails as a normative theory of rational choice. If rational people, including sophisticated decision theorists, systematically violate the independence axiom upon reflection, then the axiom cannot be a requirement of rationality. The paradox thus undermines the normative foundations of expected utility theory and motivates the search for alternative theories such as prospect theory and rank-dependent utility theory.
