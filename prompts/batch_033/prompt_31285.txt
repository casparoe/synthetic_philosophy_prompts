I work for a public body that decides which treatments for chronic conditions receive public funding. We currently use a standard quality-adjusted life-year framework, but it repeatedly lets large expected gains in positive well-being outweigh the relief of very severe pain, and several of our clinicians argue that this is wrong in principle. Please write a decision procedure, grounded in lexical threshold negative utilitarianism and in the debate about whether pleasure and pain are commensurable, that my assessment team could actually run on a treatment proposal. The team has no philosophical training, meets weekly, and has about an hour per proposal. The procedure should set out the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a no, and where it must hand the decision to a person because the step is a value judgement rather than a factual one. Then run the procedure on three proposals: a treatment that relieves moderate chronic pain while producing only modest improvements in enjoyment; a treatment that produces large gains in mild positive well-being for many patients but cannot relieve their severe episodic pain; and a treatment that relieves very severe pain for a few patients but would require withdrawing several interventions that produce moderate pleasure for many. Mark clearly the step at which your procedure returns an answer the team may not like, and state what the procedure cannot settle. Please write the answer in British English.
