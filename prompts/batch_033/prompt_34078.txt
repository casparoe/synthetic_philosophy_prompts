In a philosophy of mind course, the instructor introduced thought experiments by calling them "the philosopher's laboratory." The analogy, as she developed it, runs like this: just as a scientist isolates a variable in a controlled lab setup to test a causal hypothesis, a philosopher constructs a hypothetical scenario that isolates a conceptual factor — stripping away confounds — to test a claim about knowledge, personhood, or moral permissibility. On this view, far-fetched thought experiments (brain-in-a-vat, teletransportation, utility monsters, fission cases) are not a bug but a feature: they are like particle accelerators or ultracentrifuges, deliberately extreme conditions that reveal underlying structure you could never see in ordinary circumstances. She used the analogy to preempt the common complaint that such scenarios are too bizarre to tell us anything. If you dismiss them on grounds of weirdness, she said, you are like someone who dismisses experimental data because the lab setup doesn't look like everyday life.

I find the analogy rhetorically effective, but I suspect it is doing more persuasive work than it can bear. Can you audit it systematically? I would like: (a) a table of correspondences — what in the thought-experiment practice is supposed to map onto what in laboratory science (the scenario, the intuition elicited, the target phenomenon, the controls, the equipment, the replication, etc.); (b) the disanalogies, with a clear separation between those that are merely superficial and those that threaten the argumentative work the analogy is being asked to do; (c) an assessment of whether the conclusion — that bizarre thought experiments are as epistemically legitimate as specialized lab equipment — survives once the disanalogies are on the table; and (d) if the analogy ultimately misleads, what a better comparison would be, and what that alternative comparison implies about how much weight a thought experiment can carry.
