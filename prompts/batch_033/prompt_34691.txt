I keep noticing a pattern in EA and rationalist discussions about the far future and I don't know if it has a name. Someone argues we should prioritize reducing extinction risk because if humanity survives there could be trillions of future lives, and losing all that value would be an astronomical waste. Then someone else points out the same reasoning cuts the other way: if we're not sure the future will be net positive, then the sheer number of future lives multiplies the possible downside too, and we might actually have an astronomical reason to prevent the future from happening — or at least to focus on making sure it's good rather than just making sure it happens. The structure seems to be: you take an argument that uses "enormous number of future lives" to justify priority X, and then someone points out the enormous number is symmetric between positive and negative scenarios, so the argument doesn't pick out X unless you've already assumed the sign. I see this move made in a bunch of places — not just about extinction risk, but also about AI alignment, whether to accelerate growth, etc. Is there an established name for this specific argumentative move or the symmetry it points to? And if there is, how precisely is it defined — does it require that the original arguer actually overlooked the symmetry, or does it also apply when they're aware of it but think the sign is clear? If there's no name, what would you call it and how would you draw the boundaries? Also tell me how confident you are in your answer and what the point you're least sure about is.
