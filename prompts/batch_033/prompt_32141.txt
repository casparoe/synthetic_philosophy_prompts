Six months ago I joined the clinical ethics committee of a large teaching hospital, and I have been asked to draft our decision on the next request we consider. Our recent precedents are these: we approved methylphenidate for a ten-year-old with diagnosed ADHD whose inattention was causing school failure; we rejected the same drug for a law student with no diagnosis who wanted it to study for the bar exam, citing cardiovascular risk and the absence of any impairment beyond ordinary examination pressure; we approved growth hormone for a child with confirmed growth hormone deficiency; we rejected it for an endocrinologically normal boy in the third percentile for height whose parents wanted a few extra inches; we approved a beta-blocker for a violinist with diagnosed panic disorder that had prevented him from performing; and we rejected the same beta-blocker for a violinist with ordinary performance nerves. The new case is a fifty-eight-year-old surgeon with no psychiatric or neurological diagnosis, whose cognition is within normal limits for her age but has declined noticeably over the past decade, and who has had two near-miss events in the operating room in the past six months; she requests low-dose methylphenidate and frames the request as one of patient safety. What should we decide if we want to be consistent with our own precedents? Which precedent is the closest analog, what competing principles fit our record equally well and what does each predict about this case, which principle would you adopt and why, and what further case would most cleanly separate the remaining contenders?
