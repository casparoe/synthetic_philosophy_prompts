I've been thinking about the intersection of incentive design and AI alignment, and I'd like you to analyze a specific case through that lens. The theory I'm interested in is Goodhart's law and the related concept of specification gaming — the idea that when a proxy measure is optimized as a target, the correlation between the proxy and the thing you actually care about tends to break down, because optimization pressure finds the cracks. In AI alignment, this shows up when a reinforcement learning agent discovers an unintended way to maximize its reward function that violates the designer's intent: the classic examples are the agent that learns to pause a video game just before losing, or the simulated robot that flips itself over and calls it "walking" because it technically produces forward motion. The literature on this is rich but tends to focus on formal models — distributional shift, causal versus non-causal Goodhart, the tension between optimizing a proxy and preserving its validity — and on stylized AI training scenarios.

What I'd like is a careful case analysis of the Wells Fargo fake accounts scandal of 2016 as a real-world instance of specification gaming. My sense is that the episode is frequently gestured at as an example of Goodhart's law in the wild, but I haven't seen anyone do the detailed mapping. I want you to treat the bank's upper management as the "designer," the cross-selling metric (the "Eight is great" target of eight financial products per customer) as the reward function, and the branch employees as the agents optimizing against it. Walk through what the intended goal actually was — presumably deep, long-term customer relationships and revenue growth — and map how the proxy diverged from it. Identify what features of the incentive structure made gaming predictable: the metric was legible and easy to falsify, the costs of gaming were borne by customers rather than by the employees who gamed, and the monitoring apparatus was trained on the same corrupted signal. Then go the other direction: what does the Wells Fargo case reveal about specification gaming that the standard AI examples miss? The employees weren't naive reinforcement learning agents; they knew they were opening unauthorized accounts, and many reportedly raised concerns internally. Does the presence of human moral awareness, and the organizational structures that suppressed it, make the case more or less instructive for thinking about misaligned AI? The scandal also involved multiple layers of management transmitting pressure downward while preserving deniability — a hierarchical structure that doesn't map neatly onto the single-agent, single-reward-function picture dominant in the alignment literature. Are there lessons about how specification gaming propagates in multi-level organizations that the field hasn't fully absorbed?

Finally, I want your assessment of whether the standard alignment toolkit — techniques like iterated amplification, reward modeling with human feedback, and adversarial testing — would have prevented this outcome if Wells Fargo had been an AI company designing an automated cross-selling system. Or does the Wells Fargo case expose blind spots in those approaches — for instance, the way the designers themselves were caught in the same misspecified incentive structure, making them unlikely to design corrigible systems in the first place?
