I've been reading the socialist calculation debate for years — Mises, Hayek, Lange, the post-Soviet reassessments, the recent work on algorithmic planning — and I've reached a point where I suspect the debate isn't moving because the two sides are not actually disagreeing about the same thing, even though they appear to be. Let me lay out what I see.

Mises's original claim was that without private ownership of the means of production, there are no markets for capital goods, hence no money prices for them, hence no way to compare the costs of alternative production methods. Hayek shifted the ground: the problem is not that the equations are unsolvable but that the data aren't given — the knowledge required is dispersed across millions of agents, much of it is tacit and local, and the market process itself discovers information that no one had beforehand. Lange answered the Mises version by describing a Central Planning Board that sets provisional prices, observes shortages and surpluses, and adjusts iteratively — essentially a Walrasian auctioneer for the whole economy. On Hayek's version, Lange had less to say, though later defenders of planning have argued that modern computation, machine learning, and ubiquitous data collection change the picture.

Here's my worry. When I read Mises and Hayek carefully, they seem to be making a claim about what a price is: a price is not just a number that clears a market; it is an emergent summary of decentralized acts of exchange, and it carries information precisely because it arises from agents acting on their own local knowledge with their own property at stake. When I read Lange and his successors, they seem to be answering a different question: can a central authority compute an allocation that is Pareto-efficient and clears markets? If that's right, then the two sides are using the word "price" for different things — one for a signal generated by a particular institutional process, the other for a scarcity index that could in principle be computed any number of ways. And if that's right, the debate hasn't stalled because it's hard; it's stalled because it was never a single debate.

But I'm not sure my diagnosis is correct. Maybe the disagreement is empirical: could a planning board actually gather and process the relevant information at the speed and granularity required, or would the attempt collapse under its own complexity? Maybe it's about incentives: even if computation were adequate, would managers in a planned economy have the motivation to use information truthfully and efficiently? Or maybe it's a disagreement about the burden of proof — Mises thought he had proved planning impossible a priori, while Lange thought he only needed to show it was possible in principle, and they never agreed on what would settle the question.

I want your diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is, supported by what each side has actually argued. And I want an honest answer about what would count as progress: a distinction that dissolves the apparent conflict, an empirical test that could actually be run, a formal result that closes a loophole, or an acknowledgment that the two sides are doing different things and should stop pretending otherwise.
