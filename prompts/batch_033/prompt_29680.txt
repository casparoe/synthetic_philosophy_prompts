So there's this thing that's been bugging me about advance directives — you know, living wills, DNRs, the forms where you lay out what treatments you want if you lose capacity down the line. The whole practice is built on the idea that my past self gets to bind my future self, even when my future self might have totally different values or might not even be capable of having preferences at all.

Here's what feels inconsistant to me. In basically every other area of life, we let people change their minds. If I sign a contract to sell my house and then get cold feet, there are penalties but no one drags me to the closing at gunpoint. If I join a religion at 20 and leave it at 40, nobody says "ah but your 20-year-old self committed you." Even marriage, which is literally a lifelong vow, lets people divorce. So what is it about medical decisions that makes my earlier self a dictator over my later, incapacitated self?

And it gets weirder with dementia. There are these well-known cases where someone writes an advance directive saying "if I get dementia and stop recognizing my family, withhold antibiotics and let me die of pneumonia," but then when they actually have dementia, they seem content — they enjoy chocolate pudding, they smile at visitors, they have a quality of life their earlier self either didn't anticipate or actively discounted. And yet the standard advance-directive paradigm says: too bad, your earlier self already decided. What view of personal identity makes that make sense? Is it that the severely demented person isn't "really" the same person as the one who signed the form? Or is it that the earlier, competent self has some special authority that the later, incompetent self simply lacks? And if it's the second one, why doesn't the same logic apply to someone who's drunk, or in the grip of new love, or temporarily not thinking straight — we don't let those people's earlier selves bind them forever either.

I guess my main question is: what philosophical theory of personal identity, and what theory of autonomy or authority, does the whole advance-directive framework presuppose? Is it committed to a particular view, or is the practice compatible with several different theories that just happen to converge on the same policy recommendation? And if we swapped out the presupposed view for a different one — say, a view where the self is constantly being re-written by later experience — what would actually have to change about how advance directives work in practice?

I'm a medical resident, not a philosopher. I feel like a bioethicist or someone who works on personal identity would see the hidden assumptions here more clearly than my attending does. So think like a philosopher of medicine for this. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — what view the practice actually presupposes — and then walk me through the reasoning and the alternatives. No preamble, no restating the question. Really think through the candidates before committing; if it helps, brainstorm a couple different reconstructions of what the practice might be assuming, and then say which one does the best job of making sense of the law as it actually stands and where the others would force changes.
