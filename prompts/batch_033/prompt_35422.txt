I'm part of a political philosophy reading group that's been going through the "equality of what?" literature — Dworkin, Sen, Nussbaum, Arneson, G.A. Cohen, the whole gang. We've got two members who've been at it for months: one's a committed resourcist in the Dworkinian vein (thinks egalitarian justice is about equalizing the bundle of resources people have, with an envy test and hypothetical insurance markets doing the work), and the other's a capabilities theorist (thinks resources are just means, and what matters is what people can actually do and be — their real freedoms). They've finally agreed to try an adversarial collaboration instead of just arguing past each other every week.

I want you to draft the document they'd produce. It should have:

(1) A numbered list of claims both can sign — not just platitudes like "inequality matters," but substantive claims about the structure of the problem, the inadequacy of simple GDP metrics, the fact that both resources and capabilities are relevant at some level, and anything else their traditions genuinely share.

(2) A disagreements table. Each row should state a single point of disagreement, with the resourcist's position and the capabilities theorist's position each phrased in wording their own camp would accept. No strawmanning. If there are disagreements that turn out to be merely verbal once you get the phrasings right, flag those and explain why.

(3) The cruxes: the sub-questions such that if you settled them, at least one side would have to budge. For each crux, describe a concrete case or kind of evidence both would agree bears on it, and record what each predicts. (I suspect one crux is about whether the envy test actually captures what matters about disadvantage — the capabilities person thinks you can envy-free allocate resources and still have someone who can't participate in social life — and another is about whether capabilities can be measured or operationalized without smuggling in resource metrics through the back door.)

(4) Finally, if there's anything both agree would actually change someone's mind — a real empirical finding, a conceptual argument — note it. If they conclude that some part of their dispute is unlikely to be resolved by anything short of a clash of fundamental intuitions, say that too.

Make it feel like a document both could actually sign — not a compromise that papers over differences, but a clear map of where they agree, where they don't, and what it would take to move forward.
