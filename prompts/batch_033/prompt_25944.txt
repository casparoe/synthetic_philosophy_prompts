Write a Petrarchan sonnet that renders Augustine's definition of lying as *duplex cor*—the double heart—not as a topic but as the poem's own structural principle. The octave should consist of two quatrains, each giving voice to a different speaker: the first quatrain from someone who says something factually false but believes it to be true (and therefore, on Augustine's account, does not lie), and the second quatrain from someone who says something factually true but believes it to be false and intends to deceive (and therefore does lie). The volta, entering with the sestet, should turn inward and expose the hidden heart of each speaker—what they hold in the mind versus what they deliver with the lips—so that the reader sees the moral weight falling entirely on the division within the second speaker. The sestet’s rhyme scheme must depart from the octave's in a way that formally registers the shift from outer utterance to inner duplicity. The poem should be precise enough that a scholar of Augustine would recognize the core distinction of *De mendacio*—that a lie is judged not by the verity of the thing said but by the speaker's will to deceive—while standing on its own as a poem for someone who has never read the treatise. No overt lecture; let the contrast between the two quatrains and the disclosure in the sestet do the philosophical work.
