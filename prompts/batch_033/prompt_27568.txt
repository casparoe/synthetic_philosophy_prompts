I write a column on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am working on a piece that reads Christopher Nolan's Memento (2000) through the philosophical debate over advance directives and the authority of the earlier self. The film follows Leonard Shelby, a man with anterograde amnesia who cannot form new long-term memories and who navigates his life by means of Polaroids, handwritten notes, and tattoos he has left for himself as standing instructions. The bioethical debate concerns whether the wishes a competent person records for a future self who may no longer share her values, most commonly a person with advanced dementia, should bind that later self, and on what grounds. The parallel is almost too exact: Leonard's notes and tattoos function as advance directives, and the man following them is a later self deciding, over and over, whether to obey an earlier self whose reasons he cannot retrieve.

But I do not want the column merely to announce the parallel. I want a reading that works in both directions and that is willing to say where the analogy strains. Read the film through the problem of the earlier self's authority. Where does the film genuinely illuminate the philosophical issue, for example what it is like to be governed by instructions whose authorship one must take on faith, or the difference between obeying a directive because it is yours and obeying it because it is written on your skin? Where does it complicate the standard positions, given that the film's earlier self can be mistaken about his own past, can have his directives falsified by others, and in the film's final revelation deliberately sets his later self on a course built on a falsehood? And where does the comparison between Leonard's condition and the dementia cases at the center of the bioethics debate break down, and do those disanalogies damage the reading or make it more illuminating?

Close with an assessment of what the film contributes to the philosophical argument that the standard cases do not, and what the philosophical debate grasps that the film's thought experiment is tempted to overstate. The column should be rigorous enough for a reader who knows the bioethics literature and self-contained enough for a reader who has only seen the film. Please write the response in American English.
