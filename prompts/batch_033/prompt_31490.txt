I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, but I've ended up thinking about merit and desert because of promotion committees, performance reviews, and equity grants at my company. I went through a set of cases and wrote down my gut reactions. Now I'm trying to figure out whether there's a coherent principle behind them, or whether I need to revise some of my verdicts.

I'd like you to approach this as a professional philosopher working through a problem in reflective equilibrium. Start by identifying the tensions among my verdicts, then work toward the best principle (or small set of principles) that captures as many of them as possible, and be honest about which verdicts it cannot accommodate.

Here are my verdicts:

1. Alice works 55-hour weeks and is always available on Slack; her code is mediocre and bug-prone. Bob works 30 hours, coasts visibly, but his code is clean and reliable. They're up for the same promotion: Bob deserves it more. The output matters more than the effort.

2. Carol went to Stanford with every advantage. Dave taught himself to code on a secondhand laptop while working retail. Both are equally productive engineers now. My gut says Dave deserves more admiration for getting here — but I don't think he deserves a higher salary or a faster promotion because of his background. That feels like it should be consistent, but I can't see how.

3. Elena designed the architecture that made a new feature possible. Frank implemented it and got the public recognition. Elena deserves at least as much credit as Frank, but Frank deserves the public recognition. I'm not sure these can both be true.

4. Grace joined a startup two months before the acquisition and got a $400,000 payout. Henry was employee #3, built the core product over four years, but burned out and left six months before the acquisition — he got nothing. Henry clearly deserves more than Grace. The unfairness is in the system, not Grace's fault, but I still feel Henry was robbed.

5. Irene chose a framework after careful research, expert consultation, and a pilot. It failed because of a subtle flaw that only emerged under load. Jack picked whatever was trending on Hacker News and it worked perfectly. For the next high-stakes project, Irene deserves it over Jack. The quality of the decision matters more than the outcome.

6. Kevin hit all his OKRs by picking safe, incremental tasks. Lisa missed hers because she spent the quarter on a high-risk architecture migration the team badly needed. Lisa deserves the higher performance rating — but if we reward people for missing targets, we undermine the whole point of having targets.

Is there a unified account of desert that makes sense of these verdicts? If not, which of my verdicts do I need to give up, and what does the best available principle say about a case I haven't listed: someone who succeeds by taking credit for other people's work?
