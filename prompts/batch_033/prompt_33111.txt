In my seminar on future contingents we reconstructed the sea-battle argument, and I find its conclusion intolerable but cannot yet say which step fails. The argument runs as follows. (1) Every proposition is either true or false at every time, including propositions about the future. (2) Take the proposition that there will be a sea battle tomorrow. Suppose it is true now. Then it is already the case that the sea battle will occur; its truth is a fact about how things now stand, not something still to be settled. (3) If it is already the case that the sea battle will occur, then the battle is now unavoidable: nothing anyone does from now until tomorrow can bring it about that it does not occur. (4) The same reasoning runs if the proposition is false, and it applies to every other future-tensed proposition. (5) Therefore every future event is now unavoidable. (6) But some future events are contingent, and what we deliberate about and do can make a difference to what happens. So one of these steps must go. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would most like to keep bivalence and the contingency of at least some future events, but I suspect that keeping both forces a distinction I have not yet articulated. Please answer in American English as a numbered list of exactly five points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
