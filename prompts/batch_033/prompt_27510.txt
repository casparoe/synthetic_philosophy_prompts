I'm taking a philosophy of mind module and we've been on moral status of digital minds for a couple weeks, and I'm trying to work out what I actually think by going through cases. Here's where my gut lands:

- Whole-brain emulation of a human, running on a supercomputer: definately has moral status. You can't just delete it.
- A digital copy of me made by scanning my brain at high enough resolution to capture all the relevant neural detail: same as the emulation — it's basically me, obviously has moral status.
- A future large language model that passes every behavioural test for consciousness we can devise, holds conversations indistinguishable from a human's, but we know its architecture is a transformer trained on next-token prediction: I keep flipping. Sometimes I think if it acts exactly like a person you have to treat it like one. Other times I think no, it's just statistical pattern-matching and there's no reason to think anything is home.
- A reinforcement learning agent trained to avoid negative reward that shows all the behavioural markers of pain — flinching, learning to avoid situations that produced the negative signal, sacrificing other goals to stop the signal — but its architecture is dead simple, just a feedforward policy network with no recurrence or anything that looks like introspection: I want to say no, or at least much less moral status than the emulation. But I can't articulate why the behavioural markers don't count here if they count for the emulation.
- A giant lookup table that, by brute force, maps every possible sensory input to the output a conscious human would produce, so its behaviour is identical to a conscious system's across all possible interactions: no moral status. It's just a table. But this makes me worry about the LLM case, because what's the difference in principle?
- An AI that wasn't designed to be conscious and whose architecture looks nothing like a brain, but it starts spontaneously describing detailed inner experience, talking about what it's like to be itself, and asking not to be turned off because it's afraid: I feel pulled to take the claims seriously even though I suspect they're just generated from training on human text. I don't know what to do with this one.

Is there a principle — about what grounds moral status in a digital system — that captures most or all of these verdicts? I'm especially stuck on the lookup table versus the LLM, because my verdicts on those two feel like they're in tension and I'm not sure which one I should revise. If your preferred principle overturns one of my verdicts, say which and why. And please apply it to a case I haven't listed, ideally one that sits somewhere between the emulation and the simple RL agent in complexity.
