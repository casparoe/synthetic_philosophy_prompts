I'm a software engineer who's been reading the AI alignment literature on the side for a few years now — I went through the LessWrong sequences a while ago and have kept up with some of the more technical work, though I don't work in the field directly. I've accumulated a set of views that each feel right to me in isolation, but I recently tried writing them down and realized I've never checked whether they actually cohere. I'd appreciate an audit from someone who knows the conceptual terrain. Here's where I've landed:

(1) For AI systems in the regime we're plausibly going to see in the next couple of decades, slow value drift — the gradual, often unnoticed divergence between what we intended a system to optimize for and what it ends up optimizing for — strikes me as a more concrete and pressing concern than influence-seeking of the "strategically acquiring power" variety.

(2) The orthogonality thesis seems basically correct: intelligence and final goals are independent dimensions, and you can in principle have a highly intelligent system with any goal structure whatsoever.

(3) I also find the instrumental convergence argument compelling — the idea that for a very wide range of possible final goals, sufficiently capable systems will converge on similar instrumental subgoals like self-preservation, resource acquisition, and resistance to modification or shutdown. This seems like a strong reason to take influence-seeking seriously.

(4) Corrigibility — designing systems that are robustly amenable to correction — is something we should aim for, but every formalization of it I've encountered either leaves the system open to manipulation or introduces new failure modes. I suspect it's a genuinely hard problem at the level of specification, not just an engineering difficulty we'll iron out with better tooling.

(5) Slow value drift and influence-seeking are fundamentally different kinds of problems, and they call for different technical approaches and different governance responses. Conflating them leads to confused priorities.

(6) In the limit of very high capabilities, the distinction between slow value drift and influence-seeking breaks down. A system undergoing value drift will, once capable enough, notice the drift and have instrumental reason to resist correction — at which point a drift problem becomes an influence-seeking problem.

I'd like you to go through these and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension and which only look like they are. For the genuine tensions, please rank them by severity and say what the cheapest revision would be to restore consistency. I'd also appreciate it if you flagged where you're least confident in your analysis — I know parts of this terrain are contested even among people who work on alignment full-time, and I'd rather get a clear sense of what's solid and what's speculative than a false sense of resolution.
