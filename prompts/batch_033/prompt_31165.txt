I serve on the standing rules committee of a tenants' union with roughly nine hundred members. We hold elections and issue votes several times a month — officers, bargaining priorities, whether to ratify a proposed contract — and at least once a year someone formally proposes changing our voting method. Last spring we nearly adopted Borda count because one committee member had read that Borda rewards consensus, and no one on the committee could say precisely what that claim meant or what it would cost us. The committee has to produce a written recommendation for the full membership on each proposal, usually within a week; none of us is a social choice theorist, and we have to be able to defend the recommendation in plain language to people who will vote on it.

I want a reusable decision procedure, grounded in the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem and what is known about strategy-proofness, that the committee can run on any proposed single-winner method before we write our recommendation. For each proposal it should determine: (1) whether the theorem applies — whether the method is an ordinal, deterministic, single-winner rule with at least three possible outcomes — and exactly which condition fails when it does not; (2) what conclusion the theorem forces about honesty and manipulation when it does apply; (3) when a method falls outside the theorem, whether the failed assumption actually protects us from manipulation or merely moves it into a different part of the ballot, such as an insincere approval threshold or a strategic score; and (4) the specific distortions a method invites — burying, compromising, pushover, truncation, insincere ties — expressed as concrete guidance a lay committee member could use. The procedure should also handle hard cases: methods that permit tied rankings or truncated ballots, elections where the candidate set is not fixed in advance, and small-electorate settings where our members' preferences are often an open secret. Then run the procedure on three proposals currently before us: instant-runoff voting, Borda count, and approval voting, each for single-winner officer elections. I expect the procedure to tell us something we do not want to hear about at least one of the methods we are attached to, and I want it to say plainly what the theorem cannot decide, so that we do not mistake a result about strategy-proofness for a complete theory of fair elections.
