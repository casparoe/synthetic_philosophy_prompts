I've been attending a philosophy reading group at my school — it meets on Thursday afternoons in the library, and we've been working through a selection of classic papers in the philosophy of science. This past week we tackled the Duhem–Quine thesis, and I haven't been able to stop thinking about it. The core idea, as I understand it, is that you can never test a single scientific hypothesis by itself, because any hypothesis is embedded in a network of auxiliary assumptions about instruments, background conditions, and other theories. If an experiment yields a surprising result, you can always choose to reject one of those auxiliary assumptions instead of the main hypothesis, so the evidence never logically compels you to abandon any particular belief. But the more I turned this over in my head, the more it seemed to me that the problem runs deeper than just isolated hypothesis testing. If any set of observations is compatible with indefinitely many theories — if the evidence always underdetermines the theory — then what exactly is doing the work when scientists declare one theory better than another? It looks as though the choice between rival theories must be settled by something outside the evidence: perhaps aesthetic judgments about simplicity or elegance, perhaps the social dynamics of the scientific community, perhaps just intellectual fashion. And if that is right, I do not see how science can claim to be a distinctively rational or objective enterprise. It starts to look like a kind of organised consensus-building that happens to generate useful technology, but which has no deeper claim to tracking the truth than, say, a well-run legal system or a literary tradition. I am genuinely unsettled by this, and I would be grateful if you could walk me through where my reasoning might be going astray — or, if it is not going astray, whether we should simply accept that the epistemic authority of science is considerably weaker than we ordinarily suppose. Please write your answer in British English.
