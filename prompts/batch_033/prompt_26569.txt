I'm teaching an upper-level epistemology course this semester, and we're about to spend a week on Fitch's paradox of knowability. My students have already worked through the standard arguments for and against verificationism, and they've seen the basic move of using self-reference to generate paradoxes — the liar, the knower — so they won't be surprised that a modest-looking principle can blow up. But Fitch's argument has a particular structure I want them to internalize: it doesn't feel like a cheap trick; it feels like a genuine discovery about the limits of knowability, and the steps are tight enough that you can almost recite them.

I'd like to give them something they won't forget: a sonnet that walks through the argument, with the poetic form doing some of the logical work. The octave should set up the verificationist's attractive claim that every truth is in principle knowable — ∀p(p → ◇Kp) — alongside the humdrum observation that there are truths nobody actually knows. The volta at line 9 should be the introduction of the Fitch sentence: take any unknown truth p and form the proposition "p is true and nobody knows that p." The sestet should then trace the collapse: if that conjunctive proposition were knowable, then in some possible situation a knower would know both conjuncts; but knowing the first conjunct makes the second conjunct false, so the knower would be knowing a falsehood, which is impossible. The closing couplet should land the conclusion cleanly: the knowability principle, applied to truths about our own ignorance, forces the absurd result that all truths are actually known — so the principle must be given up, and there are unknowable truths.

I want the poem to be accurate enough that a logician would nod along, and vivid enough that an undergraduate might actually memorize it. If you can work the formal notation or the turnstile into the meter or rhyme somehow, all the better — but don't force it if it would ruin the verse.
