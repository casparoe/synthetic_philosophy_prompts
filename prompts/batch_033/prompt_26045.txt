I retired from a career in experimental physics a few years ago and have been reading my way through formal epistemology. One thing that strikes me, coming from a field where formal principles either work or they don't and you can usually tell which, is how a debate like the one over the Principal Principle can persist for decades without convergence.

By way of background: the Principal Principle, as Lewis formulated it, says roughly that a rational agent's credence in a proposition, conditional on the proposition that the objective chance of that proposition is x and on any admissible evidence, should equal x. The principle is meant to connect two notions — subjective credence and objective chance — that seem to need connecting if chance is to play its theoretical role. But the subsequent literature has been a sequence of counterexample and repair: undermining futures, which threaten the principle when the chance theory itself assigns non-zero chance to its own falsehood; the New Principle and its variants; puzzles about crystal balls and what counts as inadmissible evidence; and a steady stream of ever more refined formulations, none of which seems to have commanded consensus.

What I want is not a survey of these formulations but a diagnosis of the debate itself. My working hypothesis — and I offer it as a hypothesis, not a conclusion — is that the disagreement is not really about probability theory at all. It is about what the word "chance" is supposed to mean. If you think chance is a physical magnitude, something like a propensity or a limiting frequency, then the Principal Principle is a substantive claim about how rational agents should respond to that magnitude, and counterexamples matter. If you think chance is best understood functionally, as whatever property plays the credence-governing role specified by something like the Principal Principle, then the principle is not a substantive claim at all but a partial definition, and the project of "getting it right" looks different. And perhaps there are other conceptions of chance in play as well.

So my questions are these: Is the impasse over the Principal Principle a substantive disagreement, a verbal disagreement about the term "chance," or a case of different research programs answering different questions under a single heading? If it is substantive, what is the hidden question — empirical, formal, or conceptual — that the parties are really disagreeing about? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress? A theorem? A conceptual distinction that has not yet been drawn clearly? Or is this the kind of debate where we should not expect resolution and should instead learn to live with a plurality of formulations, each suited to a different conception of what chance is?
