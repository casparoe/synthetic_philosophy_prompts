I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, but I've fallen down a rabbit hole about underdetermination of theory by evidence and ended up with an argument that seems to prove way to much. It started when someone on a forum said that any finite dataset can be fit by infinitely many functions, so no model is ever better supported by the data than any other. I know that can't be right as a claim about machine learning — we obviously prefer a simple linear fit to a wiggly polynomial that nails every point — but when I tried to translate the point into philosophy of science, I got stuck.

Here's the argument as I wrote it out:

(1) Evidence can support a theory only if it rules out the rival theories — that is, only if the rivals would have predicted different evidence.
(2) For any theory that fits the evidence so far, you can always construct a rival that fits exactly the same evidence but makes different predictions going forward. For instance, take the standard theory and add "but the relevant laws switch off next Tuesday", or say the past was produced by a different process that just happened to mimic the standard theory up to now.
(3) So no body of evidence ever rules out all the rivals to a theory.
(4) Therefore no body of evidence ever supports any theory over its rivals.
(5) But that conclusion is absurd: the evidence really does support plate tectonics over the claim that the continents just happen to be arranged in a way that looks like they drifted apart.
(6) So either (1) or (2) must be false, and whichever one I give up, I want to know what it costs.

Wich step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I've got a vague suspicion the slide happens somewhere between "fits the evidence" and "is supported by the evidence", or maybe in (1)'s demand that evidence has to rule out rivals absolutely, but I can't make it precise. If I had to choose, I'd favour keeping (5) and losing something in (1) or (2).
