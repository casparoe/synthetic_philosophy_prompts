I just spent three hours arguing with a friend about whether there are genuine moral dilemmas — situations where, no matter what you do, you do something wrong. My friend defended dilemmas; I was skeptical. Afterward, I wrote out the following argument, which seems to me to show that genuine dilemmas are impossible. But the conclusion feels too strong, and I can't tell whether I've built a trap for myself. Here's the argument:

(1) Suppose an agent faces what appears to be a genuine moral dilemma: she ought to do A, and she ought to do B, but she cannot do both A and B.

(2) If she ought to do A, then she has a decisive reason to do A — a reason that is not outweighed or undermined by any other consideration bearing on whether to do A.

(3) Likewise, if she ought to do B, she has a decisive reason to do B.

(4) So she has decisive reasons for each of two incompatible actions.

(5) But the notion of a decisive reason is inherently comparative: a reason is decisive for an action only if it is stronger than the reasons supporting every alternative action available to the agent.

(6) Since A and B are incompatible, each is an alternative to the other. So the reasons for A and the reasons for B must be weighed against each other. At most one can be stronger, or they can be exactly tied.

(7) If the reasons for A are stronger, then the reason for B is outweighed, so the agent does not have a decisive reason to do B — contradicting (3). If the reasons for B are stronger, then the agent does not have a decisive reason to do A — contradicting (2). If they are tied, then neither reason is decisive: the agent has sufficient but not decisive reason for each, and it is permissible to do either — which means it is not the case that she ought to do A (she is merely permitted to), and likewise for B, again contradicting (1).

(8) Therefore, in no case does the agent have a decisive reason to do both A and B when they are incompatible. The original supposition — that there is a genuine dilemma — cannot be true.

This argument seems to prove that moral dilemmas are impossible. But it also seems to prove something stronger: that whenever two moral considerations pull in opposite directions, one must simply defeat the other, and the losing consideration leaves no normative trace — no remainder, no ground for regret, no reason to apologize or make amends. That strikes me as false. When I imagine Sophie choosing between saving her child and saving a stranger's child, I cannot believe that whichever child she doesn't save, she simply had no decisive reason to save that child and therefore nothing to regret.

My friend would say the argument equivocates on "decisive reason" or begs the question by assuming all reasons are comparable on a single scale. I'm not sure that's right, but I also can't bring myself to accept the conclusion. Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? If you think it doesn't go wrong — that the argument is sound — then what should I say about the regret and remainder that seem to survive even after the right choice is made?

Please say how confident you are in your diagnosis and single out the point you're least sure of.
