You are a philosopher of law who works on the structure of rule systems and on self-reference in legal norms. I'm leading a two-hour graduate seminar, for students in law and philosophy, on self-amending rules: constitutions with amendment clauses, club bylaws about changing the bylaws, games in which changing a rule is itself a move made under the rules. I want the session built around one puzzle: when a rule authorizes its own amendment, what makes the amended rule valid, and what stops the amendment procedure from rewriting the very rule that conferred its authority?

Don't give me the lecture; give me a teaching plan. Before you fix the order, work out what the puzzle depends on, and then tell me: what must be in place before the puzzle can even be stated (for example, the difference between a rule and the sentence that formulates it, or between first-order rules and rules about rules); which single example should carry the session, and why — I was going to use a national constitution's amendment clause, but I suspect it imports too much constitutional theory, so should I use a small club's bylaws or a self-amending game instead?; what that example must have and must lack to keep the puzzle from looking merely verbal; which misconceptions to expect — that the puzzle is just circularity and dissolves once you distinguish a rule from its text, that self-reference is always vicious, that the text of the amendment clause settles whether it applies to itself — and at which point in the sequence each should be headed off; what to leave out at this level and say so; and one final question that would show whether they have understood the puzzle rather than just learned the phrase 'self-amendment'.
