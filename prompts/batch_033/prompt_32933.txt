I've been working through the procreation asymmetry — the claim that we have a moral reason not to bring miserable people into existence, but no corresponding moral reason to bring happy people into existence — and I've constructed an argument that seems to show the asymmetry cannot be given a principled grounding. I find the asymmetry intuitively compelling and don't want to give it up, so I need to know which step to reject.

(1) If we have a reason not to bring a miserable person into existence, that reason must be grounded either in something about the person's welfare (a person-affecting reason) or in something about the state of the world (an impersonal reason).

(2) Suppose it is person-affecting: we have a reason not to create the miserable person because existence would be bad for her. Then, by parity of reasoning, we should also have a reason to create a happy person, because existence would be good for her. The person-affecting route cannot deliver an asymmetry.

(3) Suppose it is impersonal: the world is worse when it contains suffering, and that gives us a reason not to add suffering to it. Then, by parity, the world is better when it contains happiness, and that gives us a reason to add happiness to it. The impersonal route also cannot deliver an asymmetry.

(4) One might propose a hybrid: the reason not to create miserable people is impersonal, but no corresponding impersonal reason favors creating happy people, because the absence of happiness is not bad in the way the presence of suffering is. But this simply restates the asymmetry rather than grounding it; it does not explain why the impersonal evaluative valence should be asymmetric in the first place.

(5) Therefore the asymmetry is ungrounded: there is no principled basis for holding that we have a reason against creating miserable people while lacking a reason to create happy people.

(6) If the asymmetry is ungrounded, then we should either abandon it in one direction (accepting that we have a reason to create happy people, which seems to make procreation a moral demand) or abandon it in the other (giving up the reason against creating miserable people, which seems monstrous).

Since I am unwilling to accept either of those consequences, and I am also unwilling to treat the asymmetry as a brute moral primitive, I must have gone wrong somewhere in the argument. Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
