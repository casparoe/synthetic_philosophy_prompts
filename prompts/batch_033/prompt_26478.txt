I chair a small interdisciplinary working group on rational choice and public policy, and I've spent the past several years arguing with a colleague — a philosopher by training, while I come from behavioral economics — about whether what we call weakness of will is, at bottom, just hyperbolic discounting. We had another long argument about it last weekend, and we've decided the only way forward is to write down exactly what we agree on and where we still diverge, in a form each of us can actually sign.

The deliverable I need is an adversarial-collaboration document between two positions:

- **Position A (the "preference-reversal" camp):** What gets labeled akrasia or weakness of will is best explained as time-inconsistent preferences, modeled by hyperbolic or quasi-hyperbolic discounting. At each moment, the agent acts on their current all-things-considered ranking. There is no deep failure of agency; there are simply multiple temporally indexed selves whose rankings diverge, with no privileged "true" preference. Talk of "acting against one's better judgment" is loose folk psychology that the discounting model replaces with something precise.
- **Position B (the "genuine-akrasia" camp):** Hyperbolic discounting captures some cases of impulsive preference shift but misses the phenomenon at the core of akrasia, where an agent simultaneously judges that X is better than Y, all things considered, yet does Y — without any change in that judgment at the moment of action. This is a distinctive failure of self-governance, not a preference reversal, and it requires a different explanatory framework (e.g., a divided self, executive-control failure, or judgment-sensitive attitudes that fail to issue in action).

Please draft the document as follows:

1. A numbered "Agreed Claims" section — claims both sides accept, including ones about the phenomena themselves (e.g., that people sometimes act against their own stated reasons, that delay and proximity affect choice in systematic ways, that commitment devices can help). These should be substantive, not just truisms.

2. A "Disagreements" table with these columns: (i) the question, (ii) how Position A answers it, phrased so a defender of A would accept the wording, and (iii) how Position B answers it, phrased so a defender of B would accept the wording. Include at least the following rows: whether akratic action involves acting against one's occurrent better judgment; whether the "self" at the moment of temptation is fully rational; whether commitment devices work because they alter incentives or because they shore up agency; and whether there is a normatively significant difference between someone who pre-commits and someone who doesn't.

3. A "Cruxes" section identifying the sub-questions such that settling them would genuinely move at least one side. For each crux, specify a concrete experimental or observational result both sides agree would bear on it, and record what each side predicts. (If the two sides predict the same thing for a given crux, it's not a crux — drop it.)

4. A brief "Verbal Disputes" note identifying any part of the disagreement you judge to be merely terminological, if there is one. The whole document should be something both Position A and Position B could put their names to as a fair representation of their shared ground and remaining differences, with neither side's view caricatured.

Finally, as a machine-readable last line, provide a one-sentence summary of the document's central substantive claim — the thing both sides agree is at stake — enclosed as \answer{...}.
