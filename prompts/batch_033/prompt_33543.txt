I've become convinced by expressivism about moral language: the view that when someone says "lying is wrong," they aren't reporting a fact but are expressing a non-cognitive attitude — something like disapproval or endorsement. I understand that the Frege–Geach problem is the central challenge: moral sentences embed in conditionals, negations, and other complex constructions where they don't seem to express the attitudes expressivism says they do, yet the complex sentences are still meaningful and can figure in valid reasoning. I want to know what would count against expressivism as a research program — not just a list of objections, but a map of what would have to be shown or discovered for the view to be false or abandoned. I'm looking for the defeaters organized by how damaging they are, from fatal to merely awkward; which of them a committed expressivist would honestly concede as genuine counterevidence, as opposed to dismissing as question-begging or as problems for everyone; whether the view has been qualified over time in ways that make it harder to falsify, and if so, whether that is a problem for a theory of this kind; and what expressivism predicts that its rivals don't, and vice versa — the places where the views actually make contact with evidence rather than just talking past each other. I'm a software engineer and I think about compositionality in terms of how expressions systematically combine to yield the meaning of larger expressions. I'd like the analysis to take that perspective seriously rather than treating it as a loose analogy.
