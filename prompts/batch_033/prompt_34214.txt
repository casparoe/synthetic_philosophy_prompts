Two people in my epistemology reading group got stuck on the problem of old evidence, and I'm supposed to referee next week. I can't tell whose right, so I want you to adjudicate the actual moves, not just pick a winner.

The case we were arguing about was Mercury's perihelion: the anomaly in its orbit was known for decades before general relativity, and when GR came along and was found to imply the anomaly, everyone treated that as strong confirmation of GR. But the Bayesian formalism seems to say it can't be. If the evidence E is already known, P(E)=1, so P(H|E)=P(H), so no confirmation. Yet it obviously was confirmation.

Here's the argument as it went:

(1) A said this shows Bayesian confirmation theory is broken, or at least that it can't account for one of the clearest episodes of confirmation in the history of science.

(2) B said that's too quick. You should evaluate E against your background knowledge with E itself removed. What matters is whether GR makes the perihelion anomaly likely conditional on the rest of physics, compared to how likely the anomaly was without GR. Since it does, the Bayesian can capture the confirmation.

(3) A replied that you can't just remove a known fact from your knowledge. You know the perihelion data in all sorts of entangled ways—you know it was observed, you know the textbooks report it, you know the anomaly's size. There's no unique "you minus E" to conditionalize on, so B's recipe isn't well-defined.

(4) B said maybe the thing that was actually learned wasn't the perihelion data itself, but a new logical fact: that GR entails the data. That fact was genuinely new, and that's what did the confirming. So the Bayesian can model the episode as updating on "GR entails E," not on E.

(5) A came back that this makes confirmation too easy to game. You can always rig up a gerrymandered hypothesis that entails some known fact, and B's account seems to say the known fact then confirms it. B would have to say the gerrymander has a low prior, but the real issue is that the original formalism didn't say anything about who built a theory to fit what, and now B is smuggling in judgments about ad hocness.

(6) B said A is conflating confirmation with ad hocness. A gerrymandered hypothesis has a tiny prior because it's a package of unrelated claims, and the Bayesian formalism already penalizes that. GR gets credit because its assumptions were independently motivated and the perihelion value falls out of them, which means that on the stripped-down probability distribution the likelihood ratio P(E|H)/P(E|not-H) is large. The old-evidence problem just asks us to use a counterfactual prior, and that's not so mysterious.

(7) A said that's exactly the concession: the actual epistemic work is being done by a story about how the theory was built, what was independently motivated, and what falls out of what—none of which is in the original Bayesian machinery. And the "counterfactual prior" is just move (2) again, the ill-defined subtraction of E. So either you stick to the probability calculus, in which case old evidence can't confirm, or you bolt on all this extra structure and it's not really Bayesian confirmation theory anymore.
