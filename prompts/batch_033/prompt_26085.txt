I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on decision theory and I've been trying to get a handle on the superrationality debate — you know, Hofstadter's idea from the 80s that in a symmetric one-shot prisoner's dilemma where the players are identical in their reasoning, you should ask "what happens if we both reason the same way?" and that gets you to cooperation. I've read the original Metamagical Themas columns, the back-and-forth with Dresher, and some of the later formalizations, and honestly the debate feels completely stuck to me. Everyone's been saying roughly the same things for forty years. One side says: if the players are genuinely symmetric — same information, same reasoning — then whatever reasoning process one goes through, the other goes through too, so you should pick the action that gives the best outcome conditional on both players reasoning that way, which is cooperate. The other side says: that's not how decision theory works; each player still faces a dominance argument, and the fact that the other player is a copy of you doesn't change the payoff matrix. And then the first side says you're missing the point about what rationality requires when the players are symmetric, and around we go. My question is: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it a substantive disagreement about what rationality requires, a verbal disagreement about the word "rational," or are the two sides answering different questions — one about individual decision procedures and one about what a group of identical reasoners should jointly do? I want a diagnosis, not a survey. And given your diagnosis, is there anything that could actually move the debate, or is it just gonna spin forever? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning — no preamble, no restating the question.
