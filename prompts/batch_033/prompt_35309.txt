I've been reading about Bertrand's paradox and the principle of indifference, and I'm struck by how the debate seems to have been going in circles for over a century. The problem is dead simple: what's the probability that a randomly chosen chord of a circle is longer than the side of an inscribed equilateral triangle? Depending on how you apply the principle of indifference — pick a random radius and a random point on it, pick two random points on the circumference, pick a random midpoint in the circle — you get 1/2, 1/3, or 1/4. Everyone agrees on the math. Everyone agrees the principle of indifference needs some kind of constraint to tell you which parameterization is the right one. But the proposed fixes — Jaynes's invariance approach, restricting to "natural" parameterizations, abandoning the principle entirely and going subjective Bayesian, etc. — all have known counterexamples or costs, and as far as I can tell nobody has moved in decades. My question isn't "which solution is right" but: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it a disagreement about what "random" means, about what makes a parameterization natural or privileged, about whether ignorance can be represented by a probability distribution at all, or are the different camps just answering different questions under the same name? And given your diagnosis, is there anything — a theorem, a conceptual distinction, a new way of framing the problem — that could actually end it, or is this just a permanent feature of the landscape?
