I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of action and decision theory, fourteen students, one eighty-minute session, and we are approaching the medieval puzzle of Buridan's ass: an ass stands exactly equidistant between two equally tempting piles of hay, has no more reason to choose one than the other, and so, on the assumption that acting requires a preponderant reason, starves. I want an in-class activity that lets the students run into this problem themselves before it is named: they should make real choices between options they can see are exactly equal, then have to account for how they chose and discover that their reasons do not explain their choices. By the end they should see the gap between what they preferred and what they picked, the difference between a reason and a tie-breaker, and the fact that the completeness axiom of standard decision theory would classify the ass as indifferent between the two piles, so that its refusal to choose reflects not a gap in preference but a demand for a kind of reason that rationality does not require. The idea should surface entirely through what the students do, and the puzzle's name should not appear until the debrief.

Please give me a runnable activity with concrete rules, materials (index cards, envelopes, tokens or points, a timer), and a payoff scheme that does not require real money. I would like a first phase of binary choices between visibly identical options with small point payoffs, in which students log each choice, how long they hesitated, and the reason they would give for it, followed by the revelation that the options were identical in every payoff-relevant respect; a second phase in which small groups must write a decision rule that a simple machine, or the ass itself, could follow when the two piles are exactly symmetric, and the class tests each proposed rule for whether it smuggles in a hidden preference; and a third phase, a structured tribunal, in which one participant refuses to choose between two equal piles and the class may supply a rule, feed it, or declare it irrational, but may declare it irrational only by stating which principle of rational choice it violates. Include a timeline for the eighty minutes.

Then give me a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the underlying ideas: revealed preference and the temptation to infer a preference from a bare choice; the difference between indifference and the absence of any preference; tie-breaking as a selection procedure rather than a reason; the principle of sufficient reason and why the ass starves only if every act needs a preponderant reason; and the completeness axiom and the pressure it puts on us to treat the absence of a strict preference as indifference. Warn me about the predictable failure modes and how to respond to each — I expect students to confabulate reasons after the fact and the groups to settle too quickly on flipping a coin — and be frank about which feature of the puzzle the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply myself, such as the medieval context and the original life-and-death stakes.
