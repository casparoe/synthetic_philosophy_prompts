Here's my opening move. The problem as I see it is this: either the ethical is universal and binding, or it isn't. If it is, then Abraham's duty to Isaac trumps any private religious experience — end of story, Abraham should have refused. If it isn't universally binding, then "the ethical" was never really an obligation in the first place, it was just a strong preference, and the whole drama of the "suspension" is overwrought. The idea of a suspension seems to want it both ways: the ethical really does bind us, except when it doesn't, and the only way to tell which situation you're in is through a private, incommunicable relationship with God that looks, from the outside, exactly like being sincerely mistaken. So my question is: what's the actual difference? Not the theological difference — the epistemic difference. How would anyone, including Abraham himself, know that he's an Abraham and not a deluded fanatic?
