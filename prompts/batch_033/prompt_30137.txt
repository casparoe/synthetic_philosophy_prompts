I keep noticing a pattern in how creative work gets evaluated, across several fields, and I have not found a satisfying name for it. Consider two jazz pianists who each alter a single note in a familiar standard: one alteration is called a subtle, tasteful reharmonization, while the other is called a wrong note or a gimmick. Or consider two mathematicians who each relax a single hypothesis in a known theorem: one gets a trivial corollary, the other gets a deep new result. In both cases the raw amount of change is the same; what differs is which feature of the original was changed. Yet which features are the right ones to change seems knowable only after the fact. Beforehand, any feature looks like a candidate, and the difference between the person who finds the fruitful variation and the person who does not seems to consist largely in an ability to sense which feature is load-bearing. The best general description I can give is that a creative contribution is often a small, selective alteration of an existing structure, and that the creative act lies not in the size of the alteration but in the choice of what to alter. I know that broad terms like novelty and originality do not distinguish the two pianists, and I am not looking for a bare label. I want to know whether there is an established concept or body of work that captures exactly this — creativity as the selective adjustment of dimensions of an existing pattern, with the central difficulty being the identification of which dimensions are worth adjusting — and, whether or not there is, how to carve the boundaries between this phenomenon and its nearest neighbors.
