I'm a graduate student in a formal epistemology seminar, and two weeks ago a classmate laid out an argument during discussion that I haven't been able to shake. He claimed it shows that Bayesian epistemology is impossible because the principle of indifference is logically incoherent. The argument is maddeningly crisp, and everyone in the room could feel that something was wrong with it, but nobody managed to land a clean diagnosis before the conversation moved on. I've been turning it over since, and I still can't pinpoint exactly where the gears slip. Here is the argument as he presented it, with his numbering:

(1) The principle of indifference says: when you have no evidence favoring any of a set of mutually exclusive and exhaustive possibilities over the others, you should assign them equal probability.

(2) Suppose a factory produces cubes whose side length is between 0 and 1 cm, and you have no evidence whatsoever about the distribution. By the principle of indifference, you should treat all side lengths as equally likely.

(3) From (2), the probability that the side length is at most 0.5 cm is 0.5.

(4) The very same cube has a face area between 0 and 1 cm². You also have no evidence about the distribution of face areas. So by the principle of indifference, applied in exactly the same way, you should treat all face areas as equally likely. The probability that the face area is at most 0.25 cm² is therefore 0.25.

(5) But a side length ≤ 0.5 cm is the identical physical state of affairs as a face area ≤ 0.25 cm². So the principle of indifference forces you to assign two different probabilities — 0.5 and 0.25 — to one and the same event.

(6) Therefore the principle of indifference is logically incoherent.

(7) The principle of indifference is the only principled way to assign probabilities in the total absence of evidence. Without it, there are no rational constraints on prior probabilities at all.

(8) So Bayesian epistemology — which requires rational agents to have well-defined prior probability distributions — is impossible.

(9) But Bayesian epistemology is a viable research program; it is not impossible. So the argument must fail somewhere.

I can see that (2) and (4) cannot both be legitimate applications of the principle, but I'm not sure whether the real problem lies earlier — maybe in how the principle is formulated in (1) — or later, in the leap from (6) through (7) to (8). I'd also like to know what a defender of the principle of indifference has to give up to escape the contradiction, and whether that concession damages the principle's usefulness for the Bayesian project. Please give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, diagnosing the most important flaws in the argument or explaining what rejecting each step costs. No introduction or conclusion — just the five points.
