I'm a graduate student working on the viability of narrow content, the idea that there is a kind of representational content that two internally identical subjects share regardless of how their environments differ. In a seminar last week I floated the usual motivation for taking narrow content seriously: a speaker and her molecule-for-molecule duplicate on Twin Earth are indistinguishable from the inside, so it seems we need some notion of content that captures what they have in common psychologically. A colleague dismissed the point with Putnam's slogan, "Cut the pie any way you like, 'meanings' just ain't in the head!", and the discussion moved on as if that settled the matter. I suspect the slogan is doing more work than it can bear, but I want to be fair to it. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses that slogan might be used to assert? I would like a menu of readings, from comparatively weak claims about linguistic meaning — for example, that the extension of a term need not be fixed by an individual speaker's internal states — through stronger claims about what determines reference, whether mental content supervenes on internal states, and what a competent speaker grasps or knows in using a word. For each reading, tell me whether it is close to trivial, substantive and defensible, or an overstatement relative to what the standard Twin Earth cases actually show. Then tell me which of these readings, if any, would actually license dismissing narrow content, and which readings are compatible with a robust notion of narrow content even if true. Finally, say which reading you think someone is most likely trading on when they use the slogan to close down that kind of exchange, and whether the argument they seem to want from it is valid. Please write in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables; write it the way you would write a careful email or a letter.
