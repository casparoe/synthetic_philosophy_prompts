I'm a PhD student in moral philosophy, and next week I have to lead a fifty-minute tutorial on the separateness-of-persons objection to utilitarianism for first-year undergraduates. They've had two lectures on consequentialism already: they can state the basic act-utilitarian criterion, they know the difference between hedonistic and preference-satisfaction versions, and they've been walked through the demandingness objection and the problem of interpersonal utility comparisons. They have not read Rawls or Nozick, and the objection itself will be new to them.

Don't explain the objection to me. Plan how I should explain it: the order in which the pieces must be introduced and why each depends on the one before it, the single carrying example that should run through the whole session, the misconceptions they are likely to form and exactly where in the sequence I should head each one off, and what I should openly set aside as beyond the scope of the session. I'd also like one question I can ask in the last five minutes that will tell me whether they've grasped the objection as a structural point about the logic of aggregation rather than as just another complaint about utilitarianism being too demanding or impractical.

A few things I want you to work around. Please do not use the standard tradeoff between a large number of mild headaches and one person's torture as the carrying example; they will have seen some version of that in lecture, and I want the tutorial to force them to think about the structure of the objection rather than reaching for a familiar intuition pump. I also suspect they will arrive with at least three misconceptions ready to hand: that the objection is just the demandingness objection restated, that it amounts to asserting that individuals have rights which utilitarianism violates, and that the real problem is that the felicific calculus is impossible to carry out in practice. I need to know where each of these is likely to surface and how to defuse it without making students feel stupid for having thought it.
