I have been trying to reconstruct an argument from cosmology that seems to prove too much, and I have not been able to locate the bad step. I would like a diagnosis: identify the premise or inference that should be rejected, explain why it initially looked acceptable, and say what giving it up costs for the rest of the view.

The argument runs as follows. Suppose the universe enters a future-eternal de Sitter phase after a finite period of ordinary cosmic history in which observers like us evolve. (1) In such a phase, the late universe is dominated by a high-entropy vacuum with a horizon temperature, and thermal or quantum fluctuations will occur indefinitely. (2) Over an infinite future, any fluctuation with nonzero probability should occur, including configurations that duplicate my present brain state exactly: a 'Boltzmann brain' with the very experiences, apparent memories, and apparent grasp of physics that I now have. (3) Ordinary observers, on this scenario, are produced only during the finite early period, while Boltzmann brains accumulate without bound; over the whole history of such a universe, the Boltzmann brains with my present evidence vastly outnumber the ordinary observers with my present evidence. (4) My total evidence is exactly what is available to me now: my current experiences and apparent memories. A Boltzmann brain can have that same total evidence. (5) I should apportion self-locating credence in proportion to the relative frequency of observers in my reference class, namely those with my total evidence. (6) Therefore I should be nearly certain that I am a Boltzmann brain. (7) But if I am a Boltzmann brain, then my apparent memories of cosmology are themselves part of the fluctuation and carry no reliable information about a real past; so the physics on which (2) and (3) rest is untrustworthy. (8) The argument therefore undermines its own premises: it gives me a reason to accept (6) only by relying on memories that (6) tells me to distrust.

This is the bind. If the argument is sound, I should believe I am a Boltzmann brain; but if I believe that, I should not trust the argument that got me there. I am most reluctant to give up (5), since it looks like a principle of rationality rather than an empirical claim. Where does the argument go wrong, and what exactly does it cost to reject the step you identify?
