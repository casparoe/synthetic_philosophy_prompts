I am giving a talk next month to a reading group in formal epistemology, decision theory, and consequentialist ethics, and I am defending the following thesis. When an agent faces choices between worlds containing infinitely many value-bearers, aggregative consequentialism should stop trying to recover a complete ranking of those worlds by choosing among the familiar devices floated in the infinite-ethics literature — value-density, hyperreal valuations, expanding-sphere or order-based rules, and similar fixes. Instead, it should accept that many infinite worlds are simply incomparable by consequentialist lights, and it should evaluate options only by a partial-order, dominance-style criterion defined over the finite differences between worlds: roughly, two worlds that differ in only finitely many value-bearers are compared by how those finite differences look against the common background, while options that differ over infinite remainders are treated as on a par and cannot be ranked by the theory.

The audience is not uniform, and I want to be ready for the actual question period rather than for objections from people who already share my view. Two members are sympathetic to hyperreal aggregation and believe a total ordering can be preserved without arbitrary choices; one is a decision theorist who suspects infinite worlds are an artifact of bad modeling and will want the whole problem reframed as a limit of finite models; one is a classical utilitarian who regards giving up completeness as giving up on consequentialism itself; and the rest are agnostics, though generally impatient with any position that smells like intuition-pumping without an explicit formal criterion. Please draft a Q&A prep sheet, not a restatement of the talk. Give me the objections I will actually get from this group, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they would do, phrased the way these particular members would phrase them — for example, the hyperreal sympathizers will press me to state exactly which dominance relation I am using and why its incomparabilities are not just an evasion, and the decision theorist will press the limit-of-finite-models challenge. For each objection, include a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, a note on where that reply is soft or would need more work, and a flag for what I should concede up front rather than defend. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks, and explain why it is the dangerous one. Be committed: take a definite view of which three objections are the most damaging and what I should actually say in reply, rather than laying out the options and leaving me to choose.
