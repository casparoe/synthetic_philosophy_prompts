So I've been going back and forth with a friend who's deep into machine learning, and he keeps insisting that if you just have enough data, you can learn the right categories for describing the world — the true ontology, so to speak. I pushed back with the standard underdetermination stuff, but he wasn't impressed, and honestly I realized I don't have a great grip on the formal arguments for why some information really is inaccessible in principle, not just practically. Then I stumbled on Kevin Kelly's learning-theoretic work and the whole "ontology identification" problem — the idea that there are limits to what can be converged to even in the limit, and that the problem of induction isn't just a skeptical worry but something you can prove results about. It's been on my mind ever since, and I want to do an episode about it.

I host a small philosophy podcast, and I've managed to book a guest who's spent years defending the formal learning theory approach to the problem of induction, specifically applying it to the question of whether we can reliably identify the correct ontology from empirical data. I want to avoid the usual "here's what formal learning theory is" intro questions — my audience has the basics from a previous episode. For each of those three, include a follow-up that anticipates the most likely reply and asks the thing the guest probably hopes you won't ask.
