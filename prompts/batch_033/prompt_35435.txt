At a conference on constitutional design I attended last autumn, I found myself sitting between two political theorists who got into a remarkably sharp exchange about the Ulysses-contract model of constitutionalism. I took notes because the argument seemed to clarify something I had been confused about for years, but I still cannot decide who had the better of it — and I cover these ideas for a living.

The first, a scholar of comparative constitutional law, argued that entrenched constitutional provisions — bills of rights, supermajority amendment requirements, judicial review — are best understood as precommitment devices. Just as Ulysses bound himself to the mast to resist the Sirens, a democratic people binds itself against its own predictable future weaknesses: majority panic, factional cruelty, the temptation to suppress dissent. On this view, constitutional entrenchment is not anti-democratic but an expression of democratic self-governance across time. The second theorist, who works on democratic legitimacy, replied that the analogy collapses at the crucial point. Ulysses chose his own ropes; constitutional framers choose ropes for people not yet born. What gets called precommitment is often just the dead hand of one generation constraining the living. He added that even if amendment procedures exist, supermajority thresholds systematically favour the status quo and give veto power to entrenched minorities — which is not self-binding but asymmetric binding in disguise. The first shot back that this proves too much: all decision procedures embed status-quo biases, including simple majority rule, which privileges whatever the agenda-setter places on the table. The real question, she said, is which biases a polity can justify to itself over time. The second conceded the point about procedure but insisted there remains a difference in kind between rules of the game adopted by broad consensus and substantive outcomes ruled out in advance by a particular historical moment. He added that even if one grants the precommitment logic, it only works when the binding is genuinely self-imposed and revisable by the self who is bound — whereas constitutional amendment procedures are deliberately made so onerous that they functionally remove certain questions from democratic reach, which looks less like a Ulysses contract and more like a lock to which the key has been thrown away. At that point they had to break for the next panel, and I have been turning the exchange over ever since.

Could you adjudicate their argument move by move? For each substantive claim made by either party, assess whether it stands up, where it goes wrong, and what — if anything — both are missing about the relationship between constitutional entrenchment and democratic legitimacy.
