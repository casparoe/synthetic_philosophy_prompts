I am writing up my views on consciousness for a philosophy club presentation and would like a consistency check before I present. Here is the position I have landed on:

1. A mental state is conscious just in case the subject has a suitable, non-inferential higher-order thought to the effect that they are in that state.
2. The higher-order thought need not itself be conscious; if it had to be, an infinite regress would follow.
3. Dogs and human infants have conscious experiences.
4. In the partial-report experiments, a briefly flashed array of letters is consciously seen in more detail than the subject can later report or access, so there is more phenomenology than access.
5. A higher-order thought can misrepresent its target, so a person can have a conscious experience as of being in a state that they are not actually in, and this still counts as a genuine conscious experience.
6. What it is like to have an experience is fixed by the first-order sensory state; the higher-order thought merely makes that state conscious and does not supply its qualitative character.

I want to know whether this set of commitments is jointly tenable. Please go through them and identify which pairs are genuinely in tension, which pairs only appear to be in tension and dissolve once the relevant distinctions are drawn, and rank the genuine tensions by severity. For each genuine tension, explain what would have to give and what the cheapest revision would be. I would rather you tell me where the package actually breaks than list a cost for every commitment.
