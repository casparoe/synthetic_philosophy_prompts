I have been trying to get clear about what I think in the statue-and-clay family of cases, and I have ended up with a set of verdicts that feel right individually but that I cannot yet fit under one principle. Let me put them on the table as concretely as I can.

On Monday a sculptor has a lump of clay sitting on a shelf. On Tuesday she molds that very clay into a statue of a horse. I am confident that the statue came into existence on Tuesday, while the lump existed on Monday; and I am also inclined to say the very same lump is still there after the molding — if she flattened the clay, I would point to the result and say it is the lump she started with. But then I seem to have two things on the shelf, the statue and the lump, made of the same matter and occupying exactly the same place; and when I ask myself how many material objects are on the shelf, I want to say one, not two. Squashing makes the problem worse, not better: I say the statue is destroyed by squashing and the lump is not, which again seems to force me to distinguish them. I have the same one-object feeling about a plain, unshaped lump: I do not think there is the lump plus a quantity of clay occupying its place; there is just the clay arranged lump-wise.

Two further cases complicate things. If a landslide leaves a deposit of clay that happens to be shaped exactly like a horse, I do not think a statue has come into being at all; there is just clay in a horse shape, so mere horse-shapedness is not what makes the sculptor's case different. And if the sculptor later reworks the horse into a dragon, I say the horse statue was destroyed and a dragon statue was created, while the clay — or the original lump — persisted throughout.

Is there a principle about material constitution, about when a thing made of some matter is distinct from that matter and when it is not, that delivers all these verdicts? If there is, state it precisely and say which case motivates each clause. Then tell me what the principle predicts about cases I have not listed: a flag and the piece of cloth it is made of; a person and their body; a bronze statue whose bronze is gradually replaced atom by atom; and a smashed statue whose pieces are later reassembled into the same shape. If no single principle captures all my verdicts, tell me which of them it forces me to give up and why those are the ones to drop rather than the others. At the very end, put the principle you endorse — or, if you end with several candidates, the one you would bet on — in a single sentence on its own line beginning "Verdict:".
