I keep tripping over a thing in Bayesian confirmation theory and I dont know if it has a name. The setup is this: someone has a theory H and says it's supported by some fact E. But E was already known before H was ever formulated — it's old news, everybody's had probability 1 in it for decades. Standard Bayesian confirmation says E supports H only if P(E|H) > P(E). But if P(E) is 1, then P(E|H) is 1 too, because conditionalizing on H can't push a probability above 1. So the theory gets zero confirmation from E. Yet in practice people treat exactly this kind of thing as strong support. The standard example is how Einstein's theory got a big boost from explaining the long-known anomaly in Mercury's orbit, which had been sitting there unexplained for decades. So there's a mismatch between the formalism and scientific practice. I'm not after a quick label — I want the carve-up. Does this puzzle have its own name, and what are the main moves people make to dissolve it? Is it basically the prediction-vs-accommodation debate wearing a hat, or is it a distinct problem with its own edges? And how exactly is it related to cases where evidence is old but not certain (like something known but with probability less than 1)?
