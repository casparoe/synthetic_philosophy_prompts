I've been trying to sort out what I think about advance directives — when your past self gets to tie the hands of your future self — and my gut keeps pulling me different ways on different cases. I'm hoping you can help me find the principle, if there is one.

Case 1. Ulysses. He knows the sirens will make him temporarily insane, so he has his crew tie him to the mast and orders them not to release him no matter what he begs. The sirens sing, he screams to be untied, they refuse. That's fine. Earlier Ulysses was right to bind later Ulysses.

Case 2. Margot, at 55 and healthy, signs an advance directive saying: if I ever get advanced dementia and can't recognize my family, no antibiotics for pneumonia — let me go. At 72 she has advanced dementia, doesn't know anyone, but she's not suffering. She watches birds, laughs at cartoons, enjoys her pudding. She gets pneumonia. Antibiotics would almost certainly let her recover and go back to her pleasant demented life. Her daughter invokes the directive to refuse treatment. I think the directive should not be followed. The Margot who exists now seems to have a life she values, and the earlier Margot just didn't know what this would actually be like.

Case 3. Same as Case 2, except this Margot's dementia is miserable — she's frightened, agitated, in distress most of the time. She gets pneumonia. Directive says no antibiotics. I think it should be followed. Earlier Margot was right about what she wanted to avoid.

Case 4. David, at 25, is a devout Jehovah's Witness and signs an advance directive refusing blood transfusions. At 40 he leaves the faith and no longer objects to transfusions, but never updates the paperwork. At 42 he's in a car accident, unconscious, needs a transfusion, and the old directive is in his file. I think he should get the transfusion. The directive doesn't represent him anymore; he just neglected the bureaucracy.

Case 5. Same as Case 4, except after leaving the faith David explicitly thought "I really need to update that directive" and then kept putting it off for two years. Somehow this makes me more inclined to honor the directive, though I can't quite say why. He had his chance.

Case 6. Claire has bipolar disorder. During a manic episode she writes an advance directive saying: if I become severely depressed and refuse treatment, override my refusal and give me antidepressants involuntarily. Later she becomes severely depressed, refuses medication, and argues that manic Claire had no business making that call — depressed Claire is the one who has to live with the side effects. I think the directive should be followed. Manic Claire was protecting depressed Claire in a way depressed Claire can't appreciate.

Case 7. Same disorder, but the directive was written during a depressive episode, authorizing involuntary treatment during future manic episodes (because manic Claire blows all their savings and wrecks relationships). When manic Claire later refuses treatment, insisting she's fine and the directive is nonsense, I'm much less sure. Manic Claire doesn't seem obviously less competent than depressed Claire — maybe more competent in some ways? I'm torn.

Is there a principle that gets all or most of these verdicts? If not, which ones should I give up? And what does your best principle say about a case I haven't mentioned: someone with an advance directive who develops dementia but has occasional moments of lucidity where she says she's changed her mind — but only during those lucid windows?

Please just write in continuous prose, no headings or bullet points or anything — like an email.
