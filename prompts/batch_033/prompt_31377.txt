Two friends of mine, Dana and Marcus, got into it about whether Ian Hacking's looping effects undermine psychiatric diagnoses. Dana said looping effects show psychiatric categories can't be natural kinds: once people know they've been classified, they change how they act and how they describe themselves, and the category changes in response, so the classification is partly making up its own subject matter. A real kind, she said, shouldn't depend on people knowing they've been classified.

Marcus said that's too strong. Lots of social kinds — refugees, money, voters — are real and do real work even though they loop; the fact that people respond to a classification doesn't make the classification false or arbitrary. Hacking himself treated these as interactive kinds rather than indifferent kinds like electrons, and he didn't conclude interactive kinds are unreal.

Dana came back: the problem isn't interaction in general, it's that looping makes psychiatric categories self-validating and unstable. If a label shapes the people it's applied to, the label can look like it fits better over time, not because it tracked a real boundary that was there before the label, but because people molded themselves to it. And if people resist the label, the category shifts, so you can't tell whether a change in prevalence is a change in the condition or a change in the classification.

Marcus replied that medicine has feedback loops too: a hypertension diagnosis changes people's behavior and can change the course of the condition, and medical categories get revised all the time. We don't say hypertension isn't real. So why is looping special when the category is psychiatric?

Dana said the difference is that psychiatric categories are partly constituted by self-understanding: part of what it is to be depressed or to have ADHD includes how people understand and respond to those descriptions, whereas blood pressure exists whether or not you have a concept of it.

Marcus said that just shows they're a different kind of kind, not that they're less real. Real covers a lot of different sorts of things.
