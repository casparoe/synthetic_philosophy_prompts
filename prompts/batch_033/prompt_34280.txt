I'm a backend engineer who works on distributed systems — event sourcing, message queues, eventual consistency, that kind of thing. I've been reading about consciousness on the side, and I keep running into a debate I think I should be able to map onto concepts I already understand, but every time I try, pieces of it seem to slip through my fingers.

The debate is about the color phi phenomenon. In the classic experiment, a red dot is flashed briefly at one location on a screen, followed by a green dot flashed at a second location. Subjects don't report seeing two separate dots. They report seeing a single dot that moves from the first location to the second and changes from red to green somewhere along the way — roughly at the midpoint. The puzzle is that the second dot (the green one) hasn't even appeared yet at the time the subject would have had to start perceiving the motion, if the motion were being constructed in real time. The brain seems to retroactively edit the timeline of experience.

Philosophers have split this into two competing models, using a metaphor from political history. The Stalinesque model says the brain alters the record before it reaches consciousness — like a show trial where the verdict is written before the public sees it. The sensory editing happens pre-consciously; consciousness only ever gets the cleaned-up version. The Orwellian model says the brain presents the raw feed to consciousness and then, very quickly, rewrites the memory of what just happened — like the Ministry of Truth altering the archives after the fact. You experience the red dot, then the green dot, but the memory of having seen a single moving dot is inserted so fast that you can't tell the difference.

I want to restate this entire debate in the vocabulary of the systems I work with every day. Talk to me in terms of event queues, write-ahead logs, frame buffers, commit points, idempotency, read-repair — whatever mappings are genuinely there. I want to know: which parts of the Orwellian/Stalinesque distinction map cleanly onto distinctions we already have in distributed systems or graphics pipelines? Which parts require stretching the metaphor to the point of distortion? And which parts of the philosophical debate — if any — have no counterpart at all in the vocabulary of buffers, logs, and event streams? I'm especially interested in whether the residue points to something my engineering vocabulary is missing, or whether it suggests the philosophers' distinction is less crisp than they think it is.
