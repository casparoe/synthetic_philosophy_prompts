I lead the content policy team at a mid-sized social media platform. We maintain a set of written policies — "no harassment," "no hate speech," "no incitement to violence," and about a dozen others — and a growing internal database of precedent decisions: specific posts that were flagged, reviewed, and either removed or left up, each with a short justification. When a new post is flagged, our moderators are supposed to compare it to the closest precedents and decide accordingly. The idea is that consistency with past decisions makes our enforcement fair and defensible.

The problem I keep running into is that no matter how many precedents we accumulate, there are always new cases that fit all the existing precedents equally well under two different interpretations of the policy. A moderator will say "this is clearly harassment — look at cases A, B, and C," and another will say "this is clearly not harassment — it's consistent with the principle that actually explains A, B, and C, which is narrower than what you're assuming." And both readings are perfectly compatible with every past decision we've made. The precedents don't settle which interpretation is correct, because any finite set of past decisions is consistent with multiple ways of projecting the rule forward — ways that agree on everything we've seen so far but diverge on the new case. I have come to think this is not a temporary gap we can close by accumulating more precedents; it is a structural feature of rule-following.

I need a decision procedure my team can follow when this happens. The procedure should do three things. First, it should determine whether a new case is genuinely underdetermined by precedent or whether one side is simply misreading the existing decisions — because sometimes the disagreement is real but sometimes it isn't, and we need a filter. Second, when the case is genuinely underdetermined, the procedure should provide a structured way to resolve it that does not reduce to "the most senior person in the room picks," because that erodes consistency and morale. Third, it should flag which resolutions would create inconsistency with our other policies or with the platform's stated values, so we don't solve one problem by creating another. The procedure must be usable by moderators with no background in philosophy or legal theory, and it must produce a decision within about fifteen minutes per case, since we handle hundreds of escalations a day.

Please write this procedure, and then apply it to three worked examples: (a) a post that uses coded language to mock a protected group, where some precedents say coded mockery counts as hate speech and others say it does not if no specific slur is used; (b) a post that names a public figure and says "someone should do something about this person," where the disagreement is whether this crosses the line into incitement; and (c) a case where the procedure itself forces a resolution the team dislikes — that is, where following the procedure step by step yields an answer that feels wrong to everyone involved, and you need to explain why the procedure is still doing its job.
