Analyze Napoleon's Hundred Days, from his escape from Elba in March 1815 to his defeat at Waterloo that June, through the lens of Machiavelli's concepts of fortuna and virtù. Take the familiar outline of events as given: Napoleon lands in France, the forces sent to stop him come over to him, and he re-enters Paris and resumes power without a battle; he then moves north to strike the Anglo-Dutch and Prussian armies in Belgium before they can concentrate, fights the Prussians at Ligny and Wellington's forces at Quatre Bras without destroying either army, and is beaten at Waterloo after a rain-soaked delay, the absence of Grouchy's detached corps, and the arrival of Blücher's Prussians. I want a disciplined mapping rather than a narrative. Where in this sequence is virtù at work—foresight, boldness, timing, the capacity to impose one's will on circumstances—and where is fortuna, understood as what lies beyond the prince's control? What does Machiavelli's view that virtù masters fortune only by adapting one's conduct to the times imply about what Napoleon should have done differently, and where does the episode strain the framework? In particular, does Waterloo refute Machiavelli's preference for the impetuous over the cautious prince, or does it instead show that Napoleon had lost the virtù that had once matched his fortune? End by saying what the episode, so analyzed, reveals about the fortuna–virtù distinction itself: is it a genuinely explanatory account of political success and failure, or does it collapse at exactly the point where explanation is most needed?
