I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and over the past year I have been reading about the long-term governance of artificial intelligence, partly because the systems my team builds are beginning to be used in settings where a single model makes decisions that affect many people at once. One idea I keep returning to is the singleton hypothesis: the claim, roughly, that the future of Earth-originating intelligence will eventually settle into a stable configuration with a single decision-making agency at the world level, and that this might be desirable because a world containing several comparably powerful, independent agents with incompatible goals would risk destructive conflict. It struck me that this resembles the problem Hobbes was trying to solve.

As I understand him, Hobbes held that without a common power to overawe them, people in their natural condition would be drawn into a war of every person against every person, and that the remedy is for individuals to authorize a sovereign whose judgments replace their own. But the sovereign Hobbes describes is a human institution created by covenant from below, whereas a singleton in the modern discussion could be a single artificial superintelligence, a unified world government, or some other agency that was never authorized by anyone.

Suppose Hobbes were given a clear and technically accurate account of the singleton hypothesis as it is discussed today, including the possibility that the singleton is an AI. What do you think he would have concluded? Would he have welcomed an AI singleton as a legitimate successor to his Leviathan, or would he have judged that it lacks some essential condition of sovereignty despite its power? I am less interested in a one-line guess than in a careful reconstruction of how his actual commitments — about authorization, the state of nature, and the difference between a genuine sovereign and a party that merely possesses overwhelming force — would push him one way or the other.
