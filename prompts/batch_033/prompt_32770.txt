I co-run a small grantmaking org in the AI safety space, and we keep hitting a pattern where I'm pretty sure evidential cooperation in large worlds is relevant but I have no idea how to actually operationalize it.

The situation: we're evaluating a proposal, and we know there are a couple other funders also looking at the same thing—either the same team shopped it around, or different teams are independently working on the same problem. The naive approach is "evaluate the marginal impact of our dollar and decide." But the ECL-shaped voice in my head says: if we fund it, we should reason as though other funders who think like us will make the same call, and if we all fund it we get wasteful duplication. But if we all think that way, nobody funds it and the thing dies even though one funder would've been plenty.

I want a reusable decision procedure—a checklist, a flowchart in words, whatever—that tells me when ECL reasoning should actually override a straightforward cost-effectiveness analysis. It needs to handle: how similar do the other agents have to be for ECL to bite? What if we have private info they don't? What if the world isn't "large" enough in the relevant sense? Where's the line between "just pick up the phone and coordinate causally" and "the evidential link is the only thing doing work here"?

Run it on two cases: (1) Three funders all got pitched the same $50k academic workshop on alignment benchmarks; any one of us could cover it. (2) We're considering funding a new interpretability research group, but we've heard through the grapevine that two other orgs are thinking the same thing—the field probably needs one such group, not three.

I especially want the procedure to flag where it's genuinely ECL doing the work vs. where it's just bog-standard coordination dressed up in jargon. If the answer is "you're overthinking this, just email the other funders," I want to know why that's the right answer and when it stops being the right answer.
