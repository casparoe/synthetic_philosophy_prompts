Two of my coworkers got into a whole thing at lunch about whether LLMs are agents or not, and I've been turning it over in my head and genuinely can't figure out who's right. I'm a backend engineer so I understand the tech basics but I haven't read the philosophy side of this. Here's roughly how it went:

Dan kicked it off. He said he's been convinced for a while that LLMs are fundamentally simulators, not agents. The whole thing is next-token prediction: you train a giant model on internet text, it learns the conditional distribution, and then it samples from that distribution. He said calling ChatGPT an agent is like calling a weather simulator an agent because it produces forecasts. The outputs are impressive but there's nothing there that has goals, beliefs, or intentions.

Maya disagreed. She said yeah, pretraining is next-token prediction, but that's not the whole system. After pretraining they do RLHF, which literally optimizes the model to pursue outcomes — being helpful, harmless, and honest. She said that's reinforcement learning, the same family of algorithms used to train agents in game-playing and robotics. You're training a policy to maximize a reward signal. That's agent training.

Dan came back: even with RLHF, it's still just outputting tokens. The reward model scores candidate outputs and you shift the output distribution. The model isn't deliberating or forming intentions — it's doing constrained sampling. He said it's like a really elaborate lookup table with a preference filter.

Maya said by that logic, aren't humans also just doing constrained sampling? Dopamine and pain shift our behavioral probabilities. If you want to say the mechanism rules out agency, you need a mechanism-based definition of agency that excludes LLMs but includes humans, and she didn't think Dan had one.

Dan switched tracks. He said okay, but here's a clearer line: LLMs don't have persistent goals across contexts. He wakes up every morning as the same person with the same projects. An LLM gets re-instantiated from scratch for each conversation. There's no continuity of intention.

Maya said that's a deployment choice, not a fact about the model. If you give an LLM a goal in the system prompt and it pursues it across a hundred turns, that's goal persistence within that context. The fact that you can spin up a separate instance with a different goal just means you can spin up many agents. Also she said persistence might not be necessary for agency anyway — a short-lived agent is still an agent.

Dan said here's his actual core objection: the model doesn't have its own goals. Every goal it seems to have was put there by the prompt or the RLHF training. It's not self-directed. An agent has to be the source of its own goals in some sense, otherwise it's a tool — a really fancy tool, but still a tool.

Maya said now he was moving the goalposts — first prediction, then persistence, now self-directedness. And she thought this last one was actually the weakest: a ton of human goal-directedness is also externally shaped, by culture, by incentives, by evolutionary history. The fact that goals were put there doesn't tell you whether the thing that has them is an agent. What matters is whether it can flexibly pursue goals in novel situations, which LLMs can.

They went around a bit more after that but that was the core of it. I have my own intuitions about this but I don't trust them — it's the kind of topic where I feel like the words are doing a lot of work that I haven't inspected.
