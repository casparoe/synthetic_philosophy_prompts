I'm writing an essay on why originals matter in the arts, and I want to use Borges's story "Pierre Menard, Author of the Quixote" as my central test case. The story is famous: the narrator, a critic, describes how the fictional French writer Pierre Menard set himself the task not of copying Don Quixote but of producing, as his own and from his own thought, several chapters that would coincide word for word with Cervantes's; the narrator then insists that Menard's text, though verbally identical, is a different and far richer work, and illustrates the point by comparing the same sentence as written by each. I want a rigorous reading of the story through the philosophical debate about fakes, forgeries, and the value of originals.

Here is the debate as I understand it. One side holds that if two works are perceptually or textually indistinguishable, any difference in value must be grounded in something other than the works themselves — in our knowledge of their provenance, in the history of our reception, or in a kind of snobbery. The other side holds that a work's history of production — who made it, when, with what intentions, and with what achievement — is partly constitutive of its aesthetic identity and value, so that a perfect copy and its original can be two different works with different aesthetic properties. The story seems to sit on this fault line, but in a strange way: Menard's Quixote is not exactly a forgery, since there is no deception and no claim to have written Cervantes's book, and not exactly a copy, since the narrator insists it is a distinct work issuing from a distinct creative act.

Please give me a reading that does all of the following. First, reconstruct the philosophical problem the story dramatizes, and say which position the narrator appears to endorse and why. Second, take seriously the possibility that the story is also satirizing that position: the narrator's judgments are extravagant, and Menard's achievement is arguably absurd. How should an honest critic distribute the weight between treating the story as an argument for the historicist view of artworks and treating it as a parody of the critic who inflates a text's value on the strength of its provenance? Third, examine where the story complicates the standard positions rather than choosing between them — in particular, what it means to apply to a literary text, whose identity is normally fixed by the words on the page rather than by its history of production, the sort of reasoning we normally reserve for paintings and other autographic arts, and whether the story thereby exposes a limit in that distinction. Finally, say what the story adds to the philosophical debate that a bare argument does not: what the fictional form makes vivid or newly persuasive, and what it makes harder to state cleanly.

Treat both the story and the philosophy seriously throughout: do not reduce the story to an illustration of a thesis, and do not distort the positions to fit the story. It is fine to conclude that the story resists the reading in part, and to say exactly where.
