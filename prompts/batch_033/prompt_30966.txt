I was re-reading the Grice chapter for my philosophy of language course, and the textbook's summary of him keeps bugging me. It runs more or less like this: Grice distinguished natural meaning (smoke means fire) from non-natural meaning, which is when a person means something by an utterance. For a speaker to mean something by saying X, the speaker has to intend the audience to form a certain belief, and has to intend that belief to come about through the audience's recognition of that very intention. That, the textbook says, is what separates genuine communication from merely causing someone to believe something. When what the speaker means comes apart from the literal meaning of the words, Grice calls the result a conversational implicature. Implicatures arise because speakers follow the Cooperative Principle and its maxims — roughly, be informative, be truthful, be relevant, be clear. So if I say 'some of the guests left' I implicate that not all of them left, because a fully informative speaker would have said 'all' if they knew it. Since implicatures are things the hearer calculates, they're defeasible and cancellable, and they never form part of the truth-conditional content of the utterance. The chapter's big takeaway is that Grice thereby showed that sentence meaning can be explained entirely in terms of speaker intentions, and that whenever an utterance suggests something beyond it's literal content, that suggestion is an implicature generated by the maxims.

I know enough to suspect the last couple of claims are too strong, but I'd like an honest audit before I assign this chapter again. Go through it sentence by sentence and tell me what a careful Gricean would accept, what's a common simplification, and what's just wrong. And does the takeaway actually survive once the accurate parts are sorted out from the sloppy ones?
