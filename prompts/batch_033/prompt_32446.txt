I'm putting together a unit on intention and practical rationality, and I keep coming back to Kavka's toxin puzzle as the one case that actually separates serious theories from hand-wavy ones. For the handout I want the full spec: what any adequate theory of intention has to deliver if it's going to handle the toxin puzzle and the surrounding phenomena. Don't just give me the familiar points — give me the requirements list: the cases it has to get right (the basic toxin case, plus the variants where you can hire someone to force you to drink, or precommit, or where the money is contingent on *believing* you'll drink rather than intending, or where the drink is harmless but you've sworn you won't), the distinctions it can't afford to colapse (intending vs. wanting, intending vs. predicting, intending vs. being disposed, intending vs. hoping), and the results from decision theory or folk psychology it can't flatly contradict. For each requirement, say why it's a requirement — what breaks if a theory drops it. Then flag which ones are non-negotiable and which are just the prejudices of particular camps (the belief-first people, the desire-first people, the planning theorists, the anti-reductivists). The part I most want to see: whether any pair of requirements can't be jointly satisfied — e.g., can a theory both explain why you can't intend to drink the toxin (given that you're certain you won't) and allow intentions to respond to incentives the way they obviously do in ordinary precommitment cases? And which single requirement knocks out the most popular accounts. A table is fine for the scorecard part, but the weight should be on the requirements and the reasoning behind them.
