You are a professional philosopher with expertise in both analytic philosophy of language and classical Chinese thought, especially the School of Names. Take your time, think through at least three distinct reconstructions of the text before you commit, and resist the temptation to dismiss the dispute as merely verbal. Your task is to adjudicate an argument I overheard, move by move, as carefully as you can.

Last week I attended a mixed reading group on the White Horse Dialogue, the chapter of the Gongsun Longzi in which two speakers dispute the claim that a white horse is not a horse. Two participants got into a long argument about what the dialogue is actually doing, and afterwards both of them asked me to referee. I could not settle it, so I want you to do it.

Eleanor, a philosopher of language, took the view that the dialogue is a semantic point. The advocate does not deny that every white horse is a horse; he is denying that the compound expression white horse is interchangeable with the bare expression horse. His central move, she said, is the example of seeking a horse: a request for a horse can be satisfied by yellow or black horses, but a request for a white horse cannot. That shows the satisfaction conditions of the two terms differ. The argument is therefore about the identity of names rather than the identity of animals, and the provocative form of the conclusion is rhetorical.

Marcus, a historian of early Chinese thought, objected that Eleanor's reading is too tame and too modern. The objector in the dialogue, he said, already makes the obvious extensional point—if there is a white horse, there is a horse—and the advocate does not retreat to saying he only meant the words differ. Instead the advocate doubles down: what names colour is not what names shape. Marcus argued the dialogue is trying to prise apart the colour and the shape in the thing itself, in the spirit of the wider School of Names interest in separating hardness and whiteness. The upshot is not about usage but about the separability of aspects of a thing.

The argument went back and forth from there.

Eleanor replied that the seeking example only shows that different desires have different satisfaction conditions. If I ask for water and someone brings salt water, she said, they have brought water but not the water I asked for; that does not show salt water is not water. She concluded that the advocate has conflated the appropriateness of an answer with membership in a kind. The most charitable reading, she said, is that white horse is a hyponym of horse, and the dialogue is groping toward the distinction between sense and reference while lacking the vocabulary to state it.

Marcus answered that Eleanor was assuming the very theory of kind-membership the dialogue is challenging. In the advocate's vocabulary, horse names shape and white names colour. A shape is not a colour. The compound white horse combines two names and produces something with different criteria of use from either. The later Mohists, he added, took exactly this problem about compound names seriously. The dialogue is not failing to say something familiar; it is making a metaphysical claim about how names partition things.

Eleanor said that if the claim is metaphysical, it either collapses or proves too much. If it means that the expression white horse is not identical to the expression horse, it is trivially true. If it means that white horses are excluded from the class of horses, it is plainly false. A good reconstruction, she insisted, must find a third option, and the semantic reading gives one: the terms differ in sense while sharing extension.

Marcus replied that the third option is a kind of aspectual separation. The name white horse picks out a whole in which whiteness and horse-shape are combined; the name horse picks out one aspect alone. A whole need not be identical to one of its aspects. He admitted that the text is compressed and possibly corrupt, but he thought this reading fits the broader Gongsun Longzi better than treating the advocate as a clumsy Fregean.

Neither would budge.

Please adjudicate this exchange. Go through the moves one by one, say who is right about which specific point, say which overall reading of the White Horse Dialogue you think is best defended, and identify anything important that both of them are missing. Be as precise as you can, and do not flatten the disagreement into a simple misunderstanding.

Finally, state how confident you are in your overall verdict, and single out the point about which you are least sure.
