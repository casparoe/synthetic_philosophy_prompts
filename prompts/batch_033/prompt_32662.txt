Radio astronomers and their funders have spent decades and substantial sums listening for extraterrestrial intelligence, while the same community has mostly refused to do the symmetric thing: deliberately transmit powerful, targeted messages toward nearby stars. The handful of deliberate broadcasts that have occurred remain controversial, and many researchers have urged that active messaging be placed under a moratorium, or at least subjected to international consultation before anyone is allowed to do it again. The standing practice, then, is that listening is normal science while shouting is treated as reckless. What theory of rational choice under deep uncertainty does this practice presuppose? Does it presuppose something like the dark forest hypothesis — the idea that any civilization that announces its presence invites preemptive destruction, because no one can afford to trust another civilization's peaceful intentions — or only a weaker precautionary principle, or an expected-utility calculation with extreme asymmetric stakes, or a moral asymmetry between doing and allowing? Reconstruct the implicit theory, and be precise about what the practice strictly presupposes as opposed to what it merely fits with. Then assess whether the practice is internally coherent: if transmitting is reckless because it reveals us, why is listening not reckless too — and why does the ban survive the fact that our civilization already leaks radar, television, and other electromagnetic noise into space? Finally, say what the practice would have to look like if the presupposed view were replaced by a straightforward cost-benefit analysis of contact, and whether that replacement would force us to begin active messaging, abandon listening, or both.
