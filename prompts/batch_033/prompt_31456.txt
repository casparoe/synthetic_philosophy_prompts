I spent forty years in experimental physics before retiring, and I have lately been reading my way through modal metaphysics. The dispute between counterpart theory and transworld identity over the analysis of de re modality has come to remind me of a certain kind of impasse I saw more than once in the sciences: two frameworks that both account for the data, yet their proponents talk past each other because they never sit down and produce a structured document that separates terminological divergence from genuine disagreement, identifies precise cruxes, and states in advance what each side expects from tests both would accept as probative. In my former field, an adversarial collaboration sometimes broke that kind of stalemate, and I wonder whether the exercise would clarify things here.

I would like you to draft such a document. The two parties are a committed counterpart theorist in Lewis's tradition — who holds that individuals are world-bound and that de re modal claims are analyzed via counterpart relations across worlds — and a committed transworld identity theorist — who holds that one and the same individual exists in multiple possible worlds and that cross-world identity is primitive or grounded in individual essences. They have agreed to work in good faith, to avoid caricature, and to sign only what they can honestly endorse.

Please structure the document as follows.

First, a section of numbered claims that both parties can jointly affirm. These should not be truisms — not merely that both accept possible worlds semantics or the axioms of S5 — but substantive points of agreement about what de re modal discourse must explain, what formal resources are available, and what constraints a successful theory must satisfy. The claims should be informative enough that someone unfamiliar with the debate would learn something from reading them.

Second, a disagreements table. The left column should state the counterpart theorist's distinctive commitments and the right column the transworld identity theorist's corresponding commitments, with each entry phrased so that its own proponent would accept the wording. The rows should be aligned by sub-issue, so that Row 1 captures how the two sides differ on, say, the metaphysics of individuals, Row 2 on the semantics of modal predication, and so on. Avoid rows where the two entries are mere negations of each other — that usually signals that the disagreement has not been analyzed deeply enough.

Third, a list of cruxes: the sub-questions such that, if resolved, at least one side would revise its position. For each crux, supply (a) a concrete test case or thought experiment that both sides agree is relevant; (b) the counterpart theorist's prediction or judgment about that case; (c) the transworld identity theorist's prediction or judgment; and (d) a diagnosis of why the crux remains unsettled — for instance, whether the two sides simply have different pre-theoretic intuitions they are unwilling to surrender, or whether one side's test case presupposes a move the other side rejects.

Fourth, your own assessment as the drafter: are there portions of this dispute that you judge to be merely verbal — disagreements about which formalism to use that do not track any difference in truth conditions or explanatory commitments? If so, identify them and give your reasoning. If there are portions you judge to be genuine, state what you take the irreducibly at-stake issue to be, in terms that neither side would reject as question-begging.

I am not asking for a verdict on who is right. I am asking for a document that could advance the debate beyond the familiar back-and-forth about whether Humphrey cares about his counterparts, or whether essences survive across worlds whose differences we are free to stipulate. I want to see whether the two sides, if they made the effort, could produce something that both would sign — and whether the residual disagreement would turn out to be smaller, or sharper, or simply different, than the literature suggests.
