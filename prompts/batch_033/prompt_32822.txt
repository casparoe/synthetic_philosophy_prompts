I'm a teaching assistant for a political philosophy course, and the professor has been working through a unit on the limits of free expression. She hasn't named the framework she's using, but she's been giving verdicts on cases all semester, and I've kept a list. I want to figure out her principle before the final case comes up in discussion next week. Here are her eight verdicts so far:

1. A person punches a stranger during an argument. Verdict: Not protected.
2. A person writes an op-ed arguing that the minimum wage should be abolished. Some readers find the argument morally repugnant. Verdict: Protected.
3. A person publishes the home address of a controversial public figure, knowing the figure has received credible death threats. The figure is subsequently attacked at that address. Verdict: Not protected.
4. A person wears a t-shirt with a profane anti-government slogan in a public park where children are present. Verdict: Protected.
5. A person spreads a knowingly false rumor that a local restaurant is infested with rats. The restaurant loses most of its customers and closes. Verdict: Not protected.
6. A person publishes a memoir that describes, in unflinching detail, their experience with an eating disorder. Some readers later develop eating disorders and cite the book as a trigger. Verdict: Protected.
7. A person burns down their own house, with no intent to commit insurance fraud and no risk to neighboring properties. Verdict: Protected.
8. A person makes a credible and specific threat of violence against a coworker. Verdict: Not protected.

The new case for next week: A social media platform's recommendation algorithm, optimized to maximize engagement, systematically surfaces content to teenage users that the platform's own internal research shows significantly increases rates of anxiety, depression, and disordered eating among that age group. The content itself is not illegal. The platform has not disclosed these findings to its users.

Please structure your response in four sections, as follows:

- **Candidate principles** (roughly 250 words): Identify the two or three most plausible interpretations of the harm principle that are consistent with all eight of the professor's verdicts. State each principle clearly and walk through how it handles the pattern of verdicts — not case by case, but by explaining what the principle counts as harm and why the overall pattern follows.

- **Predictions for the new case** (roughly 200 words): For each candidate principle, explain what verdict it would yield on the algorithm case and why. If two principles converge on the same verdict, say whether they converge for the same reason or for different reasons.

- **Best guess and reasoning** (roughly 200 words): Give your best prediction of the professor's verdict on the algorithm case, with the reasoning that favors one candidate principle over the others. If her eight verdicts genuinely underdetermine the choice among the principles, say so — but still give your best guess and explain why you lean the way you do.

- **The decisive further case** (roughly 150 words): Describe a further case, not among the nine already discussed, that would cleanly distinguish the candidate principles from one another. State what each principle would say about it and explain why the professor's verdict on that case would settle which principle she is actually running.
