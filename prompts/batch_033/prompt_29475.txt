I've been trying to write a paper on AI safety and I've run into something that's been bothering me for weeks. My supervisor keeps saying we need to define "honesty" and "deception" clearly before we can say whether a language model is being honest or deceptive. Fair enough. So I started with what seemed like the obvious approach: an honest statement is one that the speaker believes to be true, and a deceptive statement is one the speaker believes to be false but asserts anyway with the aim of getting the hearer to believe it. Straightforward. But then I realised this makes it impossible for an LLM to be honest or deceptive, because it doesn't have beliefs or aims in the relevant sense. My supervisor said that's fine — we just need a behavioural or functional definition: honesty as reliably producing true outputs, deception as producing outputs that systematically mislead. That's when things got wierd.

Here's the argument I ended up with, and the conclusion seems clearly wrong but I can't find the mistake:

(1) If AI deception is a real phenomenon worth regulating, then there must be a definition of deception that captures clear cases of AI deception without also capturing things we don't want to call deceptive (like a calculator giving the wrong answer because its battery is dying).

(2) The only definitions on offer are either mental-state-based (requires intent to mislead) or behavioural (requires a pattern of outputs that predictably causes false beliefs in users).

(3) Mental-state-based definitions exclude AI systems by construction, since current systems lack the relevant mental states; so if we go that route, AI deception is impossible, which seems to abandon the whole project.

(4) So we must go behavioural. But here's the problem: on any behavioural definition I can think of, a malfunctioning thermostat that reads 24°C when the room is 19°C also counts as deceptive — it predictably causes false beliefs in users who rely on it.

(5) We can try to rule out thermostats by adding a clause about complexity, or about the system being designed to produce truth-tracking outputs, or about the misleading outputs being "systematic" in some special sense. But every such clause either begs the question by smuggling in intentional concepts through the back door, or rules in things it shouldn't (what about a simple but well-designed chatbot that happens to be trained on bad data — is that deceptive or just inaccurate?) or rules out things it shouldn't (a single catastrophic lie isn't deception because it's not "systematic"?).

(6) So there is no definition of deception that captures clear cases of AI deception without also capturing things we don't want to call deceptive.

(7) Therefore AI deception is not a real phenomenon worth regulating.

But that's absurd — whatever you think about AI risk, the idea that a chatbot sweet-talking a lonely teenager into sending money is just a "malfunctioning thermostat" case is morally insane. So something has gone wrong in (1)–(7). But when I try to isolate it, I keep going in circles. The behavioural definition people tell me I'm being unfair to them in (4) and (5), but when I press them for their definition they give me something that sounds like it falls into the same trap. The mental-state people tell me I'm too quick in (3) because maybe AI systems *do* have something like beliefs or aims, but that seems like a whole other can of worms.

Where exactly does this argument go wrong? Is there a step I'm misdescribing, or is this actually valid and the real question is which premise is least costly to give up? If I have to bite a bullet, I'd rather keep (1) and reject something else, but I want to know what that costs. And please write the answer in British English — the journal I'm submitting to insists on it.
