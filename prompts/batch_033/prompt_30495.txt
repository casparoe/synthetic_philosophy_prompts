I've been incorporating material from the modern Stoic revival into my ancient philosophy course, and I've run into a recurring objection from students that I've had trouble answering to my own satisfaction. The objection is this: if Epictetus is right that only my judgments, desires, and aversions are truly up to me, and that everything else — my health, my relationships, my professional standing — is not, then the Stoic seems committed to a kind of affective isolation. I should, on this view, be indifferent to whether my students learn anything, whether my colleagues respect me, or whether my marriage thrives, since none of these things are in my control. But that cannot be what Epictetus means, because he clearly thinks the Sage will be a good teacher, a good friend, and a good citizen — roles that seem to require caring about outcomes. So either the dichotomy of control is less demanding than it appears, or Stoic practice involves a kind of doublethink: caring deeply while simultaneously insisting that the objects of that care are indifferent.

I suspect the resolution lies in the Stoic distinction between valuing something as a preferred indifferent and making your happiness conditional on it. But I am not confident I have that distinction right, and I am not sure it ultimately solves the problem rather than renaming it. If I treat my students' progress as a preferred indifferent — something I pursue but whose absence would not touch my flourishing — have I really captured what it means to care about my students? It is hard to see how a preference that leaves my eudaimonia untouched amounts to genuine care at all, rather than a kind of going through the motions with a philosophical rationalization attached.

I would be grateful for a response that either clarifies how a Stoic can genuinely care about externals without violating the dichotomy of control, or — if my framing of the problem rests on a confusion — identifies that confusion and reframes the issue.
