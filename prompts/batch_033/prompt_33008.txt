I've been having a long-running argument with some friends about what determines the meaning of a text, and I've been keeping a list of cases that I go back to in my head. I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, so I don't know the academic literature on this, but the cases keep nagging at me and I'd like to know whether there's a coherent principle behind my reactions, or whether I need to revise some of them.

Here are the cases and my verdicts. First: a poet in 1910 writes a line about "the towers falling into ash and silence," intending it as a melancholy image of industrial decay. Decades later, after a devastating attack on a major city's twin towers, the line is widely anthologized and taught as though it were a dark prophecy. I think the line does not mean anything about that later event. The poet's intention controls, and the historical coincidence gives the line an eerie resonance but does not change its meaning. Second: a novelist writes a character as a sincere, admirable hero, but the character's speeches are so pompous and his actions so self-defeating that readers across two generations uniformly find the novel hilarious and read it as satire. The novelist's private correspondence, published after her death, confirms she meant every word of the hero's grandeur seriously. I think the novel means what the novelist intended — it is not satire — and the readers are collectively misreading it, even if the misreading is widespread and stable. Third: I write a technical specification at work that says "the system shall encrypt all data in transit." I intended this to mean TLS encryption specifically, because that's what our stack uses and I assumed anyone reading the spec would know that. The spec does not mention TLS, and a developer on another team reads it as requiring IPsec. I think the spec means what it says, not what I intended, and the other developer is right that the text as written does not rule out IPsec. Fourth: a contract between a buyer in New York and a seller in London says "delivery by Friday, March 15." The buyer intended Friday in New York, the seller intended Friday in London, and March 15 falls on different calendar days in the two time zones during the relevant window. The contract is silent on time zones. I think the contract is ambiguous, full stop — neither party's intention resolves it, and the meaning of the contract is simply underdetermined. Fifth: someone photocopies a passage from the methods section of a chemistry paper — "the solution was heated to 340 K and allowed to cool for 90 minutes before titration" — and prints it in a poetry magazine, set in verse lines, under the title "Patience." I think the meaning of the text changes when it is presented as a poem; it now means something about slowness, precision, and waiting, even though the chemist who wrote it intended none of those things. Sixth: a dead novelist's diary is discovered fifty years after her death, and in it she writes that the river in her most famous novel represents the impossibility of returning to childhood. No critic had proposed this reading, and the novel had been interpreted in entirely different terms for half a century. I think the diary does not change the meaning of the novel; the text's meaning was fixed when it was published, and the author's private thoughts are not part of the text.

When I lay these out, I can see that I am pulled in different directions. In the spec and contract cases I seem to be a textualist — the words on the page are what matter, and the author's private intention does not fix the meaning when the text underdetermines it. But in the poetry and novel cases I seem to be an intentionalist — the author's intention does control the meaning, and widespread reader response does not override it. And then in the found-poetry case I seem to think that presentation context can change meaning entirely, which cuts against both textualism and intentionalism. I would like to know whether there is a single principle, or a small set of principles, that captures all six of my verdicts, or close to all of them. If not, which verdicts should I give up, and what would a consistent position look like? Please also tell me what your proposed principle or principles would say about a case I have not considered: a legal statute whose drafters intended it to cover one kind of conduct, but whose text, read in the ordinary way at the time of enactment, clearly covers a different kind of conduct that the drafters never thought about. Finally, please tell me how confident you are in the account you are giving and which part of it you are least sure of. I would prefer the response in continuous prose, the way one would write an email or a letter — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
