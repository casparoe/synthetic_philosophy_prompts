I recently read a popular science article that summarized an idea from the philosopher Daniel Dennett about what he calls Darwin's and Turing's "strange inversions of reasoning." The article put it this way:

"Darwin showed that you don't need a designer to get design—natural selection can produce the appearance of purpose without any mind guiding it. Turing then showed the same thing about intelligence: you don't need a mind to get intelligent behavior. A computer running the right program can pass the Turing test and exhibit full human-level intelligence without understanding anything it's doing. Together, these two inversions prove that the mind is just an illusion—what we call 'understanding' is really just competently executed unconscious computation all the way down, and there's no remaining mystery about consciousness once you accept both inversions."

I found this striking but something about it felt too sweeping. I don't have a background in philosophy, but I've been reading about these topics on my own and I'd like to know: how fair is this summary to Dennett's actual view? Which parts would he endorse, which would he push back on, and does the conclusion—that consciousness is fully explained away by these two inversions—actually follow from what Darwin and Turing established? I'm less interested in whether the summary is literally word-for-word accurate than in whether it captures the right shape of the position, and if not, what the corrected picture would look like.
