So I've been thinking about this philosopher Harry Frankfurt's idea — he came up with these scenarios where a person is making a choice, but unbenownst to them, if they were about to make the 'wrong' choice, a hidden mechanism (like a chip in their brain) would kick in and force them to make the 'right' choice instead. If they make the right choice on their own, the mechanism never activates. Frankfurt argues the person is still morally responsible for what they did, even though they couldn't have done otherwise. The target here is something called the principle of alternative possibilities, which says you can only be responsible for something if you could have done otherwise.

I've been rewatching Minority Report and it feels like the movie is playing with the same basic puzzle but from the other direction. In the film, the precogs predict someone will commit a crime, and the police arrest them before they do it. So the person is prevented from acting — they never get to the moment of choice. But the whole moral question of the movie, especially the arc with Tom Cruise's character, is whether the prediction is fate or whether a person, once they know the prediction, can still choose differently. The climax basically hinges on this.

I'd like a reading of Minority Report through the lens of Frankfurt cases and the principle of alternative possibilities. Where does the movie dramatize the same issues Frankfurt is wrestling with? Where does it invert them — since in Frankfurt the person acts and we ask if they're responsible despite lacking alternatives, while in the movie the person is stopped and we ask if they would've been responsible? Does the film ultimately take a position on whether alternative possibilities matter for moral responsibility? I'm not an academic or anything, just been chewing on this on my own, so I'd appreciate it if you could unpack the ideas clearly while also taking the film seriously as a film, not just a thought experiment with actors.
