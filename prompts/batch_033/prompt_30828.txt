I've been reading through antinatalist arguments online and keep encountering the phrase "better never to have been" deployed as though it's a single, obvious truth rather than something that needs unpacking. In one exchange, someone used it to move from "life contains more suffering than happiness" to "therefore having children is wrong," and nobody pressed them on what the slogan is actually claiming. Before I engage further, I want to get clear on what I might be disagreeing with.

Can you lay out the distinct precise claims that "better never to have been" could be making? For each candidate reading, specify: (a) what exactly is being compared to what — a particular life to the nonexistence of that person? a world containing that person to a world without them? the state of having been born to the counterfactual state of never having existed? (b) whose perspective or standard of evaluation is in play — the person who would have lived, an impartial observer, all affected parties? (c) whether the claim is about a particular life, all human lives, or all sentient lives, and (d) how plausible the reading is — trivial, defensible but contentious, or false.

Then explain which of these readings, if any, would directly support the conclusion that procreation is morally impermissible, and which would require additional premises — such as Benatar's asymmetry between the absence of pain and the absence of pleasure — to get there. I want to know whether someone who rejects the asymmetry can still coherently accept some version of "better never to have been" without it entailing antinatalism.
