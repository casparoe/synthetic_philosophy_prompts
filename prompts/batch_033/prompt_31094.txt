I've been reading around in animal ethics and comparative cognition, and I keep noticing a move that bugs me but I don't have a word for. Somebody will be arguing about whether a pig or a dog or an octopus matters morally, and they'll reach for brain facts: "look at the encephalization quotient," or "this species has way more cortical neurons then that one," or "humans have the biggest cortex relative to body size, so obviously..." And then, without any further argument, the moral conclusion just follows: the bigger-brained one matters more, or can suffer more, or has a stronger right not to be harmed. But when you ask the same person about a human with a small brain or a missing chunk of cortex, they suddenly don't want to apply the same scale at all — that human still counts fully.

I'm not asking whether they're right about any particular animal. I'm asking whether this specific move — sliding from a neural measurement to a moral ranking, and then quietly exempting humans — has a name, and what the best way is to say exactly where it goes wrong. I don't just want a label; I want to know what the move is, what it isn't, and how to tell it apart from nearby things people usually reach for, like plain speciesism or the naturalistic fallacy. If there's no name, what would you call it, and how would you define it so it doesn't collapse into those?
