I'm a software engineer thinking about how to design agents that act in environments where they can't fully distinguish between states. I came across the absent-minded driver problem, and I keep flipping between two analyses of it. Could you write a dialogue between two people, each defending one side?

The problem: a driver plans a trip along a highway with two exits. He knows the payoffs in advance — exit 1 yields 0, exit 2 yields 4, and missing both exits yields 1. But he also knows he'll be absent-minded: whenever he reaches an exit, he won't remember whether it's the first or the second. He must follow the same probabilistic rule at whichever exit he finds himself at.

One character argues that the driver should pick his rule by maximizing expected utility from the starting point, before any forgetting occurs. From that vantage, the optimal rule is to exit with probability 1/3, and he should commit to this and follow it even if a different calculation seems attractive once he's at an exit.

The other character argues that decisions happen in the moment. At an exit, the driver should use his current credences — he knows he's at some exit, and he should take whatever action maximizes expected utility given that information. The ex ante calculation is a distraction; what matters is what's rational given what you actually know when you act.

Both characters should be sharp and well-motivated, with no strawmen. I want them to engage with each other's specific arguments, respond to the moves the other makes, and try to locate the real source of the disagreement. Does it come down to whether you can bind your future self? To what "rational choice" means? Or is one of them making a mistake in how they set up the probabilities?
