I'm running a reading group on free will and moral responsibility for a dozen philosophy graduate students who work mostly in other areas — ethics, language, and mind — and have at most a passing acquaintance with the free will debate. The group is split between people who think compatibilism is obviously right and people who think it is obviously a cheat, which makes every session a little tense, but in a productive way. We have been meeting fortnightly for a term, and our next meeting is on Strawson's reactive attitudes. Everyone will have read the essay beforehand.

My job in the ninety-minute session is not to summarize it but to make the architecture of the argument visible enough that they can see what work each piece is doing and why the essay has had the staying power it has. Don't explain the position to me; plan how I should structure the session. What idea has to be in place before the phrase reactive attitudes can mean anything more than emotional responses to other people's behavior? I suspect the crucial precursor is the distinction between the participant stance and the objective stance, and that the reactive attitudes are defined by their place within the participant stance — but I am not sure whether I should introduce the objective stance first, or let it emerge from the reactive attitudes themselves. What one example should carry the session (the person who steps on your hand, the colleague who forgets your name, the long-time partner who breaks a promise), and what must that example have and lack so that it draws the line between excusing and exempting conditions without collapsing them? What misconceptions should I expect — for instance, that the reactive attitudes are just emotions, or that Strawson's argument licenses a kind of quietism about the free will problem, or that the objective stance is reserved for the pathologically impaired — and at exactly which point in the sequence should I head each one off? What should I openly refuse to explain at this level (the later compatibilist literature that builds on it, the empirical psychology of blame, the question of whether the reactive attitudes justify retributive punishment), and where does the plan become misleading if I leave that out without saying so? Finally, give me one question I can ask at the end that would show whether they understand why the essay is called Freedom and Resentment rather than Freedom and Determinism — not whether they can recite the argument, but whether they see what the choice of title signals about the kind of argument it is.
