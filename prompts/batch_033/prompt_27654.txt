Frank Ramsey once said that he pictured the world the way a draughtsman draws in perspective rather than the way a surveyor draws to scale: the foreground is filled with human beings, and the stars, though enormous, come out tiny. He leans on this analogy to reach a conclusion about the meaning of life — that the size and age of the universe cannot make human life insignificant, because significance, like apparent size in a perspective drawing, is a matter of relation to a viewer rather than of absolute magnitude. I want an audit of how far the analogy really holds. First give the correspondence table: what plays the role of the foreground, the background, the scale, the viewpoint, and the drawing itself. Then identify where the mapping breaks, and separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from the ones that do not. In particular, consider what the perspective analogy quietly imports — a preexisting viewer, a chosen station point, a representational interest — that a neutral description of the universe need not contain. Does the conclusion that cosmic scale is irrelevant to meaning survive once those disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy doing the argumentative work all along? If it cannot carry the conclusion, say whether a better analogy exists, or whether the point can be made without any analogy at all.
