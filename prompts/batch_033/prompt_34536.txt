Write a sonnet about the debate between Norman Malcolm and Daniel Dennett on whether dreams are experiences. The octave should present Malcolm's challenge: if being sound asleep entails lacking consciousness, then no experiences can occur during sleep, and what we call "remembering a dream" is not the retrieval of a past mental event but the exercise of a disposition to tell a story upon waking — the telling serves as the criterion for having dreamed, not as a report of something that took place in the night. The volta should introduce Dennett's complication from his cassette-theory thought experiment, in which dream memories are causally inserted at the moment of waking, so that there is no fact of the matter about whether any experience occurred during sleep. The sestet should explore the unsettling implication that the question "did I experience that dream?" may be indeterminate — not merely hard to answer, but lacking a determinate truth value. The closing couplet should leave the reader suspended between the conviction that the dream was lived and the suspicion that it was only told. The poem should be accurate enough that a philosopher of mind familiar with the debate would recognize the positions, and the volta should do genuine argumentative work rather than merely announcing a change of topic. Please write in American English.
