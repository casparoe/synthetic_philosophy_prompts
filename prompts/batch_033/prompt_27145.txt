You are an expert in moral psychology and organizational ethics, specializing in the situationist critique of virtue ethics and its practical implications for institutional design. Think through the underlying philosophical issues carefully before producing your final answer; start by mapping out what the situationist evidence actually licenses and what it doesn't, so your procedure doesn't overclaim.

I run the ethics and professional-conduct office at a large teaching hospital (roughly 10,000 staff). For years our approach to ethical lapses has been character-based: train people in the relevant virtues, hold them individually accountable for violations, and try to hire people of good character. I've now read enough of the situationist literature—the Darley and Batson Good Samaritan study, the Milgram obedience work, the fundamental attribution error, John Doris's *Lack of Character*, and the broader argument that situational factors often swamp dispositional ones in predicting moral behavior—to think that our approach is, at best, seriously incomplete.

The problem: I don't know how to translate this insight into a routine procedure my office can use. Every month I face a different ethical-behavior problem—medication errors that go unreported, unnecessary procedures driven by revenue pressure, disrespectful treatment of patients by overworked residents, billing irregularities, conflicts of interest in purchasing. For each one I need to decide whether this is primarily a situation problem (fix the defaults, the incentives, the physical environment, the chain of command, the way information is presented) or a character problem (train, select, model, remind), and if the former, what kind of situational fix.

Please design a reusable decision procedure—a checklist or flowchart in words—that takes a specific ethical-behavior problem as input and walks the user through a sequence of questions to determine what kind of intervention is appropriate. Include:

- The questions, in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer at each step.
- Where the procedure stops with a recommendation and where it hands off to human judgment.
- Honest flags about which steps rest on strong situationist evidence, which rest on weaker or extrapolated evidence, and which are philosophical judgment calls dressed as procedure.
- What the procedure explicitly cannot do—its blind spots and limitations.

Then apply the procedure to two hospital cases:

1. Nurses and junior staff routinely fail to challenge attending physicians when they observe a clear error (wrong drug, wrong dose, wrong site), despite an explicit policy encouraging them to speak up.
2. A pattern of systematic upcoding in billing, where the investigation strongly suggests it's driven by department-level pressure to meet revenue targets rather than by individual greed.

For at least one of these cases, give me the answer the procedure produces even if it's one I—or the hospital leadership—will not want to hear.
