I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, and I keep running into the same kind of call: whether to spend time and engineering effort getting more information before we commit to something. A few times a month I'm weighing things like whether to instrument a feature for a week before deciding to kill it, whether to run another A/B test after an inconclusive one, or whether to add a bunch of analytics events before a launch just in case. The stakes are real: wasted engineering time, delayed launches, and sometimes shipping something we cant cleanly roll back. Nobody on my team has much stats background, and my EM and PM both have to sign off, so whatever I use has to fit in a one-pager and be runnable in under an hour.

I keep seeing this result called Good's theorem, or the value of information, that free information can't hurt you in expectation. I want you to turn that into a decision procedure I can actually run, not the math. I care about partial, noisy information, not just perfect information. I want a checklist: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure says stop and don't gather more data, and where it has to hand the decision to a person because it's a values call rather than a factual one. Then run the procedure on three decisions: (1) whether to spend a week instrumenting a feature before deciding whether to kill it or keep investing in it; (2) whether to run a second, bigger A/B test after the first one came back ambiguous; and (3) a PM wants to add a bunch of telemetry before a launch that's already locked in by a contract, on the grounds that more data can't hurt. Make sure the procedure gives an answer the team wont like in at least one of those, and tell me what the procedure can't do for me.

Also, please separate what is settled here from what is genuinely contested among people who know this topic, and don't present a contested view as the consensus.
