I've been thinking about a puzzle in the philosophy of science that I came across a while ago, and I recently brought it up with two friends. They ended up in a long argument and I'm not sure how to sort out who's right about what. I'd appreciate a careful adjudication.

The puzzle is sometimes called the raven paradox. The idea is that a generalization like "all ravens are black" seems to be confirmed by observing its instances — seeing a black raven gives us more reason to believe the claim. But "all ravens are black" is logically equivalent to "all non-black things are non-ravens." So by the same reasoning, observing a non-black non-raven — a red apple, a white shoe, a green leaf — should also confirm that all ravens are black. That seems absurd. You don't learn anything about ravens by looking at the shoes in your closet.

Here's how the argument between my friends went. I'll call them Dana and Lee.

Dana started by saying the paradox exposes a real problem with how we think about confirmation. If a simple and plausible principle — that a generalization is confirmed by its instances — leads to the conclusion that my red sweater confirms that all ravens are black, then something is wrong with that principle. The principle needs to be rejected or at least heavily restricted. The paradox isn't just a cute puzzle; it shows that instance confirmation, taken at face value, is broken.

Lee disagreed. Lee said the resolution is straightforward once you think quantitatively. Observing a red sweater does confirm that all ravens are black — it's just that the amount of confirmation is vanishingly small, because the class of non-black things is enormous. The intuition that the observation is irrelevant is explained by the fact that the confirmation is so tiny it's pragmatically negligible. There's no deep problem; we just need to replace the binary notion of "confirms or doesn't" with a graded notion of "confirms to some degree." On this view, the paradox is an artifact of thinking in all-or-nothing terms.

Dana pushed back. She said the quantitative move doesn't actually solve the problem, because the same red sweater also confirms the hypothesis that all ravens are white. After all, "all ravens are white" is equivalent to "all non-white things are non-ravens," and a red sweater is a non-white non-raven. So the same observation confirms two contradictory generalizations. It can't be rational for one piece of evidence to support both "all ravens are black" and "all ravens are white," even to a tiny degree. If the quantitative approach says it does, then the quantitative approach is in trouble too.

Lee answered that this objection only works if you ignore everything else you know. We already know that ravens are a kind of bird and that birds come in various colors, and we have background knowledge about how common black things are relative to white things in the relevant categories. Once you incorporate that background knowledge, the degree of confirmation a red sweater gives to "all ravens are black" is different from the degree it gives to "all ravens are white." The symmetry Dana is pointing to only holds if you pretend we're reasoning in a vacuum, which we never are.

Dana replied that this misses the point. The paradox is supposed to be about the logic of confirmation — what it means for evidence to support a hypothesis, considered in the abstract. Appealing to contingent facts about what we happen to know about birds and colors might tell us what we should believe in practice, but it doesn't resolve the conceptual puzzle. The conceptual puzzle is that two logically equivalent statements seem to be confirmed by entirely different kinds of observations, and that needs an answer at the level of the logic itself, not just a pragmatic patch.

Lee said that there is no "logic of confirmation" in the abstract, separate from background knowledge — confirmation always happens against a body of prior beliefs, and the paradox only looks like a paradox because it asks us to bracket everything we know and then act surprised when the reasoning goes weird. That's not a bug in confirmation; it's a bug in how the paradox is set up.

For each claim or rebuttal, tell me whether it's basically right, basically wrong, or somewhere in between, and explain why. Then give me your overall assessment: who is more right overall, and what, if anything, are both of them missing?

One thing I'd especially appreciate: please separate what is genuinely settled among people who work on this from what is still actively contested. I don't want to walk away thinking there's a consensus where there isn't one, or vice versa.
