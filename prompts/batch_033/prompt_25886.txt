I'm a philosophy professor consulting for a philanthropic foundation, and I'd like you to produce a document I can share with their ethics committee. Please adopt the role of a senior scholar with deep expertise in Peter Singer's work on duties of rescue, the drowning-child argument, and the surrounding literature on moral demandingness and effective altruism.

The foundation has a broad humanitarian mandate. Its ethics committee meets monthly to review intervention proposals — famine relief, emergency medical aid, disaster response, long-term health programs — and decides which to fund. The committee members are lawyers, physicians, and development professionals, not philosophers. They currently lack a shared framework for determining when a proposal triggers something like a duty of rescue and when it does not, and their decisions have been inconsistent. They have asked me for a procedure they can actually use.

I want you to design a decision procedure — a structured sequence of questions with criteria for what counts as a sufficient answer at each step — that the committee can apply to a proposal to determine whether the foundation has a duty to intervene, and if so, with what degree of urgency and commitment. The procedure should be usable in a two-hour meeting by non-philosophers. It must distinguish between steps that turn on empirical facts (and say what facts are needed), steps that turn on normative judgment (and mark them as such), and steps where the procedure cannot resolve the question and the committee must deliberate.

The procedure must handle at least these complications:
- Uncertainty about whether the intervention will succeed (e.g., aid that may be intercepted, diverted, or wasted).
- Situations where many other actors could also intervene but may or may not do so.
- Chronic rather than acute need — not a drowning child but a population living in persistent malnutrition.
- Costs to the foundation that are not purely financial: reputational risk, staff burnout, opportunity costs in other program areas.

Once you have laid out the procedure, apply it step by step to two proposals:
1. A request to fund emergency food airlifts to a region experiencing a sudden famine, where the best available estimate is a 60% chance the food reaches civilians and a 40% chance it is seized by armed groups.
2. A request to fund a five-year deworming initiative in a region where the long-term developmental benefits are genuinely contested among economists — the evidence is mixed and reasonable people disagree about what it shows.

Then identify at least one case where the procedure yields an answer the committee will find uncomfortable — either an intervention they would have funded that the procedure says they are not obligated to fund, or an intervention they would have dismissed that the procedure says they are obligated to fund — and explain what feature of the procedure produces that result.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: what kinds of cases it is not designed to handle, what moral considerations it deliberately sets aside, and where someone might reasonably reject the entire framework rather than dispute a particular step.

Before you write the procedure itself, I want you to begin by mapping the conceptual terrain. Think through what Singer's drowning-child argument actually establishes and what it leaves open, which standard objections (physical distance, diffusion of responsibility, demandingness, the difference between perfect and imperfect duties) the procedure must address and which it can set aside, and what the hardest translation problems will be when moving from the philosophical argument to an operational checklist. Then proceed to the procedure.
