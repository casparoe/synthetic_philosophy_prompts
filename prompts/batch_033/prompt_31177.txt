Two grad students in my epistemology seminar got into it after class about what the dogmatism paradox really shows, and I'm still not sure who was right. 

Quick refresher in case you haven't looked at this lately: the paradox starts from the idea that if I know P, I seem to be in a position to know that any evidence against P must be misleading. But then when someone offers me counterevidence E against P, it looks like I can reason: "I know P, so E must be misleading, so I can ignore E without even examining it." That's a dogmatic-seeming result, hence the paradox. The question is what to give up: closure (if you know P and know P entails Q, you're in a position to know Q), the idea that knowing gives you that kind of entitlement, or something else.

Anna argued the paradox is fatal to closure. If closure plus the platitude that knowledge puts you in a position to dismiss known-to-be-misleading evidence jointly imply you can rationally ignore counterevidence sight-unseen, then closure has to go.

Ben said closure isn't the problem — the problem is a confusion about *basing*. You can be in a position to know that E is misleading without it being rational to base your dismissal of E on that piece of knowledge, because you'd be relying on the very belief that E calls into question. That kind of circular reasoning doesn't transmit justification, even if the premises are known.

Anna replied that this "basing" move is a distinction without a difference. If you're genuinely in a position to know that E is misleading, then when E comes along, a responsible epistemic agent can and should reason "this must be misleading somehow" and move on — that's exactly what you do with flat-earther arguments against your well-confirmed beliefs about the shape of the planet. If being a responsible knower doesn't let you filter out noise, what does?

Ben said the flat-earth case is different because you've examined that type of evidence before. The paradox is about *unexamined* counterevidence: the whole worry is that the reasoning licenses you to dismiss evidence you haven't even looked at. Once you look, you're no longer relying on the circular inference — you've got independent grounds.

Anna came back: then the paradox isn't about knowledge at all, it's about when you're obligated to examine evidence, and that's just a pragmatic question about how to spend your time — not an epistemic paradox. Ben disagreed and said the norm against circular reasoning is epistemic, not pragmatic.

At this point their advisor walked by and said they were talking past each other because Anna was treating "in a position to know" as a deliberative license and Ben was treating it as a mere conditional fact about what's available to be known. They both made faces at that and kept arguing.
