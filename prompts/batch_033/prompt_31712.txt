I am taking a philosophy of mind course this term, and we have spent several weeks on Dennett's attack on the Cartesian theater and on his alternative, the multiple drafts model. I keep returning to the film Being John Malkovich, which seems to literalize the very picture Dennett wants to dismantle: a filing clerk discovers a small door that leads into the actor John Malkovich's head, and anyone who crawls through it sees and feels what Malkovich does, as if seated behind his eyes in a private screening room. The film, though, does not leave that picture intact. The person behind Malkovich's eyes can be displaced; a paying audience files through for fifteen minutes at a time; one character learns to take the controls and run Malkovich's body as a puppet; and the scene in which Malkovich himself enters the door and lands in a world where every face is his own and every voice can say only his name seems to push the whole idea into a kind of recursion. By the end, the boundaries among host, passenger, and puppet have broken down, and one character is left as a passenger in a mind he can watch but not steer. I would like a reading of the film through the multiple drafts model. Where does the film genuinely dramatize something like Dennett's picture, in which the self is a narrative under constant revision, distributed across many processes, with no single inner spectator to whom the whole show is presented? Where does it, despite itself, fall back on the Cartesian picture by keeping an audience and a screen in place? And what, if anything, does the film show about consciousness that the philosophical polemic misses, for example about what it means to be a character in a draft authored by someone else, or to watch your own life from inside it without being able to act? I am writing a term paper for the course and want to use the film as more than a cute illustration; I want a reading that is faithful to both the film and the philosophy, including the places where they come apart.
