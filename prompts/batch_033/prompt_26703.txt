"Present and critically compare the three main families of views on the problem of universals: realism, nominalism, and conceptualism. For each, explain its core ontological commitments and identify the most serious objection it faces."

here's what the student wrote (copied exactly):

---
The problem of universals is about how numerically distinct particulars can share the same property — e.g., how a red apple and a red fire truck can both be red, or how Socrates and Plato can both be human. There are three main positions.

Realism says universals are mind-independent entities. The strongest version is Platonic realism: universals exist in a transcendent realm of Forms, and particulars "participate" in them. This gives a clean solution to the one-over-many problem — the universal Redness is literally one thing shared by many red particulars. But Platonic realism faces the Third Man regress: if the Form of Man is itself a man (self-predication), then we need a further Form to explain what the Form and particular men share, and so on infinitely. Aristotelian realism avoids this by locating universals inside the particulars themselves (in re rather than ante rem), but then it struggles to explain how one and the same universal can be wholly present in many spatially scattered particulars at once.

Nominalism denies universals exist; there are only particulars. The simplest version is predicate nominalism: a particular is red just in case the predicate "is red" truly applies to it. But this gets the order of explanation backwards — predicates apply to things because those things share a property; they don't share a property because a predicate applies to them. A more sophisticated nominalist approach is trope theory: properties themselves are particulars. My apple's redness is a trope, the fire truck's redness is a different trope, and what we call "sharing a property" is just the exact resemblance between the two tropes. The problem is that resemblance itself seems to be a universal — so trope theory ends up smuggling universals back in through the back door.

Conceptualism, associated with Locke and later Kant, holds that universals exist only as ideas in the mind. We abstract general concepts from particular experiences. The obvious objection is that this makes similarity mind-dependent: it seems implausible that two rocks were not similar before any minds existed to conceive of them.
