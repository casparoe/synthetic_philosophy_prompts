I teach a seminar called "Mind and Machine" for advanced undergraduates — 16 students, one 80-minute session per week. This week we are on the symbol grounding problem. I want an activity that makes the problem visceral rather than merely intelligible.

Design a two-phase in-class exercise. In Phase 1, students work in pairs with a dictionary of made-up symbols: every symbol is defined solely in terms of other symbols in the set, with no definitions that bottom out in anything perceptual, motoric, or indexical. They are given tasks that appear answerable from the dictionary — for instance, "Which of these symbols refers to something you can eat?" or "Sort the following symbols into living and non-living things" — and must discover, through their own frustration, that they cannot settle any such question. In Phase 2, a small number of symbols receive grounding: either by being linked to objects or actions in the classroom, or by being given ostensive definitions that the students can act on. The students then revisit the same tasks and experience what changes and what does not.

Constraints: no technology beyond paper and pens; the dictionary should be a handout; the activity should take about 40 minutes, leaving 40 for debrief. The debrief should map specific moments in the students' experience onto the formal statement of the problem (Harnad, Searle's Chinese Room argument, and the challenge it poses to classical symbolic AI), distinguish the symbol grounding problem from the problem of other minds and from the Turing test as usually understood, and flag what the activity cannot show — in particular, that grounding a few base symbols does not by itself explain how grounding propagates through a network of interdefined terms, and that the activity may inadvertently suggest that grounding is sufficient for meaning when the problem is precisely about whether it is necessary.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. Likely candidates: students who already know the problem may ruin the reveal for their partners; some students may insist they could infer the meanings from the dictionary alone and need to be shown why this move begs the question; the objects or actions used for grounding may carry unintended associations that smuggle in more information than intended. For each, say how to handle it in the moment without turning the session into a lecture.
