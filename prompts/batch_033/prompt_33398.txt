I keep noticing a weird thing in ordinary conversation and I'm wondering if philosophers have a name for it, or at least a map of the territory.

The thing is this: there seem to be cases where someone says something that's almost certainly true, and they have great evidence, and everyone knows they have great evidence — but it still feels like they shouldn't have said it flat-out. The example I keep coming back to is the lottery. A friend buys a ticket, the odds are one in a million. Before the draw, I say "you're not going to win." Now, statistically I'm almost certainly right. I have every reason to believe it. But it feels like I've overstepped. If they ask "do you *know* I won't win?" I have to say no. And yet in everyday life I say things like "it's not going to rain tomorrow" based on a forecast that's also probabilistic, and nobody blinks. What's the difference? Why does the lottery case feel like I asserted something I wasn't entitled to, while a weather prediction with similar odds doesn't?

There's a related pattern I've noticed. Someone asserts something true, and they believed it, and they had what seemed like good evidence — but the connection between their evidence and the truth was a fluke. Like the stopped clock: you glance at a clock that's stopped, it happens to show the right time, you tell someone the time. You were right, you believed it, you had a perfectly normal reason. But the assertion still feels defective, like you got lucky in a way that undermines the whole thing. We want to say you shouldn't have said it, even though it was true and you believed it.

So I'm trying to figure out what the actual rule is that governs when you're allowed to assert something. Is the rule that you have to *know* it? If so, the lottery case is a problem — either I don't know the ticket will lose (which means I know a lot less than I think), or the knowledge rule is too permissive. Is the rule that you have to be *certain*? That's way too strong, we'd never say anything. Is it that you have to have justification that's good enough for the conversational context? Then what makes the lottery context different from the weather context?

I'm not just after a label. I want to know: what are the main candidate norms for assertion, what are the standard counterexamples that each one struggles with, and is there a position that handles both the lottery case and the stopped-clock case cleanly? If there's a term of art for the lottery-type problem specifically, I'd take that too — but I mostly want the shape of the debate, not the vocabulary.
