I keep noticing this thing and I don't know if it has a name. So here's the pattern: if someone tells me the capital of Mongolia is Ulaanbaatar, I just believe them and move on. If they tell me a restaurant has good food, I might trust them enough to go try it. But if they tell me a novel is brilliant, or a piece of music is beautiful, there's this weird resistance — not just "I'm not sure I'll agree," but something stronger, like there's something almost inappropriate about forming the belief on their say-so. Like if I said "I know Mahler's Ninth is a masterpiece because my friend told me," people would look at me funny, even if my friend is a professional conductor. And the thing is, I feel the resistance myself: even when I completely trust someone's taste, I still feel like I haven't earned the judgment unless I've experienced the thing firsthand. But I don't feel that way about, say, moral judgments — if someone I respect tells me a certain policy is unjust, I'm way more comfortable adopting that view without doing all the reasoning myself. So there's an asymmetry between aesthetic testimony and other kinds, but also between aesthetic testimony and moral testimony specifically, which is weird because both are "normative." Is there an established name for this whole cluster — the resistance to aesthetic testimony, and especially the contrast with moral testimony? And if there is, does the standard account of it actually explain why moral deference feels less problematic than aesthetic deference, or does it mostly just focus on the aesthetic side?
