I've been turning over a puzzle about promising since a friend asked me to keep a secret that I later realized I shouldn't have agreed to keep. I told her I would, and I meant it at the time, but the secret turned out to involve something I think I had an obligation to disclose. I broke the promise. But I've been unable to settle whether I did something wrong, and my attempts to think it through have produced an argument that seems to show that promising either doesn't bind at all or binds in far too many cases. I need help locating the mistake.

(1) If a promise obligates me to do X, that obligation must arise from some feature of the act of promising — call it the obligation-grounding feature.

(2) There are, as far as I can see, exactly three candidates for this feature: (a) the expectations I create in the promisee, (b) the act of will by which I commit myself, or (c) the social practice of promise-keeping and the harm to that practice if I break my word.

(3) Candidate (a) overgenerates. I can create expectations without promising — by stating an intention, making a prediction, or simply behaving in a way that leads others to rely on me. If expectations alone ground promissory obligation, then I am bound by all of these in exactly the same way I am bound by a promise. But we don't treat casual expressions of intent as having the same moral weight as promises, and it seems wrong to do so.

(4) Candidate (b) undergenerates. If my obligation depends on my actually having willed the commitment, then I can escape any promise by revealing that I didn't sincerely intend it — I had my fingers crossed, I was speaking ironically, I was lying. But we hold people to what they said, not to what they privately intended. A sincere promise and an insincere one, indistinguishable to the promisee, cannot differ in their binding force without making promising useless.

(5) Candidate (c) undergenerates in a different way. If the obligation depends on the value of the social practice, then in any case where breaking my promise wouldn't damage the practice — a promise made in secret, a promise to someone who has died, a promise whose breach no one will ever discover — the obligation disappears. But we think deathbed promises bind, and we think secret promises bind.

(6) If each candidate either overgenerates or undergenerates, and the list is exhaustive, then no feature can ground promissory obligation. But if no feature can ground it, then promises don't bind.

(7) That conclusion is unacceptable. I have been wronged by broken promises, and I have felt the weight of my own. So the argument must go wrong somewhere.

Where is the mistake? Is the trilemma in (2) genuinely exhaustive, or is there a fourth candidate I'm missing? If one of the three candidates can be defended against the charge of overgenerating or undergenerating, which one, and what does that defense require us to accept that we might find uncomfortable? I should say that I'm inclined to keep candidate (b) — the will view — if it can be saved, because it seems to capture something about promises being voluntary. But I want to know what I'd be committing myself to.
