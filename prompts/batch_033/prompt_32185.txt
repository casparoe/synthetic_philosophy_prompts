I recently read John Taurek's paper "Should the Numbers Count?" and found it unsettling in a way I'm still trying to articulate. Afterwards, I mentioned it to a friend in my philosophy reading group, and she sent me this summary of what she took the paper to be saying:

"Taurek argues that when you can save either one person or five people, you're morally required to flip a coin rather than simply saving the five. He thinks that saving the larger group would be a form of disrespect toward the one, because it treats that person's life as less valuable than the lives of the five. His argument is that since each person cares just as much about their own life, there's no impartial perspective from which five deaths are worse than one—numbers don't aggregate across persons. So the only fair thing to do is to give everyone an equal fifty-percent chance of being saved via a coin toss. The obvious problem is that this is insane: if a building is burning and you can run in and save either five children or one, you just save the five. The fact that Taurek's conclusion is this wildly counterintuitive shows that there must be something wrong with his premise about non-aggregation."

Something about her summary struck me as not quite capturing what I read, but I'm not confident enough in my grasp of the paper to pinpoint the discrepancies. I'd like you to go through her summary sentence by sentence and tell me which parts are accurate representations of Taurek's view, which are subtly off, and what a careful defender of Taurek would object to. In particular, I'm interested in whether her final move—dismissing the whole position because the conclusion seems absurd—survives a more precise statement of what Taurek actually claims. If the accurate version of Taurek's view is less vulnerable to that objection, I'd like to understand why.
