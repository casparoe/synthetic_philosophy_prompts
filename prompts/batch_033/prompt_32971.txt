Alright, I'm running a philosophy of mind reading group and we're about to dive into the combination problem for panpsychism. I want to give people a handout that helps them figure out where they actually stand, because honestly the labels get thrown around a lot and half the time people are arguing past each other.

You're an expert on philosophy of mind, especially the panpsychism literature. Please think through the logical space carefully before you write anything — start by mapping out the key distinctions and the combinations of answers they allow, then locate the actual positions in that space.

The deliverable: a decision tree / taxonomy that sorts someone into a position on the combination problem. It should start from the assumption that they're at least sympathetic to panpsychism — i.e., they think consciousness might be fundamental or widespread, not just a latecomer that shows up in brains. From there, each branch should be a yes/no question about what's possible or plausible, phrased in plain English so someone can answer without already knowing what "constitutive panpsychism" or "phenomenal bonding" means.

I want the leaves to cover at least these positions:

- Constitutive panpsychism (micro-subjects literally combine into macro-subjects, via something like phenomenal bonding)
- Emergent panpsychism (macro-subjects strongly emerge from micro-subjects; they don't reduce to them)
- Cosmopsychism / priority monism (the cosmos as a whole is the fundamental subject, and our individual minds are dissociated or derivative aspects of it)
- Panprotopsychism, both constitutive and emergent variants (the fundamental stuff is proto-conscious, not fully conscious, and experience is constituted by or emerges from it)
- Micropsychism / eliminativism (only the micro-subjects are real; there are no macro-subjects like you or me, just the appearance of them)

For each leaf: give the name and a one-sentence summary of the position's most uncomfortable commitment — the thing they have to bite the bullet on.

Also flag any logically possible combinations of answers that nobody actually defends, and briefly say why (incoherent? just unattractive? unexplored?).

Some things I think you should consider as branching points:

- Whether the fundamental relata are subjects (things with their own point of view) or proto-phenomenal properties (not themselves experiences)
- Whether subjects can literally sum or combine into a new subject, or whether that's a category mistake
- Whether macro-consciousness is constituted by the micro-level or whether it strongly emerges (with new causal powers)
- Whether you need to posit a fundamental cosmic subject to avoid the subject-summing problem

If any two positions can't be separated by the tree, say so and explain why. Don't paper over it.

The audience is smart non-specialists — grad students in other fields mostly, who've done a bit of philosophy of mind reading. The tree should fit on a couple pages. Oh, and write it in a casual tone, like you're talking them through it — it's okay if there's a typo or two.
