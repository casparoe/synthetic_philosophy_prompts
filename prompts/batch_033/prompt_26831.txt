At lunch two of my officemates got into it over a hiring comittee's decision. The committee had three members and the rule was that a candidate is hired just in case a majority finds both excellent research and excellent teaching. The members' votes on the two criteria were: member 1 yes on research, no on teaching; member 2 no on research, yes on teaching; member 3 yes on both. So members 1 and 2 each concluded don't hire, and member 3 concluded hire. That means a majority said yes on research (1 and 3), a majority said yes on teaching (2 and 3), but a majority said don't hire (1 and 2). My one officemate, Dana, said the committee's position is inconsistent: they're on record saying the candidate is excellent at both, and the rule says that's enough, so they should have hired her. My other officemate, Priya, said that's a category mistake: each member voted coherently, nobody contradicted themselves, and the committee as a body doesn't have beliefs, it just has a decision, which was no. Dana came back that the committee is now committed to three claims — excellent research, excellent teaching, and don't hire — that can't all be true given their own rule, and a body that asserts P, Q, and not-(P and Q) is in trouble. Priya said that only follows if you treat the committee like a single mind; real committees only make a decision, and the decision was no, so there's nothing to repair. Dana said fine, but then the outcome is an artifact of procedure: if they'd voted on the bottom line first, the answer is no, and if they'd voted on the criteria and applied the rule, the answer is yes — the procedure, not the evidence, picks the answer. Priya said right, so pick a procedure, but there's no neutral way to read "the majority's will" here. They asked me to referee and I couldn't. Please go through the exchange and say who's right about which points, where each goes wrong if anywhere, and what, if anything, they're both missing.
