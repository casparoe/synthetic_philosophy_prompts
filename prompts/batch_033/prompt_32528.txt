I am a novelist at work on a book whose central conceit is a modern reimagining of Nietzsche's doctrine of eternal recurrence. The protagonist is a mid-career philosopher of mind who, after a series of unsettling experiences with predictive AI systems, becomes convinced that the universe is a simulation that runs in a closed causal loop — that every conscious moment, down to the last qualitative shade, has already occurred and will occur again, identically, because the simulation resets when it reaches its terminal state. The novel is not an academic treatise, but I want the philosophical architecture to be rigorous enough that a reader who knows Nietzsche would feel the book is in genuine dialogue with him, not merely borrowing his terminology.

This has led me to a question I cannot answer on my own. Nietzsche formulated the eternal recurrence as a kind of existential test: what if a demon whispered to you that you would have to live your life over again, every detail the same, infinitely many times? The question was whether you could affirm this prospect, not whether you believed it as a cosmological claim. But the simulation-loop scenario my protagonist confronts is different in at least two ways. First, it is presented not as a hypothetical thought experiment but as a putatively true description of reality — a discovery, not a test. Second, it introduces a designer or a substrate: the simulation has an architecture, perhaps a purpose, perhaps none. Nietzsche's own framing involved no such thing; his demon was a rhetorical device, and his cosmology, insofar as he developed one, leaned on finite matter in infinite time, not on a programmed substrate.

I would like you to do something speculative but disciplined: predict what Nietzsche's position would have been on the simulation version of eternal recurrence had he encountered it. Specifically, address the following sub-questions. Would he have regarded the simulation hypothesis as a legitimate extension of his thought, or as a fundamental distortion of it? Would the presence of a simulator — however remote or indifferent — undermine the anti-metaphysical, this-worldly spirit of his philosophy, or could he have absorbed it into his project? And most importantly, would the ethical weight of the recurrence change if it were a feature of a designed system rather than a brute fact of nature? In other words, would the imperative to affirm one's life lose or gain force under the simulation reading? Please also indicate how confident you are in each major claim you make, and single out the point you are least sure of.
