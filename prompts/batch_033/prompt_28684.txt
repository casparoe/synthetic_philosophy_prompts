I serve on my university's Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. We have well-established policies governing research on vertebrate subjects, but a recent neuroscience proposal has exposed a gap in our framework that we are not sure how to fill. The principal investigator proposes to study learning and memory consolidation in the common octopus (Octopus vulgaris), using protocols—electrode implantation, food-deprivation motivation, and terminal procedures—that would trigger full committee scrutiny and require substantial justification if the subjects were mice or zebra finches. The PI argues that octopuses, however behaviorally sophisticated, are invertebrates, and our current policies simply do not classify them as regulated species. She further contends that extending vertebrate-style protections to cephalopods would set an arbitrary precedent: if we regulate octopus research on the basis of behavioral flexibility and neural complexity, consistency would seem to demand that we eventually regulate work on honeybees (which display symbolic communication and transitive inference), then on fruit flies (which exhibit sleep-like states and attentional processes), and so on down a slope toward protecting any organism that behaves flexibly. Several committee members are sympathetic to this worry but suspect the argument proves too much. Others point to the growing body of evidence for cephalopod cognition—episodic-like memory, means-end reasoning, and what some neuroscientists describe as a distributed form of consciousness—and argue that the vertebrate/invertebrate distinction is morally irrelevant and scientifically outdated.

Beneath this practical dispute lies a philosophical issue we seem poorly equipped to address: is sentience, and the moral status that accompanies it, a binary property that an organism either possesses or lacks, or does it come in degrees, such that our regulatory framework should reflect gradations of moral considerability rather than a simple on/off switch? The chair has asked me, as the committee member with formal training in philosophy, to draft a written opinion for our next meeting. The opinion should recommend a concrete policy for evaluating invertebrate research proposals going forward, engage seriously with the binary-versus-graded-status question, propose criteria the committee can actually operationalize, and give an honest treatment of the strongest objection to whatever approach I end up recommending. Please write the opinion in American English.
