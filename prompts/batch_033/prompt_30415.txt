The debate between moral particularists and generalists has been running since at least the early 1990s, from Dancy's Moral Reasons through McKeever and Ridge's Principled Ethics, Väyrynen's work on thick concepts, and the Lance and Little exchange — and yet the positions appear to have settled into a stable standoff with no clear resolution and no widely shared account of what resolving it would require. I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is, not a verdict on which side is correct. Is the impasse a sign that the two camps are answering different questions — one about the metaphysics of moral reasons (are their valences atomistic or holistic across contexts?), the other about the epistemology of moral judgment (are principles necessary for reliable moral cognition?)? Is there a hidden empirical disagreement about whether moral competence is codifiable, and if so, which side does the psychological evidence on moral cognition actually favor? Are particularists and generalists advancing a substantive ontological claim that only looks verbal because each side can redescribe the other's cases in its own theoretical vocabulary without loss? Or is this one of those disputes where the starting intuitions about what to do with a hard case — the particularist seeing a novel moral gestalt, the generalist reaching for a covering principle — simply diverge at a level prior to argument? Ground your diagnosis in what representative figures on each side actually maintain, and then say what would, given that diagnosis, count as genuine progress: a conceptual distinction that dissolves the apparent conflict, an empirical finding, a formal result about the structure of reasons, or a recognition that the question as framed is ill-posed. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
