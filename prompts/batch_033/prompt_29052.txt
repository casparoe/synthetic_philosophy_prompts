I'm an engineering director at a mid-size software company, and about once a week I get pitched a feature where someone on the team says 'we can just compute that.' I have maybe twenty minutes per pitch, a CS undergrad from a while back, and no real theory background, and I'm the one who has to sign off or send it back. I want a reusable triage procedure I can actually run, grounded in what computational complexity really tells us about where procedures hit walls — the gap between 'there exists an algorithm' and 'there is one we can actually run,' hardness results as a kind of impossibility theorem about resources rather than logic, worst-case versus typical instances, and what approximation can and can't buy you, and where a heuristic with no guarentee is still the sane choice. Not just 'NP-hard means impossible.' Write it as questions to ask in order, with what counts as a good enough answer at each step, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands off to a person because the step is a judgment call or a values call rather than a theorem — like whether a 5% error is acceptable for this product. Make sure it handles the hard case where a problem is NP-hard in the worst case but the team swears their instances are always small or easy. Then run it on three proposals: (1) a route optimizer for a delivery fleet with a hard 200-stop limit; (2) a clustering feature for a million-user dataset where the team says they'll just use k-means; (3) a fairness constraint the team wants to add to an existing recommendation model but they can't say what it does to the objective. Include at least one case where the procedure tells me to kill a feature I actually want, and say straight out what the procedure can't catch — like a problem that's technically easy but has a hidden data problem, or a team that's sandbagging the input size. And write it in American English, please.
