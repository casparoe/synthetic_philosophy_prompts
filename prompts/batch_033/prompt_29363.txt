I run a psychology and decision-making discussion group at my high school — about 12 to 15 students, mostly juniors and seniors, and we meet for one hour after school. I want to design an activity where participants make real betting choices under uncertainty and end up demonstrating the Ellsberg paradox on themselves before they know what it is. The core idea is that when people choose between a bet with known probabilities and a bet with unknown probabilities, they systematically avoid the unknown — even when that pattern of choices cannot be justified by any single consistent assignment of underlying probabilities to the unknown case. I would like the activity to use opaque bags or boxes with colored tokens so the choices feel tangible, and I want a way to collect everyone's bets anonymously before we reveal the overall pattern. No lecture up front, and the name "Ellsberg paradox" should not come up until the debrief.

Please give me the full setup: a materials list I can assemble cheaply, the exact wording I should use when presenting each round of choices to the group, a method for recording responses, and a detailed debrief that walks from what the group actually chose to the formal structure of the paradox — including what the sure-thing principle says and why the group's pattern of choices most likely violates it. Also tell me what predictably goes wrong when people run this kind of activity (do some participants not exhibit the effect, do they try to reason their way out of it mid-activity, does the order of rounds matter), and be honest about which parts of the theoretical landscape the activity cannot capture — for instance, the difference between the Ellsberg paradox and the Allais paradox, or the live debate about whether ambiguity aversion is a mistake to be corrected or a reasonable response to missing information — and what I should supply myself after the debrief to fill those gaps.
