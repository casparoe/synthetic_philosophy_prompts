I'm a journalist on the science and ideas beat, and I've been reading epistemology for a piece about how we should update our beliefs in the face of new evidence. I tripped over the dogmatism paradox and I can't shake it. Here's the argument, as I've reconstructed it:

(1) If I know that p, then p is true. (That's just the factivity of knowledge — no controversy there.)

(2) If I know that p, then for any evidence E that would, if accurate, make not-p likely, I can know in advance that E is misleading. Because p is true, and misleading evidence is just evidence that points away from the truth.

(3) If I know that some evidence is misleading, I'm rationally entitled to dismiss it without examining it. Why would I spend time evaluating something I already know is wrong?

(4) So: if I know that p, I can rationally dismiss any counterevidence to p sight unseen.

(5) But that's absurd. A person who dismisses counterevidence without looking is a dogmatist, not a rational agent. We think rational people should at least glance at the evidence against their views.

(6) So either we don't actually know most of the things we think we know (a skeptical conclusion), or one of the steps above is wrong.

Each premise feels individually plausible. (1) is barely worth stating. (2) seems like a natural consequence of knowing something — if I know my colleague didn't embezzle, and you hand me a folder of documents that appear to show she did, I know those documents are misleading. (3) just says you don't have to investigate things you already know are false. And yet (4) and (5) can't both be true, and (6) is a bad place to land.

I want to know: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If you give up (2), what else in epistemology breaks? If you give up (3), how do you explain why I should examine evidence I already know is misleading? I'm trying to figure out wether this is a genuine paradox with real teeth or a trick of wording that dissolves under scrutiny. Keep the response to 600 words, max.
