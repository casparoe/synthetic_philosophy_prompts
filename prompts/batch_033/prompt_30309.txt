**Honoring Sunk Costs Isn't Always a Fallacy**

The standard line on sunk costs is pretty unforgiving. Once you've spent money, time, or effort on something, that investment is gone — you can't get it back. So when you're deciding whether to continue, you should ignore those sunk costs entirely and just look at the marginal costs and benefits going forward. To do otherwise is to commit the sunk cost fallacy, and you'll find this in every introductory economics textbook alongside terms like "irrational" and "cognitive bias."

I think this is too quick. There are at least three kinds of cases where honoring sunk costs — i.e., letting the fact that you've already invested in something influence your decision to keep investing — is perfectly rational, even on the standard economic picture. The error isn't in honoring sunk costs; it's in having a too-narrow model of what the relevant payoffs are.

First, sunk costs as commitment. Suppose I know I have a tendency to abandon projects halfway through — I get excited, start something, then my enthusiasm fades and I want to move on to the next thing. If I'm aware of this pattern, I face a problem: I can't credibly commit to finishing things that would be worth finishing. One way to solve this is to adopt a policy of honoring sunk costs. By telling myself "I've already put in three months, so I should see this through," I'm using the sunk cost as a kind of Schelling point for when to persist. This isn't irrational — it's a second-order strategy for overcoming a first-order weakness. The alternative would be to constantly re-optimize and predictably quit too early every time.

Second, sunk costs as evidence. The fact that I invested a lot in a project is itself a piece of information about how much I valued the project at the time of investment. If I now feel like quitting, I should ask: did my circumstances change, or did my mood change? Past-me made a decision with some information and some preferences; present-me has a different mood and different framing. If there's no new material information but I just feel less motivated, the sunk costs serve as a useful signal that past-me thought this was worth it, and past-me might have been in a better epistemic position — less tired, less influenced by temporary frustration, more aware of the original reasons. Treating sunk costs as totally irrelevant throws away this potentially valuable signal.

Third, sunk costs and social legibility. We don't operate in a vacuum. If I develop a pattern of abandoning things, the people around me notice, and they adjust their behavior accordingly. They become less willing to collaborate with me on long-term projects, less willing to invest their own resources alongside mine, less willing to take my stated intentions seriously. In game-theoretic terms, honoring sunk costs is part of maintaining a reputation type in a repeated game. The "rational" thing of ignoring sunk costs in each isolated decision may be globally irrational because it destroys cooperative equilibria you could have sustained.

Now, you might object that in all three cases, what I'm calling "honoring sunk costs" is really just optimizing over a larger set of goods — commitment value, informational value, reputational value — and the sunk costs themselves aren't doing any normative work. They're just correlated with these other goods. But I think that objection misses something: the sunk costs are the mechanism through which these other goods are realized. You can't get the commitment benefit without actually treating sunk costs as reason-giving; you can't get the epistemic benefit without actually letting past investment weigh in your deliberations; you can't sustain the reputation without actually being the kind of person who finishes things because they started them. The phenomenology of treating sunk costs as mattering is part of what makes the strategy work.

I'm not saying the sunk cost fallacy doesn't exist. Obviously people throw good money after bad in ways that are genuinely destructive — staying in failing relationships, continuing doomed projects, doubling down on bad bets. And in those cases, the mechanisms I'm describing are either absent or outweighed. But I think the blanket injunction to "ignore sunk costs" is bad advice in a broader sense: it treats as a bug something that, in the right circumstances, is a feature of human decision-making architecture.
