I'm a novelist, and I host a small podcast about puzzles, gambles, and the weird ways people reason — mostly because I keep putting those things in my books. Next week I'm interviewing a philosopher who defends a specific line on the two-envelope paradox: the whole puzzle comes from quietly assuming a uniform prior over the possible dollar amounts, and once you drop that assumption there's nothing left for expected utility theory to apologize for. I'm writing a novel right now about a charachter who keeps switching envelopes, so I want to push this guest hard, not let him restate the view.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions for me? They should press where the philsoophy literature actually presses. The areas I want covered are:

- The local argument after you open. You open your envelope, see $10, and it feels like the other one is equally likely to be $20 or $5, so switching has positive expected value. Where exactly does the 50/50 step do illicit work, and why should a one-shot real-world decision need a prior over all possible amounts at all? If he gives the likely answer that the 50/50 just is a uniform prior in disguise, write the follow-up that pushes on that.
- The before/after asymmetry. Before opening, the setup is symmetric, so switching can't be strictly better than staying; after opening, the expected-value argument says switch. Why should seeing a number that doesn't tell you whether you're holding the larger or smaller amount turn a neutral act into a strictly better one? If he says the amount you see does matter once you condition on a prior, write the follow-up.
- The one-shot finite case with real cash. If he were handed two envelopes right now, looked in one, and saw an ordinary sum, would he refuse to give a flat answer about switching? Doesn't saying the problem is under-specified leave everyday decisions paralyzed? If he replies that in real life you'd have background beliefs about how much money people put in envelopes, write the follow-up.
- The infinite-expectation variants, the St. Petersburg-flavored ones where always switching can be licensed by expected-value reasoning. Does his uniform-prior diagnosis still handle those, or is the real culprit infinite expectation, and is the paradox being relocated rather than dissolved? If he says those are a different problem, write the follow-up.
- Blind switching. You decide before looking that you'll switch, then notice the same reasoning applies to the new envelope and licenses switching back, forever. What does his view say goes wrong in that loop, and why isn't it a reductio of expected-value reasoning itself? If he says the same illicit 50/50 gets reused on each envelope in turn, write the follow-up.
- What would count against him. Would a proper prior with finite mean where switching is still favored conditional on every amount you might see be a counterexample, and is such a thing even constructible? If he says unconditional symmetry rules it out unless the mean is infinite, write the follow-up pressing on whether his view is leaning on a finite-mean assumption he hasn't defended.
- A closer on what the paradox has taught us beyond the lesson that improper priors are trouble. Is there any residual psychological or philosophical puzzle left, or is it fully defused?

Make each follow-up specific to the answer he's most likely to give, not a generic why-do-you-think-that. And please write the whole thing in American English — my producer is a stickler about it.
