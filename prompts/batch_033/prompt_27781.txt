So I've been covering evolutionary biology for a few years now and one thing that keeps bugging me is the adaptationism debate — the one Gould and Lewontin kicked off in 1979 with the spandrels paper, which has been going in circles ever since. Every few years someone writes a big review declaring it settled, then someone else writes a reply saying absolutely not, and we're back where we started. The weird thing is, if you actually read the papers on both sides, everyone seems to agree on the basics. Nobody thinks every trait is an adaptation. Nobody denies natural selection is a big deal. Everyone acknowledges there are developmental constraints and drift and byproducts. So what's the actual fight about?

My working theory — and I'm a journalist, not a biologist, so treat this as a hypothesis — is that the disagreement isn't really about evolutionary biology. It's about the burden of proof. When you look at some interesting trait, should your default be that selection probably shaped it, or should you start from a null of neutrality and make the adaptationist do the work? And that's not an empirical question, it's a methodological one, maybe even a temperamental one. But I'd like someone who actually knows this litterature to diagnose it properly. Is it a verbal dispute about what counts as "adaptationism"? A genuine empirical disagreement about how often selection versus drift explains complex traits? A clash of epistemic values — some people want bold explanatory theories, others want strict hypothesis testing? Or is it, as I suspect, mostly about where the burden of proof sits, and neither side can argue the other into a different starting point?

And whatever your diagnosis is, tell me what would actually move it. A new kind of data? A formal result? Or is this just one of those things where the two camps are going to keep publishing until they retire and the next generation won't even understand what the fight was about?
