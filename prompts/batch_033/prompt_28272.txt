Exam question:
"Explain Grice's distinction between what a speaker says and what a speaker conversationally implicates. In doing so, explain the Cooperative Principle and the four categories of conversational maxims, and describe the reasoning by which a hearer derives a conversational implicature. Then analyze the following exchange: A asks, 'Did you enjoy the talk?', and B replies, 'The slides were legible.' What, if anything, does B conversationally implicate, and how is the implicature derived?"

Student's answer:
"Grice distinguishes what is said from what is implicated. What is said is the proposition the speaker expresses, and it is fixed entirely by the conventional meanings of the words uttered together with their syntactic arrangement. What is conversationally implicated goes beyond this: it is part of what the speaker means but not part of what is said.

Conversation, for Grice, is governed by a Cooperative Principle: roughly, a speaker should contribute what the conversation calls for at that point, given its accepted purpose or direction. Under this fall four categories of maxims. Quantity: give the right amount of information — neither too little nor too much. Quality: say only what you take to be true and what you have adequate evidence for. Relation: be relevant. Manner: be clear — avoid obscurity, avoid ambiguity, be brief, and be orderly.

A conversational implicature arises when the hearer, assuming the speaker is following the Cooperative Principle, infers a further proposition that would make the utterance conform to the maxims; the clearest case is when the speaker blatantly flouts a maxim, expecting the hearer to notice the flout and look for a cooperative interpretation. In the example, B's remark flouts Relation, since the legibility of the slides is not a direct answer to A's question. Assuming B is being cooperative, A infers that B did not enjoy the talk: the slides were the only good thing, so the talk itself was poor. Because the implicature is inferred rather than stated, B could cancel it by adding 'but I actually did enjoy it'; that would not be a contradiction, but it would withdraw the suggestion. The same mechanism explains words like 'but': in 'He is poor but honest,' the hearer infers from the maxims that there is a contrast between poverty and honesty, which is why 'but' and 'and' are not interchangeable."
