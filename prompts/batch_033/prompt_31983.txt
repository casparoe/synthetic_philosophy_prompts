I've been reading Darwin's The Descent of Man on my own — not for a course, just out of curiosity — and I've been particularly struck by his account of how the moral sense could have evolved. As I understand it, Darwin argues that any social animal with sufficient intellectual powers would eventually develop something like a conscience: social instincts like sympathy and loyalty get reinforced by memory, language, and the desire for approval, and over time this produces the sense of obligation we call morality. It's not a gift from above; it's a natural outgrowth of living in groups.

Around the same time, I reread Lord of the Flies, and I can't stop thinking about the two side by side. Golding gives us a group of British schoolboys — presumably raised with the same moral training — who are stranded on an island and, within weeks, have descended into hunting, tribal warfare, and murder. The conch gets smashed, Piggy gets killed, and by the end they're hunting Ralph with sharpened sticks. If Darwin is right that morality is rooted in our social instincts, why does it collapse so quickly? Or does the novel actually show something Darwin would have predicted — that the moral sense depends on the right social conditions and can unravel when those conditions are removed?

I'd like a reading of Lord of the Flies through Darwin's theory of the moral sense. Where does the novel dramatize something Darwin got right about the origins and fragility of morality? Where does it challenge what feels like a certain optimism in Darwin — the idea that the moral sense, once developed, becomes a stable internal guide that operates even when no one is watching? And what should we make of the ending, when the naval officer arrives and sees the boys as just playing games, while his own warship sits offshore? Does that final image suggest that morality is always imposed from outside, or is Golding doing something more complicated?

I'm not looking for a "Darwin was right, Golding was wrong" kind of answer, or vice versa. I'm interested in what the novel adds to the philosophical picture — what it shows about the moral sense that a treatise like The Descent of Man can't quite capture, and what Darwin's framework reveals about the novel that a casual reader might miss.
