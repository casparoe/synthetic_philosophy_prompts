I'm preparing a unit for an undergraduate aesthetics course on the Harlem Renaissance, and I keep returning to the tension between W. E. B. Du Bois's essay "Criteria of Negro Art" (published in The Crisis in 1926) and Nella Larsen's novel Passing (1929). Du Bois argues there that all art is propaganda in the sense that it inevitably serves or obstructs some cause, and that the right question to put to a Black artist's work is whether it advances the struggle for racial equality; he is also careful to insist that this demand does not mean giving up on truth or beauty. Passing is usually taught as an ambiguous, psychologically interior novel: Irene Redfield and Clare Kendry, two light-skinned Black women, one of whom passes for white; Clare's restless desire to move between the two worlds; and the climactic fall from the window, which the novel leaves radically underdetermined. I want to teach the two texts together, but I am not yet sure whether Du Bois's criterion illuminates the novel or simply convicts it.

I would like you to act as a philosopher of art who knows both texts well. Start by working out, in your own terms, what Du Bois's propaganda criterion actually demands of a novel like Passing: resist the cheap reading on which propaganda is mere slogan and Du Bois is an enemy of beauty, and state the criterion in a form he would recognize. Then give me a committed reading rather than a survey of options: does Passing, on balance, meet Du Bois's criterion, subvert it, or complicate it in a way that shows the criterion itself needs refinement? Draw on particular scenes, choices, and narrative decisions — the withholding of Irene's motives, the handling of passing, the ending — and be explicit about the moments where the novel resists your reading and an honest critic would have to concede the mismatch. Do not summarize the plot or quote from either text; assume I know both. I want the argument for a definite position, with the reasons that make it contestable and worth taking seriously.
