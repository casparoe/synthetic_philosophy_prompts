I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of mathematics, and I am writing the final exam. I want one 90-minute essay question on Skolem's paradox. The question should be designed so that a student cannot earn a passing grade merely by stating the downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem and then announcing that the apparent paradox dissolves. It should force them to do three things in sequence: state the theorem precisely enough to see how it applies to ZFC; locate the apparent contradiction — a countable model satisfying the formal statement that there exists an uncountable set — and explain in formal terms why this is not a contradiction, attending to the fact that the bijection witnessing countability exists only outside the model and is not among the model's functions; and finally, take and defend a position on what the paradox shows about the absoluteness of "countable" and about whether first-order set theory can pin down a unique intended universe of sets.

Please write the exam question itself, a model answer of roughly 650 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 marks. The marking scheme should separate an A from a B answer by naming the specific moves each must contain — for instance, the distinction between countability inside the model and countability from the metatheoretic standpoint, and the role of the model's restricted quantifiers in making the nonexistence claim true. It should also identify the specific mistakes to penalize, such as treating the paradox as evidence that ZFC or first-order logic is inconsistent, conflating the model's natural numbers with the real natural numbers, or saying that the countable model simply "leaves out" the real power set without explaining what that means formally.
