Exam Question (from a midterm in Philosophy of Mind):

Dennett proposes heterophenomenology as the proper method for a science of consciousness. Explain what the method consists in, how it differs from (a) first-person phenomenological approaches and (b) behaviorism, and what metaphysical commitments about the nature of conscious experience it does or does not carry. In particular, address whether heterophenomenology is fairly described as eliminativism in disguise.

Student Answer:

Heterophenomenology is Dennett's proposed method for studying consciousness scientifically. The researcher collects subjects' verbal reports about their conscious experiences—what they say they are feeling, seeing, thinking—and treats these reports as data. The key move is that the researcher does not assume the reports are true: the subject might be mistaken or confabulating. Instead, the researcher adopts the intentional stance toward the subject, interpreting the reports as expressions of the subject's beliefs about their own experience. The researcher then constructs a "heterophenomenological world"—a description of the world as it appears to the subject, populated by the items the subject seems to believe in. The scientific task is to explain how the subject's brain produces these reports, without presupposing that the items in the heterophenomenological world (qualia, a Cartesian theater, etc.) correspond to real entities in the brain.

This method differs from first-person phenomenology in that it brackets the first-person perspective. A phenomenologist tries to describe experience from the inside, as it is given; the heterophenomenologist treats such descriptions as data to be explained from the third-person perspective. It differs from behaviorism because heterophenomenology takes verbal reports seriously as evidence about the subject's mental life, whereas behaviorism either ignores them or treats them as just more physical behavior with no special epistemic status. Heterophenomenology grants that what subjects say about their experience is relevant to a science of consciousness, just not authoritative.

On the question of metaphysical commitments: heterophenomenology is methodologically neutral. It does not commit the researcher to the existence of qualia or phenomenal consciousness as ordinarily understood, but it also does not commit the researcher to denying their existence. The method simply collects reports and seeks to explain them. Dennett's own view is that the items in the heterophenomenological world will turn out not to correspond to anything real in the brain, and that the heterophenomenological world itself is a kind of useful fiction. But this is Dennett's substantive conclusion, not something built into the method. One could in principle use heterophenomenology and end up a realist about qualia.

As for the charge of eliminativism in disguise: the worry is that by treating subjects' reports as fictions and refusing to grant them authority, heterophenomenology has already decided against the reality of consciousness before the investigation begins. But this is unfair. The method treats reports as fictions only in the sense that it does not assume their truth; it does not assume their falsity either. It is an investigative stance, not a metaphysical conclusion. The method is neutral; Dennett's own eliminativism is a separate philosophical position.
