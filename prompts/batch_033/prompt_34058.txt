I'm giving a departmental colloquium next month at a philosophy department with a strong cognitive science orientation. My talk defends the claim that heterophenomenology — Dennett's proposal that the science of consciousness should treat subjects' first-person reports as a text to be interpreted from the third-person stance, suspending any assumption that those reports refer to real inner objects or properties — is not merely one method among others but the only method that can yield a genuine science of consciousness without smuggling in Cartesian assumptions about privileged access. The audience will include: two faculty who work in the phenomenological tradition and regard Dennett as having never understood what phenomenology actually is; three empirically-minded philosophers of mind who are broadly sympathetic to the hard problem and think something like Chalmers's or Nagel's framing is ineliminable; a neuroscientist who uses introspective reports in her experimental designs and is impatient with methodological debates that don't produce testable predictions; and a handful of graduate students trained in both analytic philosophy of mind and computational modeling, who will have read "Quining Qualia" but not much else. I've presented versions of this argument before, but never to an audience where the phenomenologists and the hard-problem people are in the same room, and I'm worried about crossfire: objections that combine their resources in ways neither group would generate alone.

Draft my Q&A prep sheet. I need the objections ordered roughly by how likely they are to arise and how much damage they'd do to my case, phrased in the vocabulary the person raising them would actually use — not caricatures. For each: a two-minute reply, a frank note on where the reply is weakest, and whether I should concede, deflect, or hold the line. Include the question I should be most relieved if nobody asks, and the question I should try to bait someone into asking because it lets me make my strongest point. I'm especially concerned about the objection that heterophenomenology cannot distinguish between a subject who is genuinely conscious and a zombie who produces identical reports — I have a reply, but I'm not sure it satisfies anyone who isn't already on board with the intentional stance. Also, the neuroscientist will want to know what heterophenomenology actually lets her do in the lab that she can't already do by just recording what subjects say and taking it at face value. If there's an objection that draws on actual Husserlian or Merleau-Ponty-style phenomenology rather than the straw-man version Dennett tends to engage with, I need to see it coming.
