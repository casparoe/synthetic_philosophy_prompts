My friend and I have been arguing about supertasks for ages, and she's got a pretty stable set of verdicts that I want to use to predict what she'll say about a new case. Her track record: on Thomson's lamp, the one that gets switched on and off infinitely many times before t=1, she says there's just no fact of the matter about whether it's on or off at t=1 — the sequence doesn't converge and that's the end of it. On Zeno's Achilles, she says he catches the tortoise, no problem: the positions converge and the infinite series sums to a finite time. On the bouncing ball that bounces infinitely many times with the heights halving, she says it comes to rest at the end, because that's the limit. On the urn where at step n you put in balls 10n+1 through 10n+10 and take out ball n, she says it's empty at the end, because every particular ball gets removed at its own step and none survives to the limit. But on the same urn with the removal order changed, so you always take out the highest-numbered ball, she says infinitely many balls remain — the ones that never get removed. So she's not one of those people who says the urn puzzle is just under-specified and the answer depends on the removal schedule; she thinks each schedule has a definite answer, and the answer changes with the schedule.

The new case I want to predict her on: at step n you put ball n into the urn and remove ball n-1 (at step 1 you just put ball 1 in). So at every finite stage there's exactly one ball in the urn, but the identity of that ball changes each step, and every particular ball gets removed one step after it's added. I keep going back and forth between empty and one-ball-but-no-fact-which-one, and I don't want to just pattern-match off the urn cases. I can see at least two rules she could be running that both fit her track record: something like take the limit of the relevant state, applied object-by-object, and say indeterminate if it doesn't converge, which would say the urn is empty; or something like take the limit of the number of balls when that limit is finite, and only fall back on individual fates when it isn't, which would say exactly one ball remains even though there's no fact which one. What would she say about this case? What's the rule she's implicitly running, what are the rival rules that fit her old verdicts just as well, and what further case would cleanly tell them apart? I care more about the structure of her rule and where its underdetermined than about the literal answer.
