I recently had a child, and not long afterward a colleague pressed a copy of John Leslie's *The End of the World* into my hands. I had encountered the doomsday argument before in passing, but reading it now, with a stake in the future I did not have before, it has lodged in my mind in a way I cannot shake. I have agreed—perhaps unwisely—to lead a session on it for a general-audience discussion group that meets at our local library. The group is bright but untrained: a retired civil engineer, two lawyers, a high-school history teacher, a nurse. They have read no formal philosophy and no probability theory beyond what they dimly recall from school. I have fifty minutes and a whiteboard.

I am not asking you to explain the doomsday argument to me. I am asking you to plan how I should explain it to them. I need the dependency structure: what concept must be in place before the next can be introduced, and why it will not land otherwise. I need a single carrying example—an analogy or scenario vivid enough that I can return to it at each pivot—that makes the reasoning feel like a discovery rather than a trick. I need a map of the misconceptions they will predictably form: the moment in the sequence where each tends to arise, why it is tempting, and how I should head it off before it hardens. And I need a check for understanding at the end—something I can ask or have them do—that will tell me whether they have grasped the argument's structure rather than merely been disturbed by its conclusion.

To be precise about what I am after: I do not want a summary of the doomsday argument, nor a list of objections and replies, nor a syllabus of further reading. I want a teaching sequence—a plan for the fifty minutes, ordered by conceptual dependency, with timing, pivots, and traps marked. Tell me what has to come first, what carries the weight, where the room will go quiet, and what, at this level and for this group, I should openly refuse to explain and say so.
