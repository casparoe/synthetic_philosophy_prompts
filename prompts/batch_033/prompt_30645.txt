I retired a few years ago from a career in physical chemistry, and I have since been working through epistemology in the way I used to work through a disputed set of measurements: I want to know which part of the apparatus is doing the work. The question I keep returning to is whether practical stakes can make a difference to knowledge itself. Let me put the target proposition precisely: there exist pairs of cases in which two subjects believe the same true proposition with equally strong evidence, and yet the subject for whom being wrong would be costly fails to know while the subject for whom nothing much is at stake knows. My own credence in that proposition — the thesis usually called pragmatic encroachment — is roughly 0.65, while a former colleague, a retired evolutionary biologist with whom I read epistemology, puts his at about 0.2.

I would like your own probability for that proposition, but I care much more about the decomposition than about the bare number. Please set out the main sub-questions on which the probability depends — for example, how much weight to give to the folk judgments elicited by high-stakes and low-stakes versions of a case; whether those patterns are better explained by the content of the word 'knows' shifting with context than by a stakes-sensitive knowledge relation; whether what moves is really outright belief, or merely one's willingness to assert 'I know'; whether knowledge must be constitutively tied to action in the way the encroachment thesis requires; and any further terms you regard as indispensable. Assign a rough probability to each, indicate how they combine into the final number, and state which term the final number is most sensitive to. Finally, tell me where a 0.65 and a 0.2 most plausibly disagree: which sub-question does a gap of that size most likely track, and what argument or evidence would move each of the two estimates most?
