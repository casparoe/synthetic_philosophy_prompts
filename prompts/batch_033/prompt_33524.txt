Question: "State Good's theorem on the value of information. Explain the proof and identify the key assumptions. Are there any situations where a rational agent might reasonably decline cost-free information, and if so, do these constitute counterexamples to the theorem?"

Student answer:

"Good's theorem states that for any rational agent who maximizes expected utility, the expected value of acquiring cost-free information is always non-negative. In other words, a rational agent should never refuse free information.

The proof is straightforward. Let a* be the agent's optimal action without the information. The expected utility of acting without information is EU(a*) = Σ_θ p(θ) u(a*, θ). Now suppose the agent receives a signal s (which could be the result of an experiment, a news report, etc.). The agent updates their beliefs to p(θ|s) via Bayes' rule and chooses the action a_s that maximizes expected utility given the new beliefs. The expected utility of having the information is Σ_s p(s) [max_a Σ_θ p(θ|s) u(a, θ)]. Since a_s is optimal given s, we have Σ_θ p(θ|s) u(a_s, θ) ≥ Σ_θ p(θ|s) u(a*, θ) for each s. Taking the expectation over s, the left-hand side is the expected utility with information, and the right-hand side simplifies to EU(a*), the expected utility without information. Therefore, the expected value of information is always non-negative.

The key assumptions are: (1) the agent maximizes expected utility, (2) the agent updates beliefs by Bayesian conditioning, (3) the information is genuinely cost-free, and (4) the agent is free to choose their action after receiving the information.

There's also a fifth assumption that's less often discussed: the theorem assumes the agent is risk-neutral. This is because it uses expected utility directly rather than applying any risk adjustment. If the agent is risk-averse, they might rationally decline free information because the information could introduce uncertainty into their decision process — they'd prefer the certainty of their current optimal action. So in that sense, risk-averse agents can have counterexamples to Good's theorem.

Additionally, the theorem assumes that the possible signals and states are known in advance so the agent can form a prior. In many real-world situations we face 'unknown unknowns' where we can't even list the possible things we might learn, and in those cases Good's theorem doesn't apply."
