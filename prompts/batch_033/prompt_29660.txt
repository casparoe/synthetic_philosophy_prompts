I work on the timing of conscious experience, and I keep returning to a distinction I would like to see dramatized rather than argued about. Write a short story built around the difference between Orwellian and Stalinesque revisions of experience.

For orientation: in the color-phi phenomenon, a subject sees a red dot flash briefly at one position and then a green dot flash briefly at an adjacent position, and reports a single dot moving continuously between them and changing color around the midpoint. The puzzle is that the reported midpoint color seems to depend on the later green flash, as if the later stimulus reached backward in time. Daniel Dennett contrasted two ways a system might produce such a report. On the Orwellian account, the subject first consciously experienced an unrevised sequence — a moving dot that was red at the midpoint, say — and that experience was subsequently rewritten in memory after the green flash arrived. On the Stalinesque account, the construction happened before anything reached consciousness: the subject's first and only conscious experience already contained the green-influenced midpoint, so there was never an unrevised experience to be overwritten. The two accounts can be adjusted to predict exactly the same reports, the same behavior, and the same phenomenology from the subject's point of view, which is precisely why the distinction is hard to cash out.

Now write the story. It may be set in a psychophysics laboratory, in a future where perceptual editing is commercially available, or in an ordinary life in which retroactive revision has begun to leak into view; the setting is yours. But the story should do philosophical work. It should make the reader feel the difference between having an experience rewritten in memory and having it rewritten in awareness, and it should press on whether that difference is real or only a way of talking. Do not settle the question in authorial asides or exposition; let the characters, the plot, and the details of the world carry the argument.
