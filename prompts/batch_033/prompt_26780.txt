Here are my gut reactions to a bunch of cases and I want to know if there's a principle that captures all of them, or at least most of them:

1. God commands that we love our neighbors and care for the poor. That seems obviously morally good, no problem there.

2. In the Old Testament, God commands the Israelites to wipe out the Amalekites — men, women, children, even livestock. I know some people say "well, God can do that, He's God," but honestly my gut says that command was morally wrong, and if the text says God commanded it, then either the text is wrong about that or I'm missing something about the context. I don't want to say genocide becomes okay just because God said so.

3. But then I think about Abraham and Isaac. God commands Abraham to sacrifice his son, and Abraham is praised for being willing to go through with it. And weirdly, I don't have the same horrified reaction to this case — I feel like there's something different about it, like Abraham's willingness shows something genuinely admirable about faith, even though if my neighbor told me God told him to kill his kid I'd call the police. Is that just inconsistent of me? Probably.

4. The classic dilemma: is murder wrong because God commands us not to murder, or does God command us not to murder because murder is wrong? If it's the first horn, morality seems arbitrary — God could have commanded the opposite and then murder would be obligatory, and that feels deeply wrong to me. But if it's the second horn, then moral standards exist independently of God and God is just really good at detecting them, which seems to demote God from being the actual source or foundation of morality.

5. My professor brought up modified divine command theory, where goodness is grounded in God's nature rather than God's commands. So God's nature is loving, that's why love is good, and God's commands flow from that fixed nature. I like this better — it avoids the arbitrariness worry because God's nature couldn't be otherwise. But here's the thing that bothers me: imagine we met an alien species whose "God" had a nature that was cruel rather than loving, and that being commanded cruelty. Would cruelty be good for them? I want to say no, cruelty is just bad, period. But then I'm back to saying moral standards are independent of God.

6. Case: someone in my actual life — say a parent — sincerely believes God is telling them to disown their gay child. I think that's morally wrong. But this person would say they're obeying God. If divine command theory is true, aren't they doing the right thing (assuming for argument's sake that God did command it, which I don't think He did, but the person believes it sincerely)? I want a theory that lets me say this person is wrong even if we bracket the factual question of what God actually commanded.

7. Last case: imagine there's no God. Does morality disappear? My gut says no — murder would still be wrong, kindness would still be good. But if I also think God is the foundation of morality (which I kind of do, as a religious person), then I should say yes, morality disappears without God. That's a tension I can't shake.
