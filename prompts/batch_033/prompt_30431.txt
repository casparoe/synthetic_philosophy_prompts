I run a small discussion group at my high school that focuses on topics in social science and behavioral economics. For the past several weeks, two of our most engaged members have been locked in a debate about information cascades: the phenomenon where people make decisions sequentially while observing earlier decisions, and sometimes a chain reaction ensues in which everyone follows the crowd even though their private information points elsewhere. One member—call her the rational-cascade camp—insists that the standard models by Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer, and Welch and by Banerjee show that abandoning your private signal can be a perfectly rational Bayesian inference: if enough predecessors chose against what your own signal suggests, you should rationally conclude that their signals collectively outweighed yours, even if each of them faced exactly the same dilemma you now face. She emphasizes that, on this picture, cascades are fragile: a single contrarian with a strong opposing signal, or a modest release of public information, can shatter a cascade quickly. The other member—call him the social-influence camp—pushes back that real-world cascades (think financial bubbles, viral trends, the spread of dubious medical practices) are rarely this tidy. He argues that the Bayesian story captures at most part of the picture, and that social conformity pressure, reputational incentives, and cognitive heuristics play an irreducible role. He also suspects that real cascades are less fragile than the rational model predicts, because once people have thrown in with the consensus they tend to rationalize it rather than remain poised to update on contrary signals.

I want them to produce an adversarial-collaboration document: something both can sign that captures what they actually agree on, formulates their disagreements precisely (in wording each side would endorse for its own position), identifies the cruxes on which the whole dispute turns, and—for each crux—describes a kind of evidence or empirical test both would accept as bearing on the question, along with each side's prediction. Please draft this document. Format it with a numbered agreed-claims section, then a disagreements table (columns: the issue, the rational-cascade position, and the social-influence position), and finally a cruxes-and-tests section. I have my own hunches about what some of the cruxes will be—I suspect one is whether adding social-pressure mechanisms to a cascade model changes the dynamics in ways the pure-Bayesian model misses, and another is whether real cascades break as easily as the rational model says they should when public information contradicts them—but please identify all the cruxes you see. Make sure the wording throughout is neutral enough that neither side could object to how its own view is characterized, and avoid letting either position get reduced to a straw man.
