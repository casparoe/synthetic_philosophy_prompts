Here's an argument that came up in a seminar, and I find it troubling because the conclusion about voting seems to spread to almost everything I do.

(1) An action is rationally justified only if its expected benefits exceed its expected costs.
(2) The expected benefit of voting in a large election is the probability that one's vote is decisive multiplied by the difference in expected value between the two outcomes.
(3) In a large electorate, the probability of being the decisive voter is astronomically small — even in a close race, it's on the order of one in hundreds of thousands or millions.
(4) So even if the stakes are enormous (preventing a war, say), the expected benefit of voting is still tiny, because an enormous value times a probability near zero yields a near-zero product.
(5) The costs of voting — time, transport, registering — are not near zero.
(6) Therefore, voting is not rationally justified.

Now here's the problem. The same reasoning seems to apply to any individual contribution to a large-scale collective outcome: recycling one bottle, reducing one's carbon footprint by a flight, donating fifty dollars to a global poverty charity, showing up to a protest, or even refraining from one act of tax evasion. In each case, the marginal impact of one person's act is negligible, and the argument would conclude the act is rationally unjustified. But if all these are rationally unjustified, then the whole domain of collective-action ethics collapses — and yet we don't think it does. So either premise (1) is too demanding, or the probability calculation in (3) smuggles in an assumption that doesn't hold in the parallel cases, or the argument is valid and we have to accept that individual contributions to collective goods are indeed irrational. But if we accept that, what's left of ethics?

I'm especially stuck on whether there's a difference between the voting case and the charity case. In the voting case, the benefit is discontinuous (you either cast the deciding vote or you don't), whereas in the charity case, the benefit might be continuous (each dollar does some expected amount of good). Does that matter, or is the expected-value calculation the same either way?
