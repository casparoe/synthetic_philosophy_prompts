Over the past several years I have followed the LessWrong and Alignment Forum debates about whether technical AI safety should organize itself around worst-case guarantees or around average-case, measured performance, and I have repeatedly had the sense that the two sides are producing arguments that pass each other by. I would like a document that forces them to converge on what they can actually jointly assert, and that makes the residual disagreement precise enough to be productive. Please write the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between (a) a worst-case-guarantees alignment researcher, who holds that a safety argument is not serious unless it bounds the probability of catastrophic behavior under adversarial conditions such as distribution shift, optimization pressure, and deliberately hostile inputs, and (b) an average-case-guarantees alignment researcher, who holds that what matters is measured performance across realistic deployments and that demanding worst-case bounds for modern learned systems is infeasible and largely beside the point. The document should be one that each side could sign without embarrassment, not a compromise that blurs their views.

Format it as follows. First, a numbered section of agreed claims, including claims about what a safety case would need to contain, what current systems do and do not give us, what the no-go results and impossibility results actually rule out, and what each side admits it has not established. Second, a disagreements table in which each row states the question, the worst-case camp's position in wording that camp would accept, the average-case camp's position in wording that camp would accept, and a note on whether the disagreement is substantive or partly verbal. Third, a crux section: for each crux, give a concrete test, experiment, or historical case that both sides agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. Candidate cruxes you might develop include whether average-case competence is robust to a system optimizing against the measures used to assess it, whether formal verification can scale to the systems that matter without altering the systems so much that the guarantee no longer transfers, whether rare catastrophic failures should dominate expected-value reasoning about existential risk, and how much weight to give to the fact that any deployed system will eventually face inputs selected by adversaries. Aim for formulations that are neither vacuous nor slanted, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not adjudicate which side is right; the deliverable is the constructive text the two camps could jointly put their names to, with the remaining disagreement made exact.
