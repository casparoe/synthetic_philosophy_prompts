I keep coming back to the fast-takeoff vs slow-takeoff debate and its weird to me how little it's moved in 15 years even though we now have way more actual data to look at (LLMs, scaling curves, the whole bit). The fast-takeoff camp points to the old intelligence-explosion argument: once a system is smart enough to improve its own code, it makes a slightly smarter version, which makes a slightly smarter version, and the thing goes vertical, so 'human-level' to 'wildly superhuman' could be days or weeks. The slow-takeoff camp points at how every real capability we've seen so far came from big teams, huge compute, and incremental engineering, and says progress will keep being continuous and spread across the whole economy rather than one system bootstrapping itself in a corner. Both sides look at the same scaling data and the same history and walk away with opposite conclusions, and I can't tell whether this is a disagreement about an empirical parameter (like returns to algorithmic improvement) or something more structural. My working theory is that 'takeoff speed' is secretly several different variables—time for a single model to self-improve, time for the world economy's growth rate to change, time from AGI to superintelligence—and the two camps are mostly talking past each other by picking different ones. Is that right, or is there a real empirical disagreement underneath? And either way, is there anything that would actually settle this, or is the question just badly posed?
