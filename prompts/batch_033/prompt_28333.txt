I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar called "Mind, Meaning, and Machine" that devotes three weeks to the symbol grounding problem. I am writing the final exam and want one essay question that cannot be answered adequately by summarizing Harnad's 1990 paper. The question should force students to distinguish three ways of understanding what the "problem" in the symbol grounding problem actually is: (i) a problem about how symbols acquire semantic content or truth conditions, (ii) a problem about how internal states come to exhibit intentionality or aboutness at all, and (iii) a problem about how an agent's internal states can be causally connected to the external world in a way that makes them count as representations of that world. These are not equivalent formulations, and different responses to the symbol grounding problem — robotic grounding, connectionist or sensorimotor approaches, hybrid symbolic-subsymbolic systems, or deflationary positions that deny there is a genuine problem — address different ones. Please write the question, a model answer of approximately 800 words, and a marking scheme. The marking scheme should specify the moves that distinguish an A answer from a B answer from a C answer, and should flag at least three common student errors (for instance, conflating the symbol grounding problem with the frame problem, treating Searle's Chinese Room argument as interchangeable with the symbol grounding problem, or assuming that adding sensory transducers automatically solves the problem without addressing what "grounding" requires).
