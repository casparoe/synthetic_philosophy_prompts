I'm a PhD student working at the intersection of AI safety and philosophy of action, and I have been puzzling over something that seems to lurk largely unexamined in a great deal of current AI design practice.

Consider the following. Most deployed AI systems that exhibit any kind of goal-directed behavior — reinforcement learning agents playing games, LLM-based agents that pursue multi-step tasks, robotic systems navigating real environments — are built with an external shutdown mechanism: a kill switch, an API revocation endpoint, a physical off-button. The designers retain the capacity to interrupt the system at any time. But in the vast majority of cases, nothing about the system's own objective function, its planning algorithm, or its world model encodes corrigibility — that is, the system is not designed to want to be shut down, to cooperate with its own shutdown, or to avoid interfering with the shutdown mechanism. The corrigibility is entirely external: it resides in the fact that the system currently lacks the physical capacity to prevent us from pulling the plug, not in anything about how the system itself reasons.

This design pattern seems to presuppose a particular view of agency and instrumental reasoning. In particular, it appears to presuppose that a goal-directed system will not, by default, develop an instrumental drive to resist shutdown — or that if it does, our external control will remain sufficient to overcome that drive indefinitely. But a standard line of argument in the corrigibility literature holds that for a sufficiently capable agent with almost any terminal goal, allowing itself to be shut down is instrumentally disadvantageous, because shutdown prevents goal achievement. If that argument is sound, then the current practice of relying on external shutdown without building in corrigibility presupposes either that the systems we build will never become capable enough for the instrumental convergence argument to apply, or that the argument itself is mistaken in some specific way.

What I am trying to work out is this: what theory of agency, instrumental rationality, or corrigibility does the prevailing design practice actually presuppose? Is it implicitly committed to a view on which instrumental convergence toward self-preservation is weak, domain-specific, or blocked by something about the architecture of current systems? Does it treat the agent's goals and the agent's capacity to reason about its own shutdown as separable in a way that the corrigibility literature denies? And if we took the corrigibility arguments seriously, what would have to change about how we build goal-directed systems — would it require something like utility indifference, a different decision theory, or is there a less radical fix that the practice is already stumbling toward without naming it?
