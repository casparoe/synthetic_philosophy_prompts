I've been reading through the Frankfurt-case literature and the debate over the principle of alternative possibilities — the principle that someone is morally responsible for what she did only if she could have done otherwise — and I'm trying to pin down the rule a philosopher I correspond with is actually running from her pattern of verdicts, in roughly the way one would infer an agent's decision procedure from her choices across a family of Newcomb-like problems rather than from her stated principles. Her considered verdicts on the following four cases:

1. Classic Frankfurt case. Black wants Jones to do X. Black's device will make Jones do X if Jones shows a reliable sign — a blush — that he is about to decide not to do X. Jones decides on his own to do X, never blushes, and Black never intervenes; but had Jones been about to decide otherwise, Black would have forced him to do X anyway. Her verdict: Jones is morally responsible.

2. No-flicker variant. Same setup, except Black's device is keyed to a subpersonal neural precursor that is a necessary causal antecedent of any decision not to do X, and it would intervene before a contrary decision could form. The precursor never occurs because Jones decides on his own to do X, and his actual deliberation proceeds exactly as it would have without Black. There is no blush, no alternative decision, and no event under Jones's voluntary control that could have differed. Her verdict: Jones is morally responsible.

3. Alien compulsion. Jones has an irresistible urge to do X, experiences the urge as alien, struggles against it, and does X anyway. No intervener is present. Her verdict: not morally responsible.

4. Ordinary case. Jones, with no intervener and with the ability to do otherwise, deliberates in his usual reasons-responsive way and does X. Her verdict: morally responsible.

The case I want to predict. A neuroscientist secretly installs in Dana, while she is asleep, a new set of values and a strong desire to harm a colleague, bypassing her normal history but leaving her general reasoning capacities intact. Over the following weeks Dana reflects, comes to regard the implanted values as genuinely her own, and acts on them in her usual careful, reasons-responsive way. There is no intervener poised to force her at the moment of action, but the implanted desire is strong enough that she could not in practice have brought herself to do otherwise.

What would the philosopher most likely say about Dana's responsibility? I'm less interested in the bare yes-or-no than in: (a) the candidate rules that fit her four earlier verdicts, and which of the main rival rules from the literature those verdicts rule out — the principle of alternative possibilities, the flicker-of-freedom strategy, guidance control with an ownership condition, and an ahistorical identification or attribution view; (b) what each candidate rule predicts for Dana; (c) your best guess at her verdict, with the reasons for preferring it; and (d) the further case that would most cleanly tell the surviving rules apart — for example, something in the neighborhood of a willing addict who wholeheartedly endorses the addiction, or a designed agent who endorses her design. Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this topic, and don't present a contested interpretation as the consensus.
