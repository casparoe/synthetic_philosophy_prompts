I am a retired physicist who has spent the last several years reading philosophy of history, and next month I will present a paper to a university history department's theory seminar. The audience consists mostly of historians of modern Europe who take it as settled that historical explanation is narrative in character and cannot be reduced to anything lawlike; they regard Hempel's covering-law model as a philosopher's mistake that has little to do with how historians actually work. Also attending are two philosophers of history, one of whom has published work defending the explanatory autonomy of narrative. My thesis, which I expect them to find obtuse, is that narrative explanations in historiography earn whatever explanatory force they possess only by depending, often tacitly, on covering-law regularities — that narrative structure itself explains nothing, and that the real explanatory work is done by the causal and counterfactual regularities embedded within it. I do not deny that historians construct narratives; I deny that narrativity contributes anything to explanation over and above those regularities.

I need a Q&A preparation sheet. Give me the six or seven objections I am most likely to face from this audience, ordered by how damaging they would be to my position if I answer them poorly. Phrase each objection the way a historian skeptical of nomological approaches would put it — in their vocabulary, reflecting their commitments. For each, provide a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and a candid note on where that reply is weakest. Include the question I should most hope nobody thinks to ask, and explain briefly what makes it dangerous.
