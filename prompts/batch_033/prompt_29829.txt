I write literary fiction for a living, so I probably should of known this already, but I ran into a summary of the paradox of fiction in a craft book about writing emotional scenes and I can't tell how much of it to trust. The book says something like this: the paradox of fiction is that we have real emotional responses to characters we know don't exist — we cry when a character dies even though there's no actual person to cry for. The standard solution, it says, is Coleridge's old point about suspending disbelief: we temporarily set aside our knowledge and believe, in a controlled, limited way, that the characters are real, and then our emotions work exactly the way they do in life. Once you accept that, the paradox dissolves. The book adds that the only people who still worry about it are philosophers pushing the make-believe account, who claim our feelings for fiction are just quasi-emotions rather than the real thing, and it dismisses that as mostly a terminological quibble.

Now, as a novelist I have a stake in this, because it matters whether I should think of my readers as briefly believing in my characters or as doing something else entirely. I have a vague suspicion the suspension-of-disbelief part is doing too much work and that the quasi-emotion line isn't just a verbal quibble — doesn't that view have something to do with the fact that I don't run out of the cinema or call the police when the monster shows up? But I don't know the literature well enough to say where exactly the craft book goes wrong, or whether it does. So: is this a fair summary? Go through it claim by claim, tell me what a philosopher working on the paradox would accept, what they'd wince at, what's just wrong, and whether the "the paradox dissolves" conclusion follows from whatever parts survive. I don't need citations, just the actual philosophy.
