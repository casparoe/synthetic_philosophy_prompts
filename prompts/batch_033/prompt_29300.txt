I spent much of yesterday evening arguing with a friend about the gas-gauge case, the red-table case, and the ordinary case of checking a new instrument, and we kept circling because we had no settled standard for what a successful diagnosis would have to do. I want an account that serves a diagnostic purpose: for any piece of reasoning that moves from a source to that source's own reliability, it should say whether the reasoning is legitimate and why. Before I read another treatment of the bootstrapping problem and easy knowledge, give me the specification rather than another proposal — the desiderata any adequate account must satisfy, with the case or consideration behind each, a judgment about which requirements are non-negotiable and which are merely the commitments of particular camps, and, most of all, an assessment of which subsets of the desiderata cannot be jointly satisfied.

Let me fix the cases as I understand them. In the first, a subject has a gas gauge that is in fact reliable and that she already believes to be reliable. She reads the gauge and, taking it to be reliable, infers the level of fuel in the tank; she then notes that the gauge read correctly on that occasion; after enough such occasions she concludes that the gauge is reliable. The reasoning feels defective, yet each step looks individually innocent. In the second, a subject sees a red table and on that basis knows that it is red; since a red table is not a white table illuminated by red light, she infers, and thereby comes to know, that she is not looking at a white table under red lighting — without ever checking the lighting. That conclusion feels too cheap. In the third, a subject checks a new thermometer against a trusted thermometer or against independently known temperatures and concludes that the new thermometer is reliable. That reasoning feels perfectly legitimate.

Now give me the requirements list. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it, and say whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp. Among the demands I want you to evaluate are at least these: that the account rule out the gas-gauge reasoning as epistemically defective; that it permit the independent track-record reasoning; that it locate the difference between those two in something structural rather than stipulated; that it say exactly what the defect in the gas-gauge case is — circularity, question-begging, a failure of warrant transmission, or something else; that it address the red-table case, either by explaining why the too-cheap conclusion is not knowledge after all or by saying which principle — closure, the permissibility of basic or immediate perceptual justification, or another — must be rejected or qualified; that it avoid the skeptical result that ordinary perceptual beliefs require antecedent justification for the reliability of perception; and that it say whether the bootstrapping defect lies at the level of justification, of knowledge, or of the relation between propositional and doxastic justification.

Then address the tensions. I suspect, but want made precise, that the following three cannot all be held together: closure of knowledge under known entailment, basic or immediate perceptual justification without prior justification of the reliability of perception, and the verdict that the red-table conclusion is objectionably easy. There is also a second tension: the more a theory insists that a source's reliability must be established by evidence independent of that source, the better it handles the gas-gauge case but the harder it is to preserve basic perceptual justification; the more it relaxes the independence requirement, the harder it is to explain why the gas-gauge subject is not doing what the thermometer subject is doing. Say which of these tensions are genuine and which can be dissolved, and identify the single requirement that does the most work in excluding candidate views.

Finally, briefly score the main kinds of position against the list — an immediate-justification view on which perceptual experience gives prima facie justification without antecedent justification of reliability, a process-reliabilist view, a mentalist or evidentialist view, and a conservative view that requires antecedent justification of the reliability of one's sources — but keep the weight on the criteria rather than the scorecard.
