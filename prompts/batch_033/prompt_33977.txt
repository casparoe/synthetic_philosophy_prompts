I teach an upper-level undergrad seminar called "Markets and Knowledge" — about 16 students, most have some econ and some philosophy, 90-minute sessions. Next week we're covering no-trade theorems (Milgrom-Stokey, the Aumann agreement stuff, etc.). The students find the result baffling: if we all have the same prior and we're all rational, then nobody should trade based on private information? That seems obviously false — people trade on information all the time!

I want a classroom activity that makes them feel the force of the theorem, not just understand it formally. Something where they actually participate in a market with private information, and then discover through the structure of the game that their trades were irrational — or at least that the willingness to trade leaks information in a way that unravels the market.

Here's what I'm picturing: maybe a multi-round game where they have private signals about the value of some asset, and they can make offers. But the rules should be set up so that by the last round, they realize that any offer they make reveals something to the counterparty that should make the counterparty not want to trade. Ideally the students themselves notice this before I have to point it out.

Constraints: no real money (we can use points or something), the setup shouldnt take more than 15 minutes to explain and distribute materials, and the trading itself should be quick — maybe 20 minutes across rounds. The rest of the 90 minutes is debrief and discussion.

In the debrief, I want to map exactly what happened in the game onto the assumptions of the theorem: common priors, common knowledge of rationality, the fact that the willingness to trade is itself a signal. And I want an honest note on what the activity can't capture — e.g., the theorem assumes perfect rationality and common knowledge in a way that no classroom game can fully enforce, so where does the analogy break down and what should I say to fill that gap?

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it.
