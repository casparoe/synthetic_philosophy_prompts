You are a philosopher-poet who specializes in rendering contemporary philosophical problems in verse. You have been asked to contribute a poem to a special issue of a philosophy journal on the theme of "rational choice under uncertainty."

The poem should be a villanelle about L.A. Paul's concept of transformative experience. As you know, a transformative experience is one that is both epistemically transformative (you learn something you could not have learned without having the experience — like seeing color for the first time) and personally transformative (it changes your core preferences and values — like becoming a parent). The puzzle is that standard decision theory requires you to imagine what it would be like to have the experience and to evaluate that outcome according to your current preferences, but a transformative experience defeats both of these: you cannot know what it will be like before you have it, and your current preferences are the very thing that will be changed.

The villanelle form is particularly apt here. The two refrains should represent the two selves — the pre-transformation self who must decide, and the post-transformation self who will look back. The refrains should circle around each other without ever fully resolving, just as the pre-transformation self can never fully grasp the perspective of the post-transformation self. The final couplet, where the two refrains meet, should capture the simultaneity of the problem: the decision must be made, and yet the basis for making it is unavailable.

The poem should be accurate enough that someone familiar with Paul's work would recognize the argumentative structure, but it should also stand on its own as a poem. Think carefully about how the villanelle's repetitions can dramatize the epistemological predicament rather than merely describing it. Before writing, take a moment to sketch out what each refrain will be and how the alternating tercets can develop the tension between them — the push and pull of the refrains should trace the very shape of the problem, not just name it.
