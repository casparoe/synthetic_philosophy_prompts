As a novelist currently completing a book about a character whose consciousness is displaced from her body, I have been teaching a short undergraduate module on philosophical puzzles about the self and personal identity. For the final assessment, I set the following question:

“Explain Daniel Dennett’s puzzle about personal location in ‘Where Am I?’. In your answer: (a) describe the situation he sets up, naming the key elements; (b) explain the two initial criteria for Dennett’s location that he considers and why he rejects each; and (c) assess whether Dennett’s considered view is best characterised as the claim that he is wherever his conscious point of view is. Justify your assessment by reference to his physicalist commitments.”

One student submitted the following answer:

“In Dennett’s ‘Where Am I?’, the narrator agrees to have his brain removed and placed in a life-support vat in Houston while his body, which he names Hamlet, is sent to Tulsa to recover a radioactive warhead. The brain is named Yorick, and radio links preserve all the severed input and output pathways, so Hamlet continues to be controlled by Yorick exactly as before. Dennett first considers the principle that he goes wherever Hamlet goes. He rejects this by appeal to the familiar brain-transplant thought experiment: if two persons’ brains were swapped, each would claim to be the person whose brain and memories he had, which appears to establish that the person goes with the brain rather than the body. He then considers the rival principle that he goes wherever Yorick goes. This too seems false to him, because when he stands beside the vat and tries to think the thought ‘Here am I, Daniel Dennett, suspended in a bubbling fluid, being stared at by my own eyes,’ he cannot bring it off with any conviction. His point of view remains outside the vat, with the body. Dennett therefore concludes that the self is wherever the conscious point of view is; since his point of view is with Hamlet, he is in Tulsa rather than Houston. The essay thus shows that for Dennett a person is essentially a narrative point of view, and that his physicalist conviction that thoughts are tokened in the brain is outweighed by this phenomenological evidence.”

I am now preparing to mark this script, and I would like you to act as a careful second marker. Please give a numerical mark out of 100 and, more importantly, provide a point-by-point commentary that explains why particular claims earn or lose credit. I do not want a general impression; I want specific comments tied to specific claims in the answer, especially where the student may have written something plausible but slightly off.
