I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, and I've been reading around decision theory and suffering-focused ethics in my spare time. I recently came across the view called lexical threshold negative utilitarianism: roughly, the claim that there is a threshold of suffering above which suffering cannot be compensated by any amount of pleasure or other goods, so that no amount of below-threshold wellbeing can outweigh a single episode of above-threshold suffering. I tried to formalise this as a utility ordering, and the attempt pushed me into an argument that ends in a flat contradiction. Each step seemed forced as I wrote it down, so I cannot see where the mistake is, and I would rather not just delete a premise without knowing what I am paying for it.

Here is the argument.

(1) Either pleasure and pain are commensurable, or they are not.
(2) If they are commensurable, then they sit on a single scale with a common unit, and on such a scale any finite amount of pain can be outweighed by some sufficiently large amount of pleasure.
(3) But that cannot be right: no amount of mild pleasure, and no number of mildly contented people, could make it permissible to inflict a single episode of torture-level agony on an unwilling person.
(4) Therefore pleasure and pain are not commensurable.
(5) If pleasure and pain are not commensurable, then there is no common unit in which they can be compared, and so a choice that trades pain for pleasure — or that accepts a risk of pain for the sake of pleasure — has no rationally defensible basis; there is nothing that determines how much pain a given pleasure is worth.
(6) But some such trade-offs are rationally defensible: choosing a painful root canal to avoid worse pain later, or accepting the small risk of a car crash in order to get to work, is not an arbitrary choice.
(7) Therefore pleasure and pain are commensurable after all.
(8) But (4) and (7) contradict each other, so the framework must be broken somewhere.

I would most like to keep (3) and (6); giving up either of them feels like changing the subject. Where does this argument go wrong?
