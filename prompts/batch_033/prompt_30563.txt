I'm giving a talk at my local AI alignment reading group next month defending the claim that the shutdown problem is basically a reductio of expected-utility maximization as a design target, and that utility indifference doesn't rescue it—it only gets button-neutrality by making the agent indifferent between worlds where the button got pressed because the human wanted it and worlds where it got pressed because the agent maneuvered the human into it, which is exactly the wrong kind of indifference. My arguement in the talk runs: (1) for a sufficiently general agent the button is always inside its action space, (2) any utility function over world-states ends up assigning the agent a preference about the button's state whenever that state correlates with anything else the agent cares about, and (3) the standard utility-indifference construction papers over (2) instead of solving it, so corrigibility has to live in the decision procedure rather than the utility function. The audience is mostly people who've been on LessWrong since the corrigibility sequence dropped and think in terms of instrumental convergence and Goodhart, plus two academic decision theorists who keep insisting that any coherent agent is an expected utility maximizer, and one engineer who's worked on RLHF and thinks the whole thing is solved by just pulling the plug.
