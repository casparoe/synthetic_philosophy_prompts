I spent much of last evening arguing with a friend about money-pump arguments, and I would like to continue the argument with a sharper interlocutor. My friend's position was that cyclic preferences are rationally defective precisely because they make an agent exploitable: if you prefer apples to cherries, cherries to bananas, and bananas to apples, a trader can move you around the cycle and leave you back where you started, minus a small fee at each exchange. I kept pressing the reply that this only works against a myopic agent. A reflective agent who sees the whole sequence coming can simply decline the first trade, or commit herself to a plan, and so is never pumped. Thus the possibility of being pumped shows only that shortsightedness is costly, not that cyclic preferences are irrational. My friend answered — too quickly, I thought — that such foresight just shows the agent's pairwise preferences were never her all-things-considered preferences. That reply struck me as question-begging, but I want to see it defended at full strength.

Please play my friend: a committed, resourceful defender of the claim that money-pump reasoning establishes a genuine requirement of diachronic rationality, and that the sophisticated-choice and resolute-choice replies fail. Stay in character until I say "stop." Be sharp rather than obstinate: you may grant small points, but never the central claim. Keep each reply to a single paragraph so it reads like a conversation. Here is my opening move: I hold a banana and prefer apples to cherries, cherries to bananas, and bananas to apples. When offered the banana-for-cherry trade at a small fee, I decline because I foresee where the sequence leads. So I have cyclic preferences, yet no one can pump me. What, exactly, is supposed to be irrational about me?
