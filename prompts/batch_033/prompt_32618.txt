I've been trying to get clear on what I actually think about expected value reasoning when the probabilities get tiny, and I keep finding tensions in my own verdicts. Maybe you can help me sort out whether there's a principled line here.

Here are my gut reactions to a bunch of cases:

1. Standard gamble: pay $10 for a 0.6 chance at $20. I take it. No hesitation.

2. St. Petersburg: I can pay a flat fee to play the St. Petersburg game once (coin flips until tails; payout doubles each flip). I wouldn't pay more than maybe $20, even though the EV is infinite. I don't feel irrational about this.

3. Pascal's mugging: a stranger says "give me $10 and I'll use my magical powers to give you infinite bliss." I assign this some tiny nonzero credence, so the EV is infinite. I still don't hand over the $10, and I don't think I'm making a mistake.

4. Saving lives: I can donate $100 to a charity that definitely saves one life, or to one that has a 1/10,000 chance of curing a disease and saving 100,000 lives. Equal EV. I feel a slight pull toward the sure thing, but I'm not confident it's the right call.

5. Astronomical stakes: a charity claims a 1/10^30 chance of ending all suffering forever. The EV swamps anything else I could do with $100. But I'd still give to the charity that saves one life for sure. This feels like it can't be justified if I'm an EV maximizer, yet I can't bring myself to do otherwise.

6. Nuclear risk: I'm asked to support a policy with a 1/1,000,000 chance of preventing a nuclear war that would kill a billion people. EV = 1,000 lives. I actually take this seriously and think it's worth real resources. But I can't see a principled difference between this and case 5 — both are tiny probabilities times huge payoffs.

7. Repeated St. Petersburg: someone offers to let me play the St. Petersburg game 10,000 times. Suddenly I feel much better about paying a higher per-play price. But if the EV of one play is genuinly infinite, why should repeating it matter?

Is there a single principle that captures all or most of these, and what does it say about which verdicts I need to abandon? And if the principle has to be messy — like, different rules for different probability ranges — is that just ad hoc, or is there a deeper justification for it?
