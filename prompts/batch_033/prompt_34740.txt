I've been thinking about the difference between lying and misleading for a while now, and I've been testing my ideas against a friend's judgements. I've collected her verdicts on six cases, and now I want to see if I can predict what she'll say about a seventh.

She's not a philosopher — neither am I — but she's thought about this a lot and her views are fairly consistent, even if she can't always articulate the rule she's running.

Here are her verdicts:

1. A colleague tells a client "I've led projects of this size before" when he's only ever been an assistant on such projects. She says: clearly wrong, a serious breach.

2. A colleague tells a client "I've been involved in projects of this size" — true, but intended to create the impression he's led them. She says: also wrong, but noticeably less bad than the lie. The client could have asked a follow-up.

3. A husband tells his wife "I was at the office until eight" when he was actually at a bar with colleagues. She says: wrong, a betrayal of trust.

4. A husband tells his wife "I had a lot of work to finish" — true, he did have work, but he finished it by six and then went to the bar, and he knows she'll infer he was at the office all evening. She says: wrong, and actually worse than the lie, because it's sneakier and shows more contempt.

5. A friend asks whether you liked her presentation. You say "It was great" when you thought it was a mess. She says: a small wrong, but sometimes kindness matters more than honesty.

6. A friend asks whether you liked her presentation. You say "You clearly put a lot of work into it" — true, but intended to dodge the question about whether it was good. She says: not wrong at all. You found something true to praise.

Now the new case. A politician is asked at a press conference whether she knew about the data breach before the public announcement. She says "I was informed through the usual channels at the same time as the board" — which is true: she was informed through the usual channels, and the board was informed at the same time, but she'd already known about it for a week through informal conversations. Her answer is literally true but grossly misleading.

What would my friend say about this case? I'm less interested in the answer itself than in working out what principle or principles could explain her pattern of verdicts, which of those principles would give different answers here, and what the best guess is for her view. If her existing verdicts don't settle it, what further case would tell the competing principles apart?

Please approach this as an expert in moral philosophy who specialises in the ethics of deception. Start by brainstorming the candidate rules that fit her verdicts, then work through which ones converge on the new case and which diverge, and give your best prediction with an honest account of the uncertainty.
