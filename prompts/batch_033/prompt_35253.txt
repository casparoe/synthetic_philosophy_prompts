I've been trying to sort out which disagreements are 'just verbal' and which are substantive, and I keep getting stuck. I've read LessWrong forever, so my first move is always to taboo the disputed word and see whether the disagreement survives — but that move keeps giving me answers I don't actually endorse when I look at concrete cases. You're a professional philosopher who works on metaontology and verbal disputes, and who takes the taboo technique seriously rather than treating it as an internet gimmick. Please think carefully, brainstorm a bit before committing, and don't just pattern-match to the standard verbal-disputes line — I want the principle that fits my cases, even if it means telling me one of my verdicts has to go.

Here are my gut calls.

- 'A tomato is a fruit, not a vegetable.' Both sides agree on all the botanical and culinary facts. Merely verbal, end of story.
- 'Is a hot dog a sandwich?' Merely verbal, basically a joke.
- 'If a tree falls in the forest and no one is around, does it make a sound?' Both sides agree that there are pressure waves and that no one has an auditory experience. Merely verbal; 'sound' is being used two ways.
- 'Is a virus alive?' Both sides agree on all the biology — it replicates, needs a host cell, etc. But I don't think this one is merely verbal. There's a real question about where the line should go, even though it's a line-drawing question.
- 'Is Pluto a planet?' Both sides agree on the orbital facts. I lean not merely verbal, because which classification we use matters for how we carve up the solar system, but I can't say why that isn't true of the tomato case too.
- 'Is a trans woman a woman?' Even if both sides agree on all the underlying facts about biology, identity, and social role, I still strongly think the dispute is substantive, not merely verbal. But I can't articulate what makes it different from the hot dog case beyond 'the stakes are higher,' and higher stakes feels like it shouldn't change whether a question is verbal.
- 'Do humans have free will?' Compatibilists and incompatibilists seem to partly mean different things by 'free will,' but I don't think the debate is just verbal. There's a substantive question about which ability is the one worth wanting, or something like that.

My rough principle is: a dispute is merely verbal iff, after you taboo the disputed term and both sides spell out the concepts they're actually using, they agree on every remaining non-linguistic question. But that rule classifies the virus and Pluto cases as merely verbal, and maybe the trans woman case too, which I don't want. So maybe I need a second clause: the dispute is still substantive if there's a real question about which of the competing concepts better deserves the word. But that second clause feels like it might be to ad hoc — 'better deserves' can do whatever I want it to do.

So: what's the principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures these verdicts? State it precisely, and for each clause say which of my cases forces it. Then apply it to cases I haven't settled: (i) is a tomato a vegetable for tariff purposes? (ii) is mathematics discovered or invented? (iii) is someone in a persistent vegetative state alive? Finally, be honest about which of my verdicts the principle can't accommodate and whether I should overturn one instead of complicating the principle. Tell me how confident you are in the principle and single out the point you're least sure about.
