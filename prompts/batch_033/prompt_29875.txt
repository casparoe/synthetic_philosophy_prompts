I've been rewatching *12 Angry Men* lately and I keep getting stuck on how it relates to the Condorcet jury theorem. The theorem says, roughly, that if each voter in a group is more likely to be right than wrong and they vote independently, then the majority is very likely to be correct and this gets more reliable as the group gets larger. So at the start of the film you've got eleven jurors voting guilty and one voting not guilty — if the theorem applied straightforwardly, that 11-1 split would be crushing evidence that the defendant is guilty.

But the whole point of the film is that the initial majority has it wrong, or at least hasn't examined the evidence properly. And the thing that corrects this isn't independent voting — it's deliberation, persuasion, people changing each other's minds, which is exactly what the theorem's independence condition rules out.

What I'm trying to get clear on is this: does the film show that the independence condition is so unrealistic that the theorem is basically irrelevant to how actual juries should work? Or is there a more interesting reading where the film is dramatising the conditions under which deliberation can *replace* independence — that is, when independent voting would have produced a bad outcome, deliberation can correct it, but only because there happens to be at least one juror (Juror 8) who's willing to slow things down and force everyone to actually look at the evidence?

I'd love a reading that goes through specific moments in the film and maps them onto the theorem's conditions. Like, which jurors are the ones whose initial votes are clearly not meeting the "more likely right than wrong" threshold — Juror 10 with his prejudice, Juror 3 with his baggage about his son — and what does that do to the theorem's applicability? There's also the question of whether the theorem's notion of a "correct answer" even makes sense in a criminal trial where the standard is "beyond reasonable doubt" — the theorem treats it as a binary true/false question, but the film is all about whether the evidence clears a threshold. Does the film show that this distinction matters, or does it collapse in practice?

And does the film end up vindicating the idea that group deliberation can track the truth, or is it more pessimistic than that — maybe the outcome is just luck, dependant on the accident of who happens to be in the room?

I'm writing an essay for a UK journal so I'd appreciate British English, and I'm not after a summary of the film or the theorem — I know both. I want the critical reading that takes both seriously and doesn't just use one to score points off the other.
