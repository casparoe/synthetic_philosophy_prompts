I am working through §201 of Wittgenstein's Philosophical Investigations, in the Anscombe translation, and I am struggling to understand the dialectical structure of the passage. Here is the full text of the section:

"This was our paradox: no course of action could be determined by a rule, because every course of action can be made out to accord with the rule. The answer was: if everything can be made out to accord with the rule, then it can also be made out to conflict with it. And so there would be neither accord nor conflict here.

It can be seen that there is a misunderstanding here from the mere fact that in the course of our argument we give one interpretation after another; as if each one contented us at least for a moment, until we thought of yet another standing behind it. What this shews is that there is a way of grasping a rule which is not an interpretation, but which is exhibited in what we call 'obeying the rule' and 'going against it' in actual cases.

Hence there is an inclination to say: every action according to the rule is an interpretation. But we ought to restrict the term 'interpretation' to the substitution of one expression of the rule for another."

I would like help with the following interpretive puzzle. The section seems to contain three distinct moves: (1) a statement of a paradox, (2) an "answer" that appears to worsen the paradox rather than resolve it, and (3) a diagnosis of a misunderstanding plus a positive claim about grasping a rule without interpretation. What I cannot settle is the relationship between these three elements. In particular:

- Is the "answer" Wittgenstein's own answer to the paradox, or is it part of the paradox itself — that is, something the paradox-generating interlocutor would say, which Wittgenstein then goes on to reject?
- If the "answer" is Wittgenstein's own, how does the claim that "there would be neither accord nor conflict here" function as a genuine answer, rather than simply restating the skeptical threat in different words?
- Where exactly does the "misunderstanding" enter? Is it a misunderstanding of what the paradox shows, or a misunderstanding that generated the paradox in the first place?
- What is the relationship between the claim that "there is a way of grasping a rule which is not an interpretation" and the earlier "answer"? Does the former supplement the latter, replace it, or correct it?

I am less interested in how Kripke or other commentators handle these questions, and more interested in an attempt to reconstruct the argument as Wittgenstein himself might have intended it — that is, to make the best sense of the order in which he presents these claims and the logical relations between them. Please walk me through the section sentence by sentence, attending to the function of each claim, and then give your overall account of what the section is doing.
