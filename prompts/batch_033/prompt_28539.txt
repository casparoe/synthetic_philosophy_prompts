I cover the intersection of culture and philosophy for a general-audience magazine, and I'm working on a long-form piece about the debates that flare up whenever an admired artist—a painter, a filmmaker, a novelist—is revealed to have done terrible things. One move I encounter constantly, from curators, critics, and ordinary consumers alike, is the insistence that "you have to separate the art from the artist." It's offered as though it names an obvious principle, but the more I listen to how different people deploy it, the more I suspect it's doing several distinct kinds of work under a single heading.

I'd like to lay out, for my readers, the menu of precise claims this slogan could be making. Here are some of the dimensions I can already see variation on: Is it a claim about aesthetic properties (the work's beauty or brilliance is unaffected by facts about its creator), or about moral permissions (it's not wrong to consume or enjoy the work), or about interpretation (biographical facts shouldn't guide how we understand the work's meaning), or about institutional policy (museums, publishers, and platforms shouldn't remove the work from circulation)? Within each of those, there are further distinctions: for instance, an aesthetic claim could be merely that the work's formal properties are unchanged by biographical knowledge, or the stronger claim that such knowledge shouldn't affect our aesthetic judgment at all. A moral-permission claim could be about private enjoyment, or about public celebration and financial support. And some versions of the slogan might be descriptive (we can, as a psychological matter, separate them) while others are normative (we ought to).

What I need is a systematic disambiguation: the distinct theses this sentence can express, from the trivial to the radical, with an assessment of each. Which readings are platitudes nobody would dispute? Which are substantive and defensible? Which are probably false? And crucially: which of these readings, if any, actually do the argumentative work the slogan is typically enlisted to perform—namely, to close the case in favor of continuing to exhibit, consume, and honor the work?
