I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am working on a piece about how people who think seriously about the aggregation of harms draw the line between many small bads and one big bad. Over the course of several interviews, a moral philosopher whose work is in this area gave me firm, off-the-cuff verdicts on a series of pairwise trade-off cases. In each case she was asked which of two outcomes is worse overall, setting aside any differences in who acts, who is responsible, or what could be done in practice.

Her verdicts so far:

1. One person endures a year of severe, chronic pain (clearly not torture) versus ten people each endure a year of equally severe pain. Her verdict: the ten are worse.

2. One person endures a year of severe, chronic pain (again clearly not torture) versus one million people each endure one day of a mild headache. Her verdict: the million headaches are worse.

3. One person endures a year of severe, chronic pain versus one million people each feel a one-second, barely noticeable twinge. Her verdict: the severe pain is worse.

4. One person loses a leg versus one thousand people each lose a leg. Her verdict: the thousand are worse.

I have not yet put to her the case my editors care about most: the familiar "torture vs. dust specks" dilemma. The new case is this: one person is tortured continuously for fifty years, versus 3↑↑↑3 people (three up-arrows; an astronomically large finite number) each getting a single dust speck in the eye for one second, with no further consequences. Take the specks to be mildly irritating but not painful.

Please give me your best prediction of her verdict on that new case, and return it as a JSON object with the following fields:

- "best_guess": an object with "verdict" (either "the torture is worse" or "the dust specks are worse") and "reasoning" (a paragraph explaining why you predict this, including which rule you think she is most likely running and why the alternatives are less plausible given her four recorded verdicts).

- "candidate_rules": an array of objects, one per rule that fits all four of her recorded verdicts. Each object has "name" (a short label), "statement" (a precise one- or two-sentence formulation of the rule), "prediction" (what the rule says about the new torture-vs-specks case), and "fit" (how the rule handles each of the four verdicts, with particular attention to cases 2 and 3, where the interesting constraints are).

- "excluded_rules": an array of objects for rules that are ruled out by her recorded verdicts. Each object has "name", "statement", and "excluding_verdict" (which verdict excludes it and why). Include at least one, such as a maximin-style rule that attends only to the single worst-off person, or a strong lexical rule that ranks any severe pain above any quantity of mild pain.

- "decisive_follow_up": an object describing the one further case I should put to her next to discriminate among the rules that still fit. It has "case" (a concrete pairwise trade-off specified in the same style as the four above) and "what_it_would_show" (which rules disagree on it and how their predictions differ).

- "confidence": a string, either "high", "moderate", or "low", with a one-sentence justification.

The value of the piece depends on the candidate rules being genuinely distinct and on your not smuggling in assumptions beyond the four verdicts. If the four verdicts underdetermine her rule, say so plainly and make your best guess explicit rather than hedging the whole answer away.
