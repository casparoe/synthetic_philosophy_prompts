I am a novelist writing a book about a small community that has learned, with certainty, that it is the last generation: its members will live out their natural lives, but after them the species will be gone, and there will be no future human beings to remember them, finish their work, or care about what they made. I have been thinking about Samuel Scheffler's afterlife conjecture — the idea that the value we find in much of what we do depends on an unspoken assumption that humanity will continue after our deaths, and that if that assumption were withdrawn, many of our activities and projects would lose their point even though our own lives and deaths would be unchanged. I want the novel to work this out concretely rather than gesture at it.

Describe the most important implications of Scheffler's afterlife conjecture for how I should write these characters. Which of their activities, attachments, and ambitions would change most, and in what ways? In particular, what does the conjecture imply about the value of making art and writing novels in such a world, and about the difference between projects whose point is essentially future-directed and projects that might retain their point when no one will come after us? I am looking for implications that are specific and unexpected, not a restatement of the conjecture.

Finally, please say what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to a different account of these implications, and where in your reasoning the account is most likely to fail.
