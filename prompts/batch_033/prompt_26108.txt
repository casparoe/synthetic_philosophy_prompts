I've been trying to work out what I actually think about the idea that conceivability is a guide to possibility, going by my gut on a range of cases:

- A golden mountain. I can picture one clearly, and that seems enough to show that a golden mountain is possible. I'm confident one is.

- The liquid in our lakes and rivers turning out to be XYZ rather than H2O, as in the Twin Earth scenario. After the scenario is explained to me, I can conceive of discovering that. But I don't think it's genuinely metaphysically possible — water is necessarily H2O.

- A molecule-for-molecule duplicate of me with no conscious experience whatsoever — a philosophical zombie. I can conceive of this. It doesn't feel like a contradiction. And I lean toward thinking it's metaphysically possible, which is part of why I find physicalism hard to accept.

- A round square. I can't conceive of one, and I'm certain none is possible. This case seems unproblematic.

- Goldbach's conjecture turning out false. I can conceive of mathematicians finding an even number that isn't the sum of two primes. But I can just as easily conceive of its turning out true. Since it's a mathematical claim, only one is possible, yet conceivability doesn't discriminate between them. So conceivability here seems to outrun possibility in at least one direction.

- That I am right now a brain in a vat. I can conceive of this, and I think it's possible — it could be true for all I'm in a position to rule out, and that's the kind of possibility I have in mind.

- My desk being made entirely of ice, though it looks and feels like wood. I can conceive of this as a sensory illusion. But I also have the strong intuition that, given the desk's actual material constitution, it isn't possible for this very desk — the one in front of me — to have been ice all along. A different desk, maybe; this one, no.

What I can't figure out is whether there's a single principle behind these verdicts. The water/XYZ case and the zombie case feel especially awkward together: in both I'm conceiving of something that contradicts a claimed necessary truth, but I come out on opposite sides. Is there a principled way to draw the line, or do I have to give up one of those verdicts? Please state whatever principle you think best captures — or repairs — my pattern, say which if any of my verdicts it forces me to overturn, and apply it to a few further cases I haven't considered.

Also, please separate what's reasonably settled in the literature on conceivability and possibility from what's genuinely contested, and don't present a contested view as the consensus.
