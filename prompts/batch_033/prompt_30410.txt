I teach a philosophy of statistics seminar and I've reached the point where I think the frequentist/Bayesian fight needs a diagnosis rather than another round of the usual arguments. The strange thing is that both sides accept the same axioms of probability, and once you fix the model and the prior (or the test) the arithmetic isn't in dispute, and yet nobody moves. The frequentist says probability is long-run frequency, that what matters is controlling error rates across repeated applications, and that Bayesian priors are just subjective opinions smuggled into the analysis. The Bayesian says probability is degree of belief, that what we actually want is the probability of the hypothesis given these data, and that p-values overstate evidence against the null. The Bayesian also likes to point out that p-values depend on the sampling plan — optional stopping changes the p-value for the same data — while the frequentist replies that the sampling plan is exactly what determines which errors you're controlling. Then the replication crisis happens, and it looks like the Bayesians finally get to say I told you so about p-hacking and the .05 threshold, except the frequentists just answer that their tools were misused and that abandoning error control would make things worse. So were back where we started.

My working hypothesis is that this is not a factual disagreement at all, and maybe not even a normative one: the two sides are doing different jobs under one heading — one side wants decision procedures with guaranteed long-run performance, the other wants a calculus for rationally updating degrees of belief. If that's right, the debate is stalled because it's a disguised verbal disagreement about the word 'probability.' But I'm not fully convinced; maybe there is a substantive disagreement about whether long-run error control or coherence with your other beliefs is the right standard for an inference to meet, and that question has a real answer. Diagnose this. What kind of disagreement is it really — verbal, substantive, two different questions, a hidden normative dispute, or something else? Ground the diagnosis in what each side actually says, and then tell me what would count as progress given you're diagnosis: a theorem, a decision-theoretic result, a new distinction, a change of question — or should we just accept that it will stay stalled, and why.
