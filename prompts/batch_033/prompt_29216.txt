On Rotten Tomatoes, a film might earn a 97% critics' score and a 63% audience score. On Metacritic, an album might receive an 89 from critics but a 5.7 from users. These numbers genuinely influence behavior: people decide what to watch, read, or listen to on the basis of aggregated review scores. Yet many of the same people who treat a high Metacritic score as a reason to believe a film is good will also insist, when they find themselves disagreeing with the consensus, that aesthetic judgment is ultimately subjective and that you cannot really know whether something is good until you experience it for yourself.

The practice of relying on aggregated review scores seems to presuppose that aesthetic properties are the kind of thing about which testimony can provide justification — that the aggregated reports of critics or other viewers can give you knowledge, or at least warranted belief, about the aesthetic quality of a work you have not encountered firsthand. But this sits uneasily alongside the widely held view that aesthetic judgment requires direct acquaintance with the work, and that the judgments of others, however numerous or expert, are no substitute for seeing, hearing, or reading the thing yourself.

What theory of aesthetic testimony does the practice of using aggregated review scores actually presuppose? Does it require the view that aesthetic testimony is a genuine source of epistemic justification, or can the practice be reinterpreted as something that does not involve aesthetic belief at all — for instance, as a purely pragmatic tool for deciding how to allocate one's limited time, or as a way of predicting one's own likely enjoyment rather than forming a belief about the work's aesthetic merit? If the practice does commit its users to optimism about aesthetic testimony, is that commitment consistent with the subjectivism they often invoke when their own judgment diverges from the consensus? And finally, if we were to become convinced that aesthetic testimony is impossible in principle — that no amount of reading reviews can give you justified aesthetic beliefs about a work — what would a consistent alternative practice look like? Would we have to abandon aggregated review scores entirely, or could we reconstruct them as serving some other function?
