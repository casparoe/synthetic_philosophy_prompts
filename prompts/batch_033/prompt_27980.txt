Question. Consider the one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma with the following payoff matrix, where the row player's payoff is listed first in each cell:

             Column: C        Column: D
Row: C       (3, 3)           (0, 5)
Row: D       (5, 0)           (1, 1)

(a) Explain why the profile (C, C) is not a Nash equilibrium, and state what the game's Nash equilibrium is.

(b) Explain the superrationality argument, associated with Douglas Hofstadter, that each player should nevertheless choose C. State precisely what assumption this argument adds beyond the usual assumptions of expected-utility maximization, perfect rationality, and common knowledge of rationality, and state what the argument does and does not establish.

Student's answer.

(a) The profile (C, C) is not a Nash equilibrium because each player has a profitable unilateral deviation: if the other player cooperates, defecting earns 5 rather than 3. Cooperating is therefore not a best response to cooperation. The unique Nash equilibrium is (D, D), even though it leaves both players worse off than (C, C) would.

(b) Hofstadter's superrationality argument is that two perfectly rational players who both know that the game is perfectly symmetric will recognize that their situations are identical, and therefore whatever line of reasoning one player follows, the other will follow as well. Consequently, the two players will choose the same action, and the only outcomes consistent with their rationality are the symmetric outcomes (C, C) and (D, D). Since each player prefers (C, C) to (D, D), each has reason to choose C, because it is the better of the two outcomes that can actually arise. The argument therefore shows that in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma between equally rational players, cooperation is the uniquely rational strategy, and that the standard game-theoretic recommendation to defect rests on ignoring the players' rationality.
