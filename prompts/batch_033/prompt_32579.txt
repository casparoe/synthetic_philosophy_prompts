In epistemology, the "bootstrapping problem" gets its name from a physical metaphor: you cannot pull yourself up by your own bootstraps. The epistemic version runs roughly as follows. Suppose you want to establish that your gas gauge is reliable. You read the gauge ("F"), you check the tank (it's full), and you note the match. You repeat this many times, and eventually you conclude: the gauge is trustworthy. But wait — each time you used a reading from the very gauge whose reliability you were trying to establish. The charge is that this reasoning is viciously circular, and that if it were permissible, you could "bootstrap" your way into justifying any faculty — perception, induction, memory — by simply using it to check itself. The metaphor is meant to do real work: it suggests that the circularity here is not benign (like the rule-circularity of a deductive system justifying its own rules) but rather a kind of impossibility — you cannot lift your epistemic standing by relying on the very faculty whose standing is in question, any more than you can physically lift yourself off the ground by tugging on your own bootstraps.

But the metaphor is more than a label; it is a picture, and pictures can smuggle in features of the source domain that lack counterparts in the target. Audit the analogy systematically. In particular:

- Lay out the correspondence: what in the epistemic bootstrapping case plays the role of the person, the bootstraps, the upward pull, the lifting, and the ground?
- Identify the disanalogies. Distinguish those that are merely incidental from those that, if the source and target differ on them, would undermine the argument the metaphor is meant to carry.
- Assess whether the conclusion — that bootstrapping reasoning cannot yield knowledge or justification — survives once the disanalogies are on the table. Does the metaphor supply intuitive force that the argument, stated without it, would lack? If the metaphor were replaced by a neutral description of the circularity, would the case against bootstrapping remain equally strong, or does the physical impossibility of the source domain do illicit work?
- If there is a better analogy for what goes wrong (or, alternatively, for why the circularity might be harmless), describe it.
