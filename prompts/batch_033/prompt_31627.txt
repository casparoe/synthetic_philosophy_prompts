Question (10 points): Describe the Knobe effect using the original chairman cases, explain what it has been taken to show about the relationship between moral judgment and ascriptions of intentional action, and identify one alternative explanation of the asymmetry that does not make moral considerations part of the concept of intentional action itself.

Student answer: In Knobe's original study, participants read about a chairman who is told that a new program will increase profits but also harm the environment, and who says he cares only about the profits; most participants judged that he harmed the environment intentionally. In the matched case where the side effect helps the environment, only a minority judged that he helped intentionally. This asymmetry between bad and good side effects—the Knobe effect—has been widely replicated. The standard interpretation is that moral evaluation drives the difference: the moral status of the side effect shapes whether participants apply the concept of intentional action. Knobe himself explains this with the culpability account, on which people want to hold the chairman blameworthy and therefore call the action intentional in order to justify that blame. This shows that ascriptions of intentional action are driven entirely by moral evaluation, with the agent's beliefs and desires playing no independent role. An alternative that does not make moral evaluation part of the concept itself is that participants read the chairman's indifference as evidence about his underlying values: in the harm case, his willingness to sacrifice the environment for profit reveals bad priorities, and it is that inferred badness, not the concept of intentional action, that drives the judgment.
