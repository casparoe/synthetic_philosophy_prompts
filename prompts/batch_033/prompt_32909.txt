In a graduate seminar on democratic theory, we've been working through the boundary problem — the question of who should constitute the demos, the set of people entitled to participate in democratic decision-making. One of my classmates has been pushing what he calls the "club analogy": deciding who belongs to the demos is like deciding who gets to be a member of a club. Just as a tennis club gets to set its own membership criteria — who can join, who can vote on club rules, who can be expelled — a democratic polity gets to decide who counts as a citizen. He uses this to argue that the boundary problem isn't really a problem at all: it's simply a matter of collective self-definition, and outsiders have no standing to complain about their exclusion because they are, by definition, not members of the collective.

I'm suspicious that the analogy is doing all the argumentative work here, and that it quietly imports features of clubs that are absent from states. For one thing, clubs are voluntary associations — you can leave, you can start a rival club — whereas states are territorial, coercive, and not so easily opted out of. For another, the analogy seems to assume that a club's decisions only affect its members, which is plainly false of states: the policies of a powerful democracy ramify far beyond its borders. But I'm not sure whether these differences are fatal to the analogy or merely superficial.

I'd like you to audit the analogy systematically. Give me a correspondence table — what in the target (a democratic polity) maps onto what in the source (a club). Then walk through the disanalogies, separating the ones that matter for the argument about outsiders' standing from the ones that are merely cosmetic. Does the conclusion that outsiders have no standing to complain about their exclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table? And if the club analogy fails, is there a better analogy that would capture what is genuinely at stake in the boundary problem?
