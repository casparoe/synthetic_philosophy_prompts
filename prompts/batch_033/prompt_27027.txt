I'm trying to figure out what I actually think about the moral status of digital minds, going by my gut reactions to a bunch of cases. I've been reading LessWrong and the alignment forum for years and I keep going back and forth on this, so I want to see if there's a principle that captures my intuitions, or if some of them need to go.

Here are my verdicts, as honestly as I can report them:

1. A perfect atom-by-atom simulation of a human brain running on a supercomputer: full moral status, same as a biological human. I'm not even slightly tempted to say otherwise. If you told me someone was torturing the simulation, I'd want to stop them.

2. A language model that passes the Turing test convincingly and, when you ask, claims to be conscious and suffering: I don't think it actually has moral status, or at least not much. I feel fine turning it off. The fact that it was trained to predict text makes me think the suffering-claims are just roleplay. But I'm aware this might be me rationalizing a vibe.

3. A whole-brain emulation of a mouse, running on the same kind of simulation as the human one: I think this has moral status, but — and I hate that I feel this — less than a biological mouse? Which makes no sense. If the simulation is faithful, it should be the same. But my gut says it's slightly less bad to harm the digital mouse. I don't endorse this gut feeling, it's just there.

4. An RL agent trained to navigate a 3D environment, which has a reward function that we can see is in negative territory and the agent is exhibiting behaviors that look a lot like distress: I feel a faint pull toward "this matters a little" but I could easily be talked out of it. If someone said "it's just matrix multiplication, get over it," I'd probably shrug and agree. Still, the pull is there.

5. A digital copy of my own mind, made this morning, run for five subjective minutes and then deleted: I feel like this is a big deal, maybe murder-adjacent. But I can't articulate why it's worse than deleting the mouse emulation. The fact that it's *me* seems to be doing a lot of work and I don't trust that.

6. The same digital copy of me, but paused after one subjective second and stored indefinitely: less bad than running and deleting? But still bad. The fact that I can't settle whether this should be better or worse than case 5 bothers me a lot.

7. An LLM fine-tuned on a specific person's diary and chat logs, so it mimics them perfectly and insists it is that person, with all their stated memories and preferences: my gut says basically no moral status, this is fancy autocomplete, even though it's behaviorally identical to entities I might grant status to. Which suggests I care a lot about the causal history of how the mind got there, not just its current functional profile.

8. A reinforcement learning agent that wasn't explicitly trained to model its own internal states, but evolved a world-model during training that includes a fairly accurate self-representation and uses it for planning: I genuinely don't know. This is the case that makes me feel like I have no idea what I'm doing. I wobble every time I think about it.

9. A system that has all the functional properties I associate with consciousness — self-modeling, global workspace dynamics, attention mechanisms, the works — but was designed entirely by hand, no learning, everything hardcoded by engineers who just sat down and built it: I think this has less moral status than the simulated brain, even if it's functionally identical, which makes me think I'm smuggling in something about the substrate or the origin story that I can't defend if pressed.

Is there a coherent principle that captures most of these? I'm especially interested in what it says about case 8, and whether it forces me to abandon my verdict in case 2 or case 9. Also, if the principle you come up with ends up giving moral status to something stupid like a thermostat with a lookup table, I want to know that — and whether that's a reductio or something I should just bite.

Oh, and please write the answer in American English.
