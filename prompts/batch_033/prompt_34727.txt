I've been rereading Peter Watts's *Blindsight* and trying to think through its central conceit with the lens of higher-order theories of consciousness, and I keep hitting a point of confusion that I'd like a careful reading to sort out.

The novel's premise, as you likely know, is that consciousness — the felt, first-person "what-it's-like" — is a metabolically expensive evolutionary deadweight, and it dramatizes this through the vampires (especially Sarasti) and the alien Scramblers, both of whom are depicted as hyper-intelligent but non-conscious. The book leans hard on the idea that you can have sophisticated cognition, strategic reasoning, and even something functionally indistinguishable from personhood without any inner life at all. The Theseus crew even discusses the Chinese Room and related thought experiments explicitly, so the book is self-consciously in conversation with the philosophy of mind.

Here is what I am stuck on. Higher-order theories of consciousness, in the tradition of David Rosenthal and others, hold that a mental state is conscious just in virtue of being the target of a higher-order representation: roughly, you are conscious of a pain when you have a thought (itself not necessarily conscious) that you are in pain. On this picture, the difference between a conscious and an unconscious state is a difference in cognitive architecture — whether the state is the object of a suitable higher-order representation — not a difference in whether the state possesses some intrinsic "phenomenal glow." So what exactly is the book claiming the vampires and Scramblers lack? Is it the absence of higher-order representations, in which case a HOT theorist would indeed say they lack consciousness? Or is the book depicting the absence of something else — raw feels, qualia, a kind of intrinsic subjectivity — and simply calling that the absence of consciousness, while leaving the cognitive architecture of higher-order representation largely intact? If the latter, is the book's framework in genuine tension with higher-order theories, or is it simply working with a different (and perhaps more folk-psychological) concept of consciousness that higher-order theories were designed to replace?

I would like a reading that takes both the novel and the philosophical theories seriously and does not reduce either to a strawman. Where does *Blindsight* genuinely engage with the structure of higher-order thought, and where does it rely on a conception of consciousness that a HOT theorist would reject as confused? Which scenes or characters would a HOT theorist point to and say "this is actually consistent with higher-order theories, for reasons the book itself does not notice," and which would force them to say "no, the book is working with a different concept entirely"? I am less interested in whether Watts "got it right" and more in whether the novel, read as a kind of extended thought experiment, puts pressure on higher-order theories in a way that the standard philosophical literature does not — or whether it ultimately talks past them.
