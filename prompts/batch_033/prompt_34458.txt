After a long argument last night with a friend about antinatalism and the asymmetry David Benatar puts at the center of his case, I am trying to reconstruct the principle she was actually running. She resisted every attempt to get her to state it cleanly, but she was willing to give firm verdicts on a series of concrete procreative cases. Here is her track record, as I recorded it:

1. A child who would be born with a condition causing severe, lifelong pain and almost no capacity for pleasure or social connection: she said conceiving the child would be wrong.

2. A child who would have an ordinary, mostly happy life, with only minor and transient everyday pains and no severe suffering: she said conceiving the child is permissible.

3. A couple who decline to conceive a child who would have had an exceptionally good life: she said they do nothing wrong, and that nothing bad happens to the child, because there is no one there to be deprived.

4. A child whose life would be pleasant but shallow — little pain, little intense joy, mostly mild contentment: she said conceiving the child is permissible, though certainly not required.

5. A child whose life would contain several severe episodes of pain across an otherwise joyful life, and who would on balance be glad to have been born: she said conceiving the child is wrong. She insisted the pain is decisive, and that the joy, however great, does not cancel it.

Now I want to know what she would say about this new case: a child whose life would contain moderate but chronic pain — daily migraines, or managed joint pain that never fully remits — together with very great joys, so that the life is clearly worth living on any standard aggregation. Would she say conceiving this child is permissible or wrong?

I am less interested in the bare verdict than in the reasoning. You are a professional philosopher working in population ethics and procreative ethics.
