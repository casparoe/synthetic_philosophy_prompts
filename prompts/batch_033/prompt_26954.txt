I'm writing a paper on the epistemic regress problem for an independent project, and the more I read, the more I suspect the debate between foundationalists, coherentists, and infinitists isn't really moving because it isn't the kind of disagreement the participants think it is. Here's what I mean. Foundationalism says that chains of justification must terminate in basic beliefs — beliefs that are justified without receiving their justification from further beliefs. The standard objection is that it's hard to see how a belief could be justified without being supported by anything else: either you're claiming the belief justifies itself (which looks circular) or you're claiming it's justified by something non-doxastic like experience (which raises the question of how non-propositional states can confer propositional justification). Coherentism says justification is a matter of mutual support among beliefs in a system, with no privileged foundation. The standard objection is the isolation objection: a perfectly coherent system could be completely detached from reality, and it's hard to see how coherence alone connects belief to truth. Infinitism says justification requires an infinite, non-repeating chain of reasons, and that we don't need to actually traverse the chain — we just need reasons available to continue it indefinitely. The standard objection is that this seems psychologically impossible and that it's unclear how an infinite chain that no one ever finishes could make a belief justified now.

Each of these objections is well known, each side has responses, and the responses generate counter-responses that everyone also knows. After decades of this, nobody seems to change sides. My working hypothesis is that the three camps are answering different questions under one heading: foundationalists are asking what structure would make a belief system objectively well-grounded in reality, coherentists are asking what structure makes a belief system rationally stable from the perspective of the believer, and infinitists are asking what structure a non-arbitrary justification would have to take if we set aside our cognitive limitations entirely. If that's right, the debate isn't a substantive disagreement so much as three projects talking past each other.

But I'm not confident in this diagnosis, and I can think of alternatives: maybe it's a genuine disagreement about the nature of justification that's simply very hard to resolve; maybe it's a disagreement about which cases or intuitions count as data (for instance, whether perceptual beliefs are paradigm cases of justification or paradigm cases of something else); maybe the impasse is a sign that the question itself — "what structure must justification have?" — is ill-posed. I want a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of the positions, and an honest answer about what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, a formal result, a change of question, or an argument that no progress should be expected and why.
