- A hospital strips identifiers from patient records and sells the dataset to a pharma company without telling patients. I think this is wrong, even though no one can be re-identified.
- A friend tells a mutual friend something humiliating about me that I told them in confidence. Wrong, full stop.
- The police run a license plate scanner on public streets and store everyone's movements for years. This bothers me deeply, but I struggle to articulate why, given that the plates are visible to anyone on the road.
- A company flies a drone over my fenced backyard at 400 feet and photographs my sunbathing. Privacy violation.
- A stranger on a crowded subway takes a photo of me and posts it online. I don't think this is a privacy violation, even if I find it obnoxious.
- My employer reads my work email account. Not a violation — it's their system, I know the policy.
- My employer reads my personal Gmail over my shoulder while I'm on my laptop at a café. That's a violation.

I'm trying to find the principle behind these, or figure out which verdict I need to overturn. You're a philosopher who works on privacy. Think through the candidate principles carefully — control over information, reasonable expectations, contextual integrity, access-based accounts, whatever else fits — and tell me which one gets the most of my verdicts right, where it forces me to eat a verdict I don't like, and what it says about those two stuck cases.
