Two friends of mine who both took the same game theory course got into it over the centipede game at a bar last week. I'll try to reconstruct it.

The way they set it up: two players take turns choosing "take" or "pass." If you take, you get a bigger share and the game ends. If you pass, the total pot grows and the other player gets to choose. The last round, if reached, gives both players a big payoff but slightly less for the one who would have taken. They were looking at a version with, say, six rounds.

(1) Dan said the rational move is to take on the very first turn. Backward induction: at the last node, the player whose turn it is will take because passing gives them less. Knowing that, the player at the second-to-last node will take to avoid getting a smaller payoff when the next player takes. And you just unravel all the way back to round one. So rational players never even get to the later rounds.

(2) Maya said that's fine as a formal result but it can't be the whole story, because if you actually play this game with real people they pass for several rounds and both end up better off. She said a theory that tells you to walk away with two dollars when you could both walk away with twenty by cooperating a little longer is missng something important.

(3) Dan replied that what real people do isn't the question — the question is what's rational. Real people play the lottery too. He said if you accept the assumptions (common knowledge of rationality, both players know the payoffs, etc.), the unraveling is airtight.

(4) Maya came back with: but if you're actually sitting there at round three, looking at the other player who just passed to you, you now have evidence they didn't follow the backward induction logic. So shouldn't you update? Maybe they're the type who will keep passing, and then passing yourself becomes rational in a way that backward induction misses.

(5) Dan said that's circular — you're using the fact that someone deviated from rational play to justify further deviations. The whole point of an equilibrium concept is that you don't get to those later nodes if everyone's rational.

(6) Maya said then the equilibrium concept is just assuming its own conclusion. She said there's a whole literature about this — the "paradox of backward induction" — and that some people argue common knowledge of rationality at later nodes is actually inconsistent with having reached those nodes, so the whole framework has a tension in it.

They went back and forth a while longer but that's the core of it. Neither convinced the other. I'm not sure who's right about what. Can you go through their exchange move by move and adjudicate — which points are solid, which are shaky, and where the real philosophical issue actually lies?
