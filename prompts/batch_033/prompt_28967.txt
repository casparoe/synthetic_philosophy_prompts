I serve as a junior analyst in the Office of the Procurator-General for the Common Tranquillity, the body that for three centuries has decided what measures may be taken to secure the contentment of the population. The Office is not a legislature; it does not consult public opinion and it does not publish its reasons, but it takes its own precedents seriously. A new proposal has been placed before us, and the Procurator has asked me to prepare a memo recommending how we should dispose of it if we wish to remain consistent with our own record. I am turning to you for help in identifying the principle that our precedents commit us to, and in predicting whether that principle dictates approval or rejection of the new case.

Here are our five most relevant precedents, stripped of identifying details but accurately summarised:

1. **The Edict of Universal Sustenance.** The Office approved the abolition of private commerce and the assumption by the state of all production and distribution of food, housing, and clothing. Every subject now receives a secure ration and assigned dwelling. The freedom to choose an occupation or to move between districts was eliminated. The decision recorded that hunger and uncertainty had been the chief sources of human wretchedness, and that the masses surrendered choice without distress once security was assured.

2. **The Petition of the Ex-Scholars.** A small circle of former academics sought permission to continue private disputations on theology and moral philosophy. The Office rejected the petition, finding that even though the disputations were inaccessible to the general populace, the mere knowledge of their existence produced a vague disquiet and restless questioning, detectable in rising rates of public drunkenness and domestic strife. The contentment of the many was held to require the suppression of this liberty even for the few.

3. **The Decree on Sacred Unity.** The Office approved the establishment of a single compulsory creed for the realm, accompanied by majestic public ritual, a fixed hierarchy, and penalties for outward deviance. Subsequent reports showed a measurable decline in suicide, vagrancy, and civil disorder. The decision noted that the human heart craves a united object of veneration more than it craves doctrinal correctness.

4. **The Recommendation on Mood-Smoothing for Office Members.** The Ministry of Health proposed that members of the Office themselves be placed on a mild regimen of the same tranquillising preparations used elsewhere in the civil service, in order to reduce internal faction and the physical toll of ceaseless responsibility. The Office rejected the proposal unanimously, recording that the clarity and resoluteness of the governors must not be blunted, and that the Office alone bears the terrible burden of seeing the world as it truly is.

5. **The Distribution of Quietude.** The Office approved the addition of a substance called Quietude to the public water supply. The substance induces gentle contentment and measurably reduces ambition, scepticism, and the impulse to question one’s station, without impairing the ability to perform assigned labour. Members of the Office were exempted from the distribution.

The new case is this. A proposal has been submitted to establish a Special Preparatory Academy. The Academy would select a small cohort of adolescents from the general population—perhaps one in five thousand—and give them a rigorous education in philosophy, moral reasoning, and the principles of statecraft. After training, they would be sent back to their home districts as junior inspectors, tasked with improving local administration and furnishing the Office with a larger reserve of competent servants. The proposal explicitly states that the Academy would not dilute the final authority of the central body. However, its architects concede that the education would unavoidably expose these adolescents to existential doubt and the anguish of free reflection, and that a small fraction might, over time, propagate unsettling thoughts among their neighbours.

Please give me the following in your memo:

- A prediction of how the Office would rule on the Academy, given the five precedents above.
- An identification of the precedent that is the closest analog, and an explanation of why.
- An articulation of the rule or principle that best rationalises our five precedents, together with at least one alternative rule that fits them equally well but would yield a different verdict on the Academy.
- For each candidate rule, a statement of which principle we would be adopting if we chose that path.
- A description of a further hypothetical case, not yet before the Office, that would cleanly separate the candidate rules and allow us to determine which one we are really committed to.

I am less interested in your own moral view than in the logic latent in our own record. Where the record genuinely underdetermines the answer, I need you to say so honestly and to make the latent choice explicit.
