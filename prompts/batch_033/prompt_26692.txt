I'm a journalist who covers the history of political ideas, and I'm working on a piece about the arguments nineteenth-century liberals used to defend colonial rule. One move keeps surfacing in the sources: John Stuart Mill argues, in Considerations on Representative Government, that despotic government over a people not yet capable of self-government can be legitimate when it aims at their improvement, in something like the way a guardian governs a ward until the ward reaches majority. The guardian–ward analogy is doing the real work in his treatment of India and of other peoples he regarded as unready for representative institutions: it recasts foreign domination as a temporary trusteeship aimed at the subject people's own development, and it makes the denial of self-government look like a duty rather than an injury. I want to know how far this analogy actually holds, and how much of Mill's conclusion it can carry.

Give me an audit I can use in the piece. First, set out the correspondence: what is supposed to map onto what — the guardian, the ward, the ward's interests, the period of tutelage, the criterion of reaching maturity, the guardian's accountability, and the exit from the relationship. Second, list the disanalogies, and mark which ones matter for the argument and which are incidental: I suspect the comparison quietly imports a disinterested guardian, an outside authority the guardian answers to, an agreed and predictable test for when the ward is fit to govern itself, and a guardian who actually leaves when that test is met, but I want the full set and I want the sorting done rigorously. Third, run the audit against the way the analogy is actually applied to British rule in India, where the tutelage was in fact open-ended and the governing body, the East India Company for which Mill himself worked, had commercial and strategic interests of its own. Finally, tell me whether the conclusion — that temporary, improvement-oriented despotism over such a people is legitimate — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the conclusion the whole time. If the analogy fails, say what a better one would be, and what it would do to the argument.

My readers will not have read Mill, so the explanation has to be self-contained, and I need to be able to say clearly where the analogy breaks without pretending the underlying historical questions are settled.
