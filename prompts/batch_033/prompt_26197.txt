I'm taking an upper-division seminar called "Technology and Political Theory," and we've spent the last three weeks on the debate that grew out of Langdon Winner's 1980 Daedalus article "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" The discussion has been rich but frustrating: people in the room clearly hold different views, yet when we try to state them, we end up talking past each other because the same terms — "politics," "design," "agency" — are being used in incompatible ways. My professor suggested that before we go any further, we should map the logical space of possible answers to the question rather than just lining up the canonical authors and summarizing them. I volunteered to produce a draft, and I'd like your help building it.

I want a decision tree (or taxonomy structured as a branching tree) that takes someone from a single starting question all the way to a named position on whether artifacts have politics. Each branching node should be a yes/no question about a concrete case or a specific claim — phrased so that someone can answer it without knowing which position their answer will lead to, and without the question itself tipping them off. The tree should be built from the logical space first (what combinations of answers are possible?) and only then populated with the actual positions that occupy those combinations.

The following six positions must appear as leaves: (1) the view that some technologies are inherently and inescapably political by their very nature — any instance of that technology type will carry the same political valence regardless of context (Winner's strong thesis, illustrated by his claim about nuclear power requiring authoritarian control); (2) the view that technologies can have politics built into their design, but this is always the result of contingent design choices that could have been made differently, and once those choices are materialized they create path dependencies that are effectively irreversible (Winner's weaker thesis, illustrated by Robert Moses's low-hanging overpasses on Long Island parkways); (3) the view that technologies embody a "technical code" reflecting dominant interests, but this code can be contested and technologies can be democratically redesigned through what Feenberg calls "democratic rationalization"; (4) the view that politics is not a property of any individual artifact or human but an effect that emerges from hybrid networks in which humans and nonhumans participate symmetrically as actants (Latour and actor-network theory); (5) the view that technologies are politically neutral in themselves and acquire political meaning only through the interpretations and negotiations of the social groups that use them (social constructivism, SCOT); and (6) the view that technology is simply a neutral instrument and politics resides exclusively in human choices about ends — artifacts are just means (instrumentalism).

For each leaf, include one sentence identifying the position's most awkward commitment — the thing its defenders have the hardest time explaining away. Also flag any region of logical space (any combination of answers at the nodes) that does not correspond to one of the six positions, and diagnose why: is the combination incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored in the literature? Finally, flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate and explain what additional question would split them.
