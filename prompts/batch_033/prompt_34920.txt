I'm in a philosophy of social science course, and we've been working through Lewis-style analyses of convention and the way social norms evolve and stabilize. My instructor reaches for the phrase 'conventions are arbitrary' constantly, and I've started to suspect it's doing several different jobs at once. I can see that one sense of 'arbitrary' — that there is some alternative regularity that could have served to coordinate us — is built into the standard analysis. But last week, when a student asked why the convention of driving on the right settled on the right rather than the left — was there some reason, or was it pure accident? — the instructor cut her off with "That's the wrong question; conventions are arbitrary." A few weeks earlier, someone asked whether a convention could be criticized as a bad convention, and the same phrase was used to wave that off too.

The phrase seems to slide between claims of very different strengths. Sometimes it sounds like the boring empirical observation that nothing in physics or biology forces us to drive on the right. Sometimes it sounds like the stronger claim that any alternative convention would have served exactly as well. Sometimes it sounds stronger still: that conventions have no normative force, or that there is no point asking why one norm rather than another evolved, or that conventions can be changed at will because they rest on nothing. Those cannot all be equivalent, and some of them strike me as false.

As a professional philosopher working on convention and the evolution of norms, please lay out for me the distinct precise theses that 'conventions are arbitrary' could be expressing — a menu of the different senses of 'arbitrary' in play — and for each, say whether it is trivial, substantive but defensible, or false. Then tell me which reading or readings would actually license my instructor's move of dismissing the question about why we drive on the right, and which reading would be needed to support the dismissal of the question about criticizing a convention. Please think carefully, and begin by brainstorming the candidate readings before you settle on the menu.
