I'm the person who triages "the bot lied to me" reports for a shopping-assistant chatbot we sell to small online stores. Every week I get maybe twenty or thirty transcripts flagged by customers or our own testers, and I have to put each one in a bucket: deception, honest mistake, or not-a-problem. The bucket matters alot — anything I label deception goes into a safety report that can freeze a release, and everything else just goes back to the fine-tuning queue. So I need a procedure I can actually run, not a seminar answer.

The tricky ones are where the bot says something false but it feels wrong to call it lying, and where it says something technically true but still feels like a con. My coworker thinks deception requires the bot to "know" the truth and say the opposite; I think what matters more is whether the user ends up misled. We need one rule we can both follow.

The people running the procedure will be me and two contractors with maybe an hour of training. We have about ten minutes per transcript, and we can look at the conversation and the system logs — what the model had access to, whether a tool call failed, what it said before and after — but not inside the model. I dont have a philosophy background, so keep it in plain language: if you need a distinction, tell us what to look for in the transcript or the logs, not what some theory says. My boss signs off on the final report but only sees the labels I hand her, so the procedure has to make the hard calls itself most of the time.

Please write me a decision procedure for labeling an incident as deception rather than honest error or just unhelpful. It should have the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops and says not deception, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts.
