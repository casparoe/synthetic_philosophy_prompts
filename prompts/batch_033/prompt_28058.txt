I've encountered a recurring argument in certain two-player games and I'm trying to pin down what it's called and what its exact scope is.

The argument works like this. Take a game where players alternate placing pieces on a board, the game always ends with a win for one side (no draws), and — crucially — having an extra piece of your own on the board can never make your position worse. To prove the first player can force a win, you assume the opposite: that the second player has a winning strategy. The first player then makes an arbitrary opening move, and for the rest of the game ignores that extra piece and follows the second player's supposed winning strategy. If the strategy ever calls for moving to the already-occupied square, the first player just makes another arbitrary move. Since an extra piece never hurts, the first player will win, contradicting the assumption. Therefore the second player cannot have a winning strategy, and — given no draws — the first player must.

What interests me is the peculiar epistemological status of this proof. It tells you that a winning strategy exists while giving you zero information about what it is. You learn that the first player can force a win, but you finish the proof with no idea what move to make. It also depends on a specific game property — that extra pieces are harmless — which seems to be doing a lot of work but isn't always made explicit.

I have two questions. First, is there an established name for this argument, and is it considered a single argument form or a family of related arguments? Second, what exactly are the necessary and sufficient conditions on a game for the argument to be valid? I can list plausible conditions — symmetry between players, no chance, no draws, extra pieces never harmful — but I'm unsure whether that list is complete, or whether some of those conditions can be relaxed. I'm less interested in the bare label than in understanding the precise boundaries of where the argument applies and where it breaks.
