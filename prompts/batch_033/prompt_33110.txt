I work as an actuary, mostly on pricing and structuring health and disability coverage, and I have no training in philosophy. In thinking about which disadvantages a society ought to repair, I've settled on a rough principle of my own: the losses that create a genuine claim on shared resources are the ones a person neither chose nor had a reasonable opportunity to control, while losses traceable to risks a person knowingly accepted don't create the same kind of claim. I've gathered that philosophers have developed a position in this neighborhood, sometimes called luck egalitarianism, organized around a distinction between brute luck and option luck. I'm fluent in my own vocabulary and an outsider to theirs.

I'd like you to restate luck egalitarianism in the vocabulary I actually use day to day — insurable peril, exclusions, pre-existing conditions, moral hazard, adverse selection, underwriting discretion, community rating, experience rating, policy limits, coinsurance — as faithfully as you can, and then tell me what does not survive the translation. For each of the view's main commitments, as you'd carve them up, say whether it has a clean counterpart in underwriting language, whether it can be carried over only by bending it out of shape, or whether it has no counterpart at all. Pay particular attention to the places where the mapping breaks. Does the brute/option luck line track the line between covered and excluded losses, or does it cut across it? Is the permission to let option-luck losses lie where they fall just underwriting freedom, or is it doing something my framework cannot see? Does underwriting vocabulary quietly import assumptions the philosophical view doesn't share — for example, that compensation must be priced in advance, or that a risk counts as chosen only if a reasonable insurer could have underwritten it? And where the translation fails, I want your judgment: is the failure a limitation of my vocabulary, or a sign that the brute/option luck distinction is doing more work than it can justify?
