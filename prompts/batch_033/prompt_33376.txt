I was at a decision theory workshop recently and overheard two participants, let us call them Soren and Lena, arguing for a solid twenty minutes about the absent-minded driver problem. I took notes because neither would budge and I could not tell who was right. I would like you to go through their exchange and adjudicate it point by point — who is right about which specific claim, who is more right overall, and what, if anything, both parties are missing.

The setup they were working with is the standard one: A driver starts at the beginning of a highway. At a first intersection, X, he can either EXIT (payoff 0) or CONTINUE. If he continues, he reaches a second intersection, Y, where he can EXIT (payoff 4) or CONTINUE (payoff 1). The driver is absent-minded: when he is at an intersection he cannot tell whether it is X or Y, and he cannot remember whether he has already passed one. He must choose a behavioral strategy — a probability p with which he CONTINUES at any intersection he finds himself at.

Here is the argument as I recorded it.

Soren: The planning-stage optimal strategy is to continue with probability two-thirds. That maximizes the ex ante expected payoff from the start of the trip. The whole idea that the driver should change his strategy once he is at an intersection is a mistake. He is the same person, he has no new information, so he should stick with the plan.

Lena: He does have new information. At the planning stage he knows he is at the start. At the action stage he knows he is at an intersection — he just does not know which one. That is a different information state. The probability of being at X versus Y is not the same as the probability of visiting X versus Y from the start. Once you are at an intersection, you must condition on having arrived at some intersection, and that changes the probabilities.

Soren: But the driver knew at the planning stage that he would arrive at X with certainty and at Y with probability p. He could have done the conditional reasoning then. And if he had, he would see that the action that maximizes expected payoff conditional on being at an intersection is exactly the same as the action that maximizes ex ante payoff. There is no inconsistency if you do the math correctly.

Lena: If you do the math correctly, you find that the action-stage expected payoff, conditional on being at an intersection, is the total expected payoff of the trip divided by the expected number of intersections visited. Maximizing that gives p equal to around 0.53, not two-thirds. The planning-stage payoff is the total expected payoff, which is maximized at two-thirds. They are different functions, so they have different maxima. The divergence is real.

Soren: The function you are maximizing at the action stage is the wrong objective. That is the expected payoff per intersection visit, but the driver only receives one payoff at the end of the trip. You are treating the trip as if it generates a payoff at each intersection, which it does not. The planning-stage objective is the right one because it gives the total expected payoff of the whole trip, and the driver should care about the whole trip.

Lena: The driver at the intersection is facing a decision right now. He does not know whether he is at X or Y. The standard decision-theoretic method is to assign probabilities to the two states and then choose the action that maximizes expected utility. The probability of being at X, given that he is at an intersection and that he is following strategy p, is one over one-plus-p. That is a straightforward application of conditional probability. If you then compute the expected utility of EXIT versus CONTINUE using those probabilities, you get a different answer than the planning stage. That is the paradox — it shows that standard decision theory with imperfect recall is inconsistent.

Soren: The inconsistency only arises if you assume that the driver can freely choose his action at the action stage without considering that the same reasoning applies at both intersections. If the driver at X considers that he will face the same decision at Y, he will realize that the optimal action-stage strategy must be a fixed point. And the fixed point is two-thirds, the same as the planning stage. The action-stage reasoning that gives 0.53 fails to take into account that the decision at Y is made by the same person using the same reasoning.

Lena: That is exactly the point. The driver at X and the driver at Y are the same person, but they have the same information state. They cannot coordinate. The action-stage reasoning that gives 0.53 is the correct one for a single decision-maker who finds himself in an information state and knows that he will follow the same policy in the same information state. It is a consistent strategy — just not the ex ante optimal one. The fact that the ex ante optimal strategy is different is the paradox. It means the driver would prefer to precommit to a strategy that he will not want to follow once he is actually at the intersection.

Soren: But that is a standard feature of many decision problems. You might want to precommit to a strategy that you would later be tempted to deviate from. That does not mean the later deviation is rational. The rational choice is to follow the precommitment if you can. And if you cannot precommit, you should still use the planning-stage reasoning because it is the perspective of the person who exists before any decisions are made. That is the perspective that considers all consequences.

Lena: Why should the planning-stage perspective be privileged? The person at the intersection is just as real as the person at the planning stage. If you think that rationality requires maximizing expected utility given your current information, then the action-stage reasoning is the only one that respects that. The planning stage is just a different information state. Neither is privileged.

Soren: The planning stage is privileged because it is the only perspective that considers the whole trip. The action-stage perspective is partial — it only considers the payoff from the current intersection onward. But the driver at the planning stage already considered that he would be at an intersection, and he already chose the strategy that maximizes the total payoff. The action-stage reasoning is just a mistake.

Lena: The action-stage reasoning does consider the whole trip from the current intersection onward. It is not ignoring anything. It is just conditioning on a different event. The fact that the two perspectives give different answers is a genuine paradox. It means that standard expected utility theory does not give a unique answer in problems with imperfect recall. That is the whole point of introducing this example.

Then give an overall judgment: who is more right, and what is the most important thing that both of them are missing or failing to address?
