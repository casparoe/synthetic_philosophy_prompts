I'm in a philosophy of mind reading group and we've been working through teleosemantics — Millikan, Papineau, Dretske, the whole project of grounding mental content in biological function. I put together an argument that I thought was going to be a tidy objection to bring to discussion, but now I'm worried it cuts deeper than I intended and I can't figure out which premise to let go of.

Here's the argument:

(1) According to teleosemantics, the content of a mental representation is fixed by its proper biological function — roughly, what states of the world it was selected to indicate or correlate with.

(2) Biological functions are fixed by selection history. A mechanism has the function of producing representation R in response to condition C because past tokens of that mechanism, producing R when C obtained, contributed to fitness.

(3) Any given selection history is describable at multiple levels of grain. The frog's prey-detection mechanism was selected because it fired when flies were present (high-level), or when small dark convex shapes moved across the retina (mid-level), or when specific ganglion-cell firing patterns occurred (low-level). These are all empirically adequate descriptions of the same selection regime — none is the "wrong" causal story.

(4) The level of description you pick determines the content you assign. If you describe the selection history at the fly level, the content is FLY; if you describe it at the retinal-pattern level, the content is RETINAL-PATTERN-X. These are distinct contents with different truth-conditions and different explanatory roles.

(5) Nothing in the selection history itself privileges one level of description over the others. Natural selection doesn't come with a label specifying which grain of description is "the" function — it just operates on causal patterns, and those patterns exist at every level simultaneously.

(6) So, from (3)–(5), teleosemantics leaves content radically indeterminate: for any mental representation, there are indefinitely many equally correct content assignments.

(7) But mental content isn't like that. When I think "there's a dog," I'm not thereby thinking "there's a furry quadruped silhouette" or "there's a pattern of photons consistent with canid morphology" — at least, not in the same sense. Content is determinate.

(8) Therefore teleosemantics fails to capture the determinacy of content, and since determinacy is a non-negotiable feature of content, teleosemantics is false.

The conclusion is way stronger than I wanted. I set out to write a modest objection about the grain problem, but this argument seems to slap down the whole project. And yet each premise feels solid individually. I'm especially unwilling to give up (7) — if content is indeterminate you lose the whole notion of truth-conditions and the explanation of behavior, and that seems like a much bigger price than just admitting teleosemantics needs a patch.

Where does this go wrong? If it doesn't go wrong — if the argument is valid — which premise is the least costly to reject, and what do you have to buy to reject it?
