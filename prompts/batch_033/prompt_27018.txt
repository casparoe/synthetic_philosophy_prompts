I'm taking a decision theory seminar and we just did the money pump argument against intransitive preferences, and I keep feeling like I'm missing whats supposed to make it a rationality argument at all. The setup is that someone prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A. A trader starts them with C and charges a small fee to swap to B, which they prefer, then a fee to swap to A, which they prefer to B, then a fee to swap back to C, which they prefer to A, so they end up back where they started minus the fees. I get the mechanics. What I don't get is why this shows the preferences are irrational. At each step the person is getting something they genuinely prefer to what they already had, so each individual trade looks perfectly rational in isolation. If I'd pay a little to have B rather than C, and a little to have A rather than B, and a little to have C rather than A, that just seems like three separate rational choices that happen to add up badly. And if I'm on to the trader's trick I can simply refuse the third trade, so I never actually get pumped. Doesn't the fact that I can refuse the last trade show the whole thing is just a scam I can opt out of, not a proof that my preferences are incoherent? Where exactly is the irrationality supposed to live?
