Here's a question that's been bugging me and I want to see what you make of it. I'm teaching Marx this semester and also reading a bunch of longtermist stuff (Bostrom, MacAskill, that crowd), and the hinge of history hypothesis keeps coming up—the idea that we're living at a uniquely pivotal moment, that our actions now have disproportionate leverage over the very long-run future. Marx obviously never encountered this idea in its modern form. But he had a full-blown theory of history, and I want to know what he'd make of it.

So: predict Marx's view on the hinge of history hypothesis. Don't just tell me he'd be for it or against it—walk me through the specific bits of his thought that would push in different directions, and then take a stand on the balance. Specifically: (a) would Marx even accept that there *could be* a hinge of history? Under historical materialism, does the structure of history allow for periods that are vastly more influential than others? (b) If he did accept it, where would he locate the hinge—would it be the transition to communism, or something else? (c) How does his view of revolutionary praxis (the unity of theory and practice, the idea that you understand history by changing it) interact with the claim that our choices now are uniquely consequential? Does it make him more sympathetic to the hinge idea, or does it cut the other way? And (d) would Marx's determinism (or whatever you want to call it—the claim that history has a direction and a logic) make him resist the idea that the hinge is *now*, as opposed to some past or future moment?

Don't worry about geting Marx "right" in some scholarly sense—I'm interested in the philosophical reasoning, not the textual evidence.
