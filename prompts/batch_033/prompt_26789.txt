I serve on my company's compensation philosophy committee. We're a ~200-person SaaS company, and from the start we've had a flat profit-sharing model: every quarter, after costs and reserves, the remaining profit is divided equally among all employees, regardless of role or seniority. It's been a point of pride and a recruiting tool.

Eighteen months ago we introduced a peer-to-peer appreciation system—internally we call it "kudos." Each employee gets a monthly allowance, deducted from what would otherwise be their profit share, that they can allocate to colleagues who helped them that month. The idea was to let people reward helpfulness that management might not see, and to preserve the equal-share baseline while adding a voluntary layer. Participation is optional; you can leave your allowance unallocated and it flows back into the general pool.

The system has been popular but the distributional effects are stark. A year of data shows that roughly 12% of employees receive over 60% of all kudos dollars. The top recipients are disproportionately the people who give internal tech talks, write the most-read documentation, mentor new hires, or are simply the most visible—the people you think of when you need help. Meanwhile, about 20% of employees receive almost nothing, and some of them report feeling that the system has made the company feel less egalitarian, not more. The phrase "popularity contest" has come up more than once.

The committee is split. One camp says the kudos system is doing exactly what it was designed to do: it lets people voluntarily reward the colleagues who actually help them, and if that produces an unequal outcome, that's fine—nobody was forced to give, and nobody had anything taken from them. The baseline is still equal; the kudos just reflect real differences in how much people contribute to their coworkers' work. The other camp says the outcome is a problem regardless of the process: we started with a commitment to equal treatment, and we've ended up with a system where a small group takes home significantly more than everyone else, which undermines the culture we built the company on.

I need to draft the committee's decision memo for the CEO. The memo should recommend one of the following: keep the system as-is, cap individual kudos receipts, restructure the allowance to be need-based rather than free-allocation, or eliminate the system and return to pure equal sharing. The recommendation needs to engage seriously with the strongest argument on the losing side—not just dismiss it. And since the CEO will want to know how firm our reasoning is, the memo should indicate how confident we are in the recommendation and call out the specific point we're least sure about.
