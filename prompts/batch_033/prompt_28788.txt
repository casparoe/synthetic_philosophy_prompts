I serve on the governing board of a small residential cooperative. We are revising our house rules, and a dispute has arisen that the board must resolve by adopting a general policy. Two members, Alvarez and Chen, live in adjacent units. Alvarez wants to practice trumpet during the early evening, from six to eight o'clock, which is the only window available given work and family obligations. Chen finds the sound of the trumpet, even when muffled, deeply distressing — it reliably triggers migraines. Chen, for their part, wants to use a scent diffuser with strong essential oils throughout their unit, which Alvarez finds nauseating and which seeps through the shared ventilation system. Each member insists that what they do inside their own unit is their private business and that the cooperative has no legitimate authority to interfere. Each also insists, however, that the board should prohibit the other's activity as a nuisance.

The board has established the following facts, which neither party disputes. If both activities are permitted, both members will engage in them, and both will be significantly worse off than if neither activity occurs — each would prefer the quiet, scent-free status quo to a world with both trumpet practice and diffuser. If only one activity is permitted, the permitted member is content and the other is not. The cooperative's charter grants the board broad discretion to regulate conduct within units when it affects other members' quiet enjoyment.

The board is divided. Some members argue that the cooperative exists precisely to handle conflicts like this and that the obvious solution is to prohibit both activities: everyone prefers that outcome to the one where both occur. Others argue that prohibiting either activity within a member's own unit sets a dangerous precedent — it amounts to telling people what they can and cannot do in their own homes, which is the sort of overreach that makes people flee cooperatives for detached houses. They say the board should respect each member's autonomy over their own unit and let the chips fall where they may, even if the result is worse for everyone.

Please draft the board's decision memo. It should recommend a concrete policy — which need not be simply "ban both" or "allow both"; you may propose a more nuanced rule — and give the reasoning for it. The memo must engage seriously with the strongest argument on the losing side and explain why, on balance, it does not carry the day. The board will vote on this memo at its next meeting, so the recommendation must be actionable.
