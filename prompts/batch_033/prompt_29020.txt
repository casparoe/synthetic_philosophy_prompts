I'm a PhD student in philosophy, working mostly on the epistemology of logic, and I've been rereading Lewis Carroll's "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles" for a chapter I'm writing. I thought I had it pinned down untill I tried to explain it to my advisor, and now I'm stuck on a pretty specific spot. The setup: Achilles wants to force the Tortoise, who accepts A and B but refuses to accept the conditional "if A and B then Z," to accept Z. Achilles gets him to accept that conditional as a new premise, C. Then this exchange happens:

"And why must I?"
"Because it follows logically from them. If A and B and C are true, Z must be true. You don't dispute that, I imagine?"
"If A and B and C are true, Z must be true," the Tortoise thoughtfully repeated. "That's another Hypothetical, isn't it? And, if I failed to see its truth, I might accept A and B and C, and still not accept Z, mightn't I?"
"You might," the candid hero admitted; "though such obtuseness would certainly be phenomenal. Still, the event is possible. So I must ask you to grant one more Hypothetical."

What I can't get straight is this: is the Tortoise's move here just a rephrasing of what Achilles already got him to accept as C, or is he pointing at something genuinely new? When Achilles adds C as a premise, what exactly has gone wrong, and why doesn't adding D, E, and so on ever fix it? I keep flip-flopping between thinking the Tortoise is being obtuse and thinking he's exposing a real difference between a premise and a rule of inference, and I'd love help sorting out which one the passage actually supports.
