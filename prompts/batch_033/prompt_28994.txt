I'm a high school student and a member of my school's philosophy club. This semester we've been reading about moral status—the question of which beings matter morally and to what degree. After working through Singer and Regan, our discussion turned to a point of disagreement that the readings didn't resolve. If we accept that sentience is the entry condition for moral considerability, does it follow that all sentient beings count equally? Or can differences in the richness of conscious experience—differences that seem to correlate, however roughly, with brain size and neural complexity—justify giving greater weight to the interests of some beings over others? One club member proposed a concrete scenario: imagine a mouse, a chimpanzee, and a human with a cognitive disability that leaves her with reasoning capacities comparable to the chimp's. If you could only save two of the three from a fire, does brain size (or what it proxies) give you a principled reason to save the chimp over the mouse and the human over the chimp? The room split, and we couldn't reach a consensus.

I would like to see this disagreement explored in a careful dialogue. Please write a conversation between two characters—call them Elena and Rafi—who are both well-versed in ethics and philosophy of mind but hold opposing views. Elena defends the position that cognitive capacity, as reflected in brain size and neural complexity, is directly morally relevant: it affects the range and depth of welfare an individual can experience, and therefore how much weight their interests carry in our deliberations. Rafi defends a threshold view: once a being crosses the threshold into sentience, all sentient beings possess equal moral standing, and differential treatment based on cognitive sophistication amounts to a kind of discrimination—what you might call speciesism by other means. The dialogue should feature genuine back-and-forth, with each character responding to the other's strongest arguments rather than talking past them. At some point, Elena should challenge Rafi on whether his view commits him to treating the life of a mouse and the life of a typical adult human as equally valuable, and Rafi should challenge Elena on whether her view has troubling implications for humans with severe cognitive disabilities. Both should offer substantive, good-faith replies rather than deflections or rhetorical moves.
