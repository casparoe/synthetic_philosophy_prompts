Lakatos's methodology of scientific research programmes was meant to steer between Popper's falsificationism and Kuhn's paradigm shifts while preserving the rationality of theory change. Before we judge whether it succeeds, we need to know what a theory of scientific change is supposed to do. Write the desiderata: what any adequate account of how science grows, stalls, or changes direction must deliver. Include the phenomena it must explain (the apparent cumulative progress of mature sciences, the persistence of anomalies without theory abandonment, the coexistence of rival research programmes, and the occasional rapid overthrow of a dominant framework), the distinctions it must draw (progressive vs. degenerating problemshifts, rational vs. irrational theory choice, and the proper unit of appraisal — individual theory, research programme, or something else), and the normative work it must do (demarcating science from non-science, offering retrospective reconstructions that do not collapse into Whig history, and providing prospective guidance that does not collapse into "anything goes"). For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it. Then mark which desiderata are non-negotiable for any rationalist account and which reflect Lakatos's own commitments that a more naturalistic or sociological approach could reasonably reject. Finally, identify whether any pair of desiderata cannot be jointly satisfied — this is the part I most want to see.
