I've been going back and forth with a friend about Kuhn for weeks now — he's a physicist who thinks philosophy of science is basically fan fiction about what scientists do, and I'm trying to convince him Kuhn is worth taking seriously. The problem is I built this argument to show him what's at stake, and now I'm the one who can't answer it.

Here's the argument I wrote out:

(1) The meaning of a theoretical term is fixed by its inferential role — the whole network of laws, assumptions, and connections it's embedded in. You don't know what "mass" means unless you know how it behaves in the theory's equations. (Meaning holism.)

(2) When a scientific revolution replaces one paradigm with another, the inferential network changes so drastically that the central terms acquire different inferential roles. Newtonian mass is conserved in all interactions; relativistic mass is equivalent to energy and not conserved. These aren't the same concept with a new property discovered — they're different concepts.

(3) If two statements use a term with different meanings, they cannot be logical contradictories. You can't contradict "the bank is steep" (riverbank) by saying "the bank is not steep" (financial institution). Likewise, when an Aristotelian says "all motion requires a mover" and a Newtonian says "not all motion requires a mover," the word "motion" means different things in each mouth, and there's no genuine contradiction — just two people talking past each other.

(4) If successive theories cannot contradict each other, they cannot make incompatible predictions. Incompatible predictions just are contradictory statements about what we'll observe: theory A says "the pointer will read 7" and theory B says "the pointer will not read 7." If the theories can't contradict, they can't issue predictions that clash.

(5) If successive theories can't make incompatible predictions, then no empirical observation can logically favor one theory over its predecessor. The evidence can always be redescribed or reinterpreted within each paradigm's own conceptual scheme to fit.

(6) If empirical evidence never logically favors one theory over its predecessor, then scientific change is not driven by evidence in the way the rationalist tradition claims. Theory choice is underdetermined by the data, and something extra — social pressure, rhetorical skill, aesthetic preference, or just the old guard dying off — must close the gap.

(7) But Kuhn spent his entire career insisting that this is not what he meant. He denied being a relativist, he said science is rational and makes progress, and he argued that incommensurability does not mean incomparability. He was adament about this.

Now (1)-(6) seem to force the conclusion that paradigm change is non-rational and Kuhn's view is relativist, flatly contradicting (7). So either Kuhn was inconsistent and didn't realize his own view's implications, or one of (1)-(6) is false. I'm most suspicious of (3) — it feels like it's asking too much of "contradiction," and maybe all Kuhn actually needs is the weaker claim that there's no neutral observation language for adjudicating between paradigms, which is compatible with there being some kind of rational comparison. But I can't make that precise, and my physicist friend keeps saying "so you admit it's all just taste then."

Give me a structured verdict.
