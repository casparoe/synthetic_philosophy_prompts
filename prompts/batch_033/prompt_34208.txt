I was at a philosophy department party last weekend and ended up overhearing a pretty intense argument between two grad students that I've been turning over in my head all week. They were arguing about transcendental arguments -- you know, the kind that try to show that something has to be the case because it's a necessary condition for the possibility of experience or self-consciousness or whatever.

The argument was about whether transcendental arguments can ever actually establish anything about the world, or whether they just show us what we're psychologically forced to believe. Max was defending the approach: he said that if you can show that your having self-conscious experience at all depends on some fact P being true, then any doubt about P is self-undermining -- the very act of doubting P presupposes the conditions that make doubting possible, which include P. So you get a genuine conclusion about the world, not just about your psychology.

Lena pushed back: she said that's exactly the sleight of hand. The argument shows at best that you must *believe* or *presuppose* P in order to make sense of your experience, but that doesn't entail P is true. She said the gap is between what's a necessary condition of *our conceptual scheme* and what's a necessary condition of *reality*. Max said that distinction collapses if you take the argument seriously, because the conceptual scheme in question is the one that makes experience possible at all, not just some optional framework we happen to use. She said that just pushes the question back: is it experience as such that requires P, or experience *as we happen to have it*? And if it's the latter, maybe we just have a limited kind of experience that forces us into a belief that could still be false.

Max then said that if Lena is right, she's committed to the idea that there's some standpoint outside our own form of experience from which we can judge it -- which is exactly what the transcendental argument is supposed to show is impossible. She said that's question-begging: he's assuming the conclusion of the transcendental argument in order to defend the transcendental argument. They went in circles for a while and eventually someone pulled them away to play beer pong.

I'm not a philosopher, and I honestly couldn't figure out who was making the better case. Can you go through their exchange move by move and tell me who's right about what? And especially: is there a way out of the impasse they reached, or is the debate about transcendental arguments genuinely stuck at this point?
