I'm a PhD student in economics working on social choice and welfare economics, and I've been loosing sleep over Harsanyi's aggregation theorem. I wrote up the following argument for a term paper and now I can't tell whether it's a proof of utilitarianism or a reductio of one of the premises. Could you act as a decision theorist who's also comfortable with the philosophy of social choice? Please think through the argument step by step — maybe start by steelmanning each premise — and then tell me exactly which step fails, or which premise is least costly to give up, and what the bill comes to.

The argument, as I have it:

(1) Each individual i has a preference relation over lotteries that satisfies the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms (completeness, transitivity, continuity, independence). So each i can be represented by a utility function u_i, unique up to positive affine transformation, and i maximizes expected utility.

(2) The social evaluator — the "ethical observer," or whatever you want to call it — also has a preference relation over the same lotteries, and it too satisfies the VNM axioms. So social preference is representable by a social utility W that ranks lotteries by expected value.

(3) Pareto: if every individual weakly prefers lottery A to lottery B, then the social evaluator weakly prefers A to B; and if everyone weakly prefers A to B while at least one person strictly prefers A to B, then the social evaluator strictly prefers A to B.

(4) Harsanyi's theorem says (1)–(3) force W to be a positive affine combination of the individual u_i: W = Σ a_i u_i + b, with each a_i > 0.

(5) But that's just weighted utilitarianism. So it looks like we've derived utilitarianism (up to the choice of weights) from nothing but expected-utility rationality for everyone, individual and social, plus Pareto.

(6) And that's what bothers me, because the same derivation seems to prove too much. Run it on a hiring committee or a corporate board and you'd "prove" that any group preference that's VNM-rational and Pareto-respecting must be a weighted sum of the members' utilities — which sounds like a representation theorem about consistent aggregation, not a moral conclusion. Worse, the argument rules out ex ante egalitarian or prioritarian social objectives over lotteries without ever saying a word about equality, fairness, or risk-sharing. It feels like a moral premise got smuggled in somewhere, but each of (1)–(3) looks, on its face, like a purely technical condition.

So: where does this go wrong? If it doesn't, I guess I have to become a utilitarian, which I'd rather not do on the strength of a theorem. I'd most like to keep (1) and (3). Name the step that fails (or the premise that's least costly to reject), explain why it looked so innocent, and spell out what giving it up costs — that is, what alternative positions open up once it's gone.
