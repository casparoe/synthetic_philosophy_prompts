In my philosophy of mind seminar this term we have been reading the recent literature on illusionism about phenomenal consciousness, and I have been struck by how little the two sides have moved in the years since the debate took its current shape. On one side, illusionists associated with Keith Frankish hold that experiences do not really possess the phenomenal properties they introspectively seem to possess: the sense of a private realm of intrinsic, ineffable qualitative properties is a kind of introspective illusion, and the task for a theory of consciousness is to explain why creatures like us are so strongly disposed to believe in such properties. On the other side, realists such as Galen Strawson answer that the position is self-defeating, because an illusion in the relevant sense is itself a conscious experience, so the view presupposes the very phenomenality it claims to debunk. Illusionists reply that illusion here means only a false representation or a mistaken theoretical posit, not something that feels like something, but realists regard this reply as changing the subject. The standoff has been sharpened by Chalmers's framing of the meta-problem of consciousness, the problem of explaining why we judge that there is a hard problem of consciousness at all: illusionists treat solving that meta-problem as the whole task, while realists think a complete solution to it would leave the hard problem itself untouched.

Rather than asking which side is right, I would like a diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement, engaging the arguments each side actually gives. Is this a substantive disagreement about what the mind contains, a verbal disagreement about the term phenomenal consciousness, a disagreement about what counts as data in the study of consciousness, or a disagreement about the standards a theory must meet before it may debunk a first-person appearance? My working hypothesis is that the deepest fault line is methodological: realists treat the first-person appearance of phenomenality as a datum with default authority that any acceptable theory must preserve, while illusionists treat the first-person reports, and the dispositions behind them, as the data, and regard the appearance of a private qualitative realm as a theoretical interpretation of those data that may be rejected. On this hypothesis, neither side can argue for its starting point in terms the other would accept, which would explain the impasse. I would like to know whether this diagnosis is correct, and, if so, what would count as progress given it; if it is not correct, what the actual source of the impasse is.
