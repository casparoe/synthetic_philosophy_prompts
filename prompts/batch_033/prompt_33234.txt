I've been thinking about this problem in ethics for a while now and I can't settle on what I think. It's about what people call the paradox of deontology. The way I understand it is: deontologists say you shouldn't do certain things — like, you shouldn't kill an innocent person — even if doing it would stop more of the same bad thing from happening. So you refuse to kill one person even though that means five other people get killed. But then the rule against killing seems to be causing more killing, not less. That feels like the rule is eating itself. I've heard people respond that deontology isn't about minimizing violations, it's about what each agent is responsible for, and that the paradox just misunderstands what deontologists are committed to. But I'm not sure that actually dissolves the worry — it might just restate it. Can you write an essay that works through whether this really is a paradox or just a sloppy way of framing the view, and actually reach a conclusion? I don't want a survey of the litterature, I want to see someone reason through it and land somewhere.
