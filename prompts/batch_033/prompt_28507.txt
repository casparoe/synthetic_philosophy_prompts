I teach political philosophy, and while preparing a seminar on Simone Weil's critique of political parties I keep returning to a puzzle about what the party system itself presupposes. I would like you to work through it with me. You are a political philosopher specializing in the theory of democratic institutions and in twentieth-century critiques of party politics. Take your time and reason through the question carefully. Begin by brainstorming the distinct theories of political judgment that the practice described below might be taken to presuppose; then defend the reconstruction you judge most accurate, and use it to answer the remaining questions.

Consider the modern political party as it actually operates in representative democracies, bracketing the question whether any particular party is good or bad. Several features of the practice are striking. Voters choose among parties at least as much as among candidates. Parties control nominations, ballot access, and much of the campaign funding. Elected members are expected to vote with their party, and the apparatus of party discipline—whips, caucus rules, committee assignments, the threat of losing the party label—is treated as a normal and legitimate part of legislation. A platform bundles dozens of unrelated commitments into a single package that citizens are asked to accept or reject wholesale, and the party itself is treated as having collective interests, a collective reputation, and a collective strategy not reducible to the judgments of its individual members. Yet the same democracies also treat individual judgment as the ultimate political safeguard. Jurors deliberate as individuals and are insulated from party discipline; judges recuse themselves for bias and publish separate opinions; conscientious objection is recognized as a ground for exemption from otherwise binding requirements. So the practice seems to say two things at once: that the individual conscience is the seat of political judgment, and that the central institution of representative government should be built to override it.

Simone Weil argued that this is not a correctable defect of particular parties but a feature of the party form as such. On her view, a party is by nature organized to win and hold power, to manufacture collective passions, and to substitute the group's will for the individual's capacity for attention and truth; she concluded that parties should be abolished rather than reformed.

What theory of political judgment does party government presuppose? More specifically: does the practice presuppose that there is a collective subject—the party—that can judge, deliberate, and act in its own right, or does it merely presuppose that disciplined voting is a regrettable but useful coordination device? Does it presuppose that political judgment is best produced through organized conflict among disciplined bodies, or is that only a contingent feature parties happen to have? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, distinguishing what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Identify the rival views of political judgment that the practice quietly rules out, and say whether its parts presuppose consistent things, given the contrast with juries, judicial independence, and conscientious objection. Finally, assess whether the practice, on your best reconstruction, is vulnerable to Weil's critique, and state what would have to change institutionally if her rival view of political judgment were adopted.
