I've been going down this rabbit hole lately — I'm a software engineer, I work on backend stuff mostly, but I got interested in mechanistic interpretability after watching some talks and reading blog posts from Anthropic and Chris Olah's group. And there's this one question that I can't shake, and honestly my coworkers are tired of me bringing it up at lunch.

The thing is: when people say they've "found a representation" inside a neural network — like, they train a sparse autoencoder on the activations of some layer in a language model and they get a feature that lights up for "deception" or "the Golden Gate Bridge" or whatever — I don't actually know what ontological status I'm supposed to assign to that. Is the feature *really there* in the network, or did the researcher basically carve nature at joints that were convenient for them? On the one hand, it's just matrix multiplies all the way down; there's no variable called `deception_bool` floating around in the weights. On the other hand, if the feature direction consistently activates when the model outputs deceptive text, and you can *causally* intervene by clamping that direction and the model's behavior changes in exactly the ways you'd predict, then insisting it's "not real" starts to sound like insisting that temperature isn't real because it's "just" mean kinetic energy. But I also worry that the sparse autoencoder is doing a lot of the work — like, maybe it's imposing structure that isn't native to the network, and we're just seeing our own reflection in the data.

So here's what I want: give me your actual credence — a number between 0 and 1 — on the following claim: **"At least some features identified by current mechanistic interpretability methods (e.g., sparse autoencoders trained on transformer MLP or residual stream activations) correspond to representations that are genuinely present in the network's computational structure, rather than being artifacts of the interpretation pipeline or useful fictions projected onto the system by the researcher."** But the number alone is basically useless to me. I want the decomposition: what are the sub-questions your credence rides on? For each one, give me a rough probability and a sentence or two explaining why you put it there. Then show how they combine — multiplicatively, additively, whatever makes sense — to produce your final number. And crucially, tell me which sub-question you're least confident in, and what specific piece of evidence (an experiment someone could actually run, a theoretical result, a particular kind of failure mode being observed) would move your credence on that sub-question the most. I'm trying to figure out where the real uncertainty lives, not just get a number to put on a dashboard.
