I've been working through an argument that seems to prove that if you accept moral luck in even one domain, you're committed to the view that no one ever deserves blame for anything. I don't believe the conclusion, but I can't locate the mistake.

(1) Two drivers, A and B, both drive home equally drunk. A hits and kills a child who runs into the road; B makes it home without incident. The only difference between them is luck.

(2) If the only difference between two cases is luck, then A and B are equally deserving of moral blame. Call this the Luck-Neutrality Principle (LNP).

(3) So A does not deserve more blame than B.

(4) Now consider two people, C and D, with identical genetic predispositions. C grows up in a loving, stable home; D grows up in an abusive, chaotic one. D develops a violent temper; C does not. The only difference in their formative circumstances is luck.

(5) If LNP applies to circumstantial luck as it does to resultant luck, then C and D are equally deserving of blame for the characters they develop. Since C did nothing blameworthy, D does not deserve blame for his character either.

(6) Now consider a violent act D commits. It flows from his violent character, for which (by (5)) he does not deserve blame. But if someone does not deserve blame for the character that produced an action, can they deserve blame for the action itself? The action is just the character expressing itself in circumstances D did not choose.

(7) So D does not deserve blame for his action.

(8) But every action anyone performs flows from their character in circumstances they did not choose, and every character is shaped by factors ultimately outside the person's control.

(9) Therefore, no one ever deserves blame for anything.

(10) But some people do deserve blame. If you deny this, you must abandon resentment, indignation, gratitude, and the entire fabric of interpersonal moral life — which seems impossible.

So LNP, which seemed modest when applied to resultant luck in premise (2), turns out to be a universal solvent. Where does the argument go wrong? I can see a few possible exits — restrict LNP so it doesn't apply across different kinds of luck, deny the move from character-blame to action-blame in (6), or bite the bullet on (10) and try to reconstruct moral life on purely forward-looking grounds — but each seems to cost more than I want to pay.

Lead with your bottom line: which step you reject, stated in the first sentence, and then give your reasoning. No preamble, no restating the question.
