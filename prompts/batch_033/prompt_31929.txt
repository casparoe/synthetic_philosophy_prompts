I serve on my university's Academic Integrity Committee, and we have been asked to rule on a proposal from the Department of Philosophy that would allow students in upper-division seminars to use large language models as a dialectical interlocutor: not to generate prose for submission, but to help them work through arguments when they are stuck. Students would be required to submit the full transcript of their interaction alongside their paper, and the paper would be graded on the quality of reasoning it displays rather than on whether the student reached it independently. The department frames this as pedagogically valuable — students learn to probe and pressure-test an argument by probing an AI, and the transcripts make their dialectical engagement visible to the instructor in a way that a finished paper never could.

Two committee members have already objected, arguing that the proposal normalizes a form of epistemic dependence that undermines the very capacities the seminars are meant to cultivate. On their view, learning to sit with the discomfort of being stuck, to work through a problem without a ready interlocutor, and to build the internal resources for self-directed reasoning is the point of advanced philosophical training, and outsourcing even the interlocutor role to a machine teaches students to reach for a crutch whenever they encounter difficulty. They want the committee to reject the proposal outright. The department chair has responded by pointing out that we already permit students to discuss their ideas with peers, attend office hours, and consult writing-center tutors — none of which we call problematic epistemic dependence. The question before us is whether the AI interlocutor is different in kind, not just in degree, from these other forms of permitted intellectual assistance, and whether that difference warrants a policy against it. The chair has also noted that a blanket rejection would be hard to enforce anyway, since students can and do use these tools regardless of what the handbook says; the only question is whether we bring the practice into the open and attach pedagogical structure to it.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before our next meeting. It should recommend a concrete decision — approve the proposal, reject it, or approve it with conditions — and it should engage seriously with the strongest consideration on the losing side. Do not simply assert that AI is a tool like any other or that it is obviously corrosive; the committee needs reasoning that acknowledges the philosophical stakes about what it means to understand something and whether understanding can be delegated. The opinion should be actionable: if you recommend conditions, specify what they are and how they would be enforced; if you recommend rejection, say what principle distinguishes the AI interlocutor from the writing-center tutor and whether that principle would also rule out other practices we currently permit.
