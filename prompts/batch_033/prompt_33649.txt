An economist in my department keeps saying a model is like a map. He points out that nobody complains that the Tube map distorts the distances between stations, or that a road atlas flattens out hills, because those distortions are exactly what let the map do its job. Then he moves to the point he actually cares about: so we shouldn't complain that a macro model assumes identical households, or that a firm behaves as-if it solves an enormous optimisation problem, as long as the model predicts well. He calls this the F-twist, and says people who fuss about realistic assumptions are confusing the map for the territory.

I can see its doing real work for him, but I don't trust it. Can you audit how far the map analogy actually holds? Give me the correspondence table first: what maps to the map, the territory, the scale, the distortions, the route, the user, the legend. Then go through where the analogy breaks, and say which breaks matter for his argument and which are harmless. I especially want to know what the map analogy quietly imports — for instance that we already know the territory independently, or that the map's simplifications could in principle be removed — and whether his conclusion (unrealistic assumptions are fine if the predictions are good) survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was doing the work all along. If there's a better analogy, say that too. And please answer in British English.
