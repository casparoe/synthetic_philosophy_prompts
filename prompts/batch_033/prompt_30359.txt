"Williams's 'one thought too many' objection is directed at utilitarianism and other impartial moral theories. He argues that these theories make excessive demands on agents because they require us to set aside our personal projects and special relationships whenever the impartial calculus says so. His central example involves a man who must choose between rescuing his wife and rescuing a stranger from some life-threatening situation. According to utilitarianism, the man ought to deliberate impartially about which rescue would produce the greatest overall utility. Williams says that if the man pauses to deliberate in this way, that very deliberation is 'one thought too many' — he should simply save his wife without needing to think about it. The deeper problem, Williams argues, is that impartial theories alienate agents from their own motivations: if the husband finds himself needing to justify saving his wife by invoking the impartial consideration that spouses are permitted to give preference to each other, he is treating his relationship as merely one source of impartial reasons among others, rather than acting from the relationship itself. Williams's ultimate point is that this kind of impartial deliberation is psychologically unrealistic, and that a viable ethical theory must make space for partiality toward those we care about. This is why Williams is generally interpreted as pushing toward some version of virtue ethics, where the agent's character and particular attachments take center stage rather than impersonal rules or calculations."
