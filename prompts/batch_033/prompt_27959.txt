I sit on the working group that decides which new diagnoses make it into the next revision of our association's classification manual, and we've got a proposal in front of us to add a new category — "persistent climate distress disorder" — for people whose anxiety, grief, or guilt about environmental change lasts more than six months and gets in the way of work or relationships. Half the group thinks this is long overdue: there are clearly people suffering in a distinctive way, and without a code they can't get reimbursed for treatment or included in research. The other half thinks we're about to create the very thing its supposed to describe: once the label exists, people will take it up as an identity, advocacy groups will form around it, clinicians will start seeing it everywhere, and the criteria will drift until we're classifying a different phenomenon than the one the proposal's authors think they're naming. I've been handed the job of drafting the group's decision memo for next month's vote. It needs a clear bottom line — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — plus a concrete timetable, and it should take the losing side's strongest consideration seriously rather than just listing objections. I keep coming back to the looping-effects worry (the idea that classifying people can change the people classified), but I'm not sure how far that gets us, since every diagnosis shapes the thing it names to some degree. So the memo has to say where the line is between "that's just normal classification" and "that's a loop we should refuse to set running." If you recommend conditions, make them concrete enough that the committee could actually adopt them — for instance, a re-evaluation schedule, a monitoring requirement, or limits on who can receive the diagnosis.
