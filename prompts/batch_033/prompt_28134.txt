I've been thinking about the moral difference between lying and misleading for a while now — reading discussions online, trying out examples on my own, and paying attention to how people actually use these categories in everyday life. What strikes me is that the debate seems to have settled into a pattern where both sides have their moves and nobody changes their mind. I want to understand why.

The basic question is whether there's a morally significant difference between lying (saying something you believe to be false) and misleading (saying something true but in a way you know will cause someone to form a false belief). On one side, people argue that lying is worse because it involves a direct betrayal: when you assert something, you invite the listener to trust that you're telling the truth, and lying abuses that invitation in a way that misleading doesn't. If I tell you the train leaves at three when I know it leaves at four, I've made you a kind of promise and broken it. But if I say the train leaves sometime after two — knowing you'll infer it leaves at three — I've been uncooperative, but I haven't directly violated the norm of assertion in the same way. On this view, the misleading person leaves the hearer some responsibility for the mistaken inference, and that shared responsibility matters morally.

On the other side, people argue that this is a distinction without a moral difference. Both actions are calculated to produce a false belief; the deceiver's goal and the victim's resulting state are identical. The person who misleads is often just exploiting a technicality to feel less guilty while achieving the same dishonest outcome. They point to cases where misleading seems just as bad as lying — for instance, carefully wording a statement to be literally true while knowing exactly what false conclusion the other person will draw, and doing so precisely to gain the same advantage a lie would have given.

I've watched this back-and-forth play out many times, and it never seems to go anywhere. My suspicion is that the two sides are actually working with different accounts of what makes deception wrong in the first place. One side locates the wrongness in the violation of a specific speech-act norm — the obligation to speak truthfully when you make an assertion. The other side locates the wrongness in the harmful outcome or in the manipulative intent, which can be equally present whether you lie or merely mislead. If that's right, then the lying-versus-misleading debate is really a proxy for a deeper disagreement about the foundations of the morality of deception, and trading examples won't resolve it until that deeper question is addressed.

But I'm not confident in this diagnosis, and I'd like it evaluated. Is this debate a substantive moral disagreement, a verbal dispute about how to define the word "lie," or a clash of bedrock moral intuitions that neither side can argue for? And given whatever diagnosis you arrive at, what would actually count as progress — a new kind of example, a conceptual distinction, a different way of framing the question — or should we expect the impasse to be permanent?
