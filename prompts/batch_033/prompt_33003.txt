I'm on the academic technology committee at my university and we're supposed to draft a policy recommendation about AI use in undergraduate courses for next semester. The thing is I just spent like three hours last night arguing about this with my friend. He's a philosophy PhD student and he kept pushing this idea that using AI for coursework doesn't just help or hurt grades — it changes what it means to actually *understand* something. His argument, roughly, was that understanding isn't just having the right output; it involves being able to trace the reasons, to see why something is the case, and that when you offload the reasoning to a model, you're not just taking a shortcut, you're outsourcing the very thing that constitutes understanding. So you might get the right answer and even be able to explain it, but the explanation is something you've *received* rather than something you've *worked through*, and the epistemic difference matters.

I pushed back that this is basically the same argument people made about calculators in math class, or spellcheck, or Google — that every new tool gets accused of destroying understanding, and then we adapt and redefine what competence looks like. He said calculators are different because they automate a mechanical step, not the reasoning itself; AI automates the reasoning. I said that's a distinction without a difference once you accept that reasoning can be decomposed into steps some of which are mechanical. We went back and forth forever and honestly I'm still turning it over.

Anyway, I need to turn this into something actionable. Draft me a short policy memo for the committee — maybe 800-1000 words — that recommends a concrete approach to AI use in undergrad courses. It should engage seriously with the philosophical worry about what it means to understand something when you're relying on a model to do the thinking for you, address the strongest counterarguments (the "it's just a tool" line, the "we've always adapted" line, and the worry that banning AI leaves students unprepared for a world where everyone uses it), and give faculty something they can actually implement. Don't just split the difference — take a real position and defend it.
