I am a PhD student in philosophy of economics, writing a chapter on how to think about false assumptions in models, and I have been trying to get my own commitments into order before I start. I would like your help extracting the principle behind my judgments—or, if there is no single principle, being told which of them I should give up.

My verdicts so far are these. I have no problem with the frictionless plane in mechanics, or with treating a cow as a sphere in a first-pass model of heat loss. I also accept the assumption of zero transaction costs in the Coase theorem: the assumption is plainly false, yet it seems to isolate the role of property-rights assignments rather than distort the conclusion. I am even comfortable with Friedman-style cases in which a skilled billiards player chooses shots as if solving the relevant physics and leaves grow as if maximizing exposure to light, so long as the point of the model is prediction. But I resist a model of financial crises that assumes rational expectations and complete markets, because the falsity of those assumptions seems to be doing the work of ruling out the phenomenon being explained. I am also uneasy about Ptolemaic epicycles: they predict planetary positions reasonably well, but the machinery is wrong in a way that seems to matter even though the predictions succeed. The pair that keeps me stuck is this: I am fine ignoring air resistance when deriving the law of falling bodies, but I am bothered when a climate model treats the atmosphere as instantly well mixed, even though both are false idealizations adopted for tractability, and I cannot yet say what separates them.

My tentative principle was roughly the one often called the F-twist: the realism of assumptions does not matter; only predictive success does. But that principle seems to license the epicycles and the crisis model, which I do not want to license. So my question is this: is there a principle that captures all or most of my verdicts, and if so, what is it? Please state it precisely, say what it predicts about a case I have not listed—for instance, a macroeconomic model that assumes a single representative agent—and be explicit about which of my verdicts, if any, the principle cannot accommodate.
