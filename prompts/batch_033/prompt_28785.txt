so i'm a novelist, and i've been chewing on this thing we all do when we talk about books. you know how it goes: someone says "the novel is about the impossibility of connection" like the meaning is just sitting there in the text, a fact about the object. then someone else says "huh, to me it was about class anxiety" and nobody goes "well which is it?" — we just nod and accept both. and when the conversation stalls someone pulls out "what do you think the author was trying to say?" as if that's the trump card. we hop between meaning-in-the-text, meaning-in-the-reader, and meaning-in-the-author like they're different camera angles on the same scene, and nobody seems bothered by the whiplash.

hofstadter had this idea about frame messages, outer messages, and inner messages — roughly, that any message comes wrapped in a "this is a message" signal (frame), plus instructions for how to decode it (outer), plus the content itself (inner). and i keep wondering: does the ordinary practice of talking about what books mean actually presuppose some coherent picture of where meaning lives? or is it just philosophically messy in a way that works fine in conversation?

so here's what i want. take the everyday practice of literary discourse — book clubs, reviews, the way readers talk after a reading — and reconstruct the implicit theory of meaning it presupposes. not what derrida or anyone said, but what the practice itself commits us to. when we switch between "the book means X," "to me it meant Y," and "the author meant Z," are we presupposing that meaning has a single location and we're just triangulating it from different angles? or are we presupposing something more like meaning is negotiated socially and the three moves are just different conversational tools? does the practice rule out any views — like, does it rule out the hardline position that meaning is entirely in the text and reader-response is just misreading? and is the practice actually consistent, or are we papering over a real tension? what would have to change in how we talk about books if we actually commited to one of these locations for meaning and dropped the others?

and if you want to use the frame/outer/inner distinction to map this — like, does the practice treat the frame message as fixed (this is a novel, read it accordingly), the outer message as shared (we all know how novels work), and the inner message as the thing we're actually arguing about? or does the practice itself blur even those boundaries?
