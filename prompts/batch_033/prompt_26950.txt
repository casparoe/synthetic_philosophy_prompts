In a reading group on medieval Islamic philosophy, one of the participants defended al-Ghazālī's occasionalism with an analogy he said made the whole doctrine intuitive: the created world is like a projected film, with God as the projector. What we take to be continuous motion and genuine causal connection is really a rapid succession of distinct frames, each of which exists only because the projector produces it; al-Ghazālī's fire and cotton appear in successive frames as if the fire did the burning, but the real work is done frame by frame by the projector, and nothing passes from one frame to the next. He concluded that occasionalism costs us nothing we actually needed from causation, and that saying fire burns cotton is just a convenient description of the sequence God chooses to project. I have been asked to give the response at the next meeting. Audit the analogy rather than simply rejecting it: give me a correspondence table for frames, projector, reel, screen, projectionist, the apparent motion, and the depicted story; then separate the disanalogies that matter for his conclusion from the ones that do not. In particular, what does the projection setup quietly import — a persisting physical medium, a mechanism that obeys laws, a film whose sequence is already fixed, a viewer whose persistence of vision supplies the continuity — that occasionalism either denies or must supply from God's will alone? Does his conclusion survive once these are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? And if the film analogy fails, is there a better analogy for what al-Ghazālī is actually claiming?
