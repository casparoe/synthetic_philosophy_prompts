I serve on my city's licensing commission. This week we vote on a permit for a business whose operations are lawful, adult, and fully consensual, but which a neighborhood association opposes as morally degrading; the opponents concede that they can point to no specific harm — no crime, no nuisance, no blight, no measurable effect on property values — only to their own strong moral revulsion. Our enabling ordinance says a license may be denied if the proposed use would be "injurious to public morals." Last night I had a long argument with a friend about whether the law may ever enforce a community's moral code as such, and I came away less certain how I should vote, not more. Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before the vote. It should recommend granting or denying the permit, give reasons the commission can actually act on, and engage seriously with the strongest consideration on the losing side. I would also like the opinion to address an inconsistency that bothers me: this same commission routinely licenses liquor stores, casinos, and payday lenders over moral objections, and regulates them only for concrete harms such as underage sales, noise, or predatory loan terms. If moral revulsion alone is a legitimate ground here, why is it not a legitimate ground there; and if it is not, what is the principle that actually distinguishes the cases, and what does that principle commit the commission to going forward?
