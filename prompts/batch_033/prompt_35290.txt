"Parfit's nonidentity problem shows that our obligations to future generations can't be grounded in the rights or interests of the particular people who will exist. Here's the reasoning: suppose we have to choose between a risky industrial policy and a conservative one. The risky policy will gradually shift the circumstances in which people meet and have children, so that after a couple of generations, entirely different people exist than would have existed under the conservative policy. If the risky policy also makes life somewhat worse—say, higher cancer rates—the people who get cancer still have lives worth living, and they can't claim we harmed them by choosing the risky policy, because the alternative for them was never existing at all. Parfit concluded that the only coherent way to say the risky policy is wrong is to adopt an impersonal consequentialist framework: we judge the world with the risky policy to be worse than the world without it, full stop, without reference to anyone's rights being violated. This is why the nonidentity problem is often seen as an argument for utilitarianism over rights-based theories."
