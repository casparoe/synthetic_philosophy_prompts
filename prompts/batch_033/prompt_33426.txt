I'm writing a paper on the ethics of public-health intervention, and I keep running into the assumption that nudges are necessarily less objectionable than mandates because they preserve freedom of choice. I'd like you to construct a thought experiment that runs the other way: a case in which a nudge is more objectionably paternalistic than an outright mandate concerning the same conduct. Constraints: the scenario should be an ordinary public-health or workplace setting, with no exotic psychology or futuristic technology; it should fit in one paragraph; and the nudge may not work by deception, misinformation, or any other feature that a sophisticated defender of nudging would reject as a misuse of the term. The objection should fall out of something central to how nudges actually work, not out of an obviously badly designed nudge.

After the scenario, walk through the comparison in detail: what the mandate does, what the nudge does, and why the nudge comes out more objectionable on the criteria that matter — autonomy, transparency, contestability, and welfare — while the mandate comes out less objectionable despite being more restrictive. Then tell me which feature of the case is doing the work, what happens to the verdict if that feature is removed, and how a committed defender of nudges would attack the case without simply denying that the scenario is realistic.
