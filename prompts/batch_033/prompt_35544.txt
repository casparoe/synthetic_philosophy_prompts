I'm teaching a course on philosophy of history and I want to use Slaughterhouse-Five to get at the tension between fatalism and the idea that history could have gone otherwise. The Tralfamadorians see all moments as fixed and just say "so it goes" — but Vonnegut also wrote the book because he was at Dresden and clearly thinks it matters to say something about it, which seems to presuppose that the past isn't simply a closed book.

I want to do a session where we read the novel through the lens of counterfactual history and historical inevitability. Where does the novel actually argue for fatalism, and where does it undermine its own fatalism? The Tralfamadorian view that every moment just *is* and nothing can be changed — is that a genuine philosophical position the book wants us to take seriously, or is it a coping mechanism Billy Pilgrim adopts because he's traumatized? And what does the novels structure — being unstuck in time — do to the idea of historical causation? If the book *does* end up rejecting the idea that history could have been otherwise, does it also give us a reason to think that counterfactual thinking about history is pointless, or just a kind of bad faith?

I want to avoid the easy reading where the novel is just "about PTSD" and the sci-fi is window dressing. I want to take the metaphysics seriously and see if the book has a genuine contribution to the philosophy of history. What scenes would you anchor the discussion around, and what should I make sure to say about the difference between psychological fatalism and metaphysical fatalism so the students don't conflate them?
