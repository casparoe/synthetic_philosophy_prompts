A friend in my decision theory reading group sketched this argument and now I can't shake it:

(1) Rational agents update their beliefs by conditionalizing on their evidence.
(2) If two agents start from the same prior and conditionalize on different evidence, any difference in their posterior beliefs is fully explained by the difference in evidence.
(3) For any two agents with different priors, we can always imagine a more fundamental "ur-prior" from which both of their priors would result by conditionalizing on their respective background experiences and upbringing.
(4) So any two rational agents can be treated as sharing an ur-prior and having different total evidence.
(5) Therefore, all disagreement between rational agents must be traceable to differences in evidence.
(6) But if all disagreement is evidence-driven, then when rational agents pool their evidence, they must converge to the same beliefs.
(7) Yet we routinely see intelligent, careful people who have access to the same evidence — think of economists disagreeing about fiscal policy, or philosophers disagreeing about free will — and they don't converge.
(8) So either these people aren't rational, or one of the earlier steps fails.

I want to keep (7) — it just seems obvious that reasonable people can disagree. But I also don't see an obvious flaw in (1) through (6). Which step would you reject, and what's the cost of rejecting it? I'm especially interested in whether rejecting (3) commits me to saying that priors are just brute arational starting points, and wether that's as bad as it sounds.
