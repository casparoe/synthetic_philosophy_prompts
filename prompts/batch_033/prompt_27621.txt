I'm on the IRB at my university and we're reviewing a proposal that's got the committee completely deadlocked, and I'm supposed to draft the decision memo for our meeting next week. The study is from a joint philosophy/psychology team and it's basically trying to run an empirical version of Nozick's experience machine. Participants would spend two weeks in an immersive VR environment that's been carefully designed to be more fulfilling than their actual lives — the simulation adapts to their preferences, they form relationships with NPCs who are programmed to be warm and interesting, they succeed at challenges calibrated to feel meaningful without being frustrating, the whole thing. After the two weeks they're debriefed, interviewed extensively about whether they wanted to leave, and followed up at one month and six months to measure life satisfaction and any lingering effects. The researchers are upfront that they want to test whether people actually would plug in, given the chance, and what that says about hedonistic versus desire-satisfaction versus objective-list theories of well-being.

Half the committee thinks this is fine — participants consent, they can leave anytime, the VR setup has safety cutoffs, and the debriefing is thorough. The other half thinks it's a psychological landmine: that showing someone a life that's strictly better than their own, then taking it away, is basically designed to cause harm; that consent is meaningless because the experience itself might change what you value (someone who goes in thinking "it's just a study" might come out genuinely believing the simulation was the real thing and their actual life is the fake); and that even if most participants are fine, the ones who aren't — the ones who get depressed, who can't readjust, who keep trying to get back in — are a forseeable harm we shouldn't approve. One member keeps saying "we wouldn't approve a study that gave people a highly addictive drug to see if they'd quit, and this is the informational equivalent."

I need to write a memo that recommends approve, reject, or approve with conditions, with reasons that both sides can actually engage with. The chair wants it to take the strongest objection seriously, not just dismiss it, and to be specific about what conditions would actually address the worry (if I go the conditions route) rather than just slapping on "enhanced debriefing" and calling it a day. What should I recommend?
