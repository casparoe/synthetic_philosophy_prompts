I sit on the advisory board of a foundation that is deciding whether to open a program on invertebrate welfare, and the central question we keep stalling on is how to think about the scale argument: even if the probability that a given insect is sentient is low, the number of insects is so enormous that the expected amount of welfare at stake could swamp every other cause we fund. I want help thinking about which open questions in this area actually deserve the most attention before we commit money, rather than a survey of the field.

Specifically, I would like you to produce a defended prioritization of the open conceptual and empirical questions that the scale argument turns on. Candidate questions include: whether nociception and aversive behavior in insects are evidence of valenced experience or merely of nociception-like processing without affect; whether the relevant probability is best understood as a subjective credence, an objective frequency across taxa, or something more like a precautionary parameter; whether welfare, if it exists, should be aggregated by raw expected sentience or discounted by a moral-weight factor tied to cognitive complexity; and whether the scale argument even has the structure its proponents claim, since vast numbers against a low probability may license very different conclusions depending on how the probabilities, the numbers, and the per-individual stakes interact.

For each question you rank highly, I want the reasoning: how much would a settled answer change the expected value of an invertebrate-welfare program relative to other animal-welfare or global-health spending; how tractable the question is with tools available to philosophers and welfare biologists over, say, a three-to-five-year horizon; and how neglected it is relative to adjacent questions. I also want to know which questions you would deliberately deprioritize even though they are intellectually central — for example, questions about the metaphysics of consciousness that may not change any practical decision at the margin — and what evidence over the next few years would most change your ranking. The deliverable is an agenda for a funder, not a list of important-sounding topics and not a bibliography.
