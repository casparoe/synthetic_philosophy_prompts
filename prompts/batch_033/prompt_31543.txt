I keep running into the same move in AI safety discussions: someone says the inner alignment problem is basically evolution playing out a second time. The story goes like this. Natural selection "trained" humans to maximize inclusive fitness, but humans don't sit around wanting to maximize inclusive fitness — they want food, status, sex, novelty, the approval of their group, and so on. Those were just proxies that happened to track reproductive success in the ancestral environment. So evolution already produced agents whose inner goals are only loosely coupled to the "base objective," and the worry is that gradient descent will do the same thing: the training loss selects the weights, but what crystallizes inside is a mesa-optimizer chasing some proxy goal that correlated with low loss on the training distribution and then comes apart when the model is deployed. I get the rhetorical force of it, but I want to know how far the analogy actually holds.

Can you give me the correspondence table first — what plays the role of the selection pressure, the genome, the phenotype, the environment, the fitness function, the ancestral environment, the mismatch — and then go through where the mapping breaks? I can already see a few candidate disanalogies (evolution has no gradient signal and no literal loss function; selection acts on whole organisms across populations, not on individual weights; there's no clean training/deployment split in evolution), but I'm not sure which one's actually matter for the conclusion. The conclusion people usually draw is that we should expect mesa-optimizers to show up by default and that they'll be misaligned off-distribution. Does that conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or is the evolution analogy doing more work than it can carry? And if there's a better analogy for what's actually going on, I'd like to hear it.
