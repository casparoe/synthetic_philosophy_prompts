I sit on the neuroethics committee of a teaching hospital. A proposal has come to us for first-in-human approval. The research team wants to treat patients with a small, well-delimited region of cortex whose surgical removal is already an accepted treatment for otherwise intractable focal epilepsy. Instead of resection, they propose to record from the region over several months, model its input–output function, and then replace it with a closed-loop implant trained on the patient's own data, leaving surrounding tissue intact. The protocol would then return to us, stage by stage, for approval to replace a second region, then a third. The company's public roadmap is candid that the endpoint is whole-brain staged replacement as a route to uploading.

The committee is split, and the two camps keep talking past each other. One side says we already approve resection of these same regions, and a functional replacement that preserves the patient's capacities is, if anything, more conservative than removing the tissue; each stage is individually well-tolerated, and there is no step at which the patient is measurably worse off. The other side says the absence of a bad step is not the absence of a bad endpoint: a long sequence of individually harmless replacements can amount to replacing the patient, the person who consents to stage seven may not be the person who consented to stage one, and approving the first stage is a de facto endorsement of a theory of personal identity this committee has no standing to settle.

I have spent enough years reading the rationalist blogs' and forums' debates about uploading, continuity, and pattern identity that I do not need a primer on the standard arguments. I need a document the committee can act on. Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—with a time frame and review milestones, and it should state and honestly answer the strongest consideration on the losing side. If you impose conditions, make them conditions the research team could actually satisfy or contest, and do not let the vote become hostage to settling the metaphysics.
