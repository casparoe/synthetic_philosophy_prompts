Ok so I've been going down this rabbit hole about origin essentialism — the idea from Kripke (I think?) that you couldn't have come from a different sperm and egg, that your particular origin is essential to who you are. And at first it sounds totally right: like, if my parents had a kid a year later, that wouldn't be me, it'd be my sibling. But then I started thinking about it and now I'm stuck in this argument that seems to prove way too much, and I can't figure out where the mistake is.

Here's the argument:

(1) I couldn't have come from a different sperm and egg than the ones I actually came from. (Origin essentialism.)

(2) The particular sperm that fertilizes the egg depends on extremely fine-grained details — what my parents ate that day, the exact time they had sex, whether my dad had been wearing tight underwear, literally which way he shifted his weight. A difference of a couple seconds changes which sperm wins.

(3) So if my parents had done almost anything differently in the hours or days before my conception, a different sperm fertilizes the egg, and by (1) I don't exist.

(4) Now zoom out. Almost any event in the world that affected my parents' lives before my conception — a traffic jam, a rainstorm, a conversation with a friend, a news story they read — could have shifted the timing of my conception by a few seconds. So for almost any historical event E before my conception: if E had gone differently, I wouldn't exist.

(5) That means when I say stuff like "if my mom had gone to a different college, I still would have existed" or "if World War II hadn't happened, I'd still have been born," those claims are false. In those worlds, I'm simply not there.

(6) But now here's the part that really bothers me. If origin essentialism is true, then it seems like no policy or action can ever harm or benefit future generations — at least not the specific people in them. If a government passes a tax break that makes couples feel more secure and have kids slightly earlier, then by (1), the kids who get born are totally different kids from the ones who would have been born otherwise. You can't say the tax break made those kids better off, because they wouldn't have existed without it. And you can't say it harmed the kids who would have been born, because they were never born. No one is made better or worse off relative to the alternative — the alternative just has different people in it.

(7) But this seems to let literally everything off the hook. Climate policy, economic policy, wars, public health — almost any large-scale action changes the timing of some conceptions somewhere. If origin essentialism is right, then as long as the people who end up being born have lives worth living, none of these things can be said to harm future people. They just change *who* exists. That's wild.

(8) That's an insane conclusion. Obviously climate policy can harm or benefit future generations. So origin essentialism must be wrong somewhere.

But step (1) still feels true to me. Like, I can't shake the intuition that if a different sperm had reached the egg, the resulting person would be my sibling, not me. And I can't tell whether the problem is in (1), or in the move from (1) to (6), or whether (6) actually follows and I just have to bite the bullet on (7) and (8). I'm especially worried there's some equivocation on "could have" or "would have" — like maybe the metaphysical sense and the moral sense aren't the same thing, and I'm sliding between them without noticing.

Can you play the role of a professional metaphysician and walk me through which step is the problem? I want you to think carefully — maybe start by restating the argument in your own words so I know you've got it, then go through each step and tell me which ones are defensible and which one breaks. I care less about which premise I have to give up and more about understanding what I'd be paying to keep (1). Like, if I want to hold onto origin essentialism, what exactly do I have to say about the wierd moral consequences? And is there maybe a way to keep (1) while avoiding the crazy conclusion that nothing matters for future people?
