I am a constitutional lawyer who has recently been going through a divorce. My spouse and I signed a prenuptial agreement at my insistence, and now, years later, I find myself resenting the constraints it places on me, even though I recognise that my present self is exactly the person my earlier self was trying to protect against. This personal experience has unsettled something I long took for granted in my professional life: the idea that a constitution is best understood as a society's Ulysses contract.

Please act as a professional philosopher specialising in political philosophy and the theory of rational choice. Before writing your final answer, take time to think through the issue carefully and begin by sketching several possible lines of response before settling on the one you judge most defensible.

The Ulysses-contract model of constitutionalism, associated most prominently with Jon Elster, treats constitutional constraints as a form of precommitment: a polity binds itself in advance so that future majorities cannot act on passion, panic, or weakness of will, just as Ulysses had himself bound to the mast so that he could hear the Sirens without steering the ship onto the rocks. But there is a familiar and, to my mind, serious disanalogy. Ulysses bound his own future self; the parties to the transaction were, in an important sense, the same person at different times. A constitution, by contrast, is typically drafted and ratified by one generation and then binds later generations who never consented to it. What looks like self-binding at the founding moment looks, from the perspective of later generations, more like being bound by the dead hand of the past.

I would like you to explain the strongest attempts to dissolve this intergenerational objection to the Ulysses-contract model. In particular: (1) What are the best arguments for treating constitutional precommitment as genuinely self-binding rather than as rule by the dead hand of the past? (2) What residual difficulties remain even after those arguments are given their due? (3) If the disanalogy cannot be fully dissolved, does the Ulysses model need to be abandoned, or can it be revised in a way that does not rest on the fiction that a polity is a single enduring agent?

Please write your answer in British English.
