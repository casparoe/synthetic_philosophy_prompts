I'm giving a department colloquium next month defending Timothy Williamson's anti-luminosity argument. My thesis is that no non-trivial mental condition is luminous: for any mental state M, there are cases where you're in M but you're not in a position to know you're in M. The core of the argument is sorites-style: suppose feeling cold were luminous; then for any small enough change in thermal sensation, if you feel cold at t_i you're in a position to know you feel cold at t_i, and since the change between t_i and t_{i+1} is too small to discriminate, you'd be in a position to know you feel cold at t_{i+1} as well — and by repeating this step we'd be forced to say you feel cold even when you manifestly don't, contradicting the luminosity assumption. The department is strong in philosophy of mind and epistemology with an internalist bent: two faculty have published on privileged access, one teaches a graduate seminar on self-knowledge, and the overall temperament is that introspection is a reliable, if fallible, guide to one's own mental life. They've read Knowledge and Its Limits and regard the anti-luminosity argument as clever rather than compelling. Prepare my Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this audience, ordered by how much damage they'd do to the argument, each phrased the way an internalist about self-knowledge would put it, with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
