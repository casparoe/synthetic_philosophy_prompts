Most jurisdictions impose longer sentences on repeat offenders: someone convicted of burglary gets more prison time if they have a prior conviction than if it's a first offense. I understand the intuitive appeal, but I'm trying to work out what this practice actually presupposes about the purpose of punishment.

So the practice seems to imply that punishment isn't fundamentally about desert for individual acts. But then what is it about? Managing dangerous people? Deterrence? Something else?

If the rationale for recidivism enhancements is public protection, why does a prior conviction count but a high risk score doesn't? That looks like an inconsistency.

I'm trying to reconstruct what theory of punishment the system is implicitly committed to here — what view of the point of punishment makes this pattern of practices coherent, which rival views it quietly rules out, and whether the system's commitments are even internally consistent. I'd also like to know what would have to change in sentencing practice if we replaced the implicit theory with a different one.
