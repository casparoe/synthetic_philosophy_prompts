I got into it last night with my friend Carlos — he's been on a Dewey kick and he keeps saying that democracy just *is* the scientific method applied to collective life. His whole argument is: a policy proposal is a hypothesis about what will improve things, implementing it is running the experiment, the next election or public backlash is the peer review, and when a policy fails that's not a scandal, it's a falsified prediction we should be grateful for because now we know something we didn't before. He used this to argue that we're way too harsh on politicians and parties when their programs don't pan out — he says we should treat a failed housing initiative or a botched healthcare rollout the way scientists treat a null result: disappointing maybe, but epistemically valuable, not a reason to fire everyone.

I pushed back pretty hard. I said experiments don't have non-consenting subjects who lose their homes, and scientists don't get to keep their grants after they blow up the lab and say "well now we know." But he had a counter for everything — he said we *do* consent, through voting, and that the alternative to experimenting is just guessing, which is worse. By the end I wasnt sure what I thought anymore.

I want to know how far this analogy actually goes. Can you do a proper audit? Give me the full mapping — what's the hypothesis, the control group, the replication, the peer review, the journal, the grant funding, the whole apparatus. Then tell me where it breaks, and which breaks matter for his conclusion (that we should be more forgiving of policy failure) versus which are just cosmetic. I'm especially worried about two things: whether the analogy sneaks in an assumption that the experimenters and the subjects are different people (in science) when in democracy they're supposed to be the same people, and whether it quietly imports the idea that there's some neutral, objective measure of "what worked" when in politics the whole fight is over what counts as working. And at the end, tell me: if the analogy is too leaky to carry his conclusion, is there a better one, or is the whole Deweyan move of modeling politics on inquiry just a category mistake?
