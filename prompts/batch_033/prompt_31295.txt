I'm in an upper-division seminar on contemporary moral philosophy, and we've spent the last three weeks on the aggregation debate: Taurek's "Should the Numbers Count?", Parfit's "Innumeracy" reply, Kamm's pairwise comparison argument, Scanlon's contractualism, and a few pieces by Hirose and Otsuka. Before I try to settle on a view, I want to step back and ask what the criteria for a successful view even are — the debate seems to pull in so many directions that I suspect people are implicitly working with different desiderata and talking past each other.

Please write out the desiderata: what any adequate theory of how we should choose when we can save different numbers of people must get right. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and tell me whether you think it's non-negotiable (any theory that fails it is dead) or whether it's a substantive commitment that one side of the debate could reasonably reject as question-begging.

Some specific issues I want the list to address:

- The distinction between aggregating claims within a single life and aggregating claims across different lives, and whether a theory that permits one but not the other can be made coherent.
- The relevance (if any) of the fact that each person has only her own life to lose, and whether this fact generates a requirement to give each person a say or a chance rather than simply maximizing lives saved.
- Whether the numbers must count in the same way for deterministic choices (I can save A or save B and C) as for choices under uncertainty (I can take an action that will save A for sure or an action that will save B and C with some probability), or whether these are different problems with different criteria.
- Whether the theory must generalize beyond one-off rescue cases to distributive justice more broadly — for instance, to how we allocate scarce medical resources — or whether rescue cases are sui generis and a theory can be adequate for them without committing itself on distribution.
- Whether a theory that gives each person an equal chance of being saved (e.g., a weighted lottery) is ruled out by the same considerations that would rule out giving the many no chance at all, or whether there is a principled difference between ignoring the numbers and merely discounting them.
- Whether the theory must respect a requirement of equal concern or equal respect for persons, and if so, what that requirement actually demands in a conflict situation — is it satisfied by impartial consideration of everyone's interests, or does it require something procedural?

If you think some subset of these desiderata cannot be jointly satisfied — that the list itself reveals a kind of impossibility result — please flag that explicitly. That's the part I would most want to see.

Finally, a brief scorecard: how do Taurek's equal-chances coin-toss, the straightforward saving-the-greater-number view, Kamm's pairwise comparison argument, Scanlon's contractualist approach, and one weighted-lottery view of your choice fare against the list? But the desiderata themselves are the main deliverable; don't let the scorecard take over.

Please write in American English.
