Couple years ago I wrote a feature for a science magazine about a climate scientist whose findings were full of caveats — the models suggest this, but only under these assumptions, and here are ten reasons to be cautious — and activists kept flattening that into slogans. My piece was about how badly he was being misread, and to make the point I wrote a section in the voice of the people misreading him, all confident and stripped of nuance, figuring the irony would be obvious from the surrounding paragraphs. You can see where this is going. Last month a think tank quoted that section in a policy brief, attributed it to me as my own view, and used it to support exactly the position I was mocking.

I put out a short note saying they'd misunderstood the piece, but a journalist freind told me I'm being naive: once it's in print, a text has a life of its own, and I don't get to patrol every future reading. My editor says the opposite — I wrote it, so what I intended is what it means, and the think tank is just wrong. To be honest I'm also a bit embarassed because I now think the piece was too clever and my own view has drifted since I wrote it, which makes it all the murkier.

More than the practical question of whether to make a fuss, I want to know: when a text gets read against the grain like this, does the author's intention still settle what it means? Is my friend right that a public text means whatever a competent reader can get out of it? And does the fact that the passage was ironic change the analysis, or is that just me looking for a loophole?
