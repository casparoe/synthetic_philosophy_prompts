I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind, and I've been trying to get clear on something methodological that keeps coming up in our reading group: when is a thought experiment doing genuine work, and when is it just an intuition pump in the pejorative sense — persuading without earning it? I've got verdicts on a bunch of cases and I want to know whether there's a principle behind them, or whether I'm just being inconsistent.

Here are my verdicts:

- Thomson's violinist: legitimate. It isolates a specific feature (bodily autonomy versus right to life) and doesn't seem to smuggle in the conclusion.
- Searle's Chinese room: not legitimate. It gets its force from imagining a little man in a room and then insisting there's no understanding — but the systems reply says the man isn't the whole system, and the setup makes that reply feel like a dodge when it shouldn't be.
- Jackson's Mary: legitimate, even though I'm a physicalist. It forces me to explain what Mary learns, and that's productive even if I think there's an answer.
- Chalmers' zombie: not legitimate. It just asserts that a physical duplicate could lack consciousness and then concludes consciousness is non-physical. The thought experiment is the conclusion.
- Parfit's teletransporter: legitimate. It separates psychological continuity from bodily continuity and makes me feel the pull of the former without forcing the answer.
- Block's Chinese nation: I go back and forth. On one hand it seems like the Chinese room scaled up; on the other, it makes vivid the claim that functional organisation alone can't suffice for consciousness, and that claim might be right even if the thought experiment is question-begging.
- Thomson's people-seeds: legitimate, but I can't articulate why it feels different from the violinist even though they're doing structurally similar work.
- Nozick's experience machine: legitimate. It isolates a single variable — experienced versus actual contact with reality — and the answer isn't forced.

The pair that bothers me most is Mary and zombies. They're both about whether physicalism can account for consciousness, and both ask us to imagine something a physicalist might deny is possible. But Mary feels like fair play and zombies feel like cheating, and I can't say why. I've only just realised how much this nags at me.

Is there a principle — or a small set of criteria — that sorts these cases correctly? And if it forces me to revise some of my verdicts, which ones should I give up? Also, what does the principle say about a case I haven't mentioned — say, Davidson's swampman? I genuinely don't know what to think about that one, and I'd rather not have my verdict on it be driven by whatever mood I'm in.
