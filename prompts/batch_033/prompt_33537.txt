Medical advance directives are a routine part of end-of-life planning: a competent person issues instructions about what medical treatments they do or do not want if they later lose decision-making capacity. These directives are legally binding in most jurisdictions. Now consider a scenario that is not uncommon in clinical practice. A person writes an advance directive stating that, should she develop severe dementia, she refuses life-extending treatment for any acute illness. Years later, she does develop severe dementia. She appears content: she enjoys simple pleasures, recognizes familiar faces, and shows no sign of distress or suffering. She then contracts pneumonia, a condition easily treatable with antibiotics. The advance directive instructs the medical team to withhold treatment. They follow it. The person who wrote the directive — the one who contemplated dementia from the outside and decided she would not want to live that way — is, in a psychologically significant sense, not the same person as the one who now has dementia and who, by all appearances, wants to go on living. The dementia has transformed her cognitive capacities, her emotional range, and the very preferences that make her life go well or badly by her own lights. What theory of personal identity and autonomy does the practice of honoring advance directives in such cases presuppose? Does it assume that the earlier, cognitively intact self has legitimate authority over the later, cognitively transformed self, and if so, on what grounds — a metaphysical claim about the persistence of personal identity across psychological change, a normative claim about which self's preferences should count, or something else? Is the practice consistent with the way the same legal and medical system treats other cases of radical psychological change — for instance, when a person repudiates earlier commitments after a religious conversion or a transformative life experience? Reconstruct the implicit theory of the self and of decisional authority that the practice relies on, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say whether the practice is philosophically defensible as it stands.
