I'm giving the eulogy at the memorial for my old boss, the former mayor of a mid-sized city, who died last month at seventy-eight. I ran his office for eleven years, so I'm the one they asked. Fifteen years ago, during what people in town still call "stadium week," he got word from a credible informant that a group planned to set off truck bombs at a high-school football game. The only way to stop it in time was to flip a man who could call the plotters off, and the man would only cooperate if the mayor made a parole warrant against his brother disappear. The mayor signed off. The brother went free, the plot was real, the attack never happened — and three months later the brother killed a man in a bar fight. The papers spent years arguing over whether that death sits on my boss's hands. The room at the service will include his family, some of the officers who ran the operation and still believe he saved hundreds of kids, and the sister of the man from the bar fight, who has told people she's coming "to see what kind of man they bury." I need a eulogy, six or seven minutes, that doesn't flinch from what he did, doesn't pretend the brother's victim doesn't matter, doesn't reduce a complicated man to that one call, and doesn't turn into a lecture. The only frame that feels true to me is the idea that some offices require a person to get his hands dirty — that a decent person in that chair sometimes has to do wrong on purpose, and the wrong doesnt become right just because it worked, and carrying the guilt is part of the job rather than proof the job was botched. Can you write it so that idea does the work without ever being named or explained? It needs to comfort people without letting anyone in the room off the hook, him included.
