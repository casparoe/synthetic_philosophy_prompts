I'm presenting a paper at a workshop next month defending the view that constitutional precommitment mechanisms — entrenched bills of rights, supermajority amendment thresholds, and strong-form judicial review — are not democratically illegitimate constraints on popular sovereignty but are constitutive of democratic self-government properly understood. My argument draws on the Ulysses contract analogy, on the claim that some preconditions of democracy (the integrity of the electoral process, basic minority protections, the conditions of political speech) cannot be left to ordinary majoritarian revision without risking the very thing being exercised, and on a distinction between external constraints imposed by an unaccountable agent and internal self-binding by a political community that recognizes its own diachronic fallibility.

The audience is a workshop of political and legal theorists. Roughly half are deliberative democrats who are sympathetic to constitutionalism but worry about the epistemic and democratic costs of insulating norms from ongoing contestation. The other half are more skeptical: agonistic democrats, popular constitutionalists, and at least one person who thinks the dead-hand problem is fatal to any form of entrenched constitutionalism. Everyone in the room accepts that democracy is more than just majority rule; the disagreement is about whether constitutional precommitment helps or hurts. I expect the popular-constitutionalist contingent to be the most aggressive questioners.

Give me a Q&A prep sheet. Order the objections by how likely and how damaging they are, phrase each one the way someone in this room would actually put it, and for each give me a two-minute reply and a candid note on where the reply is weakest. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
