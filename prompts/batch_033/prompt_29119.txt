I'm a software engineer, and I've been trying to get clear about what I actually believe about supervising AI systems, specifically the relationship between process-based and outcome-based supervision. I've ended up with five commitments, and I'd like an audit of whether they form a coherent package rather than a survey of the field.

(1) For tasks where the final output is hard, slow, or expensive for humans to evaluate directly—long-horizon coding, open-ended research, strategic planning—supervision cannot rely on the outcome alone; it has to evaluate the reasoning or the intermediate steps.

(2) Any supervisory signal ultimately has to bottom out in something a human can actually check. If a process is rewarded without any tie to a verifiable outcome, then good process just collapses into process that looks good to the graders.

(3) We should not train models to optimize for human approval of their reasoning, because that selects for reasoning that is persuasive, sycophantic, or tidy rather than reasoning that tracks what is correct.

(4) It is nevertheless possible, and in fact necessary, to train models on intermediate reasoning for problems whose final answers we cannot check at training time, by scoring individual steps for soundness, honesty, and whether the model catches its own errors.

(5) A task has a determinate correct answer only if there is some procedure, however demanding, by which a sufficiently competent judge could in principle verify a proposed answer.

I can't tell where the strain is among these, and I'm not attached to any one of them in particular. Please go through the set and identify which pairs are genuinely in tension, which pairs only appear to be, how severe each tension is, what would have to give to resolve it, and which single revision would do the most to make the whole set coherent. I'd prefer the commitments themselves examined closely rather than a general discussion of alignment.
