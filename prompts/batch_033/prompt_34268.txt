I'm giving a department colloquium next week defending a broadly Dennettian line on free will. The core claim is this: when people argue about whether we "have free will," they're typically conflating several distinct capacities — the ability to do otherwise, the ability to act on reasons one endorses, the ability to be morally responsible in a way that licenses reactive attitudes, and so on. We should disaggregate these, ask which of them we actually have, and then ask which of them are worth wanting. My positive view is that the variety worth wanting is roughly the capacity for reflective self-control: the ability to step back from immediate impulses, deliberate, and act on higher-order desires in something like the Frankfurt/Dennett sense. The libertarian ability-to-do-otherwise-in-the-same-circumstances is neither something we have nor something worth wanting. The hard determinist is right about the metaphysics but wrong about what follows.

The audience is a mid-sized analytic philosophy department — maybe thirty people. I know the room reasonably well: there's a cluster of metaphysicians who work on causation and laws (one of whom is a committed libertarian about free will), two moral philosophers who think the reactive attitudes are non-negotiable and that compatibilism is a dodge, a philosopher of mind who thinks the whole debate is confused because it rests on a Cartesian picture of the self, and a senior Kantian who will want to talk about autonomy as rational self-legislation. There are also several graduate students who've been reading Pereboom and Caruso and are sympathetic to hard incompatibilism.
