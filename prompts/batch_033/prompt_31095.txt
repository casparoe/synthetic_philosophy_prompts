I'm taking a philosophy of science course, and I've been working through an argument that seems to prove that induction is rationally empty. The steps feel individually plausible, but the conclusion is so strong that I'm convinced something must be wrong — I just can't locate the error. I'd appreciate a diagnosis.

(1) A body of evidence confirms a universal generalization "All Fs are G" when all observed Fs have been G, the sample is sufficiently large and varied, and there is no defeating evidence.

(2) Define "grue": an object is grue iff it is first examined before the year 2100 and green, or it is not first examined before 2100 and blue. Every emerald examined so far has been green and was first examined before 2100. So every examined emerald is grue. By the standard in (1), our evidence confirms "All emeralds are grue" just as strongly as it confirms "All emeralds are green."

(3) But these two generalizations make incompatible predictions about emeralds first examined after 2100: the first says they will be green, the second says they will be blue.

(4) If the same body of evidence equally confirms two generalizations that make incompatible predictions, then that evidence does not rationally warrant believing either prediction over the other.

(5) The problem generalizes: for any inductive generalization about any subject matter, we can construct a deviant, gruelike predicate that fits the same observed data while supporting a different, incompatible prediction about the unobserved.

(6) Therefore, no inductive generalization is ever rationally warranted by its evidence.

(7) That conclusion is unacceptable. It would mean that all empirical science — from fundamental physics to medicine — rests on inferences that are no more rational than their gruelike competitors. Surely we know that the sun will rise tomorrow, and we know it by induction.

I suspect the trouble lies somewhere around (1) — that confirmation requires more than bare regularity, and that we need a principled restriction on which predicates are "projectible." But every attempt I've seen to supply such a restriction either begs the question (by invoking the past success of certain predicates, which is itself an inductive claim) or reduces the distinction to a merely pragmatic preference (e.g., "green" is more entrenched in our linguistic practice than "grue"), which seems to abandon the idea that induction is genuinely truth-conducive. I find neither option satisfying.

Where does this argument go wrong, and which premise should I reject? If the right move is to reject (1) by adding a projectibility constraint, what does a non-circular, genuinely epistemic version of that constraint look like? And if no such constraint is available, which other premise should I give up, and what does rejecting it cost?
