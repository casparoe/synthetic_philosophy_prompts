I am a novelist. My last book follows a composer who undergoes gradual neural replacement—each chapter, another few percent of her brain swapped for functionally equivalent silicon—and I spent three years inside that character's head, trying to render the first-person phenomenology faithfully. The book has done well enough that the philosophy department at a nearby university has invited me to give a colloquium talk next month. They want to know what sustained imaginative engagement with the inside of the experience reveals that standard armchair thought experiments miss.

My thesis, as I plan to present it, is this: first-person phenomenology, as best I can render it in fiction, strongly suggests that gradual replacement preserves personal identity—there is no moment of death, no experiential rupture, no discoverable tipping point. But the very intuitions that make survival plausible also undermine most standard accounts of what makes survival matter. The self that walks out of the procedure is continuous with the self that walked in, yet the features we ordinarily care about preserving—biological embodiment, a particular causal history, the felt sense of being a unified subject with a determinate nature—come apart under scrutiny. Fiction, I argue, surfaces this tension more vividly than philosophical thought experiments can, because it forces the writer and reader to inhabit the middle of the process rather than merely compare the endpoints.

The audience will be about forty-five people. Most are philosophers of mind and metaphysicians who work on personal identity; several are committed functionalists, several others defend biological-continuity or animalist views, and a vocal minority think the whole debate is confused because the self is a narrative construction. There will also be a handful of neuroscientists who collaborate with the department, at least one specialist in Buddhist philosophy who works on no-self views, and a philosopher of biology who takes a dim view of idealized science-fiction scenarios. The functionalists will be sympathetic to my survival claim but hostile to the claim that survival is insufficient. The biological-continuity camp will challenge the survival claim itself. The Buddhist philosopher will likely agree with my destabilizing conclusions but for entirely different reasons. And I am genuinely unsure what the neuroscientists will say, which worries me.

I need a Q&A prep sheet. Please give me the eight or nine objections I am most likely to face from this specific audience, ordered by how damaging they are—where "damaging" means how hard they are to answer convincingly and how much of my thesis they threaten if the answer fails. Phrase each objection as someone from this audience would actually put it to me at the podium: give me their vocabulary, their framing, their tone. For each objection, supply a two-minute reply and a candid note on where that reply is weakest and what the objector should press if they are quick. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks. I know my argument has soft spots; I would rather map them now, in detail, than discover them in front of the room. As the last line of your response, give me a one-sentence summary of the single biggest vulnerability I should try to shore up before I walk in there.
