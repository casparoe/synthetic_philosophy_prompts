Question: Explain Russell's theory of definite descriptions. State the logical form Russell assigns to sentences of the form "The F is G," apply it to "The present king of France is bald" (saying whether Russell takes that sentence to be true, false, or neither), and explain how Russell would handle the negated sentence "The present king of France is not bald." Briefly contrast Russell's treatment of empty descriptions with Frege's.

Student's answer: Russell's theory says that definite descriptions such as "the present king of France" are not referring expressions in the way names are. A sentence of the form "The F is G" is really a conjunction of three claims: there is at least one F, there is at most one F, and whatever is F is G. In notation, Russell would write this as ∃x(Fx ∧ ∀y(Fy → y=x) ∧ Gx). For "The present king of France is bald," the analysis says that there exists an x who is the present king of France and who is bald; since there is no present king of France, the sentence is meaningful but false. For the negation, "The present king of France is not bald," Russell's theory predicts that it is true, because the negation of a false sentence is true: the negated sentence denies that there is exactly one present king of France who is bald. This shows that Russell's theory preserves bivalence even for sentences containing empty descriptions. Frege, by contrast, would say that the original sentence has a sense but lacks a truth value, because the description fails to refer. The theory also treats ordinary proper names as disguised definite descriptions, which is how it handles empty names such as "Pegasus" in the same way.
