I'm a reporter who covers science and ideas for a living, and I keep tripping over the same argumentative move in different fields, and I'd really like to know if it has a name.

Here's the pattern. Someone builds a model — an economic model, a climate model, an epidemiological model, whatever — that rests on assumptions everybody knows are false. Not just simplified, but false. Rational agents, perfect information, spherical cows, well-mixed populations, that sort of thing. When a critic says "but your assumptions are wrong," the modeler replies: that's not the right question. The right question is whether the model predicts accurately. If it does, the assumptions have done their job, and their realism is irrelevant. If it doesn't, we'll go back and improve the model. But the truth of the assumptions, considered on their own, is not the standard by which the model should be judged.

I've heard this from economists defending rational-choice models, from climate scientists defending paramaterizations of cloud physics that they themselves admit are physically nonsense but predictively useful, and from epidemiologists during COVID who were using models with assumptions about homogeneous mixing that everyone knew were false. And I've seen the reverse: people dismissing a model out of hand because one assumption is false, without ever checking whether the model's predictions are any good, which seems like the mirror-image mistake.

I know Milton Friedman made a version of this argument about economics in the 1950s — that the realism of assumptions doesn't matter, only predictive success does. But I see this move way beyond economics, and I'm not sure "Friedman's argument" is the right label for the general pattern. Is there a standard term in philosophy of science for this specific move? How does it relate to instrumentalism, or to the broader debate about idealization? And if there isn't one settled name, what would you call it, and how would you distinguish it from neighbouring ideas — like ordinary idealization where you know an assumption is false but you plan to relax it later, or from just being sloppy and hand-waving away criticism?
