I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, though I've read a fair amount of rationalist and EA writing. I've come to think that the separateness of persons objection is a decisive problem for utilitarianism — at least for act utilitarianism in its straightforward maximizing form. As I understand it, the objection is this: utilitarianism treats the distribution of welfare across different people as if it were no different from the distribution of welfare across different moments in a single person's life. It just sums everything up and maximizes the total. But people are separate, and the fact that a loss to you can be compensated by a gain to me is not like the fact that a loss to me today can be compensated by a gain to me tomorrow. The boundary between persons matters in a way the boundary between time-slices of one person does not, and utilitarianism flattens that distinction. I find this intuitively compelling and I think it dooms the view.

I want to know what would count against this position. I'm not asking for someone to argue the other side for the sake of it — I want an honest map of the objection's vulnerabilities, written for someone who currently finds the objection persuasive but wants to know where it might be weak. Specifically: which of the following are real threats to the objection's force, and which are not? That the objection presupposes a controversial view of personal identity and would lose its grip under reductionism about persons. That it only works against simple summing versions of utilitarianism and leaves prioritarian, egalitarian, or sufficientarian forms untouched. That the objection itself smuggles in a deontological intuition it hasn't justified and can't justify without circularity. That it can't explain why aggregation is sometimes clearly permissible — for instance, choosing to save a larger number of people from a harm rather than a smaller number. That the objection proves too much: if we can't trade off one person's welfare against another's, we also can't make policy decisions, distribute scarce resources, or even decide what to have for dinner when preferences conflict.

I'd also like to know which of these lines of reply defenders of the objection themselves take seriously, as opposed to ones they dismiss as missing the point. And please separate what is genuinely contested among philosophers who work on this from what is more or less settled — don't present a contested view as if it were the consensus.
