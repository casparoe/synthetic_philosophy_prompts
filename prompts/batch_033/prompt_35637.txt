I'm putting together a handout for a philosophy of science seminar on reduction and emergence, and I want a proper taxonomy of the positions — not just a list, but a tree where each branch is labeled by the claim that actually splits things apart. The root question should be something like: is there at least one real case where the behavior of a whole isn't derivable even in principle from the behavior of its parts plus their arrangement? From there I want it to branch on things like whether higher-level explanations are genuinely autonomous or just convenient shorthand, whether the special sciences have their own laws that don't reduce, whether 'emergence' is about our epistemic limits or about metaphysics, and whether downward causation ever happens. The leaves I need covered: eliminative reductionism, Nagel-style reductionism, weak emergence (the Bedau kind, where it's about computational irreducibility or whatever), strong emergence, and at least one variant of non-reductive physicalism. Each leaf should get a one-sentence statement of its most awkward commitment — the thing its defenders least like to admit. Also flag any logically possible positions in the tree that nobody actually holds, and say why: incoherent, just unattractive, or maybe nobody's thought to defend it yet. The audience is grad students who know the basics but haven't mapped the whole terrain.
