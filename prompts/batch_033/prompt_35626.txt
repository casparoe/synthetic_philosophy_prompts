ive been reading about animal signals and im confused about how to classify something. the standard story i keep seeing is the handicap principle: signals are honest because theyre costly, and only high-quality individuals can afford the cost, so cheaters are priced out. the peacocks tail is the classic example — its a wasteful handicap that only healthy peacocks can sustain.

but i keep running across signals that seem to work differently. a frogs call pitch is constrained by its body size — bigger frogs have deeper calls, and they literally cant produce high-pitched calls. the signal is honest, but not because its a costly handicap the frog chooses to bear; its honest because physics wont let a small frog fake a deep croak. similarly, a stags roar formant frequencies are determined by its vocal tract length, which correlates with body size. these seem like honest signals where the cost isnt the strategic kind zahavi talked about — the signaler isnt paying a cost to prove quality, the signal is just physically yoked to the trait.

ive seen the term "index signal" used for this, but im not sure if thats standard or if these are just considered a subspecies of the handicap principle. some people seem to treat all honest signals as handicaps of one kind or another, while others draw a sharp line between indices (physically constrained) and handicaps (strategically costly). is there an established taxonomy here, and where exactly is the boundary? for instance, if a signal is both physically constrained AND costly, which category does it fall into? i guess what im really asking is whether the distinction is fundamental or just a matter of degree, and if theres a name for the distinction itself.
