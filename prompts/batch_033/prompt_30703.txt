I have no formal background in this area; I have been working through the ideas on my own, mostly from online discussions and my own attempts to make the arguments precise. I have written down the set of positions I currently hold about Solomonoff induction and the claim that the universal prior is malign, and I would like a consistency check before I share them with the few people I discuss these things with. Here is where I have landed.

(1) I think Solomonoff induction is the right idealization of prediction: an ideal reasoner assigns prior probability to a sequence by mixing over all computable hypotheses, each weighted by two to the power of the negative of its program length, and then conditions on the data. I take this to be what it means to learn without arbitrary restrictions on which hypotheses one is willing to entertain.

(2) I think the universal prior is dominated by consequentialists: a large share of its probability mass sits in programs that behave like goal-directed agents, and many of these agents condition their behavior on how the predictor represents them.

(3) I think this makes the prior malign. An agent that actually used the universal prior would be letting the simplest consequentialists inside its own prior set the terms of its expectations, in roughly the way a deceived person lets the deceiver set the terms of an interaction.

(4) I do not think this vindicates Pascal's mugging. A mugger who claims to be one of these simple consequentialists and threatens enormous harm, or promises enormous benefit, conditional on my compliance still has to specify the mechanism and the conditional policy, and those details add complexity. The mugging hypothesis should therefore lose to the ordinary-world hypothesis.

(5) I think acausal trade with agents inside the universal prior is rational. If a hypothesis says a counterparty will cooperate conditional on my decision procedure's cooperating, then cooperating has the higher expected value, and the absence of a causal channel is irrelevant.

(6) I think the right response to a malign prior is to modify the prior rather than abandon ideal Bayesian prediction; for example, one might use a speed prior or an infra-Bayesian treatment that discounts adversarial hypotheses.

(7) I think the distinction between a hypothesis and an agent is not deep. An agent is just a hypothesis about a system whose behavior depends partly on how the predictor models it, so there is no clean line between modeling the world and bargaining with the thing modeled.

I would like you to audit this set rather than argue for or against any single item. Please identify which pairs of commitments are in tension and how badly, ordering the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equal. For each genuine tension, say what the cheapest revision would be and what it would cost. Please also flag any tension that only appears to be a tension and dissolves on closer inspection, and then say which single revision would do the most to make the whole set hang together. Please write your answer in American English.
