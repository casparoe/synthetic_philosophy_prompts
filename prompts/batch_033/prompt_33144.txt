I am the head of ingredient review at a mid-sized consumer-products company. My team screens ten to fifteen new ingredients or reformulations per month — preservatives, surfactants, colorants, fragrance components, delivery systems. Each dossier gets two working days from a toxicologist or chemist who has no formal training in risk philosophy, after which a senior vice president signs off. A missed hazard can mean a recall, regulatory action, and real harm to users; an over-cautious rejection can kill a product line, hand market share to competitors with weaker standards, and sometimes push the company toward an alternative whose own risks are less well characterized.

We keep hitting the same wall. Some dossiers show a genuine but inconclusive signal of harm: a high-dose animal study whose human relevance is uncertain, a plausible mechanism with no epidemiological test, an exposure route we have not modeled. Our written guidance amounts to 'no demonstrated harm, no block,' and I think that systematically underweights what we should be worried about. But the opposite rule — any suspected harm blocks — would be paralysis and would pretend the trade-offs do not exist. I want a decision procedure that operationalizes the precautionary principle for our setting: a reusable procedure that a reviewer without philosophical training can actually run in the two days available.

Please write that procedure. It should:

- specify the questions a reviewer asks, in order, and what counts as a sufficient answer at each step;
- say where the procedure terminates with 'clear to proceed,' where it terminates with 'block,' and where it terminates with 'proceed only under restrictions' — concentration caps, exposure limits, warnings, post-market monitoring, or similar — and how the choice among restrictions is made;
- distinguish the steps that are factual or technical from the steps that are value judgments, and say who in the company should own each;
- preserve the distinctions the principle depends on — severity of the suspected harm, plausibility of the causal claim, reversibility of the decision and of the harm, availability and cost of alternatives, and who bears the burden of producing the missing evidence — rather than collapsing them into a single suspicion score;
- say when the right response to uncertainty is to require more information before deciding, and who must produce it;
- deal honestly with the case where the alternatives to a blocked ingredient carry their own risks, rather than treating 'safe alternative' and 'no alternative' as the only possibilities;
- flag where the procedure will misfire, be gamed, or be misapplied, and say explicitly what it cannot do — in particular, where it cannot remove judgment and only forces that judgment into the open.

Then run the procedure on these three cases:

1. A new preservative that shows a pattern consistent with endocrine disruption in two high-dose rodent studies; the human relevance of the mechanism is disputed, there are no human data, and there are two established alternatives that cost more and have their own, better-characterized risks.
2. A nanoscale delivery ingredient with essentially no long-term toxicology. No specific harm is hypothesized; the problem is that the material is novel enough that standard safety screens do not cover it well. The business case is strong, and competitors already use similar materials.
3. A fragrance component with a small but well-established rate of skin sensitization in human patch tests — a known and quantified harm rather than an uncertain one — that the company wants to block pre-emptively because it appears in a public ingredient database and the marketing team expects negative attention.

Make sure at least one of the worked cases ends in a decision the company would rather not make, and say explicitly which case that is and why the procedure forces the decision it does.
