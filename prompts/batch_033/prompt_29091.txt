I am currently taking a philosophy of mind module, and we have been working through attempts to naturalise mental content—the project of explaining what makes a mental state about something without presupposing intentional or semantic notions. The broad idea is that a representation's content is fixed by causal relations (what reliably triggers it) or by biological function (what it was selected to track). I find the project compelling, but I keep noticing the same difficulty surfacing in different guises, and I am not sure whether it has a single established name or whether it goes by different labels in different literatures.

The difficulty is this. Suppose we say that a mental representation R means 'horse' because horses reliably cause R. But cows on a dark night also cause R, and so do pictures of horses, and so do centaur costumes. If content is just reliable causation, then R seems to mean 'horse-or-cow-in-bad-light-or-horse-picture-or-centaur-costume-or…'—a disjunction that swallows every accidental cause. The content inflates until it can never be false, and the notion of misrepresentation collapses. The same trouble seems to arise for teleosemantic accounts: if R was selected by evolution to indicate flies, what does 'indicate flies' mean such that R's firing for a stray dust mote counts as an error rather than as R's function simply being broader than we thought? I can see that the problem is about securing normativity—a distinction between correct and incorrect representation—within a purely naturalistic framework. But I cannot tell whether the causal and teleosemantic versions are genuinely distinct problems or the same problem in different vocabularies.

What I am after is not merely a name. I want to know: what is this difficulty called in the literature, if it has a settled name; what are the main strategies for resolving it; what costs each strategy incurs; and whether the teleosemantic version of the problem is importantly different from the causal version, or whether the teleosemantic approach was designed precisely to solve it. I would also be interested in whether there is any consensus about which approach handles it best, or whether the problem is generally regarded as still open.
