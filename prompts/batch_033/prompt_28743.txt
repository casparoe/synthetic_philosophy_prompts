I have been tracking my own moral judgments about a series of cases on the lying–misleading distinction over several years of teaching, and I find that I have settled into a consistent pattern without having fully articulated the principle behind it. I would like you to examine my verdicts, identify the principle or principles I appear to be running, and then predict how I would judge a new case that I have deliberately withheld from myself.

Here are my settled verdicts:

1. **The direct lie.** A colleague asks whether I submitted the department's accreditation report. I answer, "Yes, I submitted it yesterday." I have not submitted it. Verdict: wrong.

2. **The incomplete medical answer.** A patient asks whether her test results are normal. As her physician, I answer, "All your results fall within the standard reference ranges." This is true for seventeen of the eighteen metrics; the eighteenth is dangerously elevated. I know she will take my answer to mean there is no cause for concern. Verdict: wrong.

3. **The negotiation.** In a competitive business negotiation, the other side asks whether we have alternative suppliers. I answer, "We have received bids from three other firms." This is true, but all three bids are substantially worse than the offer currently on the table, and I know the other side will assume the bids are competitive. Verdict: not wrong.

4. **The unasked question.** A junior colleague is about to turn down a job offer because he believes, falsely, that the department is about to lose its accreditation. I know his belief is false, but he does not ask me about it, and I say nothing. Verdict: not wrong.

5. **The exploited ambiguity.** A friend asks whether I am seeing anyone romantically. I answer, "I am not seeing anyone at the moment." This is true in the sense that I am not currently on a date, but I have been in a committed relationship for two years, and I know my friend will take my answer as a denial that I am in a relationship. Verdict: wrong.

6. **The true answer to a different question.** A student asks whether a particular topic will be on the exam. I answer, "The exam covers everything we have discussed in lecture." This is true, but the topic the student is asking about was discussed only in section, not in lecture, and I know the student will infer that it is on the exam. Verdict: wrong.

Now, the new case on which I want your prediction:

7. **The real estate disclosure.** A home buyer asks the seller's agent, "Has this house ever had water damage?" The agent answers, "The current owners have never filed a water damage claim." This is true. However, the agent knows that the previous owners filed a major water damage claim after a flood, and that the current owners purchased the house with full knowledge of it. The agent also knows the buyer will take the answer to mean the house has no history of water damage. What is my verdict?

In your response, please do the following:

(a) Identify at least two candidate moral principles that are each consistent with all six of my settled verdicts. State each principle clearly enough that its predictions for new cases are determinate.

(b) For each candidate principle, state what verdict it would yield on the new real estate case, and explain why.

(c) Give your best prediction of my actual verdict on the new case, with your reasons for preferring one principle over the others as an account of my judgments.

(d) Propose a further case — one I have not yet considered — that would cleanly separate the candidate principles you identified. Describe the case and explain what each principle would predict for it.
