I am a philosophy professor preparing a colloquium talk, and I want a Q&A preparation sheet rather than further argumentative material. The talk defends the following thesis: the contrast between approval-directed and goal-directed agents, as it circulates in recent work on AI alignment, is not one distinction but a conflation of two. The first is a formal distinction between optimizing for states of the world and optimizing for the evaluative attitudes of another agent; the second is a causal distinction between objectives fixed in advance by designers and objectives learned online from human feedback. I argue that the formal distinction does not partition agents in any principled way, because an agent's objective can be redescribed across the line in either direction: an agent that keeps a room clean can be redescribed as seeking that its cleaning be judged satisfactory, and an agent that seeks approval can be redescribed as seeking a world-state in which a human holds a favorable evaluation of it. The safety-relevant work in the contrast, I claim, is being done almost entirely by the second distinction, which is a fact about the training process rather than about the intrinsic structure of the resulting agent. My deflationary conclusion is that arguments about the risks or safety properties of a system should be framed in terms of how and when its objective is updated, not in terms of whether the system is goal-directed or approval-directed.

The audience is a philosophy department colloquium: around twenty-five faculty and graduate students, mostly philosophers of mind, action, and cognitive science with no machine-learning background, plus three or four researchers from the university's AI group who attend regularly and will have read the abstract. The philosophers are likely to be suspicious of "objective function" talk altogether; they will want the argument restated in terms of desires, intentions, and reasons, and they will suspect that I am conflating the designer's loss function with the agent's own goal. The ML researchers will grant the formal apparatus but will likely think the redescription argument is cheap, since what matters for safety is which quantity actually drives the system's behavior, not what verbal redescription is available after the fact.

The talk is forty-five minutes with fifteen minutes for questions. Please draft my prep sheet: the seven or eight objections I will actually get from this audience, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased in something close to the words the relevant group would use, with a two-minute reply for each and an honest note on where the reply is weakest. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks, and tell me what I should concede in the opening minutes of the talk so that it does not get raised from the floor.
