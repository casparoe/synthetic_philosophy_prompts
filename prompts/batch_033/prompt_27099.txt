I started a job three weeks ago on the behavioral insights team at a state health department, mostly designing defaults and wording for a patient portal, and its made all this stuff weirdly concrete. I've been going back and forth on my actual views, and before I have to sit in a meeting and defend a real decision I want a consistency check. Here's where I've ended up:

1. Hard paternalism is off the table. Coercing or forcing competent adults for their own good is wrong even when it works.
2. Nudges are fine because they don't force — all the options stay open, so a nudge can't be said to violate anyone's autonomy.
3. Opt-out organ donation registration is a good default and I'd defend using it, even though most people never switch it.
4. A nudge is only acceptable if it's transparent — a person should be able to see what's being done to them and why.
5. If a nudge works by leaning on a bias the person doesn't know is doing the work (status quo bias, loss aversion, whatever), that's manipulation and I don't think it's acceptable.
6. The point of a nudge should be to help people get what they themselves already want, not what we've decided is good for them — we should be helping people as they themselves would judge, not as we judge.
7. Mill's harm principle is basically correct — the state shouldn't step in to protect people from themselves, so any paternalism starts with a strike against it.

Rank the real tensions by severity, and for each say what would have to give and what the cheapest fix is.
