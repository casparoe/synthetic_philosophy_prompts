I am a PhD student in philosophy of mind, and I am working as a teaching assistant for an upper-level undergraduate course. One of the midterm questions was the following:

In his treatment of dreaming, Norman Malcolm denies that dreams are experiences, while Daniel Dennett responds with a thought experiment involving a cassette-like mechanism. Reconstruct Malcolm's main argument for the claim that dreams are not experiences occurring during sleep. Then explain Dennett's cassette thought experiment and assess what it establishes: does it refute Malcolm's conclusion, support it, or show that the dispute needs to be reframed? Defend your assessment.

Here is one student's answer:

Malcolm's argument is verificationist. He holds that the only criterion for whether someone has dreamt is the report the person gives upon waking, because a person who is sound asleep cannot judge, reason, or report anything. Since there is no independent access to a private experience that is supposed to occur during sleep, it is senseless to say that a dream is an experience the sleeper has. Importantly, Malcolm is not denying that people dream; he is denying that dreaming consists in a conscious episode or inner show during sleep. He also draws the further consequence that Cartesian dream skepticism is undermined: if it makes no sense to suppose that one is having experiences and forming judgments while asleep, then the hypothesis that one might be dreaming right now cannot function as a skeptical possibility in the usual way.

Dennett responds with the cassette thought experiment. Imagine that during sleep a mechanism writes a dream narrative into memory, and at the moment of waking it plays the recording back so that the person awakes with vivid memories of a dream, although nothing was experienced during the night. The subject's waking reports and dispositions would be exactly the same as on the ordinary view that the dream was experienced during sleep. Dennett takes the two accounts to be empirically equivalent, and he concludes that this equivalence vindicates Malcolm: since no possible evidence could favor the received view over the no-experience view, the claim that dreams are experiences is unsupported, and Malcolm's conclusion is confirmed, though for empirical rather than verificationist reasons.

This is reinforced by the discovery of REM sleep. We now know that dreaming is closely tied to REM, and subjects woken from REM give reports of dreaming, so Malcolm's refusal to countenance experiences during sleep has been overtaken by the empirical facts.
