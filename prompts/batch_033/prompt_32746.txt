I've spent a fair amount of time thinking about the idea that some actions are morally good without being morally required — what I've seen called supererogation. The soldier who throws himself on a grenade, the person who donates a kidney to a stranger, the family that takes in a refugee child when no one would blame them for not doing so. These seem different in kind from things like keeping promises or not lying. With duties, if you fail to do them, you're blameworthy — you've done something wrong. With supererogatory acts, doing them makes you praiseworthy, perhaps even heroic, but not doing them doesn't make you blameworthy. You're just ordinary. Not a bad person.

The position I've come to hold is that there really are supererogatory acts. Morality isn't just a two-category system of the permissible and the impermissible. There's a third category — acts that go beyond what duty demands, that are morally excellent but not required. And I think this isn't just a matter of social convention or praise-blame practices; it reflects something genuine about the structure of moral reasons.

I want to know which challenges a reasonable defender of the view would concede as counting against them (even if they think they can be answered) and which they'd dismiss as missing the point or begging the question.

I also want an honest assessment of whether the view has been insulated against falsification — whether it's been qualified in ways that make nothing count against it anymore. And if it has, I want to know whether that's a problem for a view of this kind, or whether some degree of insulation is appropriate for a claim about the conceptual structure of morality.
