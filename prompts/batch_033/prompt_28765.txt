A couple friends of mine got into a running group-chat argument about Clifford and James that kept going for days, and I honestly couldn't referee it. It started with Marcus saying Clifford's rule is obviously too strict: you'd never be able to trust anyone or act on incomplete information if you had to have full evidential justification before every belief. I asked what rule, and he said the one in the ethics-of-belief essay where Clifford argues you're morally wrong to hold a belief unless you have sufficient evidence for it. Elena said the rule is stronger than he thinks and that the shipowner case is doing a lot of work: the guy had doubts about his unseaworthy ship, talked himself into a comfortable conviction because he didn't want the cost of an overhaul, and the ship went down. She said his sincerity didn't help because he arrived at the belief by stifling doubts. Marcus replied that the shipowner's real wrong was sending people out to sea, not the belief itself; if he had believed the ship was sound but still paid for the inspection, he'd have done nothing wrong. The belief and the action come apart, he said, so Clifford has to smuggle in downstream consequences.

Elena came back saying no, Clifford's point is about the origin of the belief, not just the action: the shipowner wronged people by becoming the sort of person who forms beliefs by suppressing evidence, and that disposition would have been a defect even if the ship had made it. Marcus said she had shifted from evidentialism to virtue epistemology, and on that reading Clifford stops giving a hard rule and starts giving a character ideal, which is fine but much weaker. Elena said it's not weaker, it's a norm about epistemic agency: you're responsible for the way you regulate your inquiry. Then Marcus played the James card: there are genuine options where evidence is genuinely silent and you have to choose anyway, and for those cases passional nature must decide. He gave the example of making a first hire for your startup, or deciding whether a new collaborator is trustworthy before there's any track record. Elena replied that those aren't belief-without-evidence cases, they're action-under-uncertainty cases: you trust the person as a policy while still letting evidence update you, and that's exactly what Clifford would allow because you're not forming a settled belief. Marcus said that's a motte: Clifford's rule as actually stated forbids the belief, and James's point is that in many practical decisions the stance you take toward the proposition is itself part of what makes the outcome, like "this person will treat me fairly" — the belief partly constructs the result.

Elena said that's a nice story but once you allow passional nature to decide for high-stakes options, you've dissolved the norm: anyone can claim their pet belief is a genuine option, and you've got no public standard left. Marcus answered that epistemic risk is personal; what's momentous for one person may not be for another, and James isn't trying to license ordinary factual beliefs anyway, only cases where the evidence can't settle it and the decision is forced. Elena then said the problem is that Clifford's principle, whichever way you read it, is a rule you can't actually follow consistently: if you only ever believed on sufficient evidence, you'd need a belief about what counts as sufficient evidence, and that second-order belief is itself underdetermined by evidence, so the rule eats its own tail. Marcus said that's a good point but it cuts both ways: if evidentialism is self-undermining, then James's claim that the passional nature is unavoidable is the more honest description of how we already are. Elena complained that's a naturalistic fallacy: just because we all do fall back on temperament doesn't make it permissable, and Clifford's whole motivation was that our beliefs leak into public life. Marcus ended with the claim that James's "right to believe" is actually an ethics of risk management, while Clifford's principle is an ethics of epistemic purity, and that real life is more like the former than the latter.
