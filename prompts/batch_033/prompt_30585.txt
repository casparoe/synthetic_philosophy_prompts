I am a novelist writing a book set in a near-future city whose judges rely on an algorithmic risk score for pretrial detention decisions. While researching the book, I worked up an argument whose conclusion I find difficult to accept, and I would like a diagnosis of where it goes wrong, if it does. The argument runs as follows.

(1) A risk score used to make high-stakes decisions about individuals should be fair across the groups it affects.

(2) Two conditions look like minimal demands of such fairness.

(a) Calibration: for any given score value, the score should mean the same probability of the predicted outcome for every group; otherwise the score speaks a different language about different people.

(b) Equalized odds: whatever threshold is used to convert the score into a decision, the false positive rate and the false negative rate should be the same across groups; otherwise the score's errors fall unevenly.

(3) A formal impossibility result shows that, unless the score is a perfect predictor or the groups have identical base rates (the same overall frequency of the outcome), no score can satisfy both (a) and (b) at once.

(4) In the cases that matter to me—pretrial risk assessment among them—the scores are imperfect and the relevant groups do not have identical base rates.

(5) Therefore, in the cases that matter, no risk score can satisfy the minimal demands of fairness.

(6) If that is right, then making the algorithm fair is not an engineering target but a contradiction, and the search for a fair score is chasing an impossibility.

(7) So either calibration or equalized odds is not actually a minimal demand, or fairness is not a property a score can have. I cannot tell which horn to take.

Where does this argument go wrong? If it does not go wrong, which premise should I give up, and what does giving it up cost? I would most like to keep calibration, because a score that means different things for different groups seems plainly misleading, but I suspect that preference is part of the problem.

Please reply as a numbered list of exactly five points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
