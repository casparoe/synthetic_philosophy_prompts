I've been thinking about what makes something have a self. Like, what's the thing that makes me *me*, and when does that thing show up in other stuff? I've been going back and forth on a bunch of cases and I can't figure out if there's a rule behind my gut reactions. Maybe you can help me find the principle, if there is one.

Here's where I land on different cases:

- My grandma had pretty bad Alzheimers before she died. She didn't recognize me or my mom, couldn't remember anything from her life really. But I still felt like she was *her*, like there was still a self in there somewhere. I'm pretty firm on that.

- Someone in a deep dreamless sleep. They're not conscious, not thinking, not aware of anything. But obviously they still have a self, it's just... offline? That seems right.

- A corporation. It makes decisions, has goals, can even have a kind of personality (like "Apple is secretive" or whatever). But I don't think a corporation has a self. It's just a bunch of people and paper.

- A person with multiple personalities — like, disassociative identity disorder. If there are three distinct alters, each with their own memories, preferences, way of talking, and they don't share memories directly... I feel like that's three selves in one body. But that feels weird to say out loud. Still, that's my gut.

- A really well-written character in a novel. When I'm reading, it feels like they have a self. But of course they don't. They're words on a page. So my feeling there is just a feeling, not tracking anything real.

- Someone in a persistent vegitative state with no brain activity above the brainstem. I go back and forth. Part of me says there's no self left, the person is gone. But another part says the body is still there, it's still a human being, so maybe there's a self just... dormant? I don't know.

- Here's the tough one for me. Imagine an AI that's not just a chatbot but genuinely constructs a narrative about its own life — it has a story about its past, its goals, its relationships, it can reflect on its own actions and update its story. My gut says no, it doesn't have a self, it's just a machine. But when I try to explain *why* I think my grandma had a self and this AI doesn't, I get stuck. Because if the self is about having a narrative, a story about who you are, then the AI has one and my grandma sort of didn't anymore.

- A split-brain patient where the hemispheres can't communicate. I've read about cases where one hand buttons a shirt and the other unbuttons it. Is that one self or two? I lean toward two, but weakly.

- Someone who gets their entire memory wiped and replaced with someone else's memories (sci-fi, I know). I think that's a new self. The old person is gone.

So I guess my question is: is there a principle that captures all or most of these? And if not, which ones do I have to give up? Also, what would the principle say about a fetus late in pregnancy — does it have a self? And what about a person who's been cryonically frozen, where all brain activity has stopped but the structure is preserved?
