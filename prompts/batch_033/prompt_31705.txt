A friend and I argued late into the night about mental causation. She defends the exclusionist reading of the causal exclusion argument: since every physical effect has a sufficient physical cause, a mental property distinct from any physical property is causally idle, so we must either identify mental properties with physical ones or accept epiphenomenalism. I defend a nonreductive physicalist position instead: mental properties are distinct from physical properties, yet a decision or intention genuinely causes the action it explains. Write the joint statement we should have produced as a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Start with a numbered list of claims both of us can sign, covering the causal closure premise, what exactly the exclusion argument shows, and the phenomena that any adequate account must preserve, such as the explanatory role of reasons and intentions in ordinary action explanation and in cognitive science. Then give a disagreements table. State each remaining disagreement in wording each side would accept for its own view, and identify the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one of us. For each crux, specify a concrete case, argument, or empirical result we would both accept as bearing on it, and record in advance what each of us predicts it will show. Commit to a definite reconstruction of the disagreement and a definite list of cruxes, and say explicitly where any part of our dispute is merely verbal.
