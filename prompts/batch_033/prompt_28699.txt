I am preparing a colloquium talk defending the coherence of Sartre's account of radical freedom and bad faith, and I need a Q&A prep sheet. The audience is an analytic philosophy department: most faculty work on free will and moral responsibility from a broadly naturalistic standpoint, several are compatibilists of a Frankfurt-style or reasons-responsive stripe, two are hard determinists who think the whole free-will debate rests on a mistake, and none specialize in existentialism or phenomenology. They will be sympathetic to empirical constraints on agency but skeptical of transcendental arguments and suspicious of what they will call "ontological inflation." My thesis is that Sartre's claim that we are "condemned to be free" is not a metaphysical assertion about libertarian free will but a phenomenological claim about the structure of first-personal experience — and that bad faith, properly understood, names a distinctive form of self-deception that analytic accounts of motivated irrationality systematically miss. Give me the six or seven objections I am most likely to face from this audience, ordered from most damaging to least, each phrased in the vocabulary an analytic philosopher of action would use, with a two-minute reply and a frank note on where the reply is weakest. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks.
