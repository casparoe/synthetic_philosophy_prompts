I'm writing an essay on Tolstoy's The Death of Ivan Ilyich and the philosophical debate about whether death is bad for the person who dies. The Epicurean line of reasoning—from Epicurus, later taken up by Lucretius—holds that death cannot be bad for the one who dies, because the person and the event never coincide: when you are present, death is not, and when death is present, you are not. So there is no subject to experience the harm. The major contemporary reply, most famously from Thomas Nagel, is the deprivation account: death can be bad for you not because it involves any positive experience of suffering, but because it deprives you of the goods you would otherwise have had. On this view, the badness of death is a matter of what you lose, not what you feel.

Tolstoy's novella seems to me to cut across both positions in ways I'm still trying to sort out. Ivan Ilyich's suffering is almost entirely anticipatory—the terror of his own annihilation, the loneliness of dying among people who refuse to acknowledge what is happening. The story seems to be about how badly he lived, not about the badness of death itself. And the famous final passage, in which Ivan Ilyich "sought his former accustomed fear of death and did not find it. 'Where is it? What death?' There was no fear because there was no death. / In place of death there was light," reads almost like a dramatic vindication of the Epicurean view: death, when it actually arrives, is nothing to the one who undergoes it. Yet the whole tragedy is driven by the fact that he is dying at forty-five, that his life is being cut short, and that the life he is losing—however empty it was—was his only shot. That sounds like a deprivation.

I want a reading that takes both the novella and the philosophical debate seriously. Does Tolstoy give us a character whose death is bad in the deprivation sense, or does the story's real target—the falseness of Ivan Ilyich's life—mean that the deprivation is almost incidental to the real harm? Which scenes carry the most weight for each side of the argument, and where does the novella resist a clean philosophical resolution? I'd like a committed reading: take a definite position on what the story ultimately shows about whether and how death is bad for the one who dies, and defend it. Don't just lay out the interpretive options—tell me what you think the novella is actually doing and why.
