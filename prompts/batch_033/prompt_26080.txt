So I'm in this philosophy reading group and we've been going through decision theory paradoxes — Newcomb, the toxin puzzle, all that. Next week we're on Death in Damascus and I volunteered to bring something for the end of the session, like a closing piece. I had this idea that a villanelle would be weirdly perfect for it. You know how a villanelle has those two refrains that keep alternating and then finally collide in the last two lines? That's basically the structure of the paradox: you've got Damascus and Aleppo, and no matter which one you pick, the logic of the situation sends you to the one where Death is. The refrains just keep trading places but the outcome is the same every time.

I want the poem to be from the traveler's perspective, and the two refrains should somehow encode the two choices — maybe one refrain is about going to Aleppo and the other about Damascus, or maybe one is about the decision and the other about the consequence. The key thing I want the form to capture is that this isn't just a prophecy problem like Oedipus where the prophecy itself causes the bad outcome through some dramatic irony. The specific thing in Death in Damascus is that your own decision procedure is what's predictable — Death isn't magic, Death is just a perfect predictor of what you'll do. So the poem should get at that claustrophobic feeling of being trapped not by fate but by the fact that your own reasoning is transparent.

You're a poet who actually understands decision theory — like, you know the difference between CDT and EDT and why this paradox is a problem for both. Before writing the full thing, maybe think out loud about what the two refrains should be and why. And don't just name-drop the paradox in the title or anything; let the structure do the work.
