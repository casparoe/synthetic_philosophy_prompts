I'm giving a one-hour talk to my department's foundations-of-physics reading group — mostly grad students who can handle the Dirac notation but have never thought carefully about interpretations. I want to spend the hour on the Born rule problem as an internal difficulty for the many-worlds interpretation: if the wavefunction always evolves unitarily and every outcome in a superposition is realized in some branch, what does it even mean to say one outcome is more probable than another, and why should an agent in a branch expect to observe Born-rule frequencies? I am not asking you to explain the problem to me; I am asking you to plan the talk. What must be in place before the problem bites — that is, what is the dependency structure of the ideas, and what should I refuse to re-litigate at the start so that the hour isn't consumed by the measurement problem itself? What one concrete worked example should I build the whole hour around, and what features must it have and what features must it lack? What are the misconceptions the audience will predictably form (I can already guess: treating branches as discrete countable things and concluding all outcomes are equally probable; assuming Everett simply postulates the Born rule as an extra axiom; confusing the Born rule problem with the preferred-basis problem; thinking the problem dissolves once you notice that rational agents should bet according to the squared amplitude because that's just what we observe) and at exactly what point in the sequence should I anticipate and head off each one? What should I openly say I am not going to resolve at this level, and what single question can I ask at the end to distinguish a student who has genuinely understood the shape of the problem from one who has merely decided that many-worlds is weird?
