I'm preparing a talk for our department's philosophy of mind workshop, and I've been trying to get clear on what I actually believe about the problem of other minds. The more I write, the more I worry that my views don't quite fit together. I'd be grateful if someone could audit this set of commitments and tell me where the tensions really are—and which ones are only apparent.

Here's where I've landed, roughly:

(1) I'm a physicalist: mental states are realized by, and perhaps identical to, brain states. There's no Cartesian dualism here.

(2) I think the problem of other minds is a genuine epistemological problem. It's not dissolved by Wittgensteinian considerations about criteria or by behaviorism. There really is something to explain about how we know others have minds.

(3) I think the traditional analogical argument—I observe my own behavior correlated with my mental states, I observe similar behavior in others, therefore I infer similar mental states—is a bad argument. It's an induction from a single case, and it seems to get the phenomenology of our actual engagement with others wrong.

(4) I think we are justified in believing in other minds, and the best account of that justification is something like inference to the best explanation: positing minds in others best explains the complexity and flexibility of their behavior.

(5) I think there is a deep asymmetry between first-person and third-person knowledge of mental states. I know my own pain in a way that is categorically different from how I know yours, and this isn't just a matter of degree or reliability.

(6) I think the skeptical challenge—solipsism, the possibility that I'm alone in the universe—cannot be decisively refuted, but I also don't think it's rationally compelling. It's a live logical possibility but not one we have reason to take seriously.

Go through these and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension and which only appear to be. For the genuine tensions, rank them by severity and say what would have to give. I'm especially worried about whether (1) and (2) can really coexist, and whether rejecting (3) while holding (4) leaves me with a justificatory gap I haven't noticed.
