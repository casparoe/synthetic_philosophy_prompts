**Question:**

In the *Groundwork*, Kant argues that an action has moral worth only when it is performed from duty, not merely in accordance with duty. In recent decades, several philosophers have advanced an alternative account: an action has moral worth when it is performed for the right reasons—that is, when the agent is motivated by the very features that make the action right. Compare these two accounts. Are they in fact equivalent, or does the Kantian view capture something that the right-reasons view misses?

---

**Student's answer:**

Kant's account of moral worth requires that an action be performed from the motive of duty, and that this motive be the agent's sole motive. His famous examples illustrate this clearly. The prudent shopkeeper who charges fair prices only to protect his reputation acts in accordance with duty but not from it, so his action lacks moral worth. The sympathetic philanthropist who helps others because he enjoys doing so also acts without moral worth, because his motive is inclination rather than duty. Only the misanthropist—who helps others despite having lost all sympathetic feeling and having no inclination to do so—acts from duty, and his action alone has genuine moral worth.

The contemporary right-reasons account holds that an action has moral worth when the agent acts for the reasons that make the action right. For example, if helping someone in distress is right because it alleviates suffering, then an act of helping has moral worth if the agent helps because it alleviates suffering—rather than, say, because they want to impress an onlooker or because they vaguely feel that helping is "the right thing to do."

These two accounts are essentially equivalent. The Kantian view can be understood as a special case of the right-reasons view: Kant holds that what makes an action right is always that it conforms to the moral law, so the right-making reason is the same in every case. The right-reasons view simply generalizes this by allowing that different actions have different right-making features. On either account, the agent must be motivated by what genuinely makes the action right, not by incidental factors.

One might object that Kant's misanthropist poses a problem for the right-reasons view. The misanthropist acts from duty, not from any concern for the suffering of others. But on the right-reasons view, the right-making feature of helping is the alleviation of suffering itself. So the misanthropist's action would seem to lack moral worth on the right-reasons view, even though Kant says it has moral worth. However, this objection fails because the right-reasons theorist can simply say that the misanthropist does act for the right reasons after all: his recognition that helping is his duty necessarily involves recognizing that the suffering of others gives him a reason to act. His motive of duty therefore includes an implicit responsiveness to the right-making features. The two accounts thus converge, and the apparent tension is merely verbal.
