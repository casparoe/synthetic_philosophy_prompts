I'm working on a piece about what makes every 'a' an 'a' — how you can look at a blackletter 'a', a geometric sans 'a', a single-story handwritten 'a', and a double-story Times 'a', and instantly file all of them under the same letter. Its the sort of question the old Letter Spirit people used to chew on, and I want to make sure my piece doesn't just wave at it vaguely.

I've been collecting candidate answers people kick around, and I'd like you to rank them from best to worst as accounts of "a-ness," with a sentence or two of reasoning for each. Here they are:

1. The topological answer: an 'a' is a closed bowl with a stem or hook on the right. (Worries: the single-story and double-story forms have pretty different topologies, and a 'd' has a bowl and a stem too.)
2. The stroke-order answer: an 'a' is whatever is produced by the same writing gesture, or ductus. (Worries: printed or typewritten 'a's have no real ductus, and the two forms are written with different gestures.)
3. The alphabet-slot answer: an 'a' is whatever fills the first slot of the alphabet, or maps to the same sound in the writing system. (Worries: this feels circular, and uppercase A and lowercase a share a slot but look nothing alike.)
4. The family-resemblance answer: there are no necessary and sufficient features, just a graded family of shapes clustering around a prototype or a few exemplars.
5. The style-role answer: an 'a' is the shape that plays the a-role within a particular font's or hand's overall style system — it's an 'a' partly because of how it lines up with the same face's 'e', 'o', 'n', and so on.
6. The historical-continuity answer: an 'a' is whatever descends by an unbroken chain of copying from the same ancestral mark.

Rank these from best to worst, and say in a sentence or two why each one lands where it does. If you think some of them aren't really competitors — e.g., one is answering a seperate question from the others — say so.
