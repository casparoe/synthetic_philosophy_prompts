I work on the standards and guidance team of a large professional body, and several times a month we consider proposals to turn a practice into a shared norm for our members: for example, how committee chairs handle declarations of interest, whether members anonymise case studies when presenting, or how speaking order is managed in formal consultations. Some of these practices become genuine unwritten conventions within a year or two, in the sense that nearly everyone follows them, expects everyone else to follow them, and would rather follow them given that others do; others remain paper rules that people nod at and ignore. We currently decide by instinct, and we are wrong often enough that it costs the body credibility. I would like you to turn David Lewis's account of convention into a decision procedure my team can actually run. The procedure should do two things: first, decide whether an existing regularity among our members is a genuine convention rather than a mere habit, coincidence, or coerced rule; second, predict whether a proposed practice is likely to become a stable convention if we promote it. I am told the account turns on more than everyone doing the same thing: it matters that people expect others to conform, that this is common knowledge, and that an alternative regularity could have served just as well. Please preserve those distinctions where they genuinely do work in practice, and drop them where they do not. My team has no philosophy background, the procedure must be usable in a two-hour meeting, and its output must be transparent enough for our policy committee, which makes the final call.

For each step, please give the question to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with a 'no', and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values or judgement rather than fact. Then run the procedure on three cases: (a) a proposal to establish an unwritten norm that members disclose any personal relationship to a candidate before serving on an awards committee; (b) the existing regularity that members almost never request recorded votes even when the rules allow them; and (c) a proposal to rotate seating at formal dinners so the same members do not always sit near the president. Please make at least one of these a case where the procedure returns an answer we will not like, and say what the procedure cannot do — in particular, where Lewis's picture of convention is being stretched to cover the messier ways norms actually evolve.

I want the answer in British English. If the request rests on a confused premise, or if you think Lewis's account cannot honestly be operationalised in this way, I would rather you said so plainly than gave me a plausible-looking checklist; and throughout, please mark which steps are genuine judgement calls dressed up as procedure.
