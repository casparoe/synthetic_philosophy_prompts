I've been working through the idea of intuition pumps and I've arrived at an argument that seems to prove too much. I'd like to know where it breaks.

(1) A thought experiment functions as an intuition pump when it is designed to elicit a particular intuitive judgment by selectively describing a scenario — foregrounding features that support the intended verdict, backgrounding features that would pull the other way.

(2) Nearly every canonical thought experiment in philosophy fits this description. The trolley problem, the Chinese room, Twin Earth, Mary's room — each is a scenario built to make one verdict feel natural, and none is a neutral description of the facts.

(3) If a method reaches a conclusion by pumping an intuition rather than by tracking the world, the conclusion carries no epistemic weight — it reflects the design of the pump, not the structure of reality.

(4) So the conclusions drawn from canonical thought experiments carry no epistemic weight.

(5) But the argument for (1)–(4) is itself a thought experiment: we are invited to imagine the construction of a scenario, to notice the selectivity, and to feel the force of the analogy between philosophical thought experiments and rigged demonstrations. If (4) is true, then this argument also carries no epistemic weight.

(6) The self-defeat is not a clever gotcha. It reveals a genuine bind: the intuition-pump critique seems correct, but if it is correct, we cannot coherently assert that it is correct. We seem forced either to carve out an exception for methodological thought experiments, or to abandon the critique, or to accept that philosophy is a practice whose epistemic status we cannot assess.

Where does this argument go wrong? I suspect there is a slide between "designed to elicit" and "merely elicits," or between "selective" and "distorting," but I cannot make the diagnosis precise. If the argument is valid, which premise is the least costly to reject, and what do I lose by rejecting it?
