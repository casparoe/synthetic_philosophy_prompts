I spent forty years as a bench biochemist, and I've only recently turned to philosophy. I'm trying to get clear about something I used to treat as unproblematic: what counts as alive. My intuitions on individual cases are firm, or at least stable, but I cannot find a principle that draws the line where I draw it. I'd like you to help me extract the principle — or, if no single principle will do, a small set of candidates — from the following verdicts.

Here are the cases:

1. A colony of Escherichia coli growing on an agar plate. Verdict: alive, no hesitation.

2. A crystallised tobacco mosaic virus particle sitting on a bench. Verdict: not alive. It is a package of RNA and protein with no metabolism of its own, and it will sit there inert indefinitely.

3. That same virus inside a tobacco leaf cell, having shed its protein coat and begun directing the host's ribosomes to produce viral proteins. Verdict: I want to say not alive, but I feel the pull of the other answer. If forced, I say not alive — it is a hijacked metabolic system, not an organism.

4. A prion — a misfolded protein that induces other proteins to adopt its conformation. Verdict: not alive. No nucleic acid, no heritable sequence in the usual sense, just a conformation.

5. A flame. Verdict: not alive. It metabolises, grows, and reproduces, but it is a sustained exothermic reaction with no information-bearing molecule, no membrane, and no evolutionary history.

6. A mule. Verdict: alive. That it cannot reproduce does not matter; it is a functioning organism, product of evolution, with homeostasis, metabolism, and a life cycle — even if that cycle is a dead end.

7. A human being declared brain-dead, maintained on a ventilator. Verdict: not alive. The organism as an integrated whole has ceased to exist; what remains is a collection of living tissues sustained by external machinery.

8. A human zygote, the single cell formed at fertilisation. Verdict: alive. It is a stage in the human life cycle, with its own intact genome, and it will develop into a full organism given the right environment. That it lacks a brain or organs seems to me irrelevant to whether it is a living thing.

9. A bacterial endospore, dormant for decades, with no detectable metabolism. Verdict: alive. It is in suspended animation, but it is an organism with the capacity to resume full metabolic activity. If I found one in a Martian rock, I would call it life.

10. A red blood cell circulating in my own bloodstream. Verdict: not alive. It has no nucleus, no genome, no capacity to divide or maintain itself beyond a few months. It is a functional component of a living organism, not an organism itself.

11. The synthetic minimal cell JCVI-syn3.0, with a genome of 473 genes, the smallest known autonomously replicating organism, built from scratch in a laboratory. Verdict: alive. It is an organism, even if humans designed its genome.

12. A giant virus of the genus Mimivirus, which carries genes for aminoacyl tRNA synthetases and has a capsid larger than some bacteria, but still cannot replicate without a host. Verdict: not alive. The extra genes do not change the fundamental dependence.

13. A lichen — a composite of a fungus and an alga or cyanobacterium, so tightly integrated that it was long mistaken for a single organism. Verdict: alive. Whether I should say the lichen is one living thing or two living things in symbiosis is a further question; set it aside. The system as a whole is alive.

14. A computer program that copies itself across a network, mutates, and is subject to selection pressure from antivirus software. Verdict: not alive. It is a pattern of bits, not a physical entity maintaining itself far from equilibrium.

15. A severed human hand, minutes after amputation, its cells still living. Verdict: not alive. It is a part, not an organism. But this case bothers me, because it makes me realise that "part of a living thing" and "living thing" are doing work I cannot articulate.

I find that I cannot reduce these verdicts to a simple checklist. The standard textbook criteria — metabolism, growth, reproduction, response to stimuli, homeostasis, cellular organisation — do not capture the pattern. The virus scores on some criteria, the flame on others, the mule fails reproduction, the endospore fails metabolism. And the severed hand and the red blood cell suggest that "being an organism" or "being a whole" matters, but I cannot say what that means without circling back to "being alive."

Is there a coherent principle — or a small set of candidates — that captures all or most of these verdicts? For each candidate principle, please tell me which of my verdicts it gets right, which it gets wrong, and whether the ones it gets wrong are verdicts I should reconsider. Also tell me what the principle says about a case I have not listed: suppose we freeze an entire human being in liquid nitrogen, with no metabolism, but with the potential (let us stipulate) to be revived intact. Is the frozen human alive, dead, or in a third state, and what does your principle say about that? Finally, if no single principle can accommodate all fifteen verdicts without becoming ad hoc, tell me which verdict or verdicts I should overturn and why.
