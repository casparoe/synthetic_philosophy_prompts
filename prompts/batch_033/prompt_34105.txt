I'm trying to get clear on my own moral views about the doing/allowing distinction, and I'd like you to work through this with me. Below are my verdicts on a set of cases. I want to know: is there a coherent principle—or a small set of candidate principles—that captures all or most of these verdicts? If no single principle gets them all, which verdict would you overturn, and why? And what does the best principle you can articulate predict about a case I haven't listed?

Here are the cases and my verdicts:

1. **Switch.** A runaway trolley is heading toward five people who will be killed. You can pull a lever to divert the trolley onto a side track where one person is trapped. I think pulling the lever is permissible. (But I also think the person on the side track would have grounds to resent it, if they survived.)

2. **Transplant.** A surgeon has five patients who will die without organ transplants. A healthy traveler comes in for a checkup. The surgeon could kill the traveler and harvest his organs, saving the five. I think this is impermissible—and not just because of bad downstream effects if we institutionalized it. Even in a one-off case with no further consequences, I think it's wrong.

3. **Withdrawal.** A competent, terminally ill patient in severe pain, with no prospect of recovery, asks her doctor to remove the ventilator keeping her alive. The doctor does so, and the patient dies. I think this is permissible.

4. **Lethal injection.** The same patient, in the same circumstances, asks her doctor to administer a lethal injection instead. I find myself thinking this is also permissible, though I'm less confident than in the withdrawal case. But I also feel there's a real difference between the two—the injection feels more like the doctor killing the patient, while withdrawal feels more like letting the disease take its course. I'm not sure whether this difference should matter.

5. **Shallow pond.** You're walking past a shallow pond and see a child drowning. You could easily wade in and save the child, but doing so would ruin your expensive shoes and make you late for work. I think you are obligated to save the child, and failing to do so is seriously wrong—on a par with harming someone, not merely a failure of generosity.

6. **Unreleased drug.** A pharmaceutical company has already manufactured a drug that could save the lives of a thousand people in a poor country. It decides not to release the drug because it can't turn a profit on it. The people die. I think the company acts wrongly, and the wrong is serious—it's not just a failure of charity.

7. **Factory not built.** A pharmaceutical company considers building a factory to produce a drug for a rare disease that kills about a hundred people a year. It decides not to build the factory, because the investment would never pay off. The people who would have been saved die. I think this is not seriously wrong—it's not morally equivalent to the unreleased-drug case, even though the outcome is the same. But I'm not sure I can articulate why.

8. **Bombing raid.** A military commander orders a bombing raid on a legitimate military target, knowing that the raid will kill fifty civilians in a nearby residential area as a foreseeable side effect. The raid succeeds in its military objective. I think the commander's action is permissible, but only if the military objective is genuinely weighty and the civilian deaths are proportionate and unavoidable.

9. **Famine relief withheld.** The same commander is in a position to air-drop food to starving civilians in a region his forces control after a conflict. The air-drop would cost his military resources and carry some risk to his personnel, but it would save roughly the same number of civilian lives as were lost in the bombing raid. He decides not to do it, prioritizing other military objectives. I think this is less bad than the bombing raid—maybe even permissible—even though the number of civilian deaths is the same. But I'm unsettled about this.

10. **Poisoned well (unintentional).** A factory owner buries a container of toxic chemicals on his property, reasonably believing it will never leak. Decades later, the container corrodes and the chemicals seep into the town's water supply, causing a dozen deaths. I think the factory owner bears some responsibility—he should have anticipated the risk—but I don't think he's guilty of murder or anything close to it. It feels more like negligence than like killing.

11. **Poisoned well (intentional).** The same factory owner buries the same container, but he knows it will leak within a year and hopes the town will be driven off the land so he can buy it cheaply. The leak happens, and a dozen people die. I think this is murder.

I recognize that some of these verdicts are in tension. The switch and the transplant seem to pull in opposite directions. The withdrawal and the lethal injection feel different but I'm not sure I can justify the difference. The unreleased-drug case and the factory-not-built case are identical in outcome but I judge them very differently. The bombing raid and the famine relief withheld also seem to pull apart.

For each case, say whether the principle delivers my verdict, and if it doesn't, say so honestly. Then apply the principle to a case I haven't listed—one that you think is a hard test for the principle, or one that reveals something I haven't noticed about my own views. Finally, if you had to overturn one of my verdicts to make the overall picture more coherent, which one would you overturn, and why?

Also: please tell me which argument or consideration would most likely change your mind about the principle you end up proposing—not just a minor tweak, but something that would make you abandon it for a different principle entirely. And where in your reasoning are you least confident?
