**Question**

Explain Bernard Williams's integrity objection to utilitarianism, paying particular attention to the role of the 'one thought too many' remark. How, if at all, does the integrity objection differ from the more familiar demandingness objection?

**Student's Answer**

Bernard Williams's integrity objection, developed in his essay 'A Critique of Utilitarianism' (published in *Utilitarianism: For and Against*, co-authored with J.J.C. Smart), targets the way utilitarianism requires agents to adopt an impartial standpoint when deliberating about what to do. The objection is not simply that utilitarianism asks too much of us—that is the demandingness objection—but that it alienates us from the very projects, relationships, and commitments that give our lives meaning and constitute our identities.

Williams illustrates this with two famous examples. In the first, Jim finds himself in a South American village where a military officer offers him a choice: shoot one innocent villager, and the other nineteen will be released; refuse, and all twenty will be killed. Utilitarianism appears to recommend that Jim shoot the one, since that produces the best overall outcome. Williams's point is not that utilitarianism gives the wrong answer here—he is careful to say that he is not trying to show that utilitarianism yields the wrong verdict—but rather that the way utilitarianism forces Jim to think about the situation is itself objectionable. Jim is required to regard his own agency as merely a causal instrument for bringing about the best state of affairs, and his deep reluctance to shoot an innocent person is treated as an irrational squeamishness to be overcome. This, Williams argues, is an attack on Jim's integrity: his ability to act from his own deepest commitments and projects.

The second example gives us the phrase 'one thought too many.' A man must choose between saving his wife and saving a stranger, both of whom are drowning. He saves his wife. Williams says that if the man had to think to himself, 'Saving my wife maximises overall utility,' or even 'In situations of this kind it is permissible to give preference to one's spouse,' that justifying thought would be 'one thought too many.' Both thoughts are problematic in the same way: they interpose an impartial justification between the man and the relationship that directly motivates his action. The man should save his wife simply because she is his wife, without needing to reach for any justifying principle at all. The demand that one's actions be justifiable from an impartial standpoint is thus itself alienating, even when the justification is not specifically utilitarian.

This is what distinguishes the integrity objection from the demandingness objection. The demandingness objection says that utilitarianism requires us to sacrifice too much of our own well-being for the sake of others—to give away most of our income, to forgo personal projects, and so on. The integrity objection, by contrast, says that even if utilitarianism did not demand extreme sacrifices, it would still be objectionable because of the way it requires us to think about our reasons for action. It is not about the quantity of the demand but about the structure of practical reasoning that utilitarianism imposes. An agent who fully internalised the utilitarian standpoint would lose the ability to act from the personal commitments that make her the particular person she is; she would suffer a loss of integrity.

However, one might worry that the two objections are not entirely independent. Part of what makes utilitarianism so demanding is precisely that it requires us to treat our personal projects as having no special weight at the level of justification. So the demandingness objection and the integrity objection may ultimately be two sides of the same coin: utilitarianism alienates us from our projects, and because those projects are what we care about most, following utilitarianism would require us to abandon them, which is extremely demanding. Williams himself seems to acknowledge this connection when he notes that the utilitarian might reply that integrity is just a form of self-indulgence.
