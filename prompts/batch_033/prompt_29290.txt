Right, I've been poking at the old Copycat letter-string microworld and I want to get clear on what a good theory of it actually has to do before I read any more implementations. The setup: you're shown a change to a source string, like abc → abd, and a target string, like ijk, and you have to do 'the same thing' to the target. The easy answer is ijl, but the whole interest is that 'the same thing' isnt given in advance: it depends on what you take the concepts to be — a letter or a group of letters, successor or predecessor, rightmost or leftmost, whether the alphabet wraps at z, and so on.

So write me the desiderata. What does an adequate theory of 'doing the same change' in this microworld have to deliver? I want the list of requirements with the case behind each: the clean cases it has to get right (abc → abd, ijk → ?); the breakdown cases where the obvious rule can't apply (abc → abd, xyz → ? — there's no successor of z, and a theory has to explain why wyz or xya is a live answer and xyy isn't); the cases where the unit of change is underdetermined (in aabbcc, does 'the last letter' mean the final c or the whole cc group — aabbcd vs aabbdd); and the cases where position in the string pulls apart from position in the alphabet (abc → abd, kji → ? gives kjj if you keep 'rightmost letter' but lji if you keep 'letter latest in the alphabet', and the theory has to say why the first wins).

Also say which distinctions a theory can't collapse: slipping the operation (successor to predecessor) is not the same as slipping the role (rightmost to leftmost) or slipping the object (letter to group), and I want to know whether these have to be seperate mechanisms or can all come out of one process.

Then mark which of the requirements are non-negotiable and which are just taste, and flag any pair you think can't be jointly met. The one I'm worried about is that the theory has to let concepts be fluid enough to slip under pressure but fixed enough to give a determinate, non-arbitrary answer — that feels like the real tension, so tell me if it is.
