I'm giving a departmental seminar next month on the wrong kind of reasons problem, and I need to prepare for the Q&A. I'm defending the view that the wrong kind of reasons problem isn't really one problem but two: the epistemic case (reasons for belief that arise from pragmatic considerations rather than evidence) and the attitudinal case (reasons for admiration, desire, resentment, etc. that arise from incentives rather than the object's features) raise structurally different issues, and attempts to solve both with a single criterion — whether it's the object-given/state-given distinction, a buck-passing move, or a fittingness-first approach — inevitably break in one domain or the other. My positive proposal is that in the epistemic case, the problem is largely a red herring produced by overgeneralizing from the practical case, and the real work is in the attitudinal case, where the WKRP reveals a genuine tension between the normativity of value and the normativity of practical reasons that resists clean analysis. I close by suggesting that the right response to the WKRP is disunified: we need different accounts of the right-kind/wrong-kind distinction for different attitude types, and that this is a feature rather than a bug.

The audience is a philosophy department with strengths in both ethics and epistemology. About half the room works on the reasons-first program and normative theory, a quarter are epistemologists (several of whom are skeptical that talk of reasons for belief maps neatly onto talk of reasons for action, and who think doxastic voluntarism is a non-starter), and the remaining quarter are generalists who know the literature but aren't invested in a particular solution. Among the ethicists there are two committed buck-passers about value who think the WKRP is the main obstacle to their view and who have published on it, and one person who thinks the whole problem is a confusion generated by ambiguous talk of "reasons" and will push back on the very framing of the debate. The epistemologists in the room are likely to resist any view that treats belief as just another attitude alongside admiration or desire — they will want to know why the epistemic case shouldn't simply be cordoned off from the start.
