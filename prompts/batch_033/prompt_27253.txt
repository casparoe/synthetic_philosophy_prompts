A friend in my moral psych seminar has been pushing this argument and I can't find the exit. It seems to prove there's no real difference between a moral preference and a moral bias, which I find impossible to live with, but I can't locate the bad step.

(1) To call a judgment 'biased' is to say it departs from the judgment that a correct or unbiased process would have produced.
(2) In the moral case, though, what the correct judgment is is itself a moral question; there's no morally neutral fact about which moral judgments are right.
(3) So when I call your moral judgment biased, I'm just applying my own moral standard to you.
(4) And my own moral standard is itself just a set of preferences (or attitudes or sentiments); there's no standard outside all such preferences from which to sort them into 'sound' and 'biased.'
(5) So 'your moral view is biased' adds nothing to 'your moral view conflicts with my preferences.'
(6) Therefore 'bias' in morality is just a rhetorical device, and there's no principled distinction between a moral preference and a moral bias.
(7) But that can't be right: it would mean we couldn't call a judge who hands down systematically harsher sentences to out-group defendants biased as opposed to merely having different sentencing preferences, and it would make the whole debiasing litterature in ethics look confused.

I can't tell whether this is valid with a false premise or whether something slides between (3) and (4). Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If I have to choose, I'd rather give up (4) than (7), but I don't see how to give up (4) without helping myself to a moral standard that's just as preference-like as the ones it's meant to police.
