I spent my career in statistical mechanics and only recently began reading philosophy of probability seriously. I have been following the debate over the Principal Principle — roughly, Lewis's idea that one's rational credence should equal the known objective chance, conditional on admissible evidence. The part that interests me is the tension between the Principal Principle and Humeanism about chance: the view that chances are not primitive modal facts but are instead given by something like the Best Systems Analysis, where the chances are whatever probabilities appear in the best systematization of the entire actual history. The problem, as I understand it, is that on a Humean view, future events can undermine present chances. If the best system says the chance of heads is 0.7, but the actual sequence of outcomes turns out to be all tails, then the best system might have been different, and the chance was never really 0.7. This seems to put the Principal Principle under pressure.

I would like to see what an adversarial collaboration between a committed Humean about chance and a non-Humean primitivist about chance would produce. Draft the joint statement they could both sign after a good-faith effort to clarify their disagreement. I want: (1) a numbered list of claims both accept — about the formalism, about what the Principal Principle says, about what needs explaining; (2) a disagreements table with precise formulations of where they part ways, phrased so each side would accept the wording of its own position; (3) the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side; and (4) for each crux, the kind of case or argument both would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. If any part of their dispute turns out to be merely verbal, say which part and why.
