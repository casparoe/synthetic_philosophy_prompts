I was at dinner with two friends recently, and they got into an argument about a story in the news, and I haven't been able to stop thinking about it. I'd like you to take on the role of a professional philosopher with expertise in moral philosophy — specifically contractualism and the debate about whether harms and benefits to different people can simply be added together — and to work through this very carefully, because I wasn't able to referee it myself.

The case they were arguing about: after a disaster, a small hospital has a single dose of a scarce drug. One patient, if he doesn't receive it, will die within days. The same dose could instead be divided into smaller doses and given to roughly thirty other patients, each of whom is in real but temporary pain — the kind that will pass on its own but is making them miserable. The hospital can't do both, there is no other treatment, and no more of the drug can be obtained. My friend Elena said the hospital must give the drug to the one patient. My friend Dev said it must go to the thirty. Here is the back-and-forth as best I can reconstruct it:

Dev: You'd really let thirty people lie there in pain to save one person? The total relief across all thirty dwarfs what the one patient gets. If we couldn't add up benefits across people, we couldn't make any public-health decision at all.

Elena: I'm not saying numbers never matter. I'm saying you can't compare them the way you're comparing them. From each of the thirty patients' point of view, what's at stake is a few days of pain. From the one patient's point of view, what's at stake is everything. A rule that says "we may let you die to spare thirty other people some temporary pain" is a rule the one patient could reasonably refuse to accept.

Dev: But turn it around: a rule that says "we may leave your pain untreated because one other person's life is at stake" is a rule each of the thirty could reasonably refuse to accept. Why should the one person's refusal count for more than thirty refusals? Unless you think a single life is worth more than any number of headaches — but then you'd be treating a life as infinitely valuable, and no actual hospital or government operates that way.

Elena: That's exactly the move I'm objecting to. You're treating thirty people's separate small burdens as if they pooled together into one big burden. But they don't pool. There is no single person who experiences the sum of the thirty pains. If there were one person with thirty headaches at once, that would be a different matter. But there isn't.

Dev: So what? Of course the pains don't pool in one mind. The point is that each of those thirty minds matters. You keep talking about the standpoint of the one patient, but you're not taking the standpoints of the thirty seriously. Each of them can say "you sacrificed my relief for a stranger." If each person matters equally, then thirty of them matter thirty times as much.

Elena: They matter equally as persons, but that doesn't mean their claims add up like weights on a scale. The question isn't how many people are on each side; it's which decision can be justified to each person. I can justify saving the one to any of the thirty by saying, "If it had been your life at stake, we would have done the same for you." But you can't justify letting the one die by saying, "Sorry, your death was the price of thirty people's comfort."

Dev: That just asserts a different method and gives it a nice name. In practice we face these choices all the time, and we do choose by numbers — road safety, flu vaccines, flood defenses. Are you really prepared to say all of that is wrong? Because if the only test is "could any individual reject it," then every triage decision, every insurance limit, and every safety standard fails the test, since someone always loses out.

Elena: No — there's a difference between a decision that imposes a small risk or burden on everyone under a scheme that benefits everyone, and a decision that picks out one identified person to bear a catastrophic burden so that many others get a minor benefit. I can accept a general policy that occasionally leaves me with a headache. I cannot accept a policy that says, in this particular case, "you are the one who dies."

Dev: But triage always picks someone out; that's what scarcity means. You haven't given a way to distinguish the acceptable cases from the unacceptable ones. You've just moved the line to "catastrophic burdens are never aggregable," which would mean we should spend unlimited amounts to save identified lives. That isn't a serious position.

For each move, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating; then say who is right about which specific points, where each goes wrong, and what, if anything, both of them are missing. I'm less interested in a one-sentence verdict than in a precise map of what holds up and what doesn't, so please think through each step carefully before settling on an overall assessment.
