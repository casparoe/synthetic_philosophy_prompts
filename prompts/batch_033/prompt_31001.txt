I've become convinced by Maimonides' view of the biblical sacrificial system: the animal sacrifices were not something God wanted for their own sake, but a concession to what a people habituated to worship through sacrifice could absorb. The Law, on this reading, redirected an ingrained sacrificial impulse away from idolatry and toward the one God — centralizing it, stripping it of its idolatrous features, and subordinating it to the intellectual worship that is the Law's real aim. What would count against this reading? I want the defeaters sorted from fatal to merely awkward: features of the sacrificial legislation that a concession story handles poorly (its sheer elaborateness, or the fact that the Torah nowhere says the cult is a compromise), what the view predicts about later developments — the prophetic critiques of sacrifice, the post-Temple turn to prayer — that rival readings on which sacrifice has intrinsic value predict differently, and where a defender would have to concede real damage rather than explain the evidence away. I'd also like a frank verdict on whether the view is so flexible, so ready to reinterpret every ritual detail as anti-idolatrous pedagogy, that it risks explaining everything and therefore nothing.
