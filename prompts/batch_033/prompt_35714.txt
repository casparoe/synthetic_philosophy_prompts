For several years, a debate has been running through the AI alignment literature — and through the reading groups and online forums that orbit it — about whether HCH (the recursively defined ideal of "humans consulting HCH") and the associated idea of factored cognition provide a useful idealization of human deliberation. The debate is well-rehearsed, the arguments on both sides are familiar to everyone in it, and yet I rarely see anyone change their mind. I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this really is, and whether it is capable of resolution.

Let me lay out the two sides as I see them.

On one side, the optimists hold that HCH — a human at the root of a tree, decomposing a question into subquestions, delegating each to a copy of themselves with the same recursive capacity, and synthesizing the results — captures something essential about what careful deliberation looks like when it is made explicit and legible. They tend to stress that HCH is not meant as a description of what goes on inside a single unaided human mind; it is a specification of what we would want an overseer to approximate, and its value is normative and architectural. The optimists often point out that real-world institutions already implement something like HCH: a lab director decomposes a research program into experiments, delegates them, and integrates the findings; a judge delegates factual findings to a jury and legal research to clerks, then synthesizes a ruling. The fact that these real delegations are messy and lossy does not refute the idealization — it shows that we need better decomposition languages and better tools, not that factoring is impossible in principle. The optimists also argue that if we cannot even specify what factored deliberation would look like, we have no target to train AI systems toward, and therefore no story about how to align systems more capable than humans.

On the other side, the skeptics argue that deliberation is not tree-structured in the way HCH assumes. Many of the judgments that matter most — is this frame appropriate? does this argument cohere? have we considered the relevant alternatives? — require holding the entire problem in view at once, and no amount of decomposition can recover that global sensitivity without the root human effectively re-deriving everything the subagents did. If the root human does re-derive everything, the factoring collapses back into unaided deliberation; if the root human merely defers to subagents whose work they have not verified, the process is no longer deliberation but aggregation, and the root's judgment is only as good as their blind trust in the tree. The skeptics also worry that HCH smuggles in an exponential amount of human labor — the tree is infinitely deep and infinitely wide — and that the interesting question is whether any bounded approximation of HCH can preserve its properties, which the idealization itself does not address. On this view, HCH is not a regulative ideal but a distraction: it describes a process that is neither psychologically real nor practically realizable, and calling it "deliberation" dresses up a committee architecture in the language of individual reasoning.

The two sides have been trading these arguments for years. The same moves recur: the optimists say "it's a normative ideal, not a psychological claim"; the skeptics reply "then stop calling it deliberation and tell me what I'm supposed to learn from it about actual reasoning." The optimists say "institutions already do this"; the skeptics reply "an institution is not a mind, and the root of an institution is not deliberating — it's managing." Nobody moves.

I have a working hypothesis about why the debate is stuck, but I want it tested. My hypothesis is that the disagreement is not a single substantive dispute but a tangle of at least two different questions being asked under the same vocabulary. The optimists are asking: what kind of process would be legible enough to oversee, and sufficiently expressive to serve as a target for training aligned AI systems? The skeptics are asking: what is the actual structure of human cognition when it reasons well, and does HCH adequately capture that structure? If this diagnosis is right, then the debate is stuck because the two sides are answering different questions — one about the design of oversight, one about the nature of cognition — and the same words ("deliberation," "reasoning," "judgment") are doing different work for each. But I suspect there is also a hidden empirical or formal question underneath: can a human, given bounded time and a tractable interface, reliably evaluate the output of a subagent without redoing the subagent's work, and under what conditions? That question is about the scalability of oversight, not about the essence of deliberation, and it might be tractable even if the philosophical dispute is not.

I want a diagnosis, not a verdict. What kind of disagreement is this? Is it partly verbal, partly a matter of different questions under one heading, and partly a hidden empirical dispute about the scalability of checking? Or is there a single substantive claim that both sides are genuinely disagreeing about, and if so, what is it? Is the impasse a sign that the question is ill-posed — that "is HCH a good idealization of deliberation?" is too ambiguous to be answered without specifying what the idealization is for and what features of deliberation must be preserved — or is it just that the question is hard? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress? A formal result about the classes of tasks that factor under bounded checking? An experiment on how well humans can evaluate the reasoning of language models in a decomposition structure? A conceptual distinction that separates the descriptive, normative, and engineering readings of the idealization? Or is this a debate where we should not expect progress, and if so, why?
