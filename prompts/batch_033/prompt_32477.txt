I'm a PhD student in epistemology, and I've been trying to work out what I actually think about the dogmatism paradox by going case by case rather than starting from a principle. The paradox, roughly, is that if you know that p, then you seem entitled to dismiss any counterevidence as misleading — since you know p is true, evidence against it must be misleading — but that licenses a kind of intellectual rigidity that feels wrong in many cases. I want to know whether there's a single principle that captures when knowledge does and doesn't permit dismissing counterevidence.

Here are my verdicts on a set of cases. In each case, I start out knowing the proposition, and then encounter counterevidence.

1. I know that 2+2=4. A stranger on the internet sends me a twenty-page document they claim is a proof that 2+2=5. Verdict: I can dismiss it without reading the document. I don't need to find the error.

2. I've just come inside and I know it's raining — I'm soaking wet, I heard the downpour seconds ago. My spouse, who is looking out the window, says "It stopped raining about ten minutes ago." Verdict: I should at least look out the window myself. I can't just dismiss it.

3. I know the defendant is guilty because I saw him commit the assault with my own eyes, in good lighting, from about fifteen feet away, and I wasn't drinking. At trial, ten witnesses I have no reason to distrust testify under oath that the defendant was at a bar across town at the time. Verdict: I should seriously reconsider. I can't dismiss their testimony.

4. I know that vaccines don't cause autism, based on the overwhelming epidemiological evidence and scientific consensus. A new meta-analysis is published in a top-tier medical journal, by authors with no known anti-vaccine affiliations, claiming to find a statistically significant link. Verdict: I should read the study carefully. Dismissing it out of hand would be unreasonable.

5. I know my partner is faithful to me, based on years of shared life, deep trust, and nothing ever suggesting otherwise. I happen to see a text notification on their phone from an unsaved number: "Last night was amazing. Can't wait to see you again." Verdict: I should investigate, not dismiss it. Something might be going on.

6. I've constructed a proof of a nontrivial theorem, checked it carefully over several days, and had it accepted by a journal. I know the theorem is true. A colleague whose mathematical judgment I respect tells me she thinks she's found a subtle error in the proof. Verdict: I should go back and re-check the proof. I can't dismiss her concern.

7. I know I left my keys on the kitchen table — I have a vivid memory of placing them there ten minutes ago, and I wasn't distracted. I walk into the kitchen and they're not on the table. Verdict: I should conclude I was wrong about where I left them, not dismiss the empty table as misleading evidence.

Is there a principle that captures all seven of these verdicts? If not, which verdicts would I have to overturn, and what's the best principle that captures the rest?
