I've been reading about predictive processing and the free energy principle in cognitive science, and there's a problem that keeps nagging at me that I'm hoping you can help me think through. From what I understand, the free energy principle claims that living systems act to minimize free energy, which is equivalent to minimizing the discrepancy between expected and actual sensory input — in other words, minimizing surprise. The brain, on this view, is constantly generating predictions about incoming sensory data and updating either its predictions or its actions to reduce prediction error.

Here's what I can't get past: if minimizing surprise is the fundamental organizing principle, then surely the optimal strategy for any organism is to seek out an environment with minimal and maximally predictable sensory input — a dark, silent room — and simply stay there. In such an environment, there is almost nothing to be surprised by, so free energy is effectively minimized. This is sometimes called the "dark room problem." But organisms manifestly do not do this; they explore, seek novelty, take risks, and engage with complex environments. So the free energy principle seems to make a false prediction about behavior, or else it must be qualified so heavily that it loses all explanatory force.

I've encountered the standard response: that organisms have prior expectations about being in certain states — being fed, being at a safe temperature, engaging in social interaction — and a dark room would violate those expectations, generating its own prediction error. But this response seems to hollow out the theory. If you can simply stipulate that the organism "expects" to be in whatever states it in fact tends to occupy, then the free energy principle is not explaining why organisms seek those states; it's just redescribing that seeking behavior in the language of prediction error minimization. The heavy lifting is being done by whatever determines the content of the expectations, and the free energy principle itself says nothing about that.

I would like to know whether I am misunderstanding the free energy principle at a more fundamental level, or whether the dark room problem really does expose a serious limitation. I would also appreciate it if you could say how confident you are in your answer and single out the point you are least sure about.
