I've been teaching a course on philosophy of mind, and last week we read Dennett's discussion of sphexishness — the idea, borrowed from Dean Wooldridge via Hofstadter, that the digger wasp Sphex exhibits a startling kind of mechanical rigidity. The wasp's routine goes: find a cricket, sting it to paralyze it, drag it to the entrance of the burrow, leave it there, go inside to inspect the burrow, re-emerge, and finally drag the cricket in. If an experimenter moves the cricket a few inches away while the wasp is inside inspecting, the wasp re-emerges, drags the cricket back to the entrance, and then — instead of simply dragging it straight inside — goes back down to inspect the burrow all over again. Move the cricket again, and the whole sequence repeats, apparently without limit. Dennett uses this to draw a line between the merely mechanical and the kind of flexible intelligence worth calling free, and ever since the class, I've been trying to figure out where I actually draw that line. My gut reactions to cases keep pulling me in different directions.

Here are my verdicts on a range of cases:

1. The sphex wasp repeating its routine when the cricket is moved: clearly mechanical, no flexibility worth calling agency.
2. A Roomba that bumps into a chair leg, backs up, turns slightly, and tries again, repeating this pattern until it escapes: I want to say this is mechanical too, but it's doing something the sphex isn't — it's exploring the space and will eventually succeed. Still, I can't bring myself to call it flexible in any deep sense.
3. A driver who always takes the same route to work, even on days when they know there's construction and it will take twice as long, because they're on autopilot and don't think to change: this feels sphexish to me, even though the driver is a human with full cognitive capacities.
4. A jazz pianist improvising over chord changes: not mechanical — genuinely flexible.
5. That same pianist, having internalized a lick so thoroughly that it comes out in certain harmonic contexts without any conscious decision: this feels flexible, not mechanical, even though it's automatic in the moment. The history matters — it was acquired through a process of flexible exploration, and that seems to make it different in kind from the sphex's routine.
6. An LLM that, when given a certain kind of reasoning puzzle, reliably produces the same wrong answer that many humans give, apparently falling into the same cognitive trap: I find myself wanting to say this is sphexish — a pattern triggered by the input and running to completion. But then I'm not sure why the human who falls into the same trap isn't equally sphexish in that moment.
7. A person with severe OCD who checks the stove exactly seven times, even though they know perfectly well they just checked it and it's off: I want to say this is mechanical in the sphex sense, even though the person is conscious and distressed. But if I say that, am I committed to saying the pianist's automatic lick is mechanical too? That feels wrong, and I need to know why it isn't.
8. An octopus in a lab that figures out how to unscrew a jar from the inside, having never encountered a screw-top before: clearly not sphexish — this is the far end of the flexibility spectrum.

I'm after a principle, or at least a principled sketch, that carves these cases somewhere sensible — that tells me what sphexishness really amounts to and what separates it from genuine flexibility. I'm especially interested in what the principle says about cases 5, 6, and 7, because that's where my intuitions feel like they might be in tension. If no clean principle captures all my verdicts, tell me which ones I should reconsider and why. Also, please say what the principle predicts for a case I haven't listed: a honeybee's waggle-dance that communicates the distance and direction of a food source — does that fall on the sphexish side or the flexible side?

Finally, please tell me how confident you are in the principle you settle on, and single out the point you're least sure of.
