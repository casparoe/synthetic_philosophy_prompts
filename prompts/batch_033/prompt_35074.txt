I am presenting a paper at a philosophy of mind colloquium next month. The paper defends constitutive Russellian panpsychism against the charge that the combination problem renders it untenable. My central argument is that the combination problem is often overstated because it tacitly relies on a model of combination drawn from physical mereology—the aggregation of spatially extended parts—and that once we abandon that model, the demand to explain how micro-subjects "add up" to a macro-subject loses its force. Instead, I argue that the relation between micro-level and macro-level consciousness is better understood as a kind of grounding relation that does not require the macro-subject to be literally composed of micro-subjects as spatial parts compose a whole. The positive view I sketch is that macro-consciousness is grounded in the total phenomenal field of fundamental entities arranged in certain dynamic, information-integrated configurations, where the grounding relation is primitive and not further analyzable in compositional terms. In other words, I deny that the combination problem demands a reductive compositional explanation and instead treat phenomenal combination as a sui generis form of metaphysical dependence.

The audience will consist of roughly twenty-five philosophers. About half are philosophers of mind with a mainstream physicalist or functionalist orientation; they think panpsychism is a non-starter and will likely press me on whether my view is empirically distinguishable from the claim that consciousness simply emerges from complex information processing. Three or four are metaphysicians who work on grounding and fundamentality; they will be sympathetic to the apparatus but skeptical about its application to consciousness. Two are card-carrying dualists who think panpsychism shares all the problems of physicalism plus some of its own. One is a philosopher of physics who will want to know exactly what I mean by "fundamental entities" and whether my view is compatible with quantum field theory. The remaining few are graduate students whose commitments are still forming.
