I was at a pub with two colleagues from work — both software engineers, like me — and the conversation somehow landed on split-brain patients. None of us has any background in neuroscience or philosophy, but we'd all come across the classic experiments at some point, and it turned into one of those arguments that keeps circling back because no one can quite land a decisive blow.

The disagreement started when Dan said that split-brain cases show consciousness isn't necessarily unified: sever the corpus callosum, and you seem to get two separate streams of experience inside one skull, each with its own perceptions, intentions, and — in some sense — its own self. Emma pushed back hard. She said the patients themselves report feeling perfectly unified, and all the strange behaviours — the left hand picking something up while the right hand puts it down, the inability to name objects presented to the left visual field — can be explained by unconscious processing in the disconnected hemisphere, not by positing a second consciousness. Dan replied that this doesn't account for the confabulation: when the left hand does something the speaking left hemisphere didn't initiate, the patient doesn't say "I don't know why my hand did that" — they invent a reason, which suggests there's someone in there generating explanations, just not someone the speaking hemisphere can access. Emma countered that confabulation is exactly what you'd predict from a single conscious system trying to make sense of its own unconsciously driven actions; it doesn't require a second consciousness any more than a dreamer inventing a narrative to make sense of random neural firing does. Dan then brought up cases where the hemispheres seem to have genuinely conflicting goals — one hand reaching for food while the other pushes it away — and argued this looks like two agents in conflict, not one agent with some unconscious tics. Emma replied that conflicting desires are routine within a single consciousness (that's what ambivalence is); the only difference here is that the conflict gets externalised into different hands because the hemispheres can't coordinate. At that point Dan said, "So your view is that there's a single consciousness that's somehow generating two simultaneous, mutually unaware streams of intentional action — at which point I'm not sure what 'unity' is even supposed to mean." Emma said, "No, my view is that there's one consciousness associated with the left hemisphere, and the right hemisphere is doing complex but unconscious processing — the unity is preserved because there was never a second conscious subject to begin with."

They eventually turned to me and asked what I thought, and I genuinely couldn't untangle it. I'd like you to go through their exchange move by move and adjudicate: who is right about which specific claim, where does each argument succeed or fail, and what — if anything — are both of them missing? If the disagreement is badly framed at the level of its assumptions — for instance, if the whole debate turns on an ambiguity in what they mean by "consciousness," or if the empirical evidence doesn't actually license the claims either of them is making — I'd rather you say that plainly than try to score points on their terms.
