PhD student in metaethics here. I've spent the last year getting pulled toward a Williams-style internalism about reasons, and I want someone to actually try to break it, because my advisors seem split between that's trivially true and that's obviously false, and neither response has been much help. The version I mean is this: an agent has a reason to do something just in case there's a deliberative path — reasoning, correcting false beliefs, exercising imagination, working out what would satisfy the things she already cares about — from her current motivational set (her desires, projects, loyalties, attachments) to a motivation to do it. If no such path exists, then saying she has a reason to do it isn't a description of how things stand with her; it's at best advice or blame dressed up as fact. On this picture there are no external reasons, not because people are selfish or because morality is optional, but because the idea of a reason that floats free of what an agent could be brought to care about by thinking doesn't explain her actions, and explanation is the only job reasons have.

Here's what I'd like from you. Tell me what would have to be true for this view to be false, and don't just rehearse the standard objections — I've already heard the amoralist and the what-about-people-with-no-relevant-desires cases. I want a map of the view's exposure. Which objections actually bite, and which would a commited internalist themselves concede count as costs? For instance: the deliberative path clause looks like it can be stretched to deliver whatever verdict you want — is that a fixable vagueness or a sign the view has no content? Does the view survive the agent whose motivational set is so impoverished that we want to say she has reasons anyway — severe depression, the sociopath, someone raised in a culture that has deadened her imagination — and if internalism has to say something revisionary there, is that a bug or just the price of the theory? What about the fact that the view makes reasons relative to an agent's contingent starting points, which seems to undercut the categorical character of moral requirements: can an internalist still say you ought not to do that, period, to someone with the wrong motivational set, and if they can't, is that a defeat or a clarification? And does any of the recent empirical work on reasoning, psychopathy, or imaginative resistance count as evidence against the view, rather than just as a new way of dressing up old counterexamples? I want the defeaters sorted from fatal to merely awkward, and an honest verdict on whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and if it has, whether that's a problem for a view whose point was never empirical.
