I have been trying to think through something that bothers me about the way governments evaluate long-term projects, and I would like help getting clear on what exactly the practice commits them to. When a government decides whether to build a flood barrier, a high-speed rail line, or a program to reduce emissions, it does not simply add up the costs and benefits as they occur over time. It converts future costs and benefits into present-day values by applying a discount rate, often a few percent per year, so that a life saved or an illness prevented fifty years from now counts for much less in the calculation than the same outcome today. Some of this I can understand as a technical matter: prices change, and if future people are richer than we are, a given amount of money may matter less to them. But the standard way of defending such a rate treats it as having a second part, sometimes called pure time preference, which is not about inflation and not about rising wealth. It is a penalty on the future simply for being future, a rate at which future wellbeing is counted for less the later it occurs.

What I want to know is what this practice presupposes about the moral status of people who do not yet exist. Does a government that discounts future wellbeing in this way thereby presuppose that a person's welfare matters less the later that person is born, or that the interests of the present generation simply weigh more than the interests of future generations? Or can the practice be read more narrowly, as only tracking the opportunity cost of capital and the diminishing usefulness of money to richer people, with no stance on whether later people count equally? I am also struck by an apparent inconsistency: many of the same governments that use such a rate also describe themselves, in other official contexts, as acting on behalf of future generations and as treating all citizens as equals. Are these parts of the practice consistent with one another? Finally, if we insisted that the mere passage of time is not by itself a reason to value a benefit less, what would the practice have to change, and which parts of it could remain intact?
