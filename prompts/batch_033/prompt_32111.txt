I'm taking a graduate seminar on fairness in machine learning, and we've spent three weeks on the impossibility results — the ones showing that calibration across groups, equal false positive rates, and equal false negative rates cannot all hold simultaneously when base rates differ and the predictor is imperfect. My professor keeps pressing us with the same question: "Fine, you can't have everything. So what do you actually want?" And I've realized I don't have a clear answer, because I don't have a clear list of what a fairness criterion is supposed to do in the first place.

I want you to write the desiderata for a criterion of algorithmic fairness, taking the impossibility results as fixed constraints on the space of possible criteria. Not a list of the statistical properties people have proposed — calibration, demographic parity, equalized odds, and so on are candidates, not desiderata. I want the deeper requirements that would let us evaluate those candidates: what must any adequate fairness criterion achieve, what trade-offs is it allowed to make, and what would disqualify it from counting as a fairness criterion at all? For each requirement, give the motivating case or consideration that makes it a requirement, and say whether it is non-negotiable or merely a preference of one camp. Then identify which subsets of your desiderata cannot be jointly satisfied given the impossibility results — that is the analytical core of the exercise. Finally, score the major families of criteria (demographic parity, equalized odds, calibration-based views, and any others you think are contenders) against your list, but keep the scoring brief — the weight belongs on the desiderata themselves.
