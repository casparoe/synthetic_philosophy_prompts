Here are my intuitions about when moral deliberation goes wrong by introducing what Bernard Williams called "one thought too many" — the thought that inserts itself between a personal commitment and the action it directly motivates, treating the commitment as something that needs an impartial justification. I am trying to figure out whether there is a principle that captures all of these verdicts, and if not, which ones I should reconsider.

1. A man's wife is drowning alongside a stranger. He dives in and saves her without hesitating. Had he paused to reason that saving her is permissible because special obligations are justified on impartial grounds, that would have been one thought too many.

2. A doctor is triaging patients after a disaster. One of the injured is her husband. She feels the pull to treat him first but forces herself to assess everyone impartially by medical urgency. This deliberation does not strike me as one thought too many — it feels appropriate, even obligatory.

3. A firefighter arrives at a burning house and learns his own child and a neighbour's child are trapped in different rooms. He runs to his child's room without deliberating. I think he should not have to deliberate, and if he did, that would be one thought too many.

4. A judge's son is the defendant in a criminal trial. The judge recuses herself. The deliberation about whether she can be impartial seems not only appropriate but required. Not one thought too many.

5. Someone is deciding which charity to donate to. Her mother died of a rare disease, and one charity funds research into it. She feels a pull toward that charity but wonders whether she should give to a more effective one instead. She deliberates. This does not feel like one thought too many — at least, not in the way the husband's deliberation would have been.

6. A close friend asks you to lie to protect a secret that, if revealed, would embarrass them but harm no one else. You feel you should do it out of loyalty, but you pause to consider whether lying is justified here. I am torn: part of me thinks this pause is one thought too many, and part of me thinks it is just ordinary moral reflection.

Is there a single principle that distinguishes cases where deliberation is alienating from cases where it is simply responsible moral thought? What does that principle say about the sixth case, and about cases I have not listed — for instance, a parent deciding whether to leave their entire estate to their own children rather than to famine relief? And which of my verdicts, if any, does the principle force me to abandon?
