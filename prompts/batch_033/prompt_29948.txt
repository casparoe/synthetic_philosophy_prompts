I've spent the last several years fairly convinced that the cross-cultural study of philosophical intuitions lands a serious blow against the way much of philosophy is actually practiced. The position I have been defending, roughly, is this: a wide range of the intuitions philosophers treat as data — about reference, knowledge, intentional action, moral responsibility, free will, and similar matters — are not invariant deliverances of a shared conceptual competence but vary systematically with culture, language, or other demographic facts; and because they vary in this way, the standard practice of treating a philosopher's own considered intuition as evidence about the concept itself needs either to be abandoned or heavily restricted. Last night I had a long argument with a friend who is a committed universalist. His line was that the cross-cultural findings are largely noise: translation artifacts, demand characteristics, non-expert respondents misapplying concepts they only partially grasp, and failures to replicate the headline results. He also pressed a more interesting point, namely that even where intuitions genuinely differ, that does not by itself show intuition is unreliable; it shows only that some intuitions are local, and the right response is to identify which intuitions are universal and lean only on those, or to refine the experimental methods until the shared underlying competence shows through.

I want to be moved by the strongest version of his case, not a strawman of it. So my question is: what would count against the variationist position as I have stated it? I would like a map of its exposure. In particular: which findings or arguments would be decisive against it, and which would be merely awkward? What would a thoughtful defender of the variationist position themselves concede as counting against it — for instance, which replication failures, which robust demonstrations of cross-cultural invariance, which evidence that the observed variation tracks philosophically irrelevant features of the experimental setup rather than the target concept? I would also like you to keep separate the descriptive claim that intuitions vary and the methodological claim that this variation matters for how philosophy should be done, since my friend insisted these are separable and I suspect he is partly right. Are there escape routes that preserve the descriptive variation but defang its methodological bite — restricting reliance to universally shared intuitions, deferring to expert judgment, or treating an intuition as evidence only about the intuiter's own conceptual scheme? And finally, be honest about whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability: is there any observation that could realistically have come back a certain way and that we would now regard as refuting it, or has it become a thesis that absorbs every failed replication as simply more variation? If it has been insulated in that way, tell me whether that is a problem for a position of this kind. What would personally persuade me most, if I wanted to keep the position honest, is a concrete list of the empirical results that would have to come out differently for me to give it up.
