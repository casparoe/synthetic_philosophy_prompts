so I've been reading about theories of causation and I keep tripping over this thing in how science actually gets done. in medicine and increasingly in social science, the randomized controlled trial is treated as the gold standard for establishing causation — like if you don't have an rct people say you haven't really *proven* it's causal. but then we also have cases where everyone's confident about causation without one: smoking and lung cancer, the effect of CO2 on temperature, etc. my question is what theory of causation this practice presupposes. it seems to fit really naturally with interventionist theories (woodward, manipulability) where causation just is about what happens when you intervene. but can a counterfactual theory (lewis-style) explain why randomization is so epistemically special, or does it end up saying rcts are just one way among many to access counterfactual truths and the priveleging of them is a kind of fetish? and then the flip side: if the practice really does presuppose interventionism, what does that mean for the causal claims we accept from good observational studies — are we being inconsistent, or can interventionism handle those too? basically i want to know whether the methodological norms of causal inference are philosophically loaded in a way that rules out certain theories of causation, or whether they're compatible with several views and the apparent loading is just that.
