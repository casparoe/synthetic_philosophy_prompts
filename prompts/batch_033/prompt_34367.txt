I recently read a long-form essay on rational choice theory that offered the following summary of the case for expected utility maximisation. The author argued that the coherence arguments work like this: if your preferences satisfy a minimal consistency condition — basically, if they are not flatly self-contradictory — then a representation theorem shows that they can be expressed as maximising expected utility with respect to some probability distribution and some utility function. So, the author claimed, you are already an expected utility maximiser whether you realise it or not; the only remaining question is which probabilities and utilities you ought to have. And if you try to resist by violating the axioms, the Dutch book argument kicks in: a clever bookie can offer you a series of bets, each of which you will accept given your preferences, but which together guarantee you a net loss. Since voluntarily walking into a sure loss is obviously irrational, the axioms are non-negotiable. The essay then concluded that these two results — the representation theorem and the Dutch book argument — jointly establish that any coherent agent must maximise expected utility, full stop, and that behavioural economics therefore poses no challenge to the normative standing of expected utility theory, because the coherence arguments are about what rationality demands, not about how people in fact reason.

I found this line of thought compelling but I am aware that these arguments have been picked over for decades by philosophers and economists, and I suspect the summary smooths over some distinctions that matter. I would like you to assess how fair it is. Please go through the claims systematically and tell me which parts a careful defender of coherence arguments would endorse, which parts overstate or conflate distinct theses, and whether the conclusion about behavioural economics survives once the accurate parts are separated from the inaccurate ones. I would like the response as a numbered list of exactly five points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion, and please use British English throughout.
