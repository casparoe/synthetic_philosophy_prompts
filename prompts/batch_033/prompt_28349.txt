Two friends of mine, Nadia and Eli, got into an argument about a third friend, Marco, whose preferences are, by his own admission, cyclical: over three coffee mugs, he prefers the blue one to the green one, the green one to the red one, and the red one to the blue one. (The objects don't matter; only the structure does.) Neither could settle it, and they asked me to referee, but I couldn't do it on the spot. Here is the exchange.

(1) Eli: Marco's preferences are irrational. Anyone with a cycle like that is a walking money pump. Start him with the red mug and offer him the green one for the red one plus a dollar; he takes it, because he prefers green to red. Offer him the blue one for the green one plus a dollar; he takes it, because he prefers blue to green. Offer him the red one for the blue one plus a dollar; he takes it, because he prefers red to blue. He ends up with the red mug he started with and is three dollars poorer. Any preference structure that lets a trader harvest money from you while returning you to your starting point is defective.

(2) Nadia: That's only true if Marco is myopic. If he sees the sequence coming—or even just knows his own preferences and suspects what the trader is doing—he can refuse the trades. A foresighted chooser with cyclical preferences won't be pumped; he'll keep the red mug and his money. So the pump doesn't show that the preferences are irrational. It shows he'd be irrational if he failed to think ahead. Preferences and foresight can coexist.

(3) Eli: That makes things worse, not better. To avoid the pump, Marco has to refuse a trade—red for green plus a dollar—that he would accept if it were offered on its own. So his choice depends on what else is on the menu, not just on what he prefers. But a preference is supposed to be a stable ranking that governs choice across contexts. If "Marco prefers green to red" doesn't tell you that he'll choose green over red when he is fully informed, then he doesn't really have a preference at all; he has a disposition that appears and disappears with the framing.

(4) Nadia: You're conflating two different choices. In the sequence, accepting the first trade is not choosing green over red; it is choosing "green, plus the future trades you can foresee, plus the fees." Marco can consistently prefer green to red while preferring "red and no trades" to "red minus three dollars after a full cycle." Those are not the same pair of options, so there is no conflict. You are treating a choice made as part of a plan as if it were a pairwise choice.

(5) Eli: Then what does his pairwise preference amount to? If it does not commit him to taking green over red when he is fully informed about his options, it does not guide choice at all. And notice why he is in this bind: the only reason he refuses the first trade is that he foresees a sequence of trades, each of which he prefers, leaving him worse off. With transitive preferences that cannot happen—a chain of preferred trades must leave you with something you prefer. So the cycle itself, not myopia, is what forces him to choose between following his preferences and avoiding a loss. That is a defect in the preferences themselves.

(6) Nadia: I accept the diagnostic point: transitivity would block the pump. But "would block a loss in this scenario" is not the same as "is required by rationality." Being generous is costly, and being risk-averse forgoes gains, but neither is irrational. The money pump shows that cyclic preferences can be exploited; it does not yet show that the preferences themselves, as opposed to myopic acting on them, are irrational. For that you would need to show that a fully foresighted chooser with cyclic preferences still fails by his own lights, and you have not done that.

(7) Eli: Here is the failure by his own lights. A foresighted Marco must refuse a trade he prefers whenever it is embedded in a sequence he disprefers. So his evaluations are fragmented: he ranks the pieces one way and the whole another way, and foresight does not reconcile them—it just lets him step around the conflict. Rationality is not only about avoiding losses; it is about having a unified standpoint from which your choices and your preferences hang together. A cycle rules that out. The money pump is not the whole argument; it is a vivid symptom of the underlying problem.

Please adjudicate this move by move. For each of the seven moves, say whether it is clearly mistaken, partly right, or clearly right, and briefly justify that rating. Also say what, if anything, both of them are missing, and who is more right overall.
