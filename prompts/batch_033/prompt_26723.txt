I have been teaching the personal identity unit in my metaphysics course for years, and I have gradually noticed that my own intuitive verdicts on the standard cases do not fit neatly into any of the theories I present to my students. I would like you to help me work out what principle, if any, they add up to. Here are the cases and what I find myself thinking about them.

1. **The standard teletransporter.** I enter a scanner on Earth; the machine records the exact state and arrangement of every particle in my body, transmits the blueprint to a receiver on Mars, and assembles a molecule-for-molecule replica there from new matter. The scanning process destroys my original body. The person who steps out on Mars has all my memories, personality traits, intentions, and emotional attachments. I believe that person is me, and I would have no hesitation about stepping into the machine.

2. **The branching teletransporter.** Everything is the same as in case 1, except that the machine malfunctions and fails to destroy my original body. Now there are two: the original me on Earth and the replica on Mars, both psychologically continuous with the pre-scan me. I still believe the replica is me — it seems perverse that the mere failure of a destruct mechanism would make the replica someone else. But now there are apparently two of me, which is difficult to make sense of. My inclination is to say that I survive as both, though I admit I do not know quite what that amounts to.

3. **Gradual neural replacement.** Over the course of a year, a neurosurgeon replaces each of my hundred billion neurons, one by one, with a silicon artificial neuron that performs exactly the same input-output function as the biological neuron it replaces. After each operation I wake up and am plainly the same person I was before. At the end of the year my brain is entirely silicon, and the person with that brain has all my memories and character. I believe I survive throughout the process, and the person at the end is me.

4. **Hemisphere transplant.** My brain is surgically removed and divided down the corpus callosum. Each cerebral hemisphere is transplanted into a different previously brainless body. Both resulting people are awake and conscious; both have my memories, my personality, and a sense of being me, though they differ somewhat in cognitive style — one is more verbal, the other more visuospatial. I do not believe either of them is me. The original me has ceased to exist, and two new people have come into being. This verdict feels firm and non-negotiable.

5. **Total psychological disruption.** I suffer a catastrophic brain injury that obliterates all my episodic memories, radically transforms my personality, and severs every project and attachment I previously had. The person who emerges shares my body and some basic skills and temperamental dispositions, but there is no psychological continuity of the sort Parfit describes. I believe this person is me — the same living human animal — but I also believe that nearly everything that mattered in my survival has been lost. Identity and what-matters have come apart.

6. **The spectrum pressure.** Consider a series of cases from case 3: at the near end, one neuron is replaced; at the far end, all neurons are replaced at once and the original body is destroyed — which is just the teletransporter from case 1. At every step, it seems I survive. Since survival is transitive, I survive at the far end, which matches my verdict in case 1. But now imagine a parallel spectrum for the hemisphere transplant: at the near end, a single neuron is transplanted from my brain to a new body; at the far end, each hemisphere is transplanted whole. If I survive the near end and survival is transitive, I survive the far end — yet my verdict in case 4 was that I do not. I cannot see what stops the spectrum argument from going through in both cases, and this troubles me.

Here is what I want from you. First, formulate a principle — or a small set of candidate principles — that captures as many of these verdicts as possible. State it precisely, and for each clause or condition, indicate which of my cases motivates it. Second, apply your principle to a case I have not described: suppose I undergo the gradual replacement from case 3, but the biological neurons removed along the way are kept alive in a nutrient bath and, at the end of the year, are reassembled into a functioning brain and body. Does that person count as me, and if so, how does your principle handle the fact that the silicon-brain person also counts as me? Third, tell me honestly which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and whether you think I should revise that verdict or complicate the principle to save it.
