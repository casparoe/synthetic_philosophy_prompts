I've been reporting on end-of-life care and dementia for a few years now, and I've ended up pretty convinced that advance directives should carry real weight — that when someone writes down what they want while they're still competent, that should bind what happens to them later, even if the later version of them seems happy enough or has different preferences. The earlier self knew what they were signing up for; the later self, especially in dementia cases, might not even grasp what's at stake. So I guess my position is: the earlier self has the authority, and we should respect that.

But I know this is a live debate in philosophy and bioethics, and I want to know what I'm missing. What would count against this view? I'm not looking for the standard "but what if they change their mind" objection — I mean the deeper stuff. What cases, thought experiments, or arguments should actually move me off this position? Which ones would be fatal versus just awkward? And am I insulating myself from counterevidence by the way I'm framing this — like, have I set things up so that no possible outcome could really count against the earlier self's authority? If the whole framing is off — if "earlier self vs. later self" is the wrong way to think about it, or if the very idea of binding a future person runs into problems I'm not seeing — tell me that straight up. I'd rather get the question reframed than get a polite answer to a confused one.
