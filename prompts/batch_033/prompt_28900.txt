So I've gotten myself into a slightly ridiculous situation. I'm a novelist — midlist literary fiction, you wouldn't know my name — and for the last three years I've been working on a book about how novelists actually think about concepts when they're writing. Along the way I fell into the cognitive science literature on concepts — prototype theory, theory-theory, conceptual atomism, all of it — and I kept feeling like none of it quite captured the slipperiness I experience at the desk. Then I found Hofstadter's Copycat project and the whole fluid-concepts framework, and it felt like someone had finally described the thing I do. So I wrote an essay arguing that the fluid-concepts model — where concepts are not stable representations but emergent, context-sensitive, slippage-prone structures built on the fly by analogy-making — is a better account of what literary artists are doing when they manipulate concepts than any of the standard philosophical theories.

The audience will be mostly philosophers of mind and cognitive psychologists. My core argument, roughly, is this: when a novelist revises a character over successive drafts, or when an unexpected metaphor reorganizes the conceptual space of a scene, what's happening isn't prototype-shifting or belief-revision — it's the kind of fluid, analogy-driven conceptual slippage that Copycat models in its letter-string microdomain. The concept isn't being retrieved from storage and updated; it's being built on the fly, under pressure from neighboring concepts in the narrative, the way "abc → abd" pressures "ijk" toward "ijl" but also toward "ijd" depending on which conceptual dimensions the system has made salient.
