So I'm writing this novel — near-future, a startup offers "gradual uploading": they replace one neuron at a time with a nanotech artificial neuron that perfectly replicates the input-output function. You stay conscious the whole time, on a hospital bed. After each replacement they ask if you still feel like you. You do. After a few million replacements — all of them — you're running on silicon. The company calls this "you, transferred."

Here's the thing. I was trying to make the science feel rigorous so I read up on the philosophy, and now I've talked myself into a corner that makes my plot fall apart. The argument goes like this:

(1) Replacing a single neuron with a functionally identical artificial one doesn't destroy personal identity — you're still you after one swap. (If it did, we'd lose our identity constantly from normal cell turnover.)

(2) If you're still you after n replacements, you're still you after n+1 replacements — one more neuron swap cant be the magic threshold where you suddenly become a different person or a philosophical zombie.

(3) So by induction, you're still you after all your neurons have been replaced. The upload is you.

(4) But now here's the problem. Suppose the original biological neurons, instead of being destroyed, are carefully preserved and then reassembled into a working brain. By the exact same reasoning — each neuron is just a functional unit, swapping one back in doesn't change who you are — the reassembled biological brain is also you.

(5) So now there are two yous. But personal identity isn't the kind of thing that can branch: one person can't be identical to two distinct future people. That violates transitivity of identity.

(6) So either the induction step (2) is wrong — there IS some threshold where you stop being you, which seems crazy because what could possibly make neuron #4,382,901 special — or the whole gradual-replacement picture of survival is busted, which means my novel's premise is nonsense.

I want to keep premise (1) and the general idea that gradual replacement preserves identity — that's the whole emotional core of the book, the idea that you can cross over without dying. But I can't see how to block the branching problem without just handwaving. Where does this argument actually go wrong, and what's the least philosophically embarassing way to write my way out of it?
