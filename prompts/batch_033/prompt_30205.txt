I run a small book club — mostly neighbors and coworkers, nobody with a philosophy background — and next month we're doing Lois Lowry's The Giver. I know it usually gets read as a book about a controlled society, but rereading it I keep getting stuck on the way it treats color. In Jonas's world color literally doesn't exist; people live in something the book calls Sameness. When Jonas sees the apple change, and later when the Giver transmits memories of color and sunlight and snow to him, he isn't really learning a new fact — he's getting something the community's whole way of talking can't even describe.

It reminded me of that thought experiment people call Mary's room: Mary knows every physical fact about color but has never actually seen it, and the question is whether she learns something new when she walks out and sees red for the first time. I think philosophers call it the knowledge argument. I want to read The Giver through that lens: does the novel dramatize the idea that there's something about color experience that no description or scientific fact can capture? Where does the analogy hold, and where does it break? Jonas doesn't know all the physical facts about color — his community suppresses them — and what he receives from the Giver are memories of someone else's experiences, not a fresh seeing of his own. Does that make him a worse Mary or a better one? And does the book take a side on whether what Jonas gains is a new kind of knowledge, or just a new ability, or something else?

Our group will split between people who read it as dystopia and people who read it as philosophy, so I'd like a reading that's fair to the book and fair to the thought experiment, one that doesnt flatten either to fit the other. Also, because we'll argue about this, please say how confident you are in the reading, and point out the one interpretive claim you're least sure of.
