I'm a novelist, and I've been struggling with a craft problem that I think runs deeper than craft. I'm hoping you can help me think it through by way of a novel I keep returning to.

I'd like you to read Dostoevsky's Notes from Underground through the lens of philosophical debates about the reliability of introspection. The underground man spends the entire novella examining his own motives — his spite, his vanity, his refusal to consult a doctor about his liver — and he seems to know himself with excruciating precision. But the more he introspects, the less he acts, and when he does act (the disastrous dinner with the schoolfellows, the cruelty toward Liza), his behaviour surprises even him. His introspection doesn't produce self-knowledge that guides action; it produces paralysis, and then, after the fact, it produces rationalisations that he himself seems to recognise as rationalisations.

I'm aware there is a philosophical literature on whether introspection is a reliable source of knowledge about one's own mental states — some argue it is direct and authoritative, others that it is constructive, inferential, or prone to confabulation. I'd like you to read the novella through that debate. Specifically: where does the underground man's introspection succeed by the standards philosophers have proposed, and where does it fail? Does the novella dramatise a kind of failure that the philosophical theories don't adequately capture — something about introspection not merely being unreliable but being itself a motivated act, a way of avoiding rather than achieving self-knowledge? And as a practitioner: what does Dostoevsky do on the page, technically, that a philosopher's account of introspection would have trouble describing? I'm less interested in which philosopher Dostoevsky anticipates than in what the novel shows that the arguments miss.

Please write your answer in British English.
