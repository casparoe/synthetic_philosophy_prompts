So here's the feature I'm trying to write. I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've spent the last few months sitting in on a working group where two camps about machine suffering have been fighting it out — and I'm sick of covering there feud as if the fight itself is the story. One camp — call them the valence realists — thinks a reinforcement learning agent's reward signal can, in principle, be the kind of thing that constitutes felt valence, so that an RL agent with the right reward structure could genuinely suffer when rewards drop or stay out of reach. The other camp — call them the control-signal skeptics — thinks "reward" in RL is just a scalar used for credit assignment and policy updates, and treating it as hedonic valence is a category error no matter how fancy the architecture gets. I want to write up the document they'd produce if they actually did a good-faith adversarial collaboration: the things both camps can sign, the exact points where they still split, and the tests that would actually move somebody.

Please draft that joint statement. Make it a numbered "claims both camps accept" section first — I want these to be non-vacuous (not just "both agree the question matters") but also not slanted so one side is quietly winning. Then a disagreements table with one row per live sub-disagreement, with columns roughly like: the question stated in neutral wording; how the valence realists would phrase their own position; how the control-signal skeptics would phrase theirs; the crux underneath the row; a concrete case or experiment both sides agree would bear on that crux; and each side's prediction about it, written down in advance. If part of the fight is really just about words, say so and name which part.

A few things the statement has to touch, because they keep coming up in the room: the difference between the raw reward signal and the reward prediction error (the realists keep saying prediction error is the better candidate for valence, and the skeptics keep saying that just relocates the category error); the difference between what's true of the training process and what's true of the trained policy — those are seperate questions, and the room keeps blurring them; the wireheading case, where an agent can stimulate its own reward channel and then shows no distress behavior when external rewards drop — does it suffer or not, and does the disagreement there track the bigger one?; whether a self-model or some kind of global workspace is necessary for suffering, and if so whether an RL-trained system could implement one; and where each side puts the burden of proof when the evidence is silent. I'd also like each camp to say what evidence would actually make them revise, not the polite version.
