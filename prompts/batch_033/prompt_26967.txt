So I've been working on this novel for a couple years now — it's about a former quant who quits his hedge fund after a breakdown and moves to a small desert town to, I don't know, escape the noise. But the thing he can't escape is the way his brain has been trained to see every decision as an expected-value calculation. He ends up running an underground poker room out of a laundromat, and the book is really about whether there are situations where the whole EV framework just... gives out. Like, not just gives the wrong answer, but gives no answer at all.

Anyway, in researching this I fell down a rabbit hole about the Pasadena paradox (Nover and Hájek, 2004) and it kind of blew my mind. The St. Petersburg paradox I already knew about — infinite expected value, sure, weird but you can patch it with diminishing marginal utility. But Pasadena is different. The game is: flip a fair coin until you get heads. If it lands heads on the nth flip, you win $2^n/n if n is odd, and you lose $2^n/n if n is even. The expected value is a conditionally convergent series — the alternating harmonic series, basically, but with terms that grow — and by rearranging the order of summation you can make it converge to literally any real number, or diverge to positive or negative infinity. The expected value is undefined. Not infinite, undefined. The math just shrugs.

I ended up writing a short essay — more like extended notes for myself — trying to work out what this means for my character. The essay argues that the Pasadena paradox is actually more interesting as a literary predicament than as a technical problem in decision theory. The technical response (reject it as pathological, or ban conditionally convergent series from the domain of admissible gambles) misses what I think is the real point: the paradox dramatizes a moment where a person's entire framework for making sense of choice collapses, and that moment is existentially interesting. My character has spent his life believing that for any decision, you can enumerate the possibilities, weight them, and pick the best. The Pasadena game shows — rigorously — that there are coherently describable situations where this procedure simply does not yield a result. The question isn't "what should you do?" but "what does it feel like to face a decision that your entire cognitive apparatus cannot process?" That's the novel.

I'd love comments on the essay — not on the math (I'm pretty sure I got it right, though if I bungled the payoff formula somewhere please flag it), but on whether the philosophical move I'm making is actually interesting or just pretentious. Specifically: am I right that the standard decision-theoretic responses to Pasadena sidestep the existential dimension, or am I just ignoring the replies because I want the paradox to be deeper than it is? And is there a danger in using a toy example from decision theory to carry this much thematic weight — does it feel forced, the way novels about math sometimes do? Be brutal, I can take it.
