I'm grading a final exam for an upper-level course in modal logic and philosophy of religion, and I've just come from a long argument with a friend about whether Gödel's proof is anything more than Anselm with modal machinery attached. I'd rather that argument not do my grading for me, so I want a cold assessment of the following answer.

Exam question: Reconstruct Gödel's modal ontological argument step by step. In doing so, explain (i) what a positive property is and what the two positivity axioms say, (ii) how the argument derives the possible existence of a God-like being, and (iii) how it moves from that possibility to necessary existence, identifying the modal principles used. Conclude by stating and briefly evaluating the strongest objection to the argument.

Student answer: Gödel's argument is a modal descendant of Anselm's, formalized by Dana Scott from Gödel's unpublished notes. Gödel defines a positive property as a property it is better to have than to lack, and lays down two principles: any property entailed by a positive property is positive, and exactly one of a property and its negation is positive. A being is God-like if and only if it has every positive property. Gödel takes God-likeness itself to be positive, and necessary existence to be positive. The proof first shows that every positive property is possibly instantiated: if a positive property P were impossible, P would entail every property, so by the first principle every property would be positive, contradicting the second. Since God-likeness is positive, a God-like being possibly exists. Next, if x is God-like, then every property x has is positive; otherwise the negation of some property x has would be positive, and x would have both a property and its negation. So God-likeness entails every property x has, and is therefore the essence of x. Since necessary existence is positive, x has necessary existence; and because God-likeness is x's essence, this yields that some God-like being exists in every possible world. From the possible existence of a God-like being together with its necessary existence, we infer that a God-like being exists necessarily and hence actually. The modal principle used in this last step is that the possible necessity of a proposition entails its necessity, and this holds in every normal modal logic. The strongest objection is that the positivity axioms are under-motivated: Gödel gives no independent criterion for why God-likeness or necessary existence should count as positive, so the proof rearranges a contested assumption rather than earning its conclusion. I think the objection succeeds, because someone who rejects the conclusion can simply reject the positivity axioms, and the proof gives them no reason not to.
