I'm a PhD student working at the intersection of moral philosophy and the philosophy of action. I've constructed an argument that seems to show that if Frankfurt-style counterexamples succeed against the Principle of Alternative Possibilities, then by parallel reasoning they also defeat the principle that coercion undermines moral responsibility. The conclusion strikes me as unacceptable, but I cannot find the flaw in the reasoning. I would appreciate a careful diagnosis.

Here is the argument:

(1) In a standard Frankfurt case, an agent is morally responsible for φ-ing despite lacking the ability to do otherwise, because the counterfactual intervener never actually intervenes: the agent φ-s on the basis of their own practical reasoning, and the mere fact that the intervener would have forced φ-ing had the agent shown signs of deciding otherwise does not diminish the agent's responsibility.

(2) Now consider a coercion case with the same structure. A mugger threatens Smith: "Your money or your life." Smith hands over his wallet. But suppose Smith, unbeknownst to the mugger, had already resolved moments before to give his wallet to the next person he met — he has just decided to divest himself of all possessions. The mugger's threat plays no causal role in Smith's decision; Smith acts on his own reasons, exactly as Jones does in the Frankfurt case.

(3) Smith could not have done otherwise than hand over the wallet: had he shown signs of resisting, the mugger would have forced compliance, by shooting or by taking the wallet by force. Yet the mugger's coercive power, like Black's device, remains counterfactual — it never actually constrains Smith's will.

(4) If the mere counterfactual presence of an intervener does not defeat responsibility in the Frankfurt case, then by parity of reasoning it should not defeat responsibility in the coercion case. Smith is fully responsible for handing over the wallet, and the fact that he acted under a threat is irrelevant to his moral responsibility.

(5) But now generalize: on this view, whether coercion undermines responsibility always depends on whether the coercive threat actually figured in the agent's practical reasoning. If the agent would have performed the same action anyway, the coercion is responsibility-irrelevant. This entails that the standard legal and moral doctrine — that a credible threat of serious harm removes or diminishes responsibility for the compelled action regardless of the agent's antecedent motivations — is false.

(6) That is an intolerable result. It would mean, for instance, that a defendant who complies with a blackmailer's demand cannot appeal to duress if it turns out they secretly wanted to do what was demanded anyway. The coercion defense would become an empirical question about the defendant's private motivations rather than a normative question about what it is reasonable to expect someone to withstand.

(7) Therefore, either premise (1) is false — Frankfurt cases do not in fact show that responsibility survives the removal of alternative possibilities — or there is a morally relevant asymmetry between Frankfurt-style interveners and coercers that blocks the parity reasoning at step (4).

I am inclined to think the asymmetry must be located in the fact that the mugger issues a threat meant to act on Smith's will, even if it contingently fails to do so, whereas Black's device is not a communicative act at all — it merely monitors and intervenes. But I am not confident that this difference can bear the normative weight required, since in both cases the actual causal path from the agent's reasons to the action is identical. If you reject a different premise, which one, and what does rejecting it cost? If you accept the asymmetry I have suggested, can you articulate it in a way that does not also threaten the original Frankfurt intuition?
