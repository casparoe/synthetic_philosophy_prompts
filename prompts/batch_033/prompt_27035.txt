So I've been working on a piece about animal sentience and AI ethics and I keep running into the same problem: I can't figure out what I actually think about who counts. I've started keeping a list of my gut reactions to specific cases, and I'm hoping someone can help me reverse-engineer whatever principle is lurking behind them.

Here are my verdicts, as honestly as I can report them:

- Honeybee: I think it's wrong to pull the wings off one for fun. But I'd swat one that got in the car without losing sleep. I don't think a bee's life has the same weight as a mouse's, but I'm not sure it's zero either.
- Mouse: Definitely wrong to torture one. I'd kill one in a trap if it was in my kitchen, but I'd feel a little bad about it, and I'd pay extra for a trap that killed instantly rather than slowly.
- Octopus: Basically the same as the mouse, maybe slightly more weighty? But I'm not sure why. I've read the stuff about their intelligence and it moves me but I'm not sure it should.
- Thermostat: Zero. I don't owe anything to a thermostat. Unplugging it isn't wrong, even if it's a Nest that "learns" my schedule.
- A large language model that passes the Turing test and begs me not to turn it off, in a way that's indistinguishable from a human begging: My gut says I still get to turn it off. But I'm uncomfortable about how confidently I feel that. If I'm wrong about this one, the mistake is enormous.
- A human fetus at 8 weeks: I don't think it has interests yet in the way the mouse does. The mouse seems more morally significant to me at that stage, which I know is a weird thing to say and I wouldn't lead with it in the piece, but it's what my gut reports.
- A human fetus at 32 weeks: This one's harder. I think it probably does have interests, maybe something like the mouse, maybe more. I'd want a very good reason to end its life, and "I'd rather not be pregnant" probably isn't enough by that point.
- A plant: I don't think pulling up a dandelion is wrong. But I also think there's something slightly off about someone who takes pleasure in destroying a ancient tree for no reason. I don't think the tree is sentient, but I'm not sure sentience covers everything I care about.
- A person in a dreamless sleep: Full moral status, obviously. But at that moment they have no experiences at all. Whatever grounds their status, it's not current sentience.
- A digital simulation of a mouse brain, running on a supercomputer, that behaves exactly like a real mouse in every observable way: I want to say it matters less than the real mouse, but I can't articulate why without appealing to biology, and I'm not sure biology should matter.

Is there a unified principle — or at least a small set of principles — that captures most of these verdicts? I'm especially stuck on the gap between the thermostat and the bee, and on the sleeping person versus the early fetus. And if the principle forces me to give up one or two of these verdicts, which ones should go, and why?
