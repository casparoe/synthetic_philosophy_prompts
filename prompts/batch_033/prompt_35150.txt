Build me a thought experiment to this spec: a realistic, one-paragraph case in which a proposition functions as a hinge commitment for an agent in roughly the Wittgensteinian sense — it stands fast, is exempt from doubt, structures the agents inquiries — while the agent also has genuinely strong, citable evidence for that very proposition and would happily produce it if asked. The case is for a handout on hinge epistemology, so don't just stipulate that the proposition is a hinge; the hinge status should fall out of how the agent actually lives and inquires. Constraints: ordinary life, no philosophers, and steer clear of the stock examples about hands or the external world. After the scenario, walk through what each of the following would say about the case, and why: (a) a hinge epistemologist of the non-doxastic sort, who says hinge certainties are not beliefs and are not known, whatever evidence happens to be lying around; and (b) a more doxastic sort, who says hinge commitments are just deeply entrenched empirical beliefs, so with evidence like this they should count as known. Then say which feature of the case is doing the work to let (a) grant the evidence and still deny knowledge, and what (b) would point to as the real difference between this agent's attitude and a normal evidence-based belief. Finish with how a defender of (a) would answer the charge that they've just defined hinge so that no amount of evidence could ever count.
