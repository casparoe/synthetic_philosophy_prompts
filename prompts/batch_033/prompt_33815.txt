Ok so I've been going down a rabbit hole on population ethics and there's this argument that's been bugging me for weeks. It feels like it has to be wrong somewhere but I can't figure out where, and every fix seems to create a different problem. Maybe you can help me pinpoint it.

Start with a world of a billion people living amazing lives — call it World A. Utility level 100, everyone's flourishing.

Now consider World A+: same billion people at 100, plus an extra trillion people with lives that are just barely worth living — utility level 0.001. The original billion are completely unaffected.

(1) A+ is at least as good as A. (Why wouldn't it be? You're adding happy lives without hurting anyone. It would be weird to say the world got worse.)

Next, consider World B: same total population as A+ (billion + trillion), same total utility, but redistributed so everyone's at the same level — roughly (1B×100 + 1T×0.001)/(1B+1T) ≈ 0.1.

(2) B is at least as good as A+. (Same number of people, same total happiness, but spread more fairly. If anything, B seems better, right?)

(3) "At least as good as" is transitive.

So by (1), (2), and (3), B is at least as good as A. That means a world where everyone lives at utility 0.1 — a life barely distinguishable from nothing — is at least as good as a world where everyone lives at utility 100. That's already wild.

But it gets worse. From B, add another huge batch of barely-happy people, equalize again, and repeat. By transitivity you eventually get to a world with an incomprehensibly large population where every single person lives a life that's just a hair above zero. And this is "at least as good" as World A. That's the repugnant conclusion.

So something's gotta give. People who work on this usually reject (1), (2), or (3). Rejecting (3) — transitivity — seems crazy for a "better than" relation. Rejecting (2) means you're fine with massive inequality even when total welfare is the same. Rejecting (1) means you think adding happy lives can make the world worse, and you need a theory of when and why.

Which one would you reject, and what's the bullet you have to bite? And if you go with a different view entirely — like average utilitarianism or person-affecting views — what's the cost of that move?

Keep it to a page max — I want the diagnosis, not a survey article.
