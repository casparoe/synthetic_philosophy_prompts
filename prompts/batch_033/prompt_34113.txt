I'm writing a paper on what Daniel Dennett called Darwin's and Turing's "strange inversions of reasoning" — the idea that competence (the ability to do something well, to produce the right output or behaviour) can arise without comprehension (understanding what one is doing, why one is doing it, or what it all means). Darwin inverted the intuitive order by showing that exquisite biological design can come from a mindless, algorithmic process of variation and selection rather than from an intelligent designer. Turing inverted it by showing that intelligent-seeming behaviour can come from a mechanical process of rule-governed symbol manipulation without any understanding on the part of the machine. The two inversions have a common structure, and in the decades since, a growing catalogue of examples — from insect navigation and slime mould problem-solving to large language models producing fluent prose — has seemed to extend the pattern. But I am not convinced we yet have a rigorous general account of what the inversion commits us to, what it rules out, and where (if anywhere) it stops. I want to step back from the historical figures and the individual examples and ask: what would an adequate general theory of competence without comprehension actually have to deliver? Before I commit to any particular account, I want the full specification of requirements.

Please write the desiderata for a theory of competence without comprehension. For each requirement, give the phenomenon or case that motivates it, and explain why it is a requirement — why a theory that fails to meet it would be inadequate or would miss the point of the inversion. The requirements should cover, at minimum: what it means to say a system exhibits competence; what it means to say it lacks comprehension; how the two can come apart (and under what conditions they cannot); whether the inversion applies to human cognition as well or bottoms out somewhere; and what the theory implies about the nature of comprehension itself (is it an illusion, a special kind of competence, an emergent phenomenon, or something else entirely?). Mark which requirements you think are non-negotiable — any theory must meet them — and which are negotiable, reflecting the preferences of a particular camp that one might reasonably reject. I am especially interested in whether any pair of requirements might be jointly unsatisfiable: if some desiderata pull in opposite directions, or if meeting one forces you to violate another, I want to know. Also, please flag the single requirement that does the most work in excluding popular or tempting accounts. Finally, as a brief appendix, give a scorecard for how the main existing approaches fare against the list: Dennett's intentional-stance view, the "mere pattern" or eliminativist view, and any other contender you think belongs in the conversation. I am less interested in the scoring than in the criteria themselves, so keep the weight on the desiderata and their motivating cases. Please write the answer in British English.
