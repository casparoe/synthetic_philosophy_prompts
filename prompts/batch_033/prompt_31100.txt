A friend who reads the same blogs I do laid out the following argument over dinner, and I've been turning it over ever since without finding a way out.

(1) An act is rational only if its expected consequences are at least as good as its costs. Expected utility is what settles what there is reason to do; "doing one's part" is not a separate source of reasons.

(2) In a large election, a single vote changes the outcome only in the states of the world where the election would otherwise be tied or decided by one vote. Even in a close race with tens of millions of voters, the probability of such a state is minuscule — standard estimates for large national elections put it on the order of one in ten million, and even a model an order of magnitude more generous would not change the next step.

(3) So the expected benefit of voting is the value of your preferred candidate's winning multiplied by at most 10^-6, and plausibly 10^-7. For that to exceed the cost of the hour it takes to vote, the value to you personally of your candidate's winning would have to be in the millions to hundreds of millions of dollars. That is not a plausible private benefit.

(4) Therefore voting in large elections is irrational.

(5) But the same structure applies to individual contributions to other large-scale collective goods. One household's recycling, one person's decision to fly less, one donation to a large aid organization, or one extra body at a climate march changes the world only if that contribution is pivotal — if some threshold is crossed that would not otherwise have been crossed. In a world of millions of contributors, the probability of being the pivotal contribution is likewise negligible.

(6) So, by parity of reasoning, recycling, reducing one's emissions, donating to large causes, and protesting are irrational too.

(7) But that conclusion is absurd. The same consequentialist standard that generates (6) also says a world in which everyone refrains from these contributions is much worse than a world in which everyone does their part. A theory of practical reason that finds no reason to do one's part in any large collective-action problem — which is to say, in nearly every problem that matters — has to be missing something.

(8) So something in (1)–(6) is false. I can't tell which step to reject, and I don't know what rejecting it costs. I'd most like to keep (1), and I'd also like to say that (4) is false; but then I need a principled reason why (4) is false while (6) still doesn't follow, and I don't have one.

Where does this argument go wrong? If it doesn't go wrong, which premise should I give up, and what does giving it up cost the rest of my views? Name the specific step or premise, explain why it looked correct, and price the standard fixes — for example, adding expressive or duty value to the utility calculus, or drawing a line between being pivotal and making a marginal contribution. If you draw that line, make it precise enough that it doesn't collapse back into the original paradox.

End with a machine-readable last line: \answer{...} containing the number of the step you reject, or, if you think the argument is valid and the real choice is among premises, \answer{valid: reject premise N}.
