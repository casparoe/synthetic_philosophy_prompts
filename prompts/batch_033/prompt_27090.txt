I'm running an upper-level epistemology seminar — 14 students, a 80-minute block — and I want the session on the Gettier problem to land as something they lived through, not something I explain. Design an in-class game where students end up holding a justified true belief that's only true by accident, and the discomfort of that shows up before I name it. No lecture up front, and the words "Gettier" and "justified true belief" don't appear until the debrief. Give me the rules, materials I can actually print or rig in a normal classroom, a points-or-tokens scoring scheme (no real money), a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the JTB analysis and then onto at least two rival diagnoses of what's missing — say, the no-false-lemma family and the defeater family. At some point every student has to make a forced, timed choice — a bet, a verdict, a ranking — so nobody gets to sit out the commitment. Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I expect someone to argue that the belief wasn't really justified, or to start gerrymandering the word "knows") and how to handle that without handing over the answer. Be honest about the one thing the game can't reproduce: the felt difference between being right by luck and being right in the right way is basically the whole problem, so a classroom simulation can only circle it — say how you'd compensate or whether you'd just flag it. Make definite design choices and defend them; don't lay out options and leave me to pick.
