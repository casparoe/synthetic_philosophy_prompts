I've been thinking about illusionism about phenomenal consciousness lately — the view that we don't actually have phenomenal properties, we just represent ourselves as having them — and I'm trying to write an essay that pushes back against it. I'm not a philosopher by training (I'm in cognitive science), so I'd love thoughts from someone who knows the literature better than I do.

Here's the rough idea. The essay would argue that illusionism faces a particular kind of self-undermining problem that's worse than the standard objection people usually raise. The standard objection is something like: if phenomenal consciousness is an illusion, then there must be a subject who is fooled by the illusion, and that subject's being-fooled seems to itself require phenomenal consciousness. Illusionists typically reply that the "subject" is just a system that represents itself as having phenomenal states, and this representation doesn't need to be phenomenally conscious. Fair enough — I think that reply works, actually.

But my argument is different. I think the problem is specifically about the *content* of the illusion. When illusionists say we have a "phenomenal concept" or a "phenomenal representation" that we apply to ourselves, what is the content of that concept? It seems like the content just *is* the qualitative feel — the reddishness of red, the hurtfulness of pain. If you say that our representation of pain is a representation of a state with a certain qualitative character, then either (a) the content is grasped by a system that genuinely understands qualitative character, which seems to require acquaintance with qualitative feels, or (b) the content is purely descriptive or functional, in which case the illusion isn't about phenomenal consciousness at all — it's about functional properties, and we've changed the subject. It's like saying someone has an illusion of being in love, but their concept of love is purely behavioural: they just think they're exhibiting certain behaviours. That's not an illusion of *being in love* in the relevant sense.

So the dilemma is: either illusionism can't specify the content of the illusion without invoking phenomenal properties (making it self-defeating), or the content is specifiable in purely non-phenomenal terms (in which case it's not an illusion about phenomenal consciousness and doesn't explain why we think there's a hard problem). I'm trying to work out whether this is genuinely a new argument or just a dressed-up version of something people have already said. Kammerer (2019) and others have made points about the nature of the illusion, but I'm not sure my framing adds anything new.

Anyway, I'd love comments on: (1) whether this argument has legs, (2) whether I'm missing an obvious illusionist response, and (3) what you'd suggest I read to sharpen it — but please don't just give me a reading list; I'm more interested in whether the argument works on its own terms. And if you don't mind, write in British English — this is for a UK-based journal and I'd like to get a feel for the style.
