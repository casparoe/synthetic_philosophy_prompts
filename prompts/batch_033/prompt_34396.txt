I teach an upper-level seminar in environmental ethics and population ethics, and I am writing the final examination. Please draft one essay question on whether habitat destruction reduces wild animal suffering, and on whether the net welfare of an ecosystem is a usable standard for conservation policy. The question should be unanswerable by reciting either the standard conservationist view that habitat loss harms animals or the slogan version of the wild-animal-suffering critique that wild lives are miserable and so less habitat means less suffering. It should force students to do at least the following:

- separate the harming of currently existing animals, whom destruction kills, starves, or displaces, from the prevention of future wild animal lives that would otherwise have come into existence, and explain why the claim that habitat destruction reduces suffering depends almost entirely on the latter;
- state the empirical assumptions about the distribution of welfare in wild populations that the conclusion requires, and say what sort of evidence could establish or undermine them;
- evaluate whether the net welfare of an ecosystem can function as a welfare target at all, given that ecosystems are not welfare subjects and that welfare would have to be aggregated across heterogeneous individuals, species, and life stages;
- take a position on what conservation policy should do under deep uncertainty about the sign of net wild animal welfare, and consider whether less destructive interventions would dominate habitat destruction on either view.

Then provide: (1) the exam question itself, with realistic instructions for students (90 minutes; roughly 1,200 words); (2) a model answer of about 800 words that works through the moves above rather than merely asserting a conclusion; and (3) a marking scheme out of 25 marks that names five specific moves a first-class answer must make, the marks each is worth, and a one-line note on how weaker answers characteristically miss each move. The marking scheme should separate an A from a B answer by naming the specific moves, not by generic descriptors.

Do not include citations, reading lists, or bibliographic references anywhere in the question, model answer, or marking scheme, and avoid verbatim quotations from any text.
