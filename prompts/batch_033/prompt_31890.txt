Question: Explain what it would mean for there to be a "free-floating rationale" — a normative reason that is not a reason for any particular agent. Drawing on the distinctions between motivating and normative reasons and between agent-relative and agent-neutral reasons, explain why neither distinction by itself settles whether such reasons exist. Then present and assess one argument for and one argument against the existence of free-floating rationales.

Student's answer:

A motivating reason is the consideration an agent acts on; it explains the action. A normative reason is a consideration that favors an action or attitude. A free-floating rationale would be a normative reason with no bearer: a reason that some state of affairs obtains, rather than a reason for anyone to do or believe anything. Agent-relative reasons mention the agent essentially — that the drowning child is my child gives me a reason to save her that a stranger does not share. Agent-neutral reasons do not mention the agent in this way: that a child is drowning gives anyone in a position to help a reason to help.

Neither distinction settles the question. The motivating/normative distinction only tells us that free-floating rationales, if there are any, would be normative rather than motivating; it does not show that normative reasons can outrun agents. The agent-relative/agent-neutral distinction is also insufficient, since agent-neutral reasons are still reasons for agents — they apply to anyone, but they are still borne by agents.

Even so, I think a free-floating rationale is really just another name for an agent-neutral reason. What makes a reason agent-neutral is that it does not depend on any particular person's desires or projects, and that is exactly what it is for a reason to float free of any particular reasoner. So anyone who accepts agent-neutral reasons, as I do, already accepts free-floating rationales.

The strongest argument for free-floating rationales starts from value. If it is better that there be less suffering rather than more, then there is a reason that there be less suffering. This reason exists even when no one is in a position to bring about the better state of affairs: before there were any agents, there was already a reason that the world be such that less suffering occurs rather than more, because the value of the state of affairs is itself a reason. The obvious objection is that value and reasons might come apart, but if it is better that p, that fact itself favors p, and favoring is what reasons do.

The strongest argument against free-floating rationales is motivational internalism: a normative reason must be capable of motivating the person for whom it is a reason, at least through sound deliberation. A free-floating rationale is not a reason for any person, so it could not motivate anyone, and therefore cannot be a reason. This argument fails, because motivating and normative reasons are distinct: a normative reason can exist without actually motivating anyone, as when someone is too weak-willed or distracted to be moved by a reason they have. Since free-floating rationales need only be normative reasons, their never actually moving anyone is no objection to them.
