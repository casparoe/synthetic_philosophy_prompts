Please give me your credence that honoring sunk costs is sometimes rational in a sense that the standard economic analysis misses — not merely instrumentally rational because of reputation effects, learning value, or the psychic cost of admitting error, but rational in virtue of what it means to be a temporally extended agent who can bind herself through commitments. Decompose the estimate into at least three sub-questions: (1) the probability that there exists a coherent normative notion of diachronic agency under which honoring sunk costs can be the right thing to do; (2) conditional on (1), the probability that this notion, rather than the standard consequentialist one, is what we ought to mean by "rational" in contexts where people actually invoke sunk costs; (3) conditional on both, the probability that at least some ordinary human cases — finishing a book you have come to hate, staying in a career you trained for, seeing through a project whose premise has collapsed — are better explained by this kind of rationality than by familiar cognitive biases. Give a number and a one-sentence justification for each term, then state your combined credence and explain how you combined them. Finally, identify which sub-question your final credence is most sensitive to and describe what evidence or argument would most shift your view on it. Please write your answer in American English.
