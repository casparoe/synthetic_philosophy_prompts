I run a reading group that doubles as a workshop series — about 12 to 15 people, mostly programmers and a few grad students, all with some exposure to the LessWrong sequences but not much formal philosophy. We've been working through the cluster of ideas around embedded agency: the observation that an agent is not a Cartesian boundary standing outside its environment, but is physically and computationally embedded in the world it's trying to model, so that the agent's own modeling activity is part of what it's modeling, its actions alter the environment it's predicting, and there is no clean "outside view" from which to get a God's-eye model of the whole system. We've read the introductory posts, and people can paraphrase the claims, but I can tell the concept hasn't fully landed — it still feels like a philosophical curiosity rather than a constraint they've run into firsthand.

I want to design a structured in-class game for the next session (90 minutes total) that makes them feel the recursion in their own decision-making before we name any of the formal machinery. The game should take about 30–40 minutes, leaving the rest of the time for debrief. I'm looking for something with clear rules, assigned roles if needed, and a points-based payoff scheme (no real money). The core mechanic should be this: players are trying to predict some aggregate property of the group's behavior, but their own predictions are part of the input that determines that aggregate property, so the modeling problem doesn't bottom out in a clean solution — it either spirals into a regress or settles into a fixed point that nobody can compute from outside. By the time the game ends, participants should have experienced the impossibility of stepping outside the system to get a detached model, and they should sense that this isn't just a practical limitation (not enough data, not enough time) but a structural one.

The debrief should map specific moments in the game onto the conceptual architecture: the Cartesian boundary assumption and what it looks like when you try to maintain it, the self-referential modeling problem, the difference between having a model of the environment and being a model inside the environment, and why the "outside view" is always already an inside view. I'd also like an honest note on what the activity cannot capture — the original framework is, after all, partly about logical uncertainty and the structure of decision theory in a way that a physical group game can only gesture at — and what I should supply in the discussion to fill that gap. What will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and how should I handle it?
