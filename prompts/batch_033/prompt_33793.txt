I was at the pub with two mates from my philosophy MA, and they got into it over Dutch book arguments. I couldn't get a word in, so I just listened.

Sam said: "Look, the Dutch book argument is straightfoward. If your degrees of belief don't satisfy the probability axioms, there's a set of bets you'd take individually that collectively guarantee you lose money. That's the definition of irrational — you're practically asking to be exploited."

Alex replied: "No, that's exactly the sleight of hand. The argument shows that if I accept all the bets, I lose. But why would I accept all of them? Once I see the package, I can just refuse. The argument proves you shouldn't hold inconsistent credences *and* be willing to bet on each one in isolation without thinking about how they combine. That's not the same as showing the credences themselves are irrational."

Sam came back: "But that's the whole point — if your credences genuinely represent your willingness to bet at those odds, then you *are* committed to each bet individually. You can't have it both ways. If you'd take bet A at those odds and bet B at those odds, you've got to take A and B together. Otherwise you're not really standing behind your credences."

Alex said: "That's just not how belief works. I can think each individual bet is fair without thinking the combination is fair. The Dutch book argument smuggles in an assumption that rational commitment is closed under conjunction of bets. But that's not an axiom of rationality — it's the very thing at issue."

At this point Sam got a bit exasperated and said: "Fine, but even if you reject the literal Dutch book, the underlying point survives. Inconsistent credences can't possibly track the world accurately. If your credence in P and your credence in not-P sum to more than one, at least one of those is objectively wrong. The bets just dramatise the incoherence."

Alex smiled: "Now you've changed the argument. That's a different claim — it's about accuracy, not about avoiding sure loss. And I don't even grant that one. If I'm 0.6 in P and 0.6 in not-P, I'm not 'wrong' in any straightforward sense. I'm just more confident in each than the probability calculus allows. Maybe that's a feature, not a bug, in cases of deep uncertainty."
