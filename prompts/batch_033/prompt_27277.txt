Diplomatic recognition is a strange practice to think about philosophically. When an entity controls a territory, has a stable population, an effective government, and a capacity to conduct foreign relations, other states still have to decide whether to "recognize" it as a state. The official story, repeated by states and by most legal doctrine, is declaratory: recognition merely acknowledges a statehood that already exists, and the entity's status depends on the objective criteria rather than on anyone's recognition. Yet the same states behave as if recognition is constitutive: unrecognized entities are shut out of international organizations, have difficulty making treaties stick or claiming sovereign immunity, and find their very statehood treated as open to question; recognition, once given, is treated as changing the entity's standing. Recognition is also routinely granted or withheld for political reasons — alliances, the fear of legitimizing secession, domestic pressure — and entities that plainly satisfy the standard criteria remain unrecognized for decades.

What theory of recognition does this practice presuppose? In particular, does it presuppose a constitutive view of recognition (recognition makes, or at least completes, what it recognizes), a declaratory view (recognition merely reports it), or some unstable mixture of the two? Reconstruct the implicit theory as carefully as you can, distinguishing what the practice strictly presupposes from what it is merely compatible with, and say where it is philosophically underdetermined. Then put this in conversation with Hegel's master–slave dialectic: the idea that self-consciousness is constituted through recognition, that recognition must be mutual between equals, and that recognition extracted from a subordinate one does not recognize as an equal is hollow. Is diplomatic recognition best read as a real-world instance of that struggle — a form of recognition that is one-sided, strategic, and therefore defective in the Hegelian sense — or does the practice not presuppose anything Hegelian, because states are not selves and legal personality is not self-consciousness? Finally, say what would have to change in the practice, or in how states describe it, if it consistently adopted one of these views rather than oscillating between them.
