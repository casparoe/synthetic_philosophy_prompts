I'm teaching an upper-level undergraduate seminar on environmental ethics, and next week we're tackling the question of whether habitat destruction reduces wild animal suffering. The students have already worked through several weeks of standard environmental ethics — the intrinsic value of species, ecosystem health, the land ethic — and most of them have settled comfortably into the view that preserving natural habitats is an unqualified good. I want to unsettle that. Not to convince them that bulldozing forests is morally fine, but to make them genuinely feel the force of the argument that a thriving ecosystem may contain enormous amounts of animal suffering (predation, parasitism, starvation, disease, competition) and that reducing habitat might, perversely, reduce total suffering in the world. I do not want to lecture this at them; I want them to discover the tension themselves through the structure of an activity, so that the intuition arises from their own decisions rather than from my framing.

The class has about 18 students, and I have a 75-minute session. Design a classroom simulation or game that makes participants confront the welfare tradeoffs built into habitat preservation. Ideally, they should be making decisions about land use — allocating parcels, choosing between conservation and development, or managing populations — and watching welfare outcomes shift, only gradually realizing that the preservationist path they instinctively favor produces more total suffering than the development path they instinctively resist. The debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto the philosophical architecture of the argument: the distinction between counting lives and counting welfare, the problem of aggregating suffering across individuals, the question of whether bringing a life into existence can be a harm or a benefit, and the move from "nature is valuable" to "nature is full of suffering." It should also anticipate what will predictably go wrong when I run it — for instance, students refusing to engage because they find the premise repellent, or mistaking the activity for an argument that we should actively destroy habitat, or getting stuck on the claim that wild animals suffer at all — and be honest about what the activity cannot capture, such as long-term evolutionary and ecological consequences that might reverse the welfare calculus or the possibility that non-sentient life has value independent of welfare. No lecture up front; the phrase "wild animal suffering" should ideally not appear until the debrief.
