I am a novelist, and I am drafting a scene in which a character — a translator who has spent years working on the Zhuangzi — writes a poem in her notebook after the death of a friend. The poem is her attempt to think through, in English, what the text says about death, and it appears later in the novel when she reads it at the funeral, to the discomfort of the other mourners. I would like you to write that poem for me.

Please write a villanelle in her voice. The two refrains should carry the philosophy. One refrain should hold the idea that death is not an ending but a transformation: what we call a person is a temporary arrangement of breath and body, lent by the process of heaven and earth and returned to it, the way one season passes into the next. The other refrain should hold the refusal to mourn death as a loss or an injury: conventional grief is a category mistake, the error of treating a phase of an ongoing process as if something had been destroyed. The villanelle's machinery should do structural work here. The refrains should return the way the seasons and the forms return; and when the two refrains come together in the closing couplet, the reader should feel that they were never two claims at all, but one claim seen from two positions in the process. It should be accurate enough that a scholar of the Zhuangzi would recognize, without any quotation, the echoes of the passage where the husband sits drumming and singing after his wife's death, the passage where the skull speaks of death as rest, and the deathbed passage about the body's return to the birds and the ants — and it should be good enough as a poem that a reader who has never opened the book would still find it moving and exact.
