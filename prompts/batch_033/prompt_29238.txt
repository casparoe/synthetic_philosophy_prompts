I am at work on a novel whose protagonist, a neuroscientist who has been completely colorblind since birth, undergoes an experimental gene therapy in her mid-thirties that for the first time gives her functional trichromatic vision. The procedure works; the novel's central scene is the moment she steps outside the clinic and sees, actually sees, the red of a geranium. I have been reading around the philosophical literature on the knowledge argument — Jackson's Mary, the ability hypothesis, the acquaintance response — and I want a short poem that can serve as an epigraph for the chapter, something that does justice to the philosophical structure of the problem without turning into a philosophy paper in verse.

I would like a sonnet. The octave should build the picture of complete physical knowledge: Mary, or my protagonist, knows every wavelength, every firing pattern, every functional role, and there is no physical fact about color vision she has not mastered from inside her monochrome room. The volta should be the moment of first seeing — the geranium, the sky, whatever you choose — and it should register as a genuine epistemic shift, not merely an emotional one. The sestet and closing couplet should do the philosophical work: not just that the experience is overwhelming or beautiful (that would be too easy), but that something new has been learned, something propositional knowledge could not deliver. I want the poem to leave a reader who knows the literature with the sense that the knowledge argument has been rendered faithfully, and a reader who does not to feel the force of the intuition that drives it. If the couplet can gesture toward what a physicalist might say in reply — that the change is in the knower, not in what is known; that an old fact has merely been encountered under a new mode of presentation — without resolving the tension, that would be ideal. The poem should earn its turn rather than announce it.
