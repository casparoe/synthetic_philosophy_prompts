In my world history class we read a short passage by Pascal on justice and force, and I have been turning it into an argument that now worries me. As I understand him, Pascal's thought is roughly this: justice with no force behind it cannot make anyone obey, because some people will always defy it; force unaccompanied by justice is tyranny, and people condemn it. So a stable order needs the two joined. But people cannot be brought to agree about what justice requires, since each side claims justice for itself, while they can be brought to agree about who is stronger, since power is plain and undisputed. So, since we could not get people to accept that the just side should rule, we settled for getting them to accept that the ruling side is just.

From this I constructed the following argument:

(1) A rule can actually govern people only if it is backed by force strong enough to make disobeying it the worse option.

(2) Force that is not accepted as just is tyranny, and people resist and condemn it.

(3) Therefore, a stable social order requires rules that are both enforced and accepted as just.

(4) People cannot be brought to agree about what justice requires, because every party claims justice for its own side; but they can be brought to agree about who is stronger, because that is plain and undisputed.

(5) So the only workable way to secure the acceptance required by (3) is to take whoever is strongest and treat that party's rules as just.

(6) But if a rule is called just only because it comes from the strongest, then "just" adds nothing to "commanded by the strongest."

(7) So in every actual order, law is just force wearing the name of justice; justice has no content of its own.

(8) Therefore, any appeal to justice against an existing order — that slavery is unjust, or that a tyrant's rule is unjust — is not a claim with real content but only a rival bid for power.

I find (8) very hard to accept, because it seems to prove too much: it would make every moral criticism of the powerful literally empty, including the criticism of tyranny that the passage itself seems to rely on when it calls force without justice "tyranny." Yet each step looked right when we discussed it. Which step should I give up, and what does giving it up cost? If the problem is not a single premise but a move between steps, say which move it is and why it looked valid. I would most like to keep the thought that condemning a tyrant as unjust is a meaningful claim, but I do not want to keep it by mere assertion.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
