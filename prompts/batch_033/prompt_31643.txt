I'm currently taking an undergraduate seminar on formal epistemology and the mathematics of rational choice, and we have just finished a unit on Kelly betting and geometric rationality. Our instructor spent a good deal of time on a distinction I want to make sure I actually understand rather than merely remember: the difference between the arithmetic-mean picture, in which a bet with a positive edge seems to invite staking as much as possible because next-round expected wealth rises linearly with the fraction wagered, and the geometric-mean picture, in which wealth compounds multiplicatively across repeated bets, a losing round shrinks the bankroll by a fraction, and the quantity that grows additively over time is the logarithm of wealth. I would like to close my notes on the unit with something that makes the distinction stick.

Could you write a Shakespearean sonnet about the Kelly criterion? I would like the first two quatrains to build up the seductive expected-value reasoning: for a bet paying net odds b with win probability p and loss probability q = 1 − p, a positive edge (pb > q) makes the all-in stake look rational, since the arithmetic mean of next-round wealth increases with the fraction bet. I would like the volta, at the start of the third quatrain, to turn to the repeated-betting and time-average picture: long-run growth is governed by maximizing expected log wealth, the geometric mean is the relevant average for a multiplicative process, and overbetting beyond the growth-optimal fraction can turn a positive-edge opportunity into a losing long-run strategy even though the bet still has positive expected value. The closing couplet should state what the criterion actually prescribes — bet the fraction f* = (pb − q)/b, the edge divided by the odds — and make clear that this is a rule about long-run growth, not about maximizing expected wealth in a single round. The poem should be precise enough that someone who works with the Kelly criterion would recognize the arithmetic-versus-geometric distinction and the overbetting point, and it should still work as a poem for someone who has never seen the formula.
