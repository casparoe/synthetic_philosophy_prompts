An argument I walked myself into, and I cannot find the exit.

(1) Epiphenomenalism about phenomenal consciousness: conscious experiences are caused by physical processes, but they cause nothing — no behavior, no brain events, no further mental states.

(2) My judgment that I am in pain is a physical event, and its causes are therefore wholly physical. The pain itself, being causally inert, is not among them.

(3) A belief is justified only if it is caused, at least in part, by what it is about. A belief about X to whose production X contributes nothing is at best a lucky coincidence.

(4) So on epiphenomenalism, my judgment that I am in pain is unjustified.

(5) The same reasoning covers every introspective judgment about experience, including the judgment that conscious experience has a qualitative character that physical description leaves out — which is the principal evidence for epiphenomenalism.

(6) A view that entails that its own central evidence is unjustified cannot be rationally held.

(7) Therefore epiphenomenalism cannot be rationally held.

I would most like to keep (1), since the arguments from the causal closure of the physical and from the conceivability of zombies seem to me sound. But then (3) looks like the only exit, and I do not know what a theory of justification looks like without it. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If the argument is valid and epiphenomenalism simply pays this price, say so — but explain how an epiphenomenalist could still take her own introspective evidence seriously.
