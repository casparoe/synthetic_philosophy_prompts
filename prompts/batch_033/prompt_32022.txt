I'm taking an epistemology seminar on testimony this term, and I've been struck by how thoroughly the reductionism/anti-reductionism debate has stalled. Both positions are clear, and yet neither side seems able to land an argument the other side experiences as a blow. As I understand them: reductionists hold that testimonial justification must be derived from other epistemic resources, paradigmatically inductive evidence that the speaker, or testimony in general, is reliable. The only version that still seems defensible is local reductionism, which drops the demand for a global proof of human veracity and instead requires the hearer to have situation-specific grounds for trusting this speaker on this occasion, grounds drawn from perception, memory, and background knowledge of how people talk. Anti-reductionists hold that testimony is a basic source of justification, on a par with perception and memory: absent defeaters, a hearer has a default entitlement to accept what she is told, in the spirit of Reid's principle of credulity. Each side has what it treats as a decisive move. Reductionists ask why, if there is a default entitlement to believe testimony, a hearer would not be justified in believing a stranger's outlandish claim on a bus. Anti-reductionists reply with Coady's circularity charge — any inductive attempt to establish the general reliability of testimony would have to rely on testimony in assembling the evidence base — and with the Burge-style point that understanding an assertion as an assertion already presents its content as true. After a term of watching the two sides trade these moves, I suspect the debate is not really about testimony, or not only about testimony. My hypothesis is that it is being driven by an unacknowledged disagreement about the nature of justification — roughly internalist reasons-access versus externalist reliable-formation — and by a difference about which cases get to count as data: the credulous child and the careless hearer on one side, the ordinary conversational exchange on the other. I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. What kind of disagreement is this, exactly: verbal, normative, factual, or a disagreement about the standards for counting something as a source of justification? If my hypothesis is wrong or incomplete, what else explains the impasse? And what would count as progress: a distinction, an argument that one side collapses into the other, a developmental-psychology finding, a new case — or should we expect no progress, and if so, why?
