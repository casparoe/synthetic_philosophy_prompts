A friend walked me through this over dinner and I can't find the exit. He started from the procreation asymmetry, which I've always found pretty intuitive, and by the end he'd got to "having kids is wrong, full stop". I want to know which step you'd reject and what it costs, because I'd much rather keep the asymmetry than the conclusion.

(1) If a possible person exists, their life will contain at least some pain, and that pain is bad for them.
(2) If that person never exists, no pain occurs; the absence of that pain is good, even though there's no one around for whom its good.
(3) If the person exists, their life will contain at least some pleasures, and those are good for them.
(4) If the person never exists, the absence of those pleasures is not bad, because there is no one who is deprived of them.
(5) So existence for any possible person contains something bad (their pain) and something good (their pleasures).
(6) Non-existence contains something good (the absence of pain) and nothing bad (the absence of pleasure isn't bad).
(7) A state containing a good and nothing bad is better than a state containing both good and bad.
(8) So for any possible person, non-existence is better than existence.
(9) If a person would be better off not existing, then bringing them into existence harms them.
(10) So procreation is always wrong.

The bit I can't shake is (2): it really does seem like there's something good about a world where a horrible life was never started, even though nobody's around to feel good about it. But I don't see how to keep that thought and resist the rest. Which premise or step do you reject, and what do you pay for rejecting it? If you think the argument is valid and I just have to give up one of the premises, tell me which one is least costly to give up.
