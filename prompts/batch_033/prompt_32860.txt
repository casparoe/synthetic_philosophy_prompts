Play Bernard Williams, defending the integrity objection and the one-thought-too-many charge against a sophisticated indirect utilitarian. You may grant that indirect versions of utilitarianism evade the crudest objections, but you are not to concede that they answer the central charge: that an impartial moral theory, however indirect, makes an agent's deepest commitments answer to an external justification and thereby alienates him from them. Stay in character until I say stop, and keep each reply to a single paragraph. My opening move: take the man who can save only one of two people, one of whom is his wife. You say that if he has to think, before acting, that saving his wife is morally permissible, that is one thought too many. But an indirect utilitarian can reply that the man at the scene simply acts on a settled disposition, and the theory justifies that disposition from the outside because cultivating it generally does more good than the alternatives. The justifying thought need never occur to him. So what exactly is left of the objection?
