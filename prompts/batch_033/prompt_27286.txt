A friend forwarded me a long argument from a Slack we're both on, between two people who are both pretty philosophically sharp, and I genuinely couldn't tell who was right by the end of it. They were arguing about moral uncertainty — specifically whether you should maximize expected choiceworthiness across the moral theories you assign nonzero credence to.

The first one, call him N, opened with the standard line: just as you should take the expected value of options under empirical uncertainty, you should take the expected choiceworthiness of options under normative uncertainty. Anything else means sometimes knowingly choosing the option that's probably morally worse, which is just as irrational as knowingly choosing the option that's probably causally worse.

The second one, M, came back that choiceworthiness isn't a shared currency like dollars or utils. Saying that lying is "minus eight" on Kant's scale and "minus forty" on the utilitarian scale presupposes exactly the common unit that doesn't exist, because each theory defines its own scale and there's no exchange rate between them.

N replied that you don't need a magic exchange rate; you just normalize. Set the least choiceworthy option each theory considers possible at 0 and the most at 1, then compare across theories on that normalized scale. M said that's hopeless, because the normalization is an arbitrary convention: you and me can pick different least/most anchors and get different answers about which act wins, so "expected choiceworthiness" doesn't pick out a unique verdict. N said sure, but that's the same situation as picking priors under empirical uncertainty; underdetermination at the margins doesn't refute the whole approch, and some answer beats no answer. M said no, because with empirical priors the utilities are at least the same kind of quantity across states, while here the anchors aren't measuring anything — they're stipulating a comparison — so the arbitrariness isn't a tolerable approximation, it's a hole in the middle of the method.

Then M added a second objection: some theories don't give you a cardinal, or even an ordinal, ranking in the cases you actually need one. A deontological theory might just say the act is forbidden, full stop, with no "amount of forbiddenness," so you literally can't place it on the scale you're averaging over. N said you can approximate: use intervals or lexicographic representations, or decision rules that handle incomparability, and anyway real people's credences are rarely over fully precise theories, so this is an objection to a straw version of the view. M said that's exactly the problem — once you start patching with interval scales and incomparability-sensitive rules, you've stopped doing expected choiceworthiness and are doing whatever ad hoc thing produces sane answers, which means the advertised justification has evaporated.

N's final move was to say the alternative is worse: if you don't let uncertainty about which moral theory is true affect what you do, you're acting as if you're certain you're right, and that's a kind of epistemic arrogance we'd never accept in any factual matter. M answered that this conflates two questions. "What should I do, given that I might be wrong about morality" is different from "what is it right to do," and the true moral theory answers the second question directly; using your credences over theories to decide what to do is itself a first-order moral commitment (roughly, a consequentialist one), not a neutral meta-principle.

That's where the thread ended. I'd love a point-by-point adjudication: at each move, who is right about what, where each of them overclaims or underclaims, and what a defensible position would say after all of this — including whatever both of them are missing. And the usual ask: how confident are you in these verdicts, and which point are you least sure about?
