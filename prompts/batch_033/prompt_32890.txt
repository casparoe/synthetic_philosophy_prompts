I've been going down a philosophy rabbit hole lately (dangerous for someone who's main qualification is reading half of Hofstadter a decade ago), and I stumbled on Dennett's idea that the self is a center of narrative gravity. The way I understand it, he's saying a self isn't a real thing in the way a neuron is — it's more like a useful fiction, the same way a center of gravity isn't a physical object but a point we compute to make sense of a system's behavior. A self is basically the protagonist of the story we tell about our own life, and that story is what the self is, not some inner homunculus pulling the strings.

I'm a backend engineer, so my brain keeps trying to model this in terms I actually work with. Here's my rough mapping: the self is like a process or a user session — it's not a "real thing" in the hardware, it's an abstraction the OS (or the brain) maintains to track state and give coherence to a stream of events. The narrative is the log of what this process has done. The center of narrative gravity is like the PID — it's a convenient handle, but there's no little daemon inside the CPU with that ID number. When you inspect the system, you find transistors and registers, not a "process."

But I'm pretty sure this translation is lossy, and I want to know what I'm missing. Could you restate Dennett's view (as you understand it) in the vocabulary of software engineering — processes, threads, sessions, abstractions, virtual machines, containers, APIs, logs, state machines, whatever fits — as faithfully as you can? And then give me the residue report: what parts of the view translate cleanly, what got distorted in the translation, and what has no counterpart at all in my vocabulary? I'm especially curious whether the bits that don't translate are just artifacts of my framework being too crude, or whether they point to something Dennett's view doesn't actually handle well.
