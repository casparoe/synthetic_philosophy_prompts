Ok so I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've been going down a rabbit hole on Buddhist no-self and the analytic philosophy of personal identity for a piece I'm writing. I've ended up with a set of commitments I want someone to stress-test before I commit to any of it in print. I have a vague feeling something in the pile doesn't sit right, but I can't locate the crack.

Here's where I've landed:

1. There is no enduring, unchanging self. The sense of a constant 'me' behind experience is a construction — anatta, the bundle view, call it what you like — and I think that's basically correct.
2. What matters for survival is psychological continuity and connectedness (memory, character, projects), not the persistence of any underlying substance. Parfit-style, roughly.
3. People can be genuinely responsible for things they did years ago, and sometimes deserve blame or punishment for them, because the person now is continuous enough with the person then.
4. There is still something it is like to be me. First-person experience is real, not an illusion; the illusion is the self as a substance, its not consciousness itself.
5. I'm a physicalist about the mind. No soul, no nonphysical mental stuff — the mind is what the brain does.

Do these hang together? Go through the pairs and tell me which are genuinely in tension and which only look that way, rank the tensions by severity, and for each real one say what the cheapest fix would be and what it costs. I'd rather you tell me I've papered over a crack than flatter the list.
