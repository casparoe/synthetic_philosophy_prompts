I've just come from a long dinner with a friend where we spent three hours on the Grand Inquisitor chapter, and I've come away less sure of my own position than when I sat down. I'd like to lay out where I've landed and ask whether the pieces actually fit together. Here are the five commitments I seem to hold:

1. The Inquisitor's empirical diagnosis is largely correct: most people, most of the time, experience freedom as a burden and would trade it for security, bread, and someone to worship — this is a defensible reading of human nature, not a caricature.

2. Despite this, the Inquisitor's normative conclusion does not follow: freedom remains the higher value, and a life of comfortable submission is a diminished life even when freely chosen, because what matters is not only what people want but what befits a human being.

3. Christ's silence throughout the Inquisitor's monologue is the only adequate response: no argument could refute the Inquisitor on his own terms, and the sole answer is the example of a life lived freely unto death — hence the kiss.

4. The Inquisitor's reading of the three temptations is, however, a misreading: Christ did not reject miracle, mystery, and authority as such, but only their deployment to compel belief rather than to invite it — there is a legitimate use of each that the Inquisitor overlooks.

5. Ivan Karamazov is not endorsing the Inquisitor; the chapter dramatises a view Ivan finds seductive but ultimately cannot accept, which is why he places it inside a poem narrated by a character who himself calls it absurd.

I need an audit. Which pairs among these five are in genuine tension, which only appear to be, and how severe is each tension? Please give me exactly five items — one for each tension or apparent tension you identify — ordered from most severe to least, with no introduction or conclusion. Each item should be a sentence or two: name the two commitments in tension, say whether the tension is real or merely apparent, and state what would have to give to resolve it.
