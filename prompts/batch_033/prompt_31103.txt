I have been reading John Taurek's article "Should the Numbers Count?" for a graduate seminar on distributive justice, and I find myself troubled by what looks like a corrosive implication of his central argument. As I understand Taurek, he claims that when you must choose between saving one stranger and saving five strangers—say, by delivering a scarce medicine—you are not morally required to save the five. His reasoning, if I am following it correctly, is that harms are borne by individual subjects one at a time. There is no super-subject who suffers the aggregated death of all five; each of the five loses only his or her own life, just as the one would. Since no one experiences a "five-person loss," the mere fact that five is a larger number does not, by itself, give you a decisive reason to favour the larger group. You might instead flip a coin, giving each person an equal chance.

But here is the difficulty I cannot shake. If we accept that harms cannot simply be summed across separate subjects because there is no unified consciousness that undergoes the sum, should we not apply the same stricture to harms that befall a single person across time? My Monday-self and my Tuesday-self are not, after all, numerically identical in the way that a single instantaneous pain-experience is identical to itself. They are linked by memory, psychological continuity, and bodily persistence, but these are relations that hold between distinct temporal stages, not the strict identity of a single enduring thing. So if I cannot aggregate the deaths of five separate people into a "five-person harm," why am I entitled to aggregate a headache on Monday and a headache on Tuesday into "two headaches suffered by one person"? It seems that Taurek's anti-aggregation principle, if taken seriously, would unravel our ability to think about anyone's diachronic well-being at all—we could never say that a life containing many pains is worse, for that person, than a life containing few. That strikes me as an intolerable result, and I suspect I am misreading Taurek somewhere. Is the separateness of persons really so different in kind from the separateness of temporal stages that the argument does not generalise, or is this a genuine instability in the view?
