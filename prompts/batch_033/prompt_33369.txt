I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of language — fourteen students, one eighty-minute session — and we have just worked through the skeptical problem in Kripke's reading of Wittgenstein: the challenge that any finite set of examples of a rule is compatible with indefinitely many different rules, so the examples alone cannot determine which continuation is correct. I want the students to feel the force of this from the inside rather than receive it as a thesis, so I would like a structured classroom activity in which they run into the underdetermination themselves before it is named, without my announcing the philosophical target in advance. My rough instinct is a teaching-and-skepticism game: a teacher tries to convey a simple rule (an arithmetic operation or a pattern) using only examples and yes-or-no judgments about proposed instances; a learner must then apply the rule to a new case; and a skeptic tries to construct a deviant rule that fits every example given so far but yields a different answer on that new case. But I am open to any format that works, as long as the students discover that the teacher's examples underdetermine the rule and that their conviction that the intended rule is obvious must come from somewhere other than the examples themselves.

Please design the full activity: roles, rules, materials such as example cards or worksheets, a scoring scheme if one would help, and an eighty-minute timeline. The debrief should map what happened in the game onto the skeptical challenge — the idea that a deviant interpretation can be made to fit all the examples and diverge only later, and the question of what the teacher possesses beyond the examples that could rule it out. Please also say what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (for instance, students importing background knowledge of the intended rule, or treating the skeptic's deviant proposal as a trick rather than as a genuine rival interpretation) and how to handle those failures in the moment. Finally, include an honest note about which feature of the philosophical problem the activity cannot reproduce — I suspect it cannot stage the fully general skeptical conclusion about all meaning, because the participants already share a natural language and a classroom context — and say what the facilitator should supply explicitly in the debrief to cover that gap.
