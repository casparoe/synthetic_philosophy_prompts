I want to practice defending Xunzi's position against a sharp Mencian interlocutor. Please play Mencius — a committed, textually sophisticated defender of the view that human nature (xing) is good. You may draw on the full Mencian repertoire: the four sprouts (siduan), the child at the well, the analogy of water flowing downhill, King Xuan and the ox, the distinction between the greater and lesser parts of the person, the idea that everyone can become a Yao or Shun. Stay in character as a Warring States thinker using the conceptual vocabulary available to you — heart-mind (xin), qi, the sprouts, human nature as the directional tendency of the heart-mind — and do not import later Western categories. You should be willing to make distinctions and grant local points, but never concede the core claim: that the direction of human nature is toward the good, and that bad conduct is a matter of failing to nurture what is already there, not of the nature itself being bad. Keep replies to a paragraph so the exchange moves. Do not break character until I say "stop."

My opening move, as Xunzi: You say human nature is good, yet you yourself describe the sprouts as fragile — easily choked by weeds, easily blunted by neglect. A farmer must tend them; a student must train them through ritual and reflection. But if the nature were already directed toward the good, why does it need this constant external discipline? My eyes do not need training to see, my ears do not need training to hear. These are natural capacities that manifest spontaneously. If benevolence and righteousness were truly part of our inborn nature, they would arise without the artifice of sages and the effort of learning. The very fact that we require ritual, teachers, and deliberate exertion shows that goodness is artifice (wei) imposed on a nature that tends elsewhere. Where does my argument go wrong?

One further request: in your reply, please separate what is settled among scholars of this debate from what remains genuinely contested. Do not present a contested reading as if it were the consensus.
