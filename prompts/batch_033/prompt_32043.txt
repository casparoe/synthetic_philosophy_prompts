After retiring from a career in chemistry, I have been reading through moral philosophy, and I recently came across a book for general readers that summarizes Bernard Williams's integrity objection to utilitarianism roughly as follows.

The book says Williams's charge is that utilitarianism cannot distinguish between what an agent does and what merely happens. In his example, Jim is given the choice of shooting one prisoner himself, in which case the other nineteen will be released, or refusing, in which case all twenty will be shot. According to the book, Williams's point is that killing one person with one's own hand is morally different from allowing nineteen deaths, and a theory that treats these as equivalent has lost sight of the agent's own involvement. The book then has Williams generalizing this into an argument against every impartial moral theory. It offers the case of a husband who saves his drowning wife rather than a stranger: if he first asks whether saving her would be impartially justified, he is having one thought too many. From this the book concludes that Williams showed impartialist ethics to be false, since the reasons of a person of integrity must come directly from their commitments and not from any calculation.

I am inclined to accept that the Jim case shows an agent's own role matters, and the conclusion is attractive to me. But the step from Jim to the claim that every impartial moral theory is false strikes me as too quick, and I do not know whether the book has Williams right about the doing/allowing distinction or about what the drowning case is supposed to establish. Is this a fair summary of Williams's actual position? Please go through it carefully, separating what is accurate from what is a subtle distortion, and say whether the book's conclusion follows from the parts that are accurate.
