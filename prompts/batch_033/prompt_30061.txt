In interdisciplinary research contexts, I keep encountering a particular analogy: people say that you cannot fully identify or critique the blind spots of a discipline using only that discipline's own tools — you have to "jump out of the system," just as in Hofstadter's MU-puzzle, where no amount of rule-following will get you from MI to MU, and you can only see why by stepping outside the formal system and noticing an invariant property (the number of I's is never a multiple of 3). The analogy is deployed to support the conclusion that genuinely understanding the limitations of, say, neoclassical economics or behaviorist psychology requires perspectives from outside those frameworks — interdisciplinary work is not just enriching but necessary.

I want you to audit this analogy in detail. Lay out what corresponds to what between the MU-puzzle and the case of disciplinary critique: what plays the role of the axioms, the rules of inference, the target claim, the invariant, and the act of jumping out. Then identify where the mapping breaks. In particular, address whether the analogy quietly imports features of formal systems — decidability, fixed rules, a sharp inside/outside boundary — that intellectual disciplines lack, and whether those differences matter for the intended conclusion. I want a committed response: take a definite position on whether the conclusion that external critique is necessary survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and give your reasons.
