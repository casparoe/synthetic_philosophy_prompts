I read a piece in a popular science magazine about why psychiatric diagnoses seem to change from one generation to the next, and it spent several pages explaining an idea from the philosopher Ian Hacking that I want to get straight before I repeat it to anyone. As I understood it, the article's account ran something like this. Hacking's central claim is that psychiatric classifications are unlike chemical classifications, because classifying people changes the people classified. Once a label such as multiple personality disorder or depression becomes available, patients take it up as a way of understanding themselves: they reinterpret their memories, shape their symptoms, and present themselves in terms the label makes available. The classification therefore helps to bring about the very phenomenon it names — this is the looping effect. Hacking contrasts these interactive kinds with indifferent kinds, such as electrons or bacteria, which do not care how they are classified. The article then drew the lesson that psychiatric categories are not discovered but made, that the conditions they name are at bottom social constructions rather than natural facts, and that psychiatric research is caught in a circle, because it is always studying a moving target that the classification itself has helped to create.

I find the first part of this plausible — it does seem that people respond to the labels they are given — but the conclusion feels too strong, and I cannot tell where the overstatement enters. Which parts of this account would Hacking, or someone defending the view carefully, accept? Which parts would they say misrepresent it? And does the article's conclusion — that psychiatric kinds are invented, socially constructed rather than natural, and scientifically circular — actually follow from the accurate parts?
