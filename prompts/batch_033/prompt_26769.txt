Two developers on my team, Mark and Soren, got into an argument at lunch about the ethics of a product decision, and it spiralled into a debate about moral philosophy that I'm still trying to sort out. I'll recount the main moves as I remember them.

(1) Mark said that when we prioritise which bugs to fix, it's obvious that we should fix the one that affects millions of users over the one that affects a single user, even if the millions each experience only a minor annoyance while the single user experiences a crash. He said this is just common sense, and any moral theory that says otherwise is broken.

(2) Soren replied that this is exactly the kind of thinking that lets companies walk all over individuals. He said he'd been reading about a philosopher called Scanlon who argues that you can't just add up lots of small complaints to outweigh one person's serious complaint, because each person is a separate individual and their reasons can't be combined into a single sum. He said that's why we rightly think it's wrong to let someone suffer severe pain just to prevent millions of people from missing fifteen minutes of a football broadcast.

(3) Mark pushed back: if you really can't aggregate complaints across people, then how do you ever justify saving a larger group over a smaller one? Suppose a building is on fire and you can only evacuate one floor. Surely you should save the floor with fifty people rather than the floor with one person. But if each person's complaint against being left to die is considered individually, and you can't sum them, then what reason do you have to choose the fifty? Each individual on the crowded floor has exactly the same complaint as the single person on the other floor. Soren's view seems to imply that the numbers don't count, which is absurd.

(4) Soren answered that the fire case is different because the harms are of the same kind and magnitude. His view does allow the numbers to count when the competing claims are comparable — you can justify a principle of saving the greater number by considering each person's claim pairwise. What it blocks is letting many trivial complaints outweigh one person's serious complaint, because the quality of the reasons differs, not just the quantity.

(5) Mark said this sounded like an ad-hoc patch. He asked: what about intermediate cases? Where exactly does a complaint stop being trivial and start being comparable? A hundred people with moderate headaches versus one person with a broken leg? A thousand people with a mild headache versus one person with a severe migraine? If you can't aggregate, you need a principled way to sort complaints into tiers, and it's not clear that any such sorting exists that isn't just a disguised way of aggregating across different magnitudes.

(6) Soren replied that the tiers are implicit in the very idea of reasonable rejection: a principle that allows one person to suffer a serious harm so that others can avoid a trivial inconvenience is one that the victim could reasonably reject, and no amount of multiplication of the trivial inconvenience changes that, because the reasons of the many don't combine into a single stronger reason.

At this point they had to get back to stand-up, and I was left with the sense that they were both making good points but were talking past each other.
