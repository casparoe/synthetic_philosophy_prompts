I am a novelist. For the past three years I have been working on a book in which a character—a neuroscientist—becomes convinced that her husband, after a severe brain injury, is a philosophical zombie: physically and behaviorally indistinguishable from the man she married, but with the lights out inside. The book is not a philosophy tract; it is a novel about marriage, grief, and the terror of not knowing whether anyone else is really there. But the philosophical architecture has to be sound, or the whole thing collapses into empty metaphor.

I have read enough to know that the zombie thought experiment is not merely a literary device. It is the engine of the conceivability argument against physicalism: zombies are conceivable; if conceivable, then metaphysically possible; if metaphysically possible, then physicalism is false. I have also read enough to know that the two sides—those who take zombie-conceivability to refute physicalism, and those who regard the whole apparatus as confused—have been talking past each other for decades, and that the literature is now so vast and technical that a non-philosopher can barely find her footing.

I would like you to produce the document that a committed zombie-theorist (someone who endorses the conceivability argument against physicalism) and a committed physicalist (someone who denies that zombies are coherently conceivable in the relevant sense) would produce after a year-long adversarial collaboration. They are not trying to water down their views. They are trying to produce a joint statement that both can sign—and that someone like me can read.

The document should include:

1. A numbered list of claims that both parties accept. These should not be trivial. They should capture the substantive common ground—about the structure of the argument, about what physicalism asserts, about what conceivability involves, about what is not being claimed—that a reader genuinely needs in order to understand what the fight is about.

2. A table of disagreements. Each row should state the disagreement in a way that both parties would accept as a fair characterization of their own side. No strawmen. The wording should be precise enough that each party would say, "Yes, that is what I am asserting, and that is what my opponent denies."

3. A section on cruxes. For each crux—each sub-question such that if it were settled one way, at least one party would change their view—state what it is, and then, for each side, what argument, case, or consideration they believe would settle it, and what they predict the outcome would be.

4. A section on what both parties agree would actually move the debate. Are there empirical discoveries, conceptual clarifications, or new arguments that both would accept as genuine progress, even if they predict different outcomes?

I want the document to be philosophically serious—something a working philosopher in either camp could read without wincing—but I also want it to be legible to a novelist who has spent three years thinking about what it means to look at someone you love and wonder whether anyone is home.
