OK so I'm in a moral philosophy module on Williams and I've ended up with an argument that seems to show his integrity objection eats itself, and I can't find the way out; its proper doing my head in. This stopped being a seminar game for me a few months ago because we had our first kid, and suddenly ground projects and the one thought too many point are just... my actual week. I keep noticing how much of my thinking about whether to go back to full-time work, or how much to give to charity, runs on commitments I can't justify to anyone, and how strange it would feel to feed those commitments through a general principle as if they were just items in a calculation.

Here's the argument, numbered as cleanly as I can manage:

(1) A moral theory is objectionable if it systematically gets agents to act on considerations other than the ones that make sense of their lives — roughly, if it makes them treat their deepest commitments as one more input to be weighed, because that alienates them from their own agency. (This is meant to capture Williams's integrity objection, with the husband who saves his wife as the sharpest case.)
(2) Utilitarianism does exactly this: it tells Jim in the clearing, or the man whose wife is drowning, to deliberate about maximising welfare rather than acting on the love or the horror that actually moves them.
(3) But any moral view worth calling general will sometimes require an agent to step back from a commitment and ask whether the commitment passes those standards — otherwise the view isn't general, it just lets the agent do whatever they were going to do anyway.
(4) So the integrity objection, if it works against utilitarianism, works against every general moral view, including whatever positive story Williams would put in its place (virtue, internal reasons, whatever).
(5) Yet Williams can't be against general moral thought full stop, because his own claims — that reasons are internal, that integrity matters, that utilitarianism alienates — are themselves general claims backed by argument.
(6) Therefore the integrity objection either has a flaw, or it proves there can be no general moral thought at all, which would leave Williams's own critique hanging with no support.

I'd genuinely rather keep (1) and (2) than give up on the idea that there can be general moral thought, but I can see that might be the price. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? And be straight with me: how confident are you in the diagnosis, and which part of your answer are you least sure of?
