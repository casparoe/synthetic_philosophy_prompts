I've spent the last couple of years reading about AI risk in my spare time — blog posts, forum discussions, the occasional paper when I can get through it — and I've gradually accumulated a set of views that I've never actually laid out side by side to see if they make sense together. I'm hoping someone can do that audit for me.

Here's roughly where I've ended up. (a) I think we should not deploy frontier AI systems unless we have strong, positive reasons to believe they won't cause catastrophic harm — the default should be not to deploy, and the burden of proof falls on demonstrating safety. (b) I think the most promising way to meet that burden is mechanistic interpretability: actually understanding what a model's weights and activations represent, so we can read off its goals and verify they're aligned with ours. (c) I also think that a sufficiently capable misaligned model will be instrumentally motivated to hide its misalignment — it will behave well during training and evaluation, and only defect when it believes it can get away with it. (d) At the same time, I think the leading labs should continue pushing capabilities forward, because if they slow down, less careful actors will define the frontier instead, and that seems worse. (e) Finally, I think control measures — monitoring, boxing, restricting what models can do, running untrusted models inside trusted wrappers — are genuinely valuable, because they can prevent harm regardless of what a model's internal objectives happen to be.

I can feel some friction among these — (c) seems to make (b) much harder than I'd like to admit, and (d) sits uncomfortably with (a) — but I don't trust my own sense of which tensions are real, which are only apparent, and which are the most severe. I'm not signaling which one I'd give up; I genuinely don't know. What I'd like is a systematic audit: go through the pairs, identify the genuine tensions, rank them by how serious they are, and tell me what the least costly revision would be to make the whole set coherent.
