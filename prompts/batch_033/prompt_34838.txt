I've been defending a higher-order theory of consciousness — roughly, the view that a mental state is conscious if and only if the subject has a suitable higher-order representation of that state. I'm most drawn to the higher-order thought version: a first-order mental state (say, a visual percept of red) is conscious just in case I have a non-inferential, occurrent thought to the effect that I am in that state. The higher-order thought itself is not typically conscious unless it in turn becomes the target of a yet higher-order thought.

I want to know what would count against this view. I'm not asking for a list of standard objections I can find in a textbook; I want a map of the view's exposure. Which observations, empirical results, thought experiments, or theoretical considerations would genuinely threaten it? Which would be fatal and which merely awkward? I'd like the defeaters ranked by how decisive they are. I also want to know which of these a committed higher-order theorist would concede as counting against the view (rather than dismissing as question-begging or misdescribed), and whether the theory has been qualified in ways that insulate it from falsification — and if so, whether that insulation is a problem for a theory of this kind.

Please structure your response as follows:

1. **The view restated** (short: ~1 paragraph). Restate the higher-order thought theory in its strongest, most defensible form, clarifying what "non-inferential" and "suitable" need to mean for the view to work.

2. **What the view commits itself to** (~2 paragraphs). Spell out what the theory predicts about cases: which creatures or states should and should not be conscious, what kinds of evidence should correlate with consciousness, and what the theory rules out that rival theories (first-order representationalism, global workspace, integrated information) permit.

3. **Defeaters, ranked from fatal to awkward** (the core of the response, ~4–5 paragraphs). For each candidate defeater, state the consideration, explain why it threatens the theory, and assess how decisive it is. Include at least: empirical findings that would contradict the theory's predictions about which creatures or brain states are conscious; conceptual problems (misrepresentation, targetless HOTs, the "just more representation" worry); introspective or phenomenological counterevidence; and theoretical costs the view might incur elsewhere.

4. **What a defender would concede vs. dismiss** (~2 paragraphs). Distinguish the objections a sophisticated higher-order theorist would acknowledge as genuine costs or unresolved problems from those they would reject as resting on misunderstandings of the view.

5. **Insulation and its significance** (~1 paragraph). Assess whether the theory has been qualified in ways that make it unfalsifiable, and whether that matters for a theory of consciousness.
