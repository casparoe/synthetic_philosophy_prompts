I am a novelist. The novel I am writing turns on a long, bitter intellectual quarrel between two decision theorists who are now forced into an adversarial collaboration. One is a committed subjective expected utility theorist: he holds that all uncertainty is, at bottom, probability, and that the Ellsberg choices reveal an error, a confound, or a failure to think through the sure-thing logic. The other is a defender of ambiguity-averse choice: she holds that the Ellsberg pattern is not an error but a stable and defensible preference for known probabilities, to be modeled with a set of priors and a maxmin criterion, or with non-additive probabilities, rather than with a single subjective distribution. Their joint document is a structural element of the novel — chapters will open with excerpts from it, and the plot's resolution depends on the exact wording of what they could and could not sign.

Please draft that document. Treat both positions with full technical respect; neither character is a straw man, and neither is converted. The document should be precise enough that a decision theorist reading the novel would find nothing embarrassing, while the opening agreed claims should still be legible to an intelligent lay reader.

The document should contain:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims. These must include the formal set-up of the Ellsberg paradox — an urn of 90 balls, 30 known to be red and 60 black or yellow in unknown proportion; the two canonical choice pairs (a bet on red versus a bet on black, and a bet on red-or-yellow versus a bet on black-or-yellow); the modal pattern (red in the first pair, black-or-yellow in the second); and the precise sense in which that pattern cannot be represented by any single probability distribution over the three colors. The agreed claims should also record non-vacuous common ground about what the dispute is not — for instance, that it is not about whether people in fact make the modal choices, and not about whether a decision-maker could, by introspection, assign a second-order probability to the urn's composition and thereby reduce the problem to risk.

2. A disagreements table. For each row: the question at issue in neutral language; the expected utility theorist's position, worded so that he would sign it as his own; the ambiguity-averse theorist's position, worded so that she would sign it as hers. Neither position should be phrased as the mere denial of the other's.

3. A section on cruxes. Identify the sub-questions such that settling them would materially move at least one side. I expect the real cruxes to include some of the following, but you should not treat my list as authoritative — if the genuine crux lies elsewhere, say so and justify it: whether the pattern survives when the ambiguity is symmetric across the choice pairs or when the decision-maker is fully informed of the urn's construction and the sampling protocol; whether the pattern is better explained by rational suspicion about the experimenter or by a prior over the urn's composition than by a taste for known probabilities; and whether the pattern is dynamically consistent when the agent is offered opportunities to revise or to hedge across bets.

4. For each crux, the concrete test, argument, or experiment both characters would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance and written in that character's own voice as a commitment, not as a narrator's summary.

Throughout, where part of the disagreement is verbal or terminological — for example, whether the word "aversion" itself begs the question, or whether "belief" may permissibly refer to a set of distributions — say so explicitly, and make clear which part of the disagreement survives once the vocabulary is fixed.

Do not write this as a third-person report of their views. Write the document itself, in the formal, forensic, slightly reluctant shared voice of two people signing something together for the record.
