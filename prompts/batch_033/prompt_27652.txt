I've been thinking for a while now about what makes something the same thing over time — not in any formal or academic way, just turning over cases in my head and trying to get clear on what my own judgments commit me to. I've settled on what I think about a handful of cases, and now I've run into a new one where I genuinely don't know what to say. I'd like you to look at the pattern of my earlier verdicts and tell me what I should say about the new case if I want to be consistent with myself.

Here are the cases I've already worked through and where I stand on them:

Case A — The ship with replaced planks: A wooden ship has each of its planks replaced one by one over many years, until none of the original timber remains. I say it's the same ship at the end. The fact that the materials are all different doesn't matter; what matters is that there was an unbroken history of the ship through each replacement.

Case B — The ship with reassembly: Same setup as A, but someone has been collecting all the discarded original planks and, once the last one is removed, reassembles them into a ship. Now there are two ships. I say the one that stayed in continuous service is the real Ship of Theseus; the reassembled one is a replica built from the original materials.

Case C — The statue and the clay: A sculptor takes a lump of clay and molds it into a statue of a horse. I say the statue just is the clay in a certain shape — there aren't two things occupying the same space. When the sculptor squashes it, the statue ceases to exist and the lump carries on, but that's just the clay losing its statue shape, not evidence that there were two objects all along.

Case D — The mended sweater: My grandmother knit me a wool sweater. Over the years, as sections wore thin, she replaced the yarn bit by bit — first one sleeve, then the collar, then the body — until none of the original yarn remained. I say it's the same sweater. But if she had instead unraveled the whole thing, kept the yarn, and knit a completely different garment like a scarf, I'd say it's not the same sweater, even though the yarn is identical. Gradual replacement preserves identity in a way that total destruction and remaking does not.

Case E — The river: You can't step in the same river twice because the water is always different, yet we still call it the same river. I say it is the same river, because what makes it that river isn't the particular water but the ongoing flow within the same banks and course. If an earthquake suddenly diverted the river into a new channel, I'd say the old river is gone and a new river now flows in the new bed, even if it's the very same water that was flowing a moment before.

Case F — The chess club: A chess club has met every week for thirty years in the same room, under the same name, playing by the same rules. All the founding members have long since left, and none of the current members ever met any of them. I say it's the same club. A club isn't made of its members the way a ship is made of planks; it's the ongoing practice and institutional continuity that matter.

Now here's the case I'm stuck on. A friend of mine comes from a family that once owned a well-known bakery, founded by his great-grandfather. Over the decades, the business was sold to new owners outside the family, moved to a different neighborhood, had its name slightly altered, and shifted its focus from rustic breads to French pastries. But the new owners deliberately preserved the original sourdough starter, kept the original brick oven, hung the founder's portrait on the wall, and told every customer the founding story. Recently, my friend bought the business back, moved it to the original location, and restored the original name. He wants to know: is this the same bakery his great-grandfather founded?

Given my pattern of verdicts across cases A through F, what should I say? I'm less interested in the bare yes or no than in understanding what principle or principles seem to be driving my earlier judgments, whether more than one plausible principle fits those verdicts, and — if they pull in different directions on the bakery case — what each would say and which one fits my overall pattern better. If there's a further case I could think through that would cleanly separate the candidate principles, I'd like to hear what that case is and why it would settle things.
