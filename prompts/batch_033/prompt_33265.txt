I'm a PhD student in metaethics preparing a handout for our department's ethics reading group. We've spent the term on evolutionary debunking arguments — Street, Joyce, Enoch, Vavova, Copp, and a few others — and I want to end with a diagnostic tool: a decision tree that maps the logical space of the debate.

The tree should branch on yes/no questions that someone could answer without knowing the jargon. Each question should be a concrete claim about what follows from what — for example, "If you learned that your conviction that causing unnecessary suffering is wrong was produced by a process that would have produced that conviction whether or not it was actually wrong, would that give you reason to doubt it?" — rather than a "do you agree with Street?" prompt. The leaves should be named positions, each with a one-sentence gloss of what the view commits you to.

The tree must include as leaves: Street's Humean constructivism, Joyce's error-theoretic moral skepticism, Enoch-style third-factor realism, a Copp-style naturalist realist response, and the Vavova-style "debunking proves too much" response. If your tree cannot distinguish some of these — for instance, if Street and Joyce agree on the epistemic upshot but diverge on what to do about it — flag that explicitly and explain what the residual disagreement is about.

I also want you to note any logically possible leaves that are unoccupied: combinations of answers that yield a coherent position but that, as far as you know, nobody defends in print. Give a one-sentence diagnosis for each (e.g., "unattractive because it grants the debunker's central premise and then denies the conclusion on no independent grounds," or "coherent but underexplored").

The tree should be built from the logical space outward: first the questions, then the answer combinations they permit, then the actual positions mapped onto that space. Don't start from a list of names and fit questions to them. The audience is graduate students who have done the reading, so you can assume familiarity with the papers, but the tree itself should be usable by someone who hasn't.

Please keep the response under 800 words, with the tree — questions and leaves — taking up the bulk of that.
