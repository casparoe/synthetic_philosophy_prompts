I'm taking a graduate seminar on social choice theory and collective decision-making, and we've been working through the literature on judgment aggregation. I understand the basic setup of the discursive dilemma: you have a group of individuals, each with logically consistent judgments on a set of interconnected propositions, and when you aggregate their judgments proposition-by-proposition using majority rule, the resulting collective judgment set can be logically inconsistent. The classic illustration is the three-judge court deciding a breach-of-contract case, where each judge's reasoning is internally consistent but majority voting on the two premises — was there a valid contract? was there a breach? — yields a majority that says there was a contract, a majority that says there was no breach, and therefore, by the legal doctrine that liability requires both, a majority against liability, even though a majority of the individual judges found for liability when considering the case holistically.

What I'm struggling with is understanding what exactly makes this a *discursive* dilemma rather than merely a technical aggregation problem — one more entry in the catalogue of things that can go wrong when you add up votes. Pettit and others seem to think it has special significance for deliberative democracy and for the idea that groups can be rational agents in their own right, but I'm not sure I see why the dilemma bites in a way that, say, the Condorcet paradox does not. I'd like a careful explanation of what the dilemma actually consists in: who exactly faces it, and what are they forced to choose between? In particular, I'd like to understand the contrast between the premise-based procedure and the conclusion-based procedure as responses to the dilemma, and why neither response is fully satisfactory if you care about the group's claim to rationality. And I'd also like to know why we can't simply dissolve the problem by denying that groups are the kind of thing that can have beliefs or make judgments in the first place — if groups aren't agents, why should we care whether their "judgments" are consistent? I'm looking for an explanation that engages with the philosophical stakes rather than just restating the formal result or rehearsing the standard examples.
