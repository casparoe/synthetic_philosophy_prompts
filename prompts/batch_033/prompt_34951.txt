I chair the state's Criminal Sentencing Commission. We are in the final stages of revising our actuarial risk assessment instrument, which judges are required to consult at sentencing and in pretrial detention decisions. The instrument works by assigning a defendant a recidivism probability based on the rearrest rate of a statistical reference class that includes the defendant. The problem that has deadlocked the commission for two months is the reference class problem. Any individual defendant belongs to indefinitely many reference classes, and the predicted rearrest rate varies dramatically depending on which one we select. Our analyst can place the same defendant in a class defined by age bracket and prior felony count (yielding, say, a 34% rearrest rate), or by zip code and employment status (yielding 52%), or by the specific offense of conviction (yielding 28%), or by participation in substance-abuse treatment while incarcerated (yielding 19%). The statisticians on our staff have been clear: this is not a technical problem with a technical solution. It is a philosophical problem about how to assign probabilities to singular cases, and there is no consensus in the statistical or philosophical literature on how to choose the uniquely correct reference class.

The commission is now split into three factions. One faction wants to mandate the narrowest reference class that still meets a minimum sample-size threshold, on the grounds that it is the most specific to the individual. A second faction wants to require the instrument to report a range — the minimum and maximum rearrest rates across all reference classes that are statistically reliable — and let the judge weigh the range against the case's particulars. A third faction wants to abandon the actuarial approach entirely and move to a structured clinical assessment model, where a trained evaluator makes a holistic judgment informed by the data but not mechanically bound to any single reference class. I need to draft a decision memo, to be circulated before our next meeting, that recommends one of these approaches — or a fourth, if there is a better one — and explains the reasoning. The memo must take the reference class problem seriously as a philosophical difficulty: it cannot simply pick a class and proceed as if the problem has been solved. But it must also be actionable. The courts need an instrument they can actually use, and the commission needs a policy it can vote on.
