I chair my university's rules and governance committee. Our faculty senate bylaws have a single amendment clause — Article XIV — which reads: "These bylaws may be amended by a two-thirds vote of the full senate." A group of senators has now proposed an amendment to Article XIV itself, changing it to require a four-fifths supermajority instead. They have the two-thirds votes to pass it under the current rule. The senate chair is unsure whether to rule the motion in order and has asked our committee for a written opinion.

The opponents' argument is this: if the amendment passes, then the new rule says it takes four-fifths to amend the bylaws — but the amendment itself only got two-thirds. So the amendment was enacted by a procedure that, according to its own content, wasn't sufficient. That looks like a kind of self-undermining: the amendment can't consistently claim to be valid under the rule it creates. The proponents reply that this is just how amendment procedures work — you use the old rule to change the old rule, and the new rule governs going forward. That's the whole point of having an amendment clause.

Draft me the memo I'll submit to the senate chair. Give me a definite recommendation: rule it in order or out of order. I want the reasoning to take the losing side's strongest point seriously, not just dismiss it. And don't appeal to case law or statutes — there isn't any that squarely addresses this, and I don't want a memo that pretends otherwise. I need a conceptual arguement, not a Westlaw search.
