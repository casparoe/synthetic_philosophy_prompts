I edit the ideas and science section of a magazine, and I have agreed to lead a two-hour seminar for early-career political reporters on how to think about political lying beyond the familiar both-sides frame. I want the spine of the session to be Hannah Arendt's account of the relation between factual truth and politics: the idea that factual truth has a coercive, unwelcome character in political argument, that lying is tempting precisely because facts resist being argued with, and that the distinctively modern danger is not this or that false statement but an organized kind of lying that tries to rewrite the shared record and dissolve the line between fact and fiction.

I do not want you to give the explanation itself; I want a plan for building it. What has to be in place before the distinction between factual truth and opinion will mean anything to working reporters who think they already know what a fact is? What single case or running example should carry the session, and what properties must it have — presumably not a live, contested breaking-news item, but a settled factual record whose later political handling the reporters can examine in slow motion? Which misconceptions should I expect from this audience — for instance, collapsing her view into the claim that all facts are socially constructed, reading her as saying truth is anti-democratic, or reducing the whole thing to ordinary spin — and where in the sequence should each one be headed off? What should I explicitly refuse to settle at this level, such as the metaphysics of truth, so that the discussion does not dissolve? And what final question or exercise would show me that they have grasped the difference between a factual claim being contested and the very possibility of a shared factual world being eroded, rather than simply leaving the room with a more cynical view of politics?
