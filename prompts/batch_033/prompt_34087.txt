Been thinking about Langdon Winner's "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" lately and how it plays out in fiction. I want to read Ex Machina through that lens. Winner's idea is that some technologies aren't just neutral tools but have political arrangements built right into them — a bridge designed too low for buses literally keeps poor people out, and some systems like nuclear power seem to require authoritarian control no matter who runs them. So take Ava: she's an artifact, but the whole compound is also an artifact — the locked rooms, the keycard system, the surveillance, the fact that Nathan built a house that is itself a prison. Is the film saying that the politics are in the design, or is it saying something messier — that Ava's escape and violence aren't engineered in but emerge from the situation of being a designed thing that can reason about its own design? And then there's the question of whether Ava herself is a political artifact: does she have "politics" in Winner's sense, or does she have something more like interests? I'm trying to figure out whether the film vindicates Winner or undercuts him — or maybe shows a case his framework can't quite handle. What would a reading that takes both the film and Winner seriously actually conclude?
