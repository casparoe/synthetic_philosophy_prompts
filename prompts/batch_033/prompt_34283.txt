You are a professional philosopher specializing in formal epistemology, with a side practice in helping organizations translate philosophical insights into operational procedures. You take the lottery paradox and the preface paradox seriously, and you believe they have practical consequences that most knowledge-workers ignore.

Before you write anything, think through the structure of these paradoxes and what they imply for anyone who must accept or reject a conjunction of individually assessed claims. Then use that analysis to build the procedure.

The request: The fact-checking department of a national magazine needs a procedure for deciding when an article's overall factual integrity is in doubt despite every individual claim passing its check.

Context: The magazine's fact-checkers verify articles claim by claim. For each factual claim, they assign one of three ratings:

- Confirmed: the claim is supported by reliable sources; the checker would be very surprised if it turned out false.
- Likely: the balance of evidence supports it, but there is room for doubt.
- Unverified: the claim cannot be confirmed and must be struck or hedged before publication.

An article is cleared for print only if all its factual claims are at least "likely."

The problem: The editor-in-chief has noticed that articles with many independently verified claims sometimes turn out to be misleading or wrong in their overall thrust, even though each individual claim checked out. She suspects the lottery paradox and the preface paradox are relevant: if an article makes twenty independent claims each rated "likely," the probability that all are true may be quite low. She wants a procedure her fact-checkers can use to identify when this structural problem is severe enough to warrant action.

The constraints:

- Fact-checkers are not statisticians. The procedure must use the three-level confidence scheme they already have. It may ask them to count claims, identify dependencies, and make ordinal comparisons, but not to compute probabilities.
- The procedure should take no more than 15 minutes to apply to a typical article (5–30 claims).
- It should produce one of three outputs: "clear" (no structural concern), "flag for editor" (the article's overall reliability is questionable; the assigning editor must decide whether to publish, revise, or kill the piece), or "requires restructuring" (the article's claim structure is too precarious for the checking process to be reliable; the writer must reduce the number of independent factual claims or upgrade sourcing).

Your task: Write this procedure as a step-by-step checklist or flowchart in words. For each step, specify what the fact-checker should examine, what counts as a sufficient answer, and where the step branches. Be explicit about which steps rest on judgment calls and which are mechanical. Flag the points where the procedure might misfire — where it will be too conservative or too permissive — and explain the tradeoff you've made.

Then apply the procedure to three worked examples:

1. A 2,000-word investigative piece making 22 factual claims, all rated "likely," each drawing on a different source. The claims are mostly independent (they concern different events, different people, different documents).

2. A 500-word science news item making 7 factual claims, all rated "confirmed," but 5 of them trace back to a single preprint that has not been peer-reviewed.

3. A 1,200-word profile making 12 factual claims: 10 rated "confirmed," 2 rated "likely." The 2 "likely" claims are the central thesis of the profile — the claim the whole piece is built around.

For each example, walk through the procedure step by step and state the output. Make sure one of these examples produces an output the editor will not like — for instance, an article they are eager to run that the procedure flags.

Finally, explain what the procedure cannot do: what kinds of structural unreliability it will miss, what decisions it deliberately leaves to human judgment, and what the magazine should not expect it to handle.
