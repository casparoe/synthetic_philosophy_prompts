I'm a high school student, and I've been on my school's competitive debate team for two years. Over that time I've noticed a recurring pattern that I don't have a name for, and I can't tell whether it's a recognized failure mode or just something that happens in local tournaments. A side will sometimes win not because its case is stronger, but because its case is too hard to check. In one round, the affirmative spent its constructive speech laying out eight separate contentions, each with sub-points and evidence, so the negative had to spend all of its speeches chasing details; the judge later admitted she could not actually assess most of the contentions and voted on the ones she could follow. In a different setting, I watched someone defend a claim with a long chain of technical steps that nobody in the room, including the judge, could verify in the time available; when the opponent could not locate the exact error on the spot, the chain was treated as sound. The common thread is that the argument's force came from how hard it was to evaluate, rather than from whether it was actually sound.

I've also been reading about a proposal for keeping advanced AI systems honest: two AIs debate a question in front of a human judge, one arguing for the correct answer and one against it, and the human simply decides which side argued better. The hope is that even when the judge cannot verify the answer directly, the truthful system's rebuttals will expose the false one. But the pattern I described seems to threaten this proposal directly. If one debater can make its case so intricate, so long, or so dependent on background knowledge the judge lacks that the judge cannot tell whether the rebuttals actually land, then a less accurate side might beat a more accurate side purely because checking the former's case requires more effort. This feels different from lying, different from drowning someone in many weak arguments where the individual points are bad, and different from an ordinary gap in the judge's expertise that could in principle be closed.

Is there an established name for this phenomenon, and is it a distinct failure mode for the AI-debate proposal, or does it reduce to something already understood? If there is no established name, I'd like a definition that separates it from the flood-of-weak-arguments move, from deliberate vagueness, and from a mere expertise gap, and that says where the boundary lies: how hard to check does an argument have to be before it counts, and does the problem get worse as the debaters become more capable than the judge? A bare label won't satisfy me; I want to know exactly what the phenomenon is and where its edges are.
