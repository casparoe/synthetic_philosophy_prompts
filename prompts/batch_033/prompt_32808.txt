Two evolutionary biologists in my department got into an argument at a seminar last week about the human chin, and I've been turning it over ever since. The chin — that bony protrusion at the front of the mandible — is unique to Homo sapiens; even our closest relatives among the hominins lacked anything quite like it. The argument went roughly as follows:

(1) Mara opened by arguing that the chin is a textbook spandrel in the Gould–Lewontin sense: a structural byproduct of the reduction of the face and dentition during hominin evolution, with no adaptive function of its own. She said the chin emerges as a consequence of the differential growth rates of the alveolar and basal parts of the mandible — when the tooth-bearing portion shrinks faster than the basal bone, you get a projecting chin. Treating it as an adaptation, she said, is a just-so story with no independent evidence.

(2) David pushed back: that's too quick. The chin is a sexually dimorphic trait — more pronounced in men — and it appears at sexual maturity, which is exactly the profile you'd expect for a trait shaped by sexual selection. He said the "spandrel" label is often deployed as a conversation-stopper rather than a testable hypothesis, and that the chin's uniqueness to our species actually makes it more interesting, not less, as a candidate for a selective explanation.

(3) Mara replied that sexual dimorphism doesn't automatically imply sexual selection — plenty of dimorphic traits are byproducts of hormonal differences with no selective history of their own. She also pointed out that the chin has no known mechanical function in chewing or speech that would explain its prominence, and that the burden of proof should be on the adaptationist who claims a trait was shaped by selection, not on the person who says we don't have enough evidence to conclude that.

(4) David answered that the burden-of-proof point is a methodological claim, not a biological one, and that it cuts both ways. In practice, he said, the spandrelist position can be unfalsifiable: any trait you can't explain you can just call a byproduct, and that's not science — it's a concession of ignorance dressed up as an explanation. He added that the adaptationist program has a track record of generating testable predictions, whereas the spandrelist approach often amounts to saying "it's developmental" and stopping.

(5) Mara came back with: the adaptationist position is the one that's unfalsifiable, because for any trait you can always concoct some selective story after the fact — that's exactly what Gould and Lewontin meant by the Panglossian paradigm. The spandrelist approach, she said, at least makes a positive claim: that the chin is predicted by known developmental constraints on mandibular growth. That claim can be tested against the fossil record and against comparative data from other primates, and it doesn't require inventing a selective pressure every time a trait is puzzling.

I wasn't sure who had the better of it, and the seminar ended before anyone else could weigh in. Can you go through this exchange move by move and adjudicate: who is right about which specific point, where does each argument overreach, and what, if anything, are both parties missing about the logic of the adaptationism-versus-spandrelism debate — particularly about how the two positions relate to the broader question of what counts as an evolutionary explanation?
