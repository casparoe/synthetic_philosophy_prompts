For an upper-level undergraduate seminar in political philosophy, I'd like a decision tree on the legitimacy of constitutional precommitment — the idea that a polity can legitimately bind its future decision-making through constitutional provisions that are harder to revise than ordinary legislation. The tree should take a student from a starting question about whether precommitment can ever be legitimate to a named position, with each branch node tracking a genuine fault line in the debate.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names: first identify the questions on which positions genuinely come apart, then locate the actual positions within the space those questions define. Aim for six to eight leaves. The positions that must appear include: the strong Ulysses model (precommitment as rational self-binding that enhances autonomy), the dead-hand objection (constitutions illegitimately bind later generations who never consented), the proceduralist view (precommitment is legitimate only for procedural rules, not for substantive values or ends), and the fictionalist view (precommitment is never genuinely binding but serves as a useful coordination device). If your tree generates unoccupied regions of the logical space, label them and briefly say why no one defends them — whether the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Each branching question should be one a student can answer without already knowing which position it leads to. Phrase them in terms of concrete commitments — for instance, whether hypothetical consent can ground obligation, whether the later self is sufficiently continuous with the earlier one for precommitment to count as self-binding rather than other-binding, whether the possibility of amendment at sufficient cost resolves the legitimacy problem, and whether some decisions are so consequential that insulating them from ordinary politics is justified even if it frustrates present majorities. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate and explain what further distinction would be needed.

Finally, include a short note identifying which branching question in your tree you take to be the most philosophically contestable — the node where a reasonable person's answer is most likely to shift if they encounter the right argument — and say what kind of argument would change your own answer at that node, or where your reasoning about how to structure the tree is most likely to fail.
