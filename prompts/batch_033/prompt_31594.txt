You are an expert in contemporary analytic metaphysics, with a deep command of mereology and of the literature on material composition. I am a philosophy professor putting together an advanced graduate seminar on the Special Composition Question — van Inwagen's question of when, if ever, some things compose a further thing. Before my students read the candidate answers, I want them to have a specification of what any adequate answer must accomplish. Take your time, think the problem through from first principles, and begin by brainstorming a wide range of candidate constraints before you refine, organize, and justify them. Do not simply summarize the standard positions; the deliverable is the list of desiderata, with the reasoning behind each. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations.

Write the desiderata that an adequate answer to the Special Composition Question must meet, where the purpose is a serious metaphysical account of when composition occurs, not a merely practical or conventional one. For each desideratum, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, and say whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of one camp.

Your list should engage at least the following, and show how each generates a constraint:

- the clear cases: a table assembled from its parts, an organism and its cells, a heap of sand, and the trout-turkey, understood as the scattered object consisting of a trout and a turkey;
- the two limiting answers — nihilism, on which nothing ever composes, and universalism, on which any plurality whatever composes — and what a restricted answer must do to place itself between them without drawing a metaphysically arbitrary line;
- the argument from vagueness that purports to show that composition cannot be vague: a restricted answer must either supply a sharp and metaphysically natural boundary, accept that its boundary is arbitrary, or explain where the argument goes wrong;
- the relation between composition and constitution, as in a statue and the lump of clay, and whether an answer to the composition question may simply prescind from it;
- the problem of the many, as in a cloud and its many candidate boundaries, and whether a theory of composition must dissolve it or may inherit it;
- the epistemic status of ordinary composite objects: whether the theory vindicates, revises, or explains away our ordinary beliefs, and at what cost;
- the question whether composites are something over and above their parts, and whether an answer to the composition question may remain neutral on it.

After the list, flag any subset of the desiderata that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and identify the single requirement that does the most to exclude the main contenders. If the upshot is that the standard list is itself inconsistent, so that every answer must violate at least one plausible requirement, say so explicitly — that is the part I most want to see. End with a brief scorecard of how nihilism, universalism, and the view that composition occurs exactly when some things are caught up in a single life fare against your list.
