Two engineers on my team got into an argument at lunch about insect sentience and I can't figure out who's right. They weren't debating whether insects actually are sentient — they agreed the science is unsettled. The dispute was about what's called the "scale argument": the idea that even if the probability of insect sentience is very low, the sheer number of insects (on the order of 10^18 at any given time) means the expected amount of suffering is so vast that it demands serious moral attention.

Here's roughly how it went.

(1) Dan said the scale argument is basically sound. If you assign even a 1% credence to insects being sentient, the expected number of suffering insects is around 10^16, which dwarfs most other moral concerns. He said refusing to act on that because the probability is low is just scope insensitivity.

(2) Mara pushed back: "1% is pulled out of the air. The probability could be orders of magnitude lower. And at some point, multiplying a tiny probability by a huge number is just a formal trick — we should demand some minimum evidential threshold before we treat something as a moral patient."

(3) Dan replied that this is exactly how we reason about other risks. We take a 1% chance of killing a person very seriously. The math doesn't change just because the numbers are large: a 0.0001% chance of killing a quadrillion beings is the same expected death toll. Refusing to multiply because the probability feels small is the fallacy.

(4) Mara said the cases aren't analogous because the probabilities aren't independent. If we're wrong about whether insects are sentient, we're wrong about all of them — it's one binary question applied to a large population, not a bunch of independent dice rolls. The expected-value framing treats each insect as an independent gamble, which misrepresents the structure of the uncertainty.

(5) Dan said that doesn't matter: expected value doesn't require independence. If you're uncertain whether a room contains a person, you multiply the probability by the harm of one death. If you're uncertain whether 10^18 insects are sentient, you multiply by 10^18. The uncertainty is about the predicate, but the expected value calculation is the same.

(6) Mara said this leads to a reductio. If the argument works for any non-zero credence, it works for 10^-100, which is absurd. There has to be a cutoff or a different way of handling the probability. She said Dan's position implies we should be paralyzed by an infinite number of possible moral patients across any domain where we can't rule things out with certainty.

(7) Dan said the reductio only works if you think credences can be arbitrarily low in a way that matters. In practice, he said, our credence in insect sentience is constrained by analogy to mammals, behavioral evidence, and neural complexity — it's not 10^-100, it's in some range like 0.1% to 10%, and anywhere in that range the numbers are still staggering.

They went back and forth for a while and never resolved it.
