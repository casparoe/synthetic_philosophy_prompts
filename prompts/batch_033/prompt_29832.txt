I'm a software engineer at a mid-size company and I end up running the postmortem for most of our production incidents — maybe two or three a month. One thing that keeps coming up is whether the incident was caused by something we didn't do: a check we didn't write, a runbook step that got skipped, an alert threshold we never set, a test that was never added. We write these up as causes or contributing factors, and the wording drives what gets fixed and sometimes who gets blamed; in a couple cases our customer contracts have SLA credits tied to the root-cause wording, so its not just an internal label. I have no philosophy background, but a friend of mine does and he says there's a whole literature on causation by omission — whether absences can be causes at all, whether the right test is counterfactual ('would the incident still have happened if we'd done the thing?'), and whether the answer secretly depends on what we expected somebody to do. He says our postmortem fights are basically that debate with a Jira ticket attached.

What I want is a reusable decision procedure my team can actually run on an incident report — a checklist or flowchart in words — for deciding when an omission should be listed as a cause of an incident. It has to be something an ordinary engineer can run in maybe 20 minutes during a postmortem, without me in the room to explain the theory. It should say what questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops and says 'not a cause' or 'contributing factor', and where it has to hand the decision to a person because it's a values call rather than a factual one. Please ground it in whatever the philosophy of causation actually knows about omissions, but translated into language we can use: keep the distinctions that survive the translation, and tell me which ones you dropped and why. Then run the procedure on three anonymized cases from our backlog: (1) a database outage where nobody had set a max-connection limit and a runaway query exhausted the pool; (2) an incident where the on-call engineer skipped a step in the runbook that would have caught a bad config before deploy, but the step had been wrong or misleading for months and everyone who had run it recently had skipped it too; (3) an incident where two independent safeguards were both missing — a validation check and an alert — and either one alone probably would have prevented the customer-facing error. I especially want to see a case where the procedure gives an answer my team won't like, and I want you to be honest about what the procedure cannot do — where it's really a management or blame question dressed up as a causal one.
