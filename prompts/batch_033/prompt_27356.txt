I'm giving a research talk next month to the safety and evaluations group at a large AI lab, and I'm defending the following thesis: for systems capable of catastrophic failure, average-case performance metrics are systematically the wrong kind of evidence for deployment decisions, and the field should reorganize safety evaluation around explicitly bounded worst-case guarantees — over specified adversarial perturbation sets, adversary models, or agreed failure classes — even when those guarantees are partial and conservative. My argument, in outline, is that expected loss over a fixed benchmark distribution is dominated by typical-case behavior and can improve while tail risk worsens; that deployment distributions shift and contain adversarial or near-adversarial inputs, so the benchmark distribution is not the relevant one; and that for catastrophic risk what matters is the worst plausible loss rather than the mean. I conclude that a system should not advance toward deployment without some such bound, and that the burden of argument should fall on anyone claiming the relevant worst-case set is too narrow or too broad.

The audience is mostly empirical ML researchers and engineers who build evaluation suites, plus a few safety researchers who work on red-teaming and robustness, and one or two safety-case leads. They care about not shipping dangerous systems, but they are skeptical of guarantees that do not survive contact with real deployment; they worry that worst-case framing will block useful systems over contrived failures, and they suspect that chasing worst-case bounds will itself become a target that gets gamed.
