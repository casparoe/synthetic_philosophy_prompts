I'm preparing a unit on the metaphysics of spacetime for my graduate seminar next month, and I keep returning to an argument that has bothered me since I first studied it as a doctoral student. The hole argument, in the form John Earman and John Norton gave it, seems to prove that a perfectly natural realist view about spacetime — substantivalism, the thesis that spacetime points are genuine entities with their own identity, not merely a relational structure parasitic on matter fields — entails that general relativity is radically indeterministic. The reasoning feels airtight, yet the conclusion seems to demand that we abandon something we have independent reason to want. I am not looking for a survey of the literature; I am asking where, precisely, the argument goes wrong and what we pay to say so.

Here is the argument as I would present it to my students:

(1) General relativity is a deterministic theory in the following sense: specify the metric and matter fields on a Cauchy surface, together with the Einstein field equations, and the state of the entire spacetime manifold is thereby determined.

(2) The field equations are generally covariant. This means that if ⟨M, g, T⟩ is a model — M a manifold of spacetime points, g the metric field, T the stress-energy tensor — then for any diffeomorphism d on M, the tuple ⟨M, d*g, d*T⟩ is also a model that satisfies the same equations.

(3) According to substantivalism, the points of M are real individuals. Applying a diffeomorphism shifts the geometrical and material properties from one set of points to another, and this yields a genuinely distinct physical situation, not merely a notational variant or a redescription of the same world.

(4) Now select a "hole": a proper open region H of M. Choose a diffeomorphism d that acts as the identity everywhere outside H but is non-trivial inside H. Take a model whose fields are fully fixed on a Cauchy surface lying outside H. The diffeomorphically transformed model agrees with the original on that entire Cauchy surface and on its causal past, but diverges from the original inside H.

(5) From (2) and (3), these two models describe distinct possible worlds, both consistent with the laws and both sharing exactly the same initial data.

(6) Therefore, if substantivalism is true, GR is radically indeterministic: specifying the data on a Cauchy surface fails to pick out a unique future evolution, because for every lawful evolution there exists a diffeomorphically scrambled counterpart, equally lawful, that diverges from the first inside the hole.

(7) This conclusion is intolerable. GR is not radically indeterministic in this sense; the field equations are not pathologically deficient the way this reasoning suggests.

(8) Hence, by reductio, substantivalism is false.

The difficulty, as I see it, is that rejecting substantivalism appears to push us toward relationism, which has its own well-known troubles — accounting for inertial structure, making sense of empty spacetimes, and explaining the success of the substantivalist's explanatory practices without surreptitiously borrowing them. Sophisticated substantivalists try to deny premise (3) by insisting that diffeomorphically related models represent the same physical situation, but I remain unsure whether this move genuinely preserves realism about spacetime or merely renames the relationist's position. Others have tried to reject premise (1) by arguing that determinism should be understood at the level of gauge-equivalence classes rather than individual models, but I worry this simply relocates the problem without solving it.

I would like you to diagnose the argument. Which premise or premises would you reject, and what are the philosophical costs of doing so? I am particularly interested in whether premise (3) can be denied in a way that does not collapse into relationism, and whether anyone has succeeded in walking that line without changing the subject. If you think the argument is valid and we must simply accept one of the unpalatable conclusions, tell me which one is least costly and why.
