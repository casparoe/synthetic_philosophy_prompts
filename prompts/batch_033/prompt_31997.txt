The debate between democratic theorists and epistocrats has been running in political philosophy for at least two decades, and by now the moves on both sides are thoroughly mapped. The epistocrat argues that we would not let uninformed people perform surgery or fly a plane, so it is puzzling why we let them choose the government; the democrat replies that political equality is a requirement of justice, not a mere instrument, and that any competence test will encode the biases of whoever designs it; the epistocrat replies that existing democracies already restrict the franchise by age, citizenship, and mental competence, so the question is only where to draw the line, not whether to draw one; the democrat replies that the historical track record of epistocratic proposals is a series of exclusionary nightmares; and the debate restarts. I want to understand why this debate has stalled. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not really about the likely consequences of epistocratic institutions — both sides are speculating about counterfactuals that neither can test — but about whether political equality is a basic requirement of justice, in which case no gain in outcomes could justify epistocracy, or a purely instrumental arrangement, in which case it could in principle be traded off. Diagnose the structure of this disagreement: is it a clash of bedrock normative intuitions that neither side can argue for, a hidden empirical disagreement about how badly democratic governments actually perform, a disagreement about the burden of proof, or something else? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — or is the impasse itself telling us something about the limits of what political philosophy can settle?
