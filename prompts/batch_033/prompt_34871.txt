I'm a student in an upper-level undergraduate seminar on contemporary ethical theory, and I've been asked to help draft a question for the final exam. The course spent three weeks on Peter Singer's argument that our duties to the global poor are far more extensive than we ordinarily acknowledge, grounded in the drowning-child analogy from his 1972 paper "Famine, Affluence, and Morality." The professor wants a question that separates students who have genuinely worked through the argument's structure from those who can only rehearse the analogy.

Please write:

1. An exam question that focuses on the role of salience in Singer's argument. In the drowning-child case, the child's need is vividly and immediately present to the rescuer; in the global-poverty case, the suffering is distant and mediated by reports and statistics. Singer treats this difference as morally irrelevant to the duty to rescue. The question should ask students to evaluate whether salience really is morally irrelevant in the way Singer needs it to be, and it should require them to engage with at least one serious argument that salience does matter morally — not just psychologically or motivationally. The question should be answerable in about 90 minutes.

2. A model answer of roughly 700–900 words. The model answer should not simply defend Singer; it should work through the strongest version of the objection and then assess whether Singer's framework can absorb it, deflect it, or must be revised in response to it.

3. A marking scheme that distinguishes an A, a B, and a C answer. For each grade band, name the specific moves the answer must contain (or fail to contain). Also identify at least two common misunderstandings that would pull an otherwise competent answer down — for example, confusing salience with proximity, or treating the objection as merely about motivational psychology rather than about the normative structure of duties.

In your model answer, please indicate how confident you are in each of the main argumentative moves and single out the point you are least sure of. The model answer should itself reflect the fact that this is a live philosophical issue rather than a settled one.
