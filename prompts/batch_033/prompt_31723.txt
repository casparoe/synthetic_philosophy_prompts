Question: Explain the distinction between meritocratic and desert-based principles of distributive justice. Give one example of a case in which the two principles would recommend different distributions, and discuss what this divergence reveals about the normative foundations of meritocracy.

Answer: The distinction between meritocracy and desert-based justice is that meritocracy distributes goods based on merit — roughly, a combination of talent and effort that produces valuable outcomes — while desert-based justice distributes goods based on what people morally deserve. The key difference is that desert is a moral notion that can take into account factors beyond productive contribution, such as need, sacrifice, or the quality of one's will, whereas merit is typically understood in terms of demonstrated ability and achievement.

A classic example where they diverge is the case of two equally talented and hardworking people who both strive for the same promotion. One succeeds because of favorable market conditions; the other fails because of a recession that kills the project. On a meritocratic view, the one who succeeded has demonstrated more merit, since merit is partly about actual outcomes, and therefore deserves the promotion. On a desert-based view, both are equally deserving because they put in the same effort and have the same talents, and the difference in outcomes is purely a matter of luck.

This divergence reveals that meritocracy can be unfair because it rewards luck. Rawls famously criticized meritocracy on these grounds, arguing that since our talents and even our willingness to exert effort are shaped by the natural and social lottery, distributing goods based on merit effectively rewards people for factors they did not earn. Rawls therefore advocated for a desert-based distribution governed by the difference principle, which ensures that inequalities benefit the least advantaged. On this view, people deserve the fruits of their choices only if the system of rewards is structured to help everyone, especially the worst-off.

In conclusion, while meritocracy and desert-based justice overlap in many cases, they come apart when luck plays a role in determining outcomes, and this divergence shows that meritocracy lacks a sound normative foundation unless it is grounded in a deeper principle of desert.
