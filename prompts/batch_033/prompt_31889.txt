ok so I've been going in circles on this for like two weeks and I think I've built an argument that proves way too much, but I can't find the bad step. it's about Max Black's two-sphere universe.

quick setup: Black asks us to imagine a universe containing nothing but two iron spheres, exactly alike in every qualitative respect — same size, shape, composition, temperature, everything. they're two feet apart. the question is: are they one object or two? Black says they're obviously two. but the identity of indiscernibles (PII) says no two distinct objects can share all properties. so if they're two, PII is false — unless we count relational properties (like "being two feet from the other sphere") as the properties PII ranges over.

here's the argument I built. it seems to show that if Black's spheres are two, we can never count objects in any situation, which is obviously nuts.

(1) suppose Black's spheres are two distinct objects. (assume for reductio)
(2) they share all qualitative intrinsic properties, by construction.
(3) so what makes them two must be either (a) their relational properties, or (b) a primitive non-qualitative thisness — a haecceity — that each has and the other lacks.
(4) take (a) first: sphere A has the property "being two feet from sphere B" and sphere B has the property "being two feet from sphere A." these are different relational properties. so PII is fine as long as we count relational properties.
(5) but now consider a universe with just one sphere. by the same reasoning, can't we say it's actually two spheres that exactly coincide? they share all intrinsic properties, and they also share all relational properties — there's nothing else in the universe for them to relate to differently. they are indiscernible even with respect to relational properties. so if we're allowed to count two objects in the Black case, what stops us from counting two in the single-sphere case? and if two, why not seventeen? why not infinitely many?
(6) take (b) instead: objects have primitive haecceities. but then for any object or collection of objects, there's no qualitative constraint on how many haecceities are present. a single chair could be one chair, or seventeen coincident chairs with distinct haecceities, and the qualitative facts are exactly the same either way. so we can never know, from any qualitative inspection, how many objects are in a situation.
(7) but we do know how many chairs are in the room. we do it all the time.
(8) so (1) must be false: Black's spheres are not two distinct objects. they are one.

but that conclusion also seems insane — they're two feet apart! they're at different places! how could they be one object?

so the argument seems to prove that either Black's two spheres are one object (insane) or we can never count objects (also insane). where did I go wrong? be specific about which step and why it looked fine, and tell me what I have to give up if I reject it.
