I am drafting a short article for a volume on early modern ethics, and I would be grateful for your comments on the central argument before I develop it further. The article concerns a tension in Leibniz's account of disinterested love. Leibniz analyzes love as taking pleasure in the happiness or perfection of another, and he defines justice as universal benevolence ordered by wisdom. At the same time, in the debates over pure love that set Fénelon and Bossuet against one another, Leibniz wants a middle path: as I read him, he holds that the beloved's good is the end of love, while the pleasure is only an effect or accompaniment of perceiving that good, not the end for the sake of which one loves. A genuinely disinterested love therefore need not be purged of all pleasure.

My worry is that this end-effect distinction is less stable than it looks, given the initial definition. If the pleasure is not a contingent by-product but part of what makes an attitude count as love at all, then the other's good is being pursued as the intentional content of a state whose value for the agent consists partly in its being pleasant. The question is whether Leibniz can distinguish the content of the end from the source of its value for the agent, and whether that distinction is enough to deflect the charge that his account collapses disinterested love into a refined egoism. I am inclined to think the distinction does real work, but that it needs to be supplemented by Leibniz's recurring idea that in genuine love one takes the other's good as one's own, so that the contrast between self-interest and other-interest is softened rather than resolved.

Could you comment on three things? First, whether this reconstruction of Leibniz's position is fair, especially the use of the end-effect distinction. Second, whether the objection is best framed as a psychological point about motivation or a normative point about the kind of concern love requires. Third, whether the proposed supplement, taking the other's good as one's own, actually addresses the worry or merely relocates it.
