I'm taking a course on the philosophy of intellectual property this term and something's been bugging me that I keep going back and forth on. We've got these three practices that all seem to be about "owning ideas," but they pull in totally different directions. Copyright law says you can't own an idea, only the particular expression of it: if I independently write a song with the same chord progression and even a similar melody, I'm fine, but if I copy your exact lyrics, that's infringement. Facts, plot premises, and general methods are free for anyone to use. Patent law then seems to do the opposite—it hands someone a temporary monopoly on an idea or invention, so long as it's new, useful, and non-obvious, and the inventor has to fully disclose how it works. But a law of nature or a mathematical formula can't be patented no matter how clever the discovery. And then academic norms are different again: if I take someone's idea and put it in my own words, that's still plagiarism, even if the source is in the public domain, and even if the author has been dead for centuries—copyright law wouldn't care at all. So one practice treats ideas as unownable, another treats them as ownable only when they're novel and disclosed, and a third treats them as ownable forever in a reputational sense, where you owe attribution for any idea you didn't think of yourself.

I'm trying to figure out whether these three practices are actually inconsistent, or whether they're just tracking different things under the same word "idea." What view of the ownership of ideas does this pattern presuppose, if any single view? Reconstruct the implicit theory or theories that would make sense of it, be careful to seperate what the practices strictly presuppose from what they merely happen to be compatible with, and then tell me what would have to change if we rebuilt the whole system on one theory—say a Lockean labor-mixing view of ownership, or a pure incentive view where ideas get protected only insofar as that encourages people to create and disclose them. It just seems wierd that the same culture runs on three different answers to the same question.
