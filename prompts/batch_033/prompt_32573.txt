I'm giving a department colloquium defending the claim that qualia are an incoherent posit—that there is no fact of the matter about whether a quale has changed or one's reactive judgment of it has changed, and that the concept cannot carry the explanatory weight asked of it. My central argument is Dennett's Chase and Sanborn intuition pump: two coffee tasters at Maxwell House each report that they used to like the coffee but no longer do; Chase says the taste is the same and his standards have risen, Sanborn says the taste itself has changed. I argue that nothing in the first-person data, the third-person data, or the underlying neurology could settle whether these are two instances of the same phenomenon or two different ones, and that this in-principle indeterminacy shows the concept of qualia is empty.

The audience is a philosophy department with a strong philosophy-of-mind group: three or four phenomenal realists (one wrote a dissertation on Chalmers, another works on the knowledge argument), two representationalists who think qualia are real but reduce to intentional content, one illusionist broadly sympathetic to my position, and several epistemologists and language-philosophers who will care more about the structure of the argument than about consciousness per se. I have forty minutes for Q&A.

Give me a prep sheet for the Q&A: the objections I will actually get from this audience, ordered by how much damage they would do to my argument, each phrased the way a member of this audience would put it. For each objection, give me a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is weakest. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks. Keep the whole response to at most 800 words.
