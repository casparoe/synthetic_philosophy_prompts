Under international humanitarian law, a soldier who kills an enemy soldier in combat hasn't commited a crime, even if he's fighting for the aggressor in a plainly unjust war and even if he volunteered. But if the same soldier deliberately kills a civilian, that's a war crime, even if the civilian's death would have helped his side more than killing the enemy soldier would. So the law treats being in uniform as a kind of switch that makes you killable, and treats the justice of the cause as irrelevant to whether killing you is lawful. What theory of moral liability does this practice presuppose? Does it actually presuppose the moral equality of combatants—that soldiers on both sides have the same permission to kill each other—or is it compatible with the view that unjust combatants do wrong but the law simply declines to punish them for it? And how does that square with the fact that the same legal system will prosecute the leaders who planned the aggressive war while leaving the ordinary soldiers who carried it out alone? I want the implicit theory reconstructed, with a clear statement of what the practice strictly presupposes versus what it merely fits with, and a sense of what would have to change in the law if we replaced that presupposed view with the view that a soldier's liability to be killed depends on wether his side is fighting justly.
