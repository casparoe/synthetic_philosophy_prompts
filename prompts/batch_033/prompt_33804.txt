A friend of mine teaches a unit on experimental philosophy and has a stable set of verdicts on the standard "side-effect" vignettes. I have been trying to reverse-engineer the rule she is actually running, because I suspect the famous textbook asymmetry is too coarse to capture her. I would like you to model her as a classifier over cases and predict her next verdict, with the reasoning made explicit.

All of her vignettes share the same template. A manager is told that a plan will achieve a goal and will also have a foreseen side effect; the manager says she cares only about the goal and not at all about the side effect; the plan goes ahead and the side effect occurs. The question is always whether the manager brought about the side effect intentionally. Here are her verdicts.

1. Wetland damage. Goal: profit. Side effect: a nearby wetland is damaged. Verdict: intentional.
2. Wetland cleanup. Goal: profit. Side effect: a nearby wetland is cleaned up. Verdict: not intentional.
3. Longer shifts. Goal: profit. Side effect: warehouse staff are put on longer shifts, in breach of the company's own safety rules. Verdict: intentional.
4. Accessibility. Goal: profit. Side effect: the office lobby becomes wheelchair-accessible. Verdict: not intentional.
5. Rival. Goal: profit. Side effect: a smaller competitor is driven out of business by an aggressive but legal pricing change that breaks no law, regulation, or agreement and is within standard competitive practice. Verdict: not intentional.

I can already see that a flat rule that says bad side effects are intentional and good ones are not cannot survive her verdict on the rival case, so I am trying to work out what more precise rule does.

The new case is this. A manager is told that a project will increase profit and will also clean up a nearby wetland, but the cleanup method will exceed a technical discharge limit in the company's permit. The exceedance is harmless: the regulator's own analysis concludes it would improve the wetland and pose no risk, but it is still a breach of the permit. The manager says she cares only about profit and not at all about the permit or the cleanup. The project goes ahead, and the wetland is cleaned up. Did she intentionally clean up the wetland?

Predict my friend's verdict, but I care much less about the bare prediction than about the rule that generates it. Please identify the candidate rules that fit all five of her earlier verdicts, say what each predicts for the new case, give your best guess with the reasons you prefer that rule, and specify one further vignette on the same template that would cleanly discriminate among whatever candidates remain after the new case is considered.

Return your answer as a JSON object with this exact schema:

- "candidate_rules": an array of objects, each with:
  - "name": a short label for the rule;
  - "statement": the rule in one or two sentences;
  - "fits_prior_verdicts": true only if the rule gets all five earlier verdicts right;
  - "prediction_for_new_case": one of "intentional", "not intentional", "lean intentional", or "lean not intentional";
  - "reasoning": how the rule reaches that prediction on the new case.
- "best_guess": an object with:
  - "verdict": your predicted verdict for the new case;
  - "preferred_rule": the name of the rule your prediction rests on;
  - "confidence": "low", "medium", or "high";
  - "reason": why you prefer that rule over the other candidates that also fit her earlier verdicts.
- "discriminating_case": an object with:
  - "case": a one-paragraph vignette on the same template;
  - "prediction_under_each_rule": a map from each candidate rule name to the verdict it predicts for that vignette;
  - "what_it_would_show": what each possible verdict from my friend would confirm or eliminate.
- "residual_uncertainty": a short note on what her five earlier verdicts and the new case still leave open, and why you could not resolve it from the data given.
