I've got myself in a tangle over Dutch book arguments and I can't find the way out. A friend lays it out like this. (1) If your degrees of belief don't satisfy the probability axioms, a bookie can offer you a set of bets, each of which you'll accept at your own fair odds, that together guarentee you lose money no matter what happens. (2) So rationality requires degrees of belief to satisfy the probability axioms. (3) But the same style of argument seems to work against a reasoner whose degrees of belief do satisfy the axioms but who hasn't yet noticed all the logical consequences of what they believe: the bookie just exploits the gap between what the reasoner will accept and what their beliefs already commit them to. (4) No finite reasoner can track all the logical consequences of their beliefs. (5) So the Dutch book argument would show that every finite reasoner is irrational. (6) That's absurd. (7) So the Dutch book argument must be unsound, and probability-coherence isn't a rational requirement after all. I'd like to keep (2), actualy, but I can't see which step to give up or whether the move from (2) to (3) is even legitimate. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
