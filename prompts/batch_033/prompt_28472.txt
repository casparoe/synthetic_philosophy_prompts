I've been going down a rabbit hole on the cognitive penetration of perception debate and honestly I'm starting to lose my mind. You know the question: can what you believe or want literally change how things look to you — not just how you interpret them, but the perceptual experience itself? The debate's been running for decades and it feels totally gridlocked. On one side you've got the penetrability folks pointing to studies where, e.g., wearing a heavy backpack makes a hill look steeper, or where people who've just eaten a bunch of calories see portion sizes differently, or where holding a gun biases you toward seeing neutral objects as guns. On the other side you've got the impenetrability camp (channeling Fodor's modularity) arguing that all these effects are post-perceptual — they're judgment biases, response biases, or shifts in attention, not changes in perception proper. They've got their own studies and their own devestating methodological critiques of the other side's paradigms. And round and round it goes.

What's bugging me is that I can't even tell what kind of stalemate this is anymore. Is it a substantive empirical disagreement where one killer experiment could settle it, but we just haven't designed it yet? Is it a verbal dispute where the two sides are using "perception" to mean different things — one means conscious visual experience, the other means the output of some early encapsulated module? Is it a disagreement about standards of evidence, where each side's methodological scruples conveniently rule out the other side's best evidence? Or is the whole question ill-posed because there's no sharp joint between perception and cognition in the first place?

I want you to think like a philosopher of cognitive science who's been watching this debate for years. Start by brainstorming the possible diagnoses, then give me your best account of what's really keeping this thing stuck. And be honest: is there a path forward, or should we just admit the question was badly framed from the start?
