I'm taking a set theory course this semester and we just spent a week on equivalent formulations of the axiom of choice. My professor kept saying that AC, Zorn's lemma, and the well-ordering theorem are all equivalent over ZF, but that the equivalence is a bit misleading because the two directions aren't equally hard. I tried showing that Zorn implies AC and it felt almost too easy: you take a family of nonempty sets, consider the partially ordered set of partial choice functions ordered by extension, check that every chain has an upper bound (the union), apply Zorn, and then argue that a maximal partial choice function has to be defined on everything. That's like five lines.

But then I looked at the other direction, AC implies Zorn, and it seems to need transfinite recursion, or Hartogs' theorem, or something much heavier. I'm not asking you to walk through the whole proof. I want to understand the conceptual difference between the two directions: why is one direction nearly trivial and the other not, even though they're logically equivalent? And if Zorn's lemma is equivalent to AC anyway, why do algebraists reach for it all the time to get things like maximal ideals or bases? What does Zorn package up that AC alone dosen't? I feel like I'm missing the point of why this lemma has its own name.
