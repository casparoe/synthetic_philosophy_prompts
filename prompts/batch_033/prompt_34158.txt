ok so I'm on the ethics advisory board of a fertility clinic and we have to get a decision memo round to the medical director by friday. We already offer PGT for single-gene disorders (Tay-Sachs, CF, that sort of thing) and for chromosomal abnormalities, and the board is fine with that. Two of our physicians want to add an optional extra: polygenic embryo screening for a panel of non-disease traits — predicted height, educational attainment, and a general health index built from a genome-wide score. Patients would see the scores alongside the disease results and could use them to choose among viable embryos. Half the board says this is just an extension of what we already do (we already rank embryos for disease risk, so adding more information respects patient autonomy). The other half says it crosses a line from preventing disease to selecting children for expected advantages, and a clinic shouldn't be in that business even if patients want it.

I was arguing about this with my friend last night, and he kept pushing that the disease/enhancement line is hopelessly vague anyway — parents already shape their kids' expected outcomes by choosing a partner, deciding when to conceive, moving to a good school district, and nobody calls that eugenics. My counter was that there's something different about selecting among embryos trait by trait, but I couldn't quite make it stick, and now I'm worried our policy would be built on a distinction we can't defend.

Draft the short memo I'll circulate: it should recommend a concrete policy (offer it, refuse it, or offer it with conditions), give the actual reasons, and take seriously the strongest argument on the other side — not a strawman. If you think the board's framing is off, or the disease/enhancement distinction can't carry the weight we're putting on it, say that plainly and tell me what we should be asking instead, rather than just writing the memo around a broken distinction. And be honest about how confident the recommendation is — if it's close, say it's close.
