I teach a graduate seminar on decision-making under uncertainty (about 16 students, one 90-minute session), and I have recently been making professional decisions in which I caught myself treating options with known probabilities quite differently from options whose probabilities I could not pin down, so I want to teach this material in a way that actually lands. Design an in-class game that makes students feel the force of ambiguity aversion before any theory is named. They already know expected utility theory but have not seen the Ellsberg paradox. The game should have them make a series of real, points-based choices between lotteries with known composition (for instance, an urn with 50 red and 50 black balls) and lotteries with unknown composition (for instance, an urn with 100 balls in unknown red/black proportions), arranged so that most students prefer the known-probability option in both directions and thereby produce a pattern that no single assignment of subjective probabilities can rationalize. Include at least one control pair where both options have known probabilities, so the class can see that what is doing the work is not ordinary risk aversion. Give me exact rules, materials, a payoff scheme that does not require real money, and a debrief that reconstructs the students' choices on the board and shows exactly where expected utility fails. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it—students hedging, changing answers to look consistent, or being primed by the setup—and how to handle it, and be frank about which feature of ambiguity aversion the game cannot reproduce on its own (for example, the distinction between ambiguity about the urn's composition and mere uncertainty about the draw) and that I will need to supply.
