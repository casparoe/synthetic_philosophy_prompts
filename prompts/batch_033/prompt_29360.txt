Me and a friend got into it last night about the dogmatism paradox, and I'm still annoyed about it. He was defending the line that if you genuinely know something, then any evidence against it has to be misleading — because what you know is true, so evidence against it points away from the truth. From that he concluded you're rationally allowed to shrugg off counterevidence without even locating the flaw in it. I kept saying that can't be right, because it reads like a liscense for anyone to dismiss anything they're confident about, but I couldn't pin down exactly which step was the bad one.

Before I go read the back-and-forth, I want the spec instead. What would a theory of how a rational person should respond to evidence against something they know actually have to deliver? Give me the requirements list: the cases it has to get right (the person who runs the same "I know, so this is misleading" move on a false or crazy belief; the person who learns their own reliability is worse than they thought; the known proposition that gets a serious-looking challenge; the case where you can't yet say what's wrong with the evidence), the distinctions it can't collapse (lowering confidence vs reopening the question, misleading evidence vs evidence you can't yet explain, first-person vs third-person uses of the move), and the structural constraints it has to respect — factivity, closure of knowledge under known entailment, and the fact that knowers are fallible. For each requirement say what case or consideration forces it, and say whether it's non-negotiable or just one camp's preference. Then flag which pairs of requirements you suspect can't be jointly satisfied, and tell me which single requirement does the most to rule out the easy answers. Skip the scorecard of named authors and anything I'd have to look up; I want the criteria themselves.
