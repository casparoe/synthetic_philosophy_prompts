I'm a few weeks into an epistemology course and we've been working through the Meno. There's this passage where Socrates asks why knowledge is more valuable than true belief, and the example is that if you want to walk to Larissa, a true belief about the road works just as well as knowledge. The discussion in class has been going in circles and I'm getting frustrated.

Here's where I'm stuck. The way I see it, the whole problem dissolves once you have the justified-true-belief analysis of knowledge. If you just have a true belief — say, you guess the right road and happen to be correct — then your belief is true by accident. It's unreliable. It won't generalize to other cases. Knowledge, by contrast, involves justification, which means your belief is connected to the truth in the right way. So obviously knowledge is more valuable: it's reliable, it's stable, you can use it to reason further. My professor says I'm confusing the Meno problem with a different question, and that reliability isn't the point. But then what is the point? It feels like Socrates makes a problem where there isn't one, and twenty-five hundred years of philosophy has just been nodding along. What am I not getting?
