I'm a PhD student in philosophy of biology, and I've been working through the literature on definitions of life for the past several months. I'm finding that most papers I read start by proposing a definition — metabolic, genetic, autopoietic, Darwinian, you name it — and then defend it against counterexamples, but I want to step back and get clearer on what the criteria for a good definition even are before I evaluate any particular proposal. The problem is that the desiderata people implicitly bring to the table seem to pull in fundamentally different directions. Some want a definition that captures all and only the things we pre-theoretically count as alive. Some want one that picks out a natural kind, so that the category "life" carves nature at its joints rather than at our contingent intuitions. Some want a definition that is operationalizable for astrobiology or origins-of-life research — something you could actually use to decide whether a sample from Mars or a self-organizing chemical system in a lab counts. And some want one that is purely structural, abstracting away from the specific chemistry of Earth life so as not to be parochial. I'm not convinced these are all compatible, and I suspect the literature would benefit from making the tensions explicit rather than letting each camp talk past the others.

So here's what I'd like. Acting as an expert in the philosophy of biology, write me the full desiderata list — the requirements that any adequate definition or theory of life must meet, or at minimum must be measured against. For each desideratum, give me the case or consideration that motivates it: the reason it's a requirement rather than just a preference of one camp. Then flag which are non-negotiable (any definition that fails them is simply a non-starter) and which reflect the legitimate but parochial interests of a particular research program. Most importantly, I want to know which pair, or larger subset, of these desiderata are plausibly jointly unsatisfiable given what we know about the actual range of biological cases — viruses, endospores, obligate intracellular parasites, frozen tardigrades, prions, transposons, and so on. That's the part I most need help with: I suspect the list itself is a kind of result, and I want to see where the tensions are sharpest. If you think one particular borderline case or class of cases does more work than any other in exposing those tensions, say which one and why, and whether any proposed definition has come close to finessing it. Please write the answer in American English, and take the time to think through the structure carefully before laying it out — I'd rather have a well-organized list of eight or ten genuine desiderata with serious motivating cases than a sprawling inventory of everything anyone has ever asked a definition of life to do.
