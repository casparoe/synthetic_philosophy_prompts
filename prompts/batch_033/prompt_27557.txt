After a career in the natural sciences, I have recently begun reading philosophy, and I have found myself unable to pin down a slogan that kept recurring in a discussion of Newcomb's problem and its transparent-box variant. One participant repeatedly insisted that a rational agent must be "updateless," and that in such problems one should act as one would have decided to act before making the relevant observation—for example, before seeing the contents of the boxes. He used this to reach two conclusions: that one should take only the opaque box in the standard Newcomb problem, and that one should still take only the opaque box in the transparent variant even after seeing that it is empty.

I would like to understand what this slogan could actually be claiming. What distinct precise principles might the instruction to be "updateless" or to "choose the policy one would have precommitted to" express? For each candidate reading, please say what exactly it directs an agent to do, whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, or outright false, and whether it genuinely supports the conclusion drawn in the transparent-box case. I am less interested in which reading is canonical than in seeing the alternatives laid out as a menu with the verdicts separated. In particular, it would help me to distinguish readings on which the slogan is only advice about literal precommitment, readings on which it collapses into one of the standard decision theories, and readings on which it amounts to a distinct proposal about evaluating whole policies rather than individual acts.
