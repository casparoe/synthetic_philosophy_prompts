I'm preparing a seminar that pairs Hofstadter's "Aunt Hillary and the Ant Fugue" from *Gödel, Escher, Bach* with Orson Scott Card's *Ender's Game*, and I want a careful philosophical reading of the novel through the lens of the ant-colony-as-mind question. In Hofstadter's dialogue, Aunt Hillary argues that an ant colony could have a mind—beliefs, desires, a stream of consciousness—even though no individual ant does; the mind is an emergent pattern at the level of the colony, not something located in any particular ant, not even the queen. *Ender's Game*, by contrast, presents the Formics as a hive mind where consciousness is centralized in the Hive Queen: individual workers are non-sentient, and when the Queen dies, the mind dies with her.

I want you to read the novel against Hofstadter's proposal. Does the book ever genuinely entertain the possibility that Formic consciousness is distributed rather than queen-centered, or does it simply take the queen-as-mind model for granted? What do we learn about the Formic mind from the scenes where Ender interacts with the larval queen—does the novel treat the queen as the mind, or as a node through which the mind expresses itself? And in the final chapters, when Ender takes the cocoon and becomes the Speaker for the Dead, is the novel implicitly revising its theory of where the Formic mind resides, or does it remain committed to the view that saving the queen is saving the mind?

I am less interested in a summary of either text than in a reading that lets each pressure-test the other: where Hofstadter's model reveals assumptions in the novel that the novel itself never questions, and where the novel's dramatic choices—particularly Ender's empathy with the enemy and the moral horror of xenocide—raise difficulties for a purely functionalist, emergentist account of mind that the ant-colony thought experiment glosses over.
