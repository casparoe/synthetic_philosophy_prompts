During a rebranding project I was recently part of, I watched a type designer defend a highly abstracted logo mark to a client. The client said the custom letterform no longer looked like an 'a'. The designer replied, in effect, that this was a category error: an 'a' is not any particular shape but a theme, and individual typefaces are variations on that theme, much as a melody remains itself when transposed, slowed, or reorchestrated. On his account, the mark was not a distorted 'a' but a legitimate variation on the theme of 'a', and objecting to its unusual construction was like complaining that a jazz arrangement departs from the lead sheet. I found the analogy persuasive at the time, but I have since wondered whether it was carrying the whole argument, and I keep coming back to the underlying question of what makes every 'a' an 'a'.

I would like you to audit this analogy. Give me an explicit correspondence table: what in the letterform domain plays the role of the theme, the variation, transposition, instrumentation, the listener's recognition, and any other elements you take to be doing work. Then identify where the mapping breaks. I especially want to know which features of musical identity the analogy quietly imports into letterform identity that may not be there — and which of those disanalogies matter for the designer's conclusion, as opposed to being harmless. Finally, tell me whether the conclusion that the mark is a legitimate 'a' survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether some other analogy would do better for capturing what makes every 'a' an 'a'.
