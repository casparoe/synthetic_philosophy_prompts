I'm a PhD student in moral philosophy, currently embedded with the trust and safety team of a large social platform. The team decides, several times a quarter, whether to ship a change that will slightly worsen the experience of a very large number of users while preventing a severe harm to a much smaller number: added login friction to stop account takeovers, comment delays to stop targeted harassment, reduced reach to stop stalking. The team has no shared framework for when many tiny costs may be summed against a few large ones, and it relitigates the question every time. Each decision gets about an hour of analysis, is made by non-philosophers, and must be documented for legal review.

Write a decision procedure the team could actually run. Ground it in the strongest considerations from the philosophical debate about aggregating tiny harms — the dust-specks-versus-torture choice and the surrounding arguments about whether, when, and how small harms add up — but translate those considerations into questions a product manager or trust and safety analyst can answer. The procedure should specify: the questions to ask in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure returns yes or no; and where it must stop and hand the decision to a person because the step is a value judgment rather than a factual one. Flag any step that looks factual but is really a judgment call dressed up as measurement.

Then run the procedure on two proposals. First: adding a mandatory verification step that costs every one of roughly 100 million monthly users about five seconds at each login, to prevent account takeover for a few thousand journalists and activists. Second: holding every first-time commenter's post for ten minutes before it appears, which delays millions of harmless comments, to prevent a small number of public figures from receiving a flood of abusive replies. I expect the procedure to give the team an answer it will not like in at least one of these cases; state that plainly. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do — which cases it will systematically misdecide, and where the underlying philosophy itself runs out.
