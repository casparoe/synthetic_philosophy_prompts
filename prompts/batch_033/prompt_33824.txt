I've come across a puzzle in decision theory and want to know whether it has an established name, and, more importantly, how the underlying phenomenon should be carved up. A fair coin is tossed until it lands heads, and the player receives a payoff whose magnitude grows with the number of tosses—roughly doubling each time, but with alternating signs. The probability of reaching the nth outcome is the usual halving sequence. If I multiply each possible payoff by its probability and add the terms in the natural order in which the outcomes occur, the sum converges to a finite positive number. But the sum is unstable under reordering: by listing the same mutually exclusive outcomes in a different order—grouping positive terms together first, or taking several positive terms for each negative term—the partial sums can be made to approach any number at all, positive or negative, or to oscillate. So the same lottery seems to have a different expected value depending merely on how its possible outcomes are listed.

I know this is not the St Petersburg case, where the probability-weighted sum diverges to infinity. Here the natural-order sum is finite, but the expectation is not invariant under a seemingly harmless change in the order of summation. Does this configuration have a name in decision theory or probability? Is the expected value best described as undefined, as defined only relative to an arbitrary ordering convention, or as something else? I'm less interested in a bare label than in the exact diagnostic: what property of the sequence does the work, how does this differ from nearby paradoxes, and what, if anything, would a principled fix look like?
