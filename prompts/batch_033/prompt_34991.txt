I'm a PhD student in psychology working on the relationship between incentives and belief formation — specifically, whether offering someone a reward for holding a belief can give them a genuine reason to hold it. A colleague in the philosophy department recently summarized the wrong kind of reasons problem for me in a way that seems directly relevant to my project. Here is what they said, as best I can reconstruct it:

"The wrong kind of reasons problem is usually introduced with a case like this: an evil demon credibly threatens to torture you unless you admire him. The threat gives you a reason to admire him — a pragmatic reason — but it plainly does not make the demon admirable. The puzzle is to explain why some considerations count as reasons for having an attitude without counting as reasons that bear on whether the attitude is fitting, correct, or appropriate to its object. The standard solution, most associated with Derek Parfit's work on reasons, distinguishes state-given reasons from object-given reasons. A state-given reason is a reason to be in some mental state that derives from the benefits of being in that state — avoiding torture, in the demon case. An object-given reason is a reason that derives from features of the object the attitude is about — the demon's actual qualities. Once you have this distinction in hand, the problem is essentially resolved: only object-given reasons can make an attitude fitting, and state-given reasons are simply reasons to bring it about that you have the attitude — reasons for self-manipulation, not reasons that speak to the attitude's correctness. The remaining controversy is mostly about whether pragmatic reasons for belief should be classified as state-given or as something else, which is why the problem matters for your work on motivated reasoning."

I want to use this framework in my dissertation, but I need to know how much of this summary I can trust before I build on it. Could you go through it and tell me which parts are accurate, which are oversimplified or misleading, and whether the problem is genuinely as settled as my colleague's summary makes it sound? I'm especially concerned about the claim that the state-given/object-given distinction "essentially resolves" the problem — if there are significant objections to this framework, or if the distinction itself turns out to be harder to draw than the summary suggests, I would need to know that before relying on it.
