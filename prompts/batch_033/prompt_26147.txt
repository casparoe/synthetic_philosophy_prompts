So my friends Marcus and Lena got into it last night over the old Death in Damascus parable — you know, the one where the guy sees Death in Damascus making a threatening gesture, borrows a horse and flees to Aleppo, and then meets Death there who says he was surprised to see him in Damascus that morning because his appointment was in Aleppo all along. I'd heard the story a million times but they started arguing about what it actually shows, and I've been turning it over in my head ever since. Here's roughly how it went:

Marcus said the story is a genuine paradox about foreknowledge and freedom. If Death infallibly knew the guy would be in Aleppo, then it was true all along that he'd end up there. The man couldn't make that false. So his flight wasn't really free — he was always going to end up in Aleppo no matter what he thought he was deciding.

Lena said that's a modal scope mixup. From "Death knows X" and "necessarily, if Death knows X then X," you can't conclude "necessarily X." The guy could have stayed in Damascus — it's just that if he had, Death would've known that instead. Foreknowledge doesn't force the outcome; it just tracks it.

Marcus came back and said: but if Death's foreknowledge is genuinely infallible, there's no possible world where the guy stays and Death was wrong about him going to Aleppo. So in every possible world, the guy goes to Aleppo. And that sure sounds like "couldn't do otherwise" to me.

Lena said possible worlds depend on what you hold fixed. If you hold Death's actual knowledge fixed, then yeah, the guy goes to Aleppo in all those worlds. But the question is whether he has the ability to do otherwise in the relevant sense, and ability is standardly assessed by holding fixed the past and the laws, not facts about the future.

Marcus said wait — Death's knowledge is in the past. Death knew yesterday where the guy would be today. So by your own standard, we hold it fixed, and the guy still can't do otherwise.

Lena said there's a difference between the past fact that Death had a certain belief and the content of that belief, which makes essential reference to the future. The past fact is just that Death tokened "the man will be in Aleppo." Whether that token amounts to knowledge depends on a future fact. The man's ability to do otherwise is about whether he can falsify the content — not whether he can retroactively change Death's past mental state. If he'd stayed in Damascus, Death's token would simply have been false, not knowledge.
