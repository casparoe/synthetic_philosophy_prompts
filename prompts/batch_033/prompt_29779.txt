I've been following the deontology vs. consequentialism debate for something like fifteen years now, mostly through the rationalist blogosphere and the stuff that gets linked from it, and I've gotten to the point where I think the whole thing is stuck in a way that's worth diagnosing rather than participating in.

The specific impasse I have in mind is the paradox of deontology. You know the setup: deontologists say you can't kill one innocent person to save five, because there's a constraint against killing. But if you don't kill the one, five get killed — so now there are five killings instead of one. If the constraint is supposed to minimize killings, it's doing the opposite. The deontologist says no, the five deaths aren't killings by me, they're killings by someone else, or they're lettings-die rather than killings, or I didn't intend them. The consequentialist says that's just word games; the constraint is supposed to protect people from being killed, and it's failing at that.

I've read the major moves. Agent-relativity. The doing-allowing distinction and why it's supposed to matter. The intending-foreseeing distinction and the closeness problem. Kamm's attempts to ground constraints in something about the person rather than the outcomes. Scheffler's agent-centered prerogatives. And the consequentialist responses to all of it. After all that, I can predict what each side will say to any new argument, and neither side's confidence seems to have shifted at all.

So my question is: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? I have a few hypotheses but I dont trust them. Is it a substantive disagreement about a fact — like, is there a fact of the matter about whether the doing-allowing distinction has the moral significance deontologists claim, and we just can't access it? Is it a verbal dispute where "morality" means impartial goodness to one side and something about the shape of an individual life to the other? Is it that the two sides are answering different questions — one about which world is better, one about what I may do — and calling both of them "ethics"? Or is it bedrock intuitions all the way down, and the arguments are just post-hoc rationalization?

And then: given your diagnosis, what would count as progress? A new conceptual distinction? An empirical discovery about how people actually reason? A formal result? Or should we just accept that this one isn't going to get resolved and move on to other questions?

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, no preamble and no restating of the question. American English.
