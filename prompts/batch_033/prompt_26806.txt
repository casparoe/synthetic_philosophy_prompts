For a graduate seminar on epistemic game theory, I'd like you to write a decision-tree handout that takes a student from a concrete strategic situation to the named position that best captures what common knowledge of rationality commits us to predicting there. The student begins by accepting that the players are Bayesian expected-utility maximizers and that their rationality is common knowledge; the tree should then sort them by the further features of the situation, with each branch node phrased as a question the student can answer about the case without already knowing which position it leads to.

The leaves must include at least the following positions: the rationalizability-only view, the correlated-equilibrium view, the backward-induction view, the global-games selection view, the Aumann agreement view, the no-trade view, and the email-game or coordinated-attack view about the fragility of approximate common knowledge.

Build the tree from the logical space of assumptions rather than from the list of names: first lay out the branch questions and the combinations of answers they allow, then locate each named position in that space. The branch questions should include whether the players share a common prior, whether their information structures are partitional, whether the interaction is one-shot or dynamic, whether the payoff-relevant state is common knowledge or observed only through noisy private signals, and whether the student requires the prediction to survive when common knowledge holds only approximately. For each leaf, give the combination of answers that reaches it, one sentence stating the position, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. For instance, the backward-induction leaf must say what rationality means at nodes that common knowledge of rationality itself says will not be reached.

Explicitly flag any pairs of leaves the tree cannot cleanly separate — I suspect Aumann agreement and the no-trade theorem will be one such pair, since they rest on nearly the same assumptions — and diagnose every unoccupied combination of branch answers as incoherent, unattractive, or merely underexplored. Aim for something a student could walk through in about ten minutes with a concrete case in hand.
