I keep encountering theories of vagueness—supervaluationism, epistemicism, many-valued and degree-theoretic approaches, contextualism—and each seems to talk past the others about what a theory of vagueness is even supposed to do. Before I read another one, I want the desiderata laid out explicitly. What must an adequate theory of vagueness deliver?

For each requirement, give the case, puzzle, or datum that motivates it, and then assess whether it is non-negotiable or merely a preference of one camp. I am especially interested in the following:

- The sorites paradox itself. What exactly does a theory have to do to count as having handled it—block the inference, dissolve the puzzle, explain away its pull? If merely blocking the paradox (e.g., by rejecting one premise) is enough, what further explanatory burden does the theory carry to account for why the paradox seemed compelling in the first place?
- Borderline cases. A theory must explain what it is for a predicate to have borderline cases, and why we cannot resolve them by stipulation. But is it also required to explain why some predicates admit borderline cases while others do not—or is that a separate question a theory of vagueness can leave to someone else?
- Higher-order vagueness. If a theory draws a sharp boundary between the clear cases and the borderline cases, it seems to recreate the sorites at a higher level. Is handling higher-order vagueness a genuine requirement, or is it a ladder we can kick away once the first-order paradox is resolved? If it is a requirement, what counts as a satisfactory treatment—eliminating the appearance of a higher-order boundary, or merely showing that it is harmless?
- Logical revisionism. Must a theory preserve classical logic, or is it permitted to revise it—and if so, how much revision is too much? If a theory gives up bivalence or excluded middle, what does it owe us in return: a complete alternative logic, a translation manual back to classical reasoning, or something else?
- Penumbral connections. Vague predicates do not float free of one another: nothing can be clearly red and clearly orange all over, and someone who is clearly tall is thereby not clearly short. A theory must respect these connections. Is this a separate requirement, or does it fall out of other desiderata? Does it constrain the logic of vague predicates in a way that rules out otherwise attractive candidates?
- The forced-march sorites. Some theories handle the logical paradox but collapse when asked to classify each item in a sorites sequence one by one, since the theorist is forced either to draw a sharp cutoff or to give answers that seem to violate the theory's own semantics. Is the forced march a fair test of a theory, or does it smuggle in an assumption the theory is entitled to reject—such as that every statement in a series must be answerable?

Finally: is there any subset of these desiderata that cannot be jointly satisfied? I have heard it suggested, for instance, that handling higher-order vagueness while preserving classical logic forces an infinite hierarchy of metalanguages, and that avoiding this requires sacrificing something else. If that is right, spell out the tension—the impossibility result, or the near-impossibility result, is more interesting to me than any particular positive theory. And if you had to pick one desideratum that eliminates the largest number of candidate theories in a single stroke, which one is it, and why?
