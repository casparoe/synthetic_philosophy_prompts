I'm in a philosophy of medicine seminar this semester and we've been doing this exercise where the professor gives us her intuitive verdicts on whether various conditions count as diseases, and we're supposed to reverse-engineer what theory of disease she's operating with. She hasn't told us yet—it's kind of a running puzzle across the whole course. Here's the list I've been keeping in my notes:

- Mild myopia (nearsightedness): she says it's a disease.
- Infertility in a 25-year-old with no other health issues: disease, even if the person doesn't want kids.
- Pregnancy: not a disease.
- Menopause: not a disease.
- ADHD: disease.
- Being in the shortest 5% of the population with no underlying pathology: not a disease.
- Chronic lower back pain that persists for years with no imaging findings: disease.
- Lactose intolerance in adulthood: not a disease.

Next week we're discussing a new one: a genetic variant that's really common in East Asian populations (ALDH2 deficiency) where you can't metabolize alcohol properly, so drinking causes facial flushing, nausea, headache, and elevated long-term cancer risk if you drink. It's present in something like a third to half of people in some populations. She wants us to come in ready to say whether it's a disease and why.

The thing is, I can see her pattern fitting either a naturalist view (disease = biological dysfunction relative to species-typical functioning) or a normativist view (disease = disvalued condition), or maybe some hybrid like Wakefield's harmful dysfunction account. But each of those theories hits a snag somewhere in her list. The lactose intolerance verdict especially threw me—she said it's not a disease, but if nearsightedness is, what's the difference? Is she using some kind of statistical prevalence cutoff? Or is it about whether the condition impairs you in your actual enviroment? And the chronic pain case seems hard for a naturalist, while the infertility case (disease even if you don't want kids) seems hard for a normativist.

I'm trying to figure out: (a) which theory or combination best fits her verdicts, (b) what she'll say about the alcohol intolerance case, and (c) what additional case would most cleanly distinguish between the leading candidate theories. If her pattern is genuinely ambiguous between two theories, I want to know what principle I'd be committing to with each interpretation.
