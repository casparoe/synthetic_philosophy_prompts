I serve on the governance committee of a small housing cooperative with roughly two dozen members, and we are currently rewriting our decision-making rules. We had converged on two principles that seemed independently compelling. First, each member should have a protected sphere of personal decisions — what they do in their own room, what they eat, whether they attend optional meetings — in which the group may not overrule them. Second, whenever a proposal would make at least one member better off and no one worse off, the cooperative should be able to adopt it. A friend with training in formal philosophy mentioned that Amartya Sen showed in the 1970s that these two commitments, together with the assumption that the group can rank any set of alternatives, are enough to generate collective preference cycles even when every individual's preferences are fully consistent. I have worked through a sketch of the Lady Chatterley example and understand the formal mechanics, but I am having trouble translating the theorem into actual design choices.

What are the most important implications of Sen's liberal paradox for how we should rewrite the cooperative's rules? I am less interested in restating the theorem than in its practical upshots: which of the two principles should give way in which contexts; whether the right response is to restrict the agenda the group may consider, to weaken the Pareto principle, or to make the protected personal sphere conditional in some way; and whether a cycling result of this kind should genuinely worry a group of our size or is largely a theoretical artifact. Please rank the implications by how much they should change our rules, and flag any that sound dramatic but on reflection do not follow.
