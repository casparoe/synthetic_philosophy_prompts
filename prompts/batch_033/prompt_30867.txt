I'm giving a forty-five-minute talk next month at an AI lab whose alignment team is mostly engineers and decision theorists who treat value alignment as the problem of eliciting preferences and aggregating them into a utility function, and who care mostly about whether a proposal is implementable and whether it actually constrains a learned system; a Rawlsian political philosopher and a deliberative democrat will also be in the room. My thesis is that alignment should be reframed as a constitutional problem under reasonable pluralism rather than a maximization problem, with liberal neutrality as the guiding constraint. An aligned AI should not optimize for any single aggregate of individual values, whether a learned social welfare function, an average of stated preferences, or the values of a representative human. I argue that any such aggregate either smuggles in one comprehensive conception of the good while claiming to be neutral, or inherits the impossibility results of social choice; the alternative is to constrain the system by a small set of legitimacy conditions that could be endorsed from within each reasonable comprehensive doctrine in the population it serves, and to remain neutral among those doctrines beyond those constraints. The one-line version: stop trying to find the right value function; start designing the right side-constraints.
