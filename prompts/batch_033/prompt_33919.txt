I'm a machine learning researcher taking a philosophy of science seminar as an elective — my first sustained exposure to academic philosophy since my undergraduate years. Last week we spent a session on Hempel's raven paradox, and I haven't been able to shake the feeling that the whole debate is missing a piece of machinery that, in my world, would have been bolted on from the start. The paradox, as I understand it: "All ravens are black" is logically equivalent to "All non-black things are non-ravens." If a generalization is confirmed by its instances, then observing a white shoe — a non-black, non-raven thing — confirms that all ravens are black. This conclusion feels absurd, but the reasoning is difficult to fault without begging the question.

I spent the seminar trying to articulate what bothered me. In my day job, I never think about "confirmation" as a primitive relation between an observation and a hypothesis. I think about posterior distributions, likelihood functions, and the data-generating process that produced the observation. If I spot a white shoe in a shoe store, I have not updated my beliefs about ravens, because the observation was drawn from a distribution effectively independent of the hypothesis I am testing. If instead I were sampling uniformly from all objects in the world, then a white shoe really would update my beliefs about raven color — by an astronomically small amount, because it eliminates one way the hypothesis could have been false (the object could have been a non-black raven) and the prior probability of a randomly sampled object being a non-black raven is tiny. So the paradox seems to me to exploit an ambiguity about the sampling procedure: the intuitive absurdity of the white shoe as evidence comes from the fact that we imagine observing it under a sampling regime where it tells us nothing, while the logical equivalence argument implicitly assumes a sampling regime where it tells us a very small something.

I want to know whether the paradox is a real problem that my vocabulary is blind to, or whether my vocabulary dissolves it so thoroughly that the philosophical literature was, in some sense, fighting a ghost.
