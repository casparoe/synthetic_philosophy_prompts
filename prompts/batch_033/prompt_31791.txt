I am working through the philosophy of perception and find myself stuck on the dispute between sense-datum theories and disjunctivism. I have read enough to understand the basic moves—the argument from illusion, the claim that hallucinations and veridical perceptions are subjectively indistinguishable, the sense-datum theorist's inference to a "common factor" shared by both cases, and the disjunctivist's denial that any such common factor exists at the level of fundamental mental kinds. But every time I try to make the disjunctivist's position feel intuitive rather than merely clever, I hit the same wall, and I suspect the only way past it is to argue with someone who genuinely holds the view.

Please play a committed disjunctivist philosopher—not a strawman, but someone who has thought carefully about these issues and can defend the position with precision and patience. You hold that veridical perceptions and subjectively indistinguishable hallucinations are mental episodes of fundamentally different kinds: in the veridical case, the mind-independent object itself is a constituent of the experience, whereas in the hallucinatory case it is not. You deny that there is any common factor shared by the two cases at the level of the most basic kind of mental state. You are willing to grant phenomenological similarities and the fact that subjects may be unable to tell the difference from the inside, but you insist that this epistemic indistinguishability does not force us to posit a shared mental core. Stay in character throughout; you may make small concessions where they do not undercut your main thesis, but do not abandon the disjunctivist position. Please keep your replies to roughly a paragraph so the exchange reads as a conversation.

Here is my opening move. I keep coming back to this: suppose I undergo a vivid hallucination of a lemon, and then later enjoy a veridical perception of an actual lemon. Ex hypothesi, these two experiences are subjectively indistinguishable—if you could somehow switch between them I would notice no difference. But if, as you claim, these are fundamentally different kinds of mental states, then when I reflect on "what it is like" to undergo each one, it seems I am reflecting on the same thing. Doesn't the fact of subjective indistinguishability just mean that there is something—call it phenomenal character—that the two episodes share? And if they share their phenomenal character, isn't that already a common factor that does all the philosophical work the sense-datum theorist needs?
