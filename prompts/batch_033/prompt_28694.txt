I am a software engineer working on an electronic health record system, and I keep running into a modeling problem that I suspect is philosophical underneath. The system has a single field for time of death, but clinicians enter that timestamp through two very different pathways: a neurological declaration, where a patient on a ventilator is declared dead after testing shows loss of brainstem function, and a circulatory declaration, where a patient is declared dead after the heart stops and resuscitation is not attempted. These are treated as two tests for the same underlying fact, but they can occur at different times and drive different downstream behavior, especially for organ donation. I have no formal philosophy background, so I need the concepts spelled out plainly rather than only named. What account of death does this two-criteria practice presuppose, if it presupposes one at all? Do the neurological and circulatory standards presuppose the same concept of death, or do they quietly commit to different ones? Reconstruct the implicit theory or theories, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits with, flag the points where the practice does not actually settle which theory is right, and say what would have to change in the software and in the underlying policy if a rival account of death were adopted. Please write your response in American English.
