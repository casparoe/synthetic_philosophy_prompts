I keep noticing this move in arguments and I'm wondering whether it's been discussed properly — if it has a name, what the main positions are, etc.

Someone holds a belief they're confident about — say, that a particular medication works, or that an election wasn't rigged, or that their partner isn't cheating. And let's say they're actually right and have good grounds. Then someone presents them with what looks like counterevidence: a study claiming the medication is no better than placebo, a video purporting to show ballot-stuffing, a dodgy-looking text. And their response is, in effect: "Since I know this belief is true, whatever you're showing me must be misleading, so I don't need to examine it."

The frustrating thing is, it's hard to say they're reasoning badly. If they genuinely know the belief is true, then by definition the counterevidence is misleading, and it seems fine to disregard evidence you know is misleading. But something still feels off — if everyone reasoned like this whenever they were confident, no one would ever revise anything, and that can't be right.

I'm not just after a label. I want to know how this problem has been carved up: is it one paradox or a few related issues, where does the reasoning actually go wrong (if it does), and what are the main ways people have tried to block it? And if you think the best response requires giving up something like epistemic closure or the idea that knowledge is stable under known entailment, say so — I want to know what the costs are.

Please write in British English.
