I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a piece about why well-informed people keep disagreeing about things like climate models or economic forecasts. A pop-econ book I'm reading sums up Aumann's agreement theorem like this: 'Aumann proved that two perfectly rational people with the same information can't agree to disagree. Once they exchange their probability estimates, they're forced to wind up with identical beliefs. So when smart, honest people still disagree after talking it out, the only possible explanations left are irrationality, bias, or somebody hiding evidence.' That sounds too strong to me — I vaguely remember there's a 'common prior' assumption doing a lot of work, and that the theorem is more conditional then this. But I don't know enough to say exactly which parts are fair. Go through the summary and tell me what a decision theorist or formal epistemologist would accept, what they'd wince at, and whether the last sentence follows from anything true in the summary.
