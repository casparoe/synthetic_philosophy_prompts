Here is what I am puzzled about.

The Kelly criterion tells us that when an agent faces a long sequence of independent, positive-expectation gambles, she should wager a fraction of her bankroll that maximizes the expected logarithm of wealth. Betting more than this fraction leads to almost-sure ruin in the limit; betting less sacrifices achievable growth. This seems to give the Kelly fraction a special normative status. It is not simply one admissible strategy among many — it appears to be the uniquely correct bet size for any agent who wants to avoid eventual ruin. And since ruin means going to zero and being unable to continue, avoiding it looks like a structural requirement on agency itself, not a mere preference.

But here is the tension. Maximizing geometric growth just is maximizing expected log-wealth. That is maximizing expected utility under logarithmic utility. So the Kelly criterion seems to build a specific utility function into its foundations. And yet it is routinely presented as a general constraint on rational betting — invoked with normative force against anyone who would overbet, regardless of their risk attitudes. I have seen people say things like: "If you bet more than full Kelly, you are making a mistake, period — the math doesn't care about your utility function."

So which is it? Is the Kelly criterion a universal rationality requirement that binds any agent on pain of ruin, or is it merely the optimal strategy conditional on already having logarithmic utility? If it is the latter, why does the ruin argument still feel compelling even when one does not have log utility? What exactly is the conceptual error in someone who thinks the Kelly criterion carries normative force independent of her utility function?
