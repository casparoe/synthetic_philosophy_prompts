A couple of weeks ago I overheard two colleagues arguing at lunch about something that happened at work, and I haven't been able to stop thinking about it. I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, so I don't have the tools to sort this out myself. I'd like someone to go through their exchange and tell me who was right about what.

The situation: A third colleague, Dave, had been asked by our VP in a one-on-one whether his team's project was on track for the Q3 deadline. Dave said, "We've completed every milestone that was on the roadmap as of the last quarterly review." This was true. What he didn't say was that two months after that review, the scope had been expanded substantially, and none of the new work had been started. The VP left the meeting believing the project was in good shape. When the scope expansion came to light later, Dave defended himself by saying everything he'd said was true.

Here's how the argument went:

(1) Mark said Dave lied to the VP. "He knew the VP would walk away with a false picture, and he chose his words to make that happen. That's lying."

(2) Sarah said that's not what lying is. "Lying requires saying something false. Dave was careful to say only true things. You can criticize him for being misleading, but calling it a lie is just inaccurate — and the difference matters."

(3) Mark replied that this is exactly the kind of hairsplitting that lets people deceive with a clean conscience. "The whole point of the truth-telling norm is that we shouldn't deliberately cause false beliefs. Whether you do it with a false sentence or a carefully chosen true one is a technical detail about the mechanism. If I sell you a car and say 'the engine was replaced last year' while leaving out that the replacement engine has already failed, I've deceived you just as much as if I'd lied. The moral wrong is the same."

(4) Sarah said the difference isn't just technical. "If there's no moral difference, then every time you don't blurt out every relevant fact you know, you're morally equivalent to a liar. That can't be right. There's a reason we distinguish between what someone asserts and what they merely fail to mention. Dave's VP could have asked follow-up questions. The burden isn't only on the speaker."

(5) Mark said that's a fair point about everyday conversation, but this was a status meeting where Dave knew the VP was relying on him for an accurate picture. "In that context, saying something that's technically true but designed to mislead is functionally the same as lying. The VP wasn't in a position to ask follow-ups about scope changes he didn't know existed. Dave exploited an information asymmetry he was specifically supposed to close."

(6) Sarah conceded that Dave's behavior was wrong, but maintained it wasn't lying. "The word 'lying' carries a particular moral weight — it means you've violated a bright-line rule. Misleading is often wrong too, but it lives in a grayer zone where context and the relationship between the parties matter more. Collapsing them makes it harder to think clearly about either. Not all wrongful deceptions are lies, and calling them all lies obscures what's distinctive about each."

They went back and forth a bit more but that was the core of it. I found both of them persuasive at different moments, and I honestly couldn't decide. Could you adjudicate this move by move? I'm especially interested in whether Sarah's distinction between lying and misleading can bear the moral weight she wants it to — or whether Mark is right that in high-stakes contexts where one party is clearly relying on another, the distinction collapses.
