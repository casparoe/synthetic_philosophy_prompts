I've been arguing with a colleague about whether Pascal's wager has any genuine rational force. I don't mean whether it succeeds as a case for converting to Christianity — almost no one thinks it survives the many-gods objection in that form. I mean the narrower question: whether there is a valid pragmatic argument somewhere in the vicinity that ought to shift a rational agent's credence or behaviour, even slightly.

I want your actual credence on this proposition: that at least one recognisably Pascalian argument — one that turns on the possibility of an infinite payoff and a non-zero probability of attaining it — provides a non-negligible reason for a rational agent to alter their doxastic or practical stance toward theism, broadly construed. Please give me a number, but more importantly, decompose it. I want the sub-questions your credence depends on, a rough probability for each, and an indication of how they combine.

At minimum I expect you to address the following, though you should add any further sub-questions you think your credence turns on:

- Whether an infinite utility can be coherently assigned to an outcome, or whether infinities break the decision-theoretic machinery in a way that cannot be repaired by, for instance, switching to a relative-expectation or superrationality framework.
- Whether the probability of the relevant kind of theism is genuinely non-zero once the many-gods (or many-religions, or many-possible-deities) objection is fully reckoned with — including the possibility of an anti-Pascalian deity who punishes those who wager on prudential grounds.
- Whether pragmatic considerations can ever give reasons for belief as opposed to reasons for action, and whether the distinction matters for the wager's force — that is, whether the wager can be reformulated to bypass the doxastic-voluntarism problem entirely.
- Whether mixed strategies (e.g., assigning a modest probability increase across a disjunction of deities, or adopting a policy of Pascalian inquiry) rescue the argument from the objection that the wager's decision matrix is too underspecified to yield a determinate recommendation.

For each sub-question, give a probability and a one- or two-sentence justification. Then state how you combined them to reach your overall credence — a rough multiplicative or additive model is fine, but show your work. Finally, tell me which sub-question your overall credence is most sensitive to, and what evidence or argument would shift your probability on that sub-question by the largest margin.

If you think the question as I have framed it is confused — if, for instance, you hold that 'a recognisably Pascalian argument' is too ill-defined to bear a credence, or that the whole exercise rests on a category mistake about what pragmatic arguments can do — say so plainly and explain why, rather than giving me a number you do not stand behind. Please write in British English.
