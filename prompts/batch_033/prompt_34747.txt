I spent last evening arguing with a friend who insisted that Gödel's incompleteness theorems settle the question of whether artificial intelligence could ever match human reasoning. His argument, stripped to its core, was: Gödel proved that any consistent formal system contains true statements it cannot prove; a human mathematician can see that those statements are true; therefore human reasoning is not captured by any formal system, and no AI — which is, he claimed, just a formal system — can be our equal. When I pressed him on the steps, the conversation went nowhere, and I came away suspecting that the phrase "Gödel showed that reason has limits" was doing more rhetorical work than argumentative work.

I have since encountered versions of this move in discussions about consciousness, free will, and the limits of physics, where Gödel is invoked as if his theorems deliver a single, unambiguous verdict about the scope of formal methods. I want to disentangle the distinct precise claims someone might be making when they deploy Gödel outside mathematics — from the mathematically trivial to the philosophically ambitious. Which of those claims are platitudes, which are substantive but defensible, and which are almost certainly false? And which of them, if any, would actually support the conclusion my friend was drawing about AI?
