I've been trying to write down my position on satisficing and bounded rationality for a seminar paper, and I'd like an audit of whether the package hangs together before I commit to it. I hold all of the following:

(1) Descriptively, bounded rationality is correct: real agents search options sequentially, maintain an aspiration level, and stop at the first option that meets it rather than optimizing over the full choice set.

(2) Satisficing is not merely a second-best imposed by our limitations. It is rationally permissible, and in realistic choice environments with costly search it is often the rationally appropriate stopping rule.

(3) In a small, fully specified choice from a fixed set of options with no search or computation costs, maximizing expected utility is rationally required, and satisficing there would be a mistake.

(4) Aspiration levels are endogenous: they adapt to experience, rising when acceptable options come easily and falling after repeated failure.

(5) Whatever normative force satisficing has comes entirely from the costs of search and computation, not from any intrinsic value in stopping at "good enough."

(6) A decision procedure counts as rational only if an agent who predictably follows it cannot be systematically exploited by the order in which options are presented.

Tell me which of these commitments are actually in tension, which pairs only look like they are, and how severe each genuine tension is. I'd like the tensions ranked by severity, with the cheapest revision for each, and then your judgment about which single revision would do the most to make the set coherent. I'd rather you push on the joints than tell me the whole thing is fine.
