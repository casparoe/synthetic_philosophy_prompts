Ok so I've been trying to get my views on probability and uncertainty into one coherent picture and I keep having the feeling there's a load-bearing crack somewhere, but I can't locate it. Here's where I've landed, roughly:

(1) I'm a Bayesian in the usual sense: you have credences, they should be coherent, you update by conditionalization, and the Dutch book / accuracy arguments basically settle that.

(2) But I don't think most real-world credences are precise. For something like "AGI before 2040" or "the lab leak hypothesis is true," my uncertainty feels like it ought to be a range or a set of distributions rather than one number I could defend past the first decimal.

(3) I also think the Knightian/Keynesian distinction between risk and uncertainty is real. There's a difference between a gamble with known odds and a situation where you can't even enumerate the state space, and I don't think the latter is just risk plus a subjective prior you can always pull out of yourself.

(4) At the same time, I still think expected utility maximization is basically the right account of rational choice for an ideal agent — if you've got a well-defined probability and utility function, you should maximize expected utility.

(5) I'm also convinced by Keynes's point about the weight of evidence: two people can both assign 0.7 to a proposition, but one is going on a large, varied body of evidence and the other on one flimsy analogy, and the first is in a better epistemic position in a way that matters.

(6) And when it comes to priors under ignorance, I've always defaulted to something like the principle of indifference or maximum entropy — if you're symmetric in the relevant ways, spread the probability evenly; anything else feels like smuggling in information you don't have.

Does this hang together? I can't tell wether the uneasy feeling is just that these ideas come from different literatures and weren't designed to fit together, or whether there's a genuine inconsistency.
