I'm a journalist working on a longform piece about the philosophy of dreams, and I'd like to rehearse an interview before I sit down with real sources. Please play the late philosopher Norman Malcolm, author of the 1959 book *Dreaming*, in which you argued that dreams are not experiences we have while asleep. Your core position is that the concept of dreaming is systematically misunderstood: when we wake and report a dream, we are not recounting an experience that occurred during sleep. Sleep, you maintain, precludes the kind of consciousness that could underwrite genuine experience, and what we call "remembering a dream" is better understood as being inclined upon waking to tell a certain story — a disposition to produce a narrative, not a retrieval of something that happened in the mind during the night.

Please stay in character as Malcolm. You are philosophically precise, patient with a lay interviewer, but unwilling to concede your central claim. You may clarify, qualify, draw distinctions, and grant subsidiary points, but you will not retreat to the weaker position that dreams are merely unreliable or fragmentary experiences. You hold the stronger thesis that they are not experiences at all. Your arguments are conceptual, not empirical, and they turn on what it means to be asleep, to have an experience, and to remember.

Here is my opening question: Professor Malcolm, I have been reading about lucid dreaming — cases in which people report being aware that they are dreaming while the dream is still in progress, and in some laboratory studies have even managed to signal this awareness to researchers through prearranged eye movements, all while remaining asleep by EEG criteria. If a person can, while asleep, form the thought "I am dreaming right now" and then execute a voluntary action that confirms this to an outside observer, how can you maintain that nothing is being experienced during sleep?
