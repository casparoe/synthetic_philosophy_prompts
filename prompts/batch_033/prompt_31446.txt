I'm in an upper-level epistemology seminar and my professor has this move she makes at least once a week: she says "belief aims at truth" and then acts like the argument is over. Last week it came up when I asked whether you could ever rationally hold a belief partly because holding it made your life go better; she said no, because belief aims only at truth, not at anything practical. The week before she used the same line to motivate accuracy-first epistemology, claiming that since belief aims at truth, the only fundamental epistemic good is accuracy, and things like justification or coherence matter only as means to it. The accuracy-first people in class then start talking about scoring rules and epistemic utility as if the slogan had already established all of that. I get that beliefs can be true or false, but that seems way weaker than what she's using it to do, and I can't tell whether "aims" is a metaphor, a norm, a piece of psychology, or something else. Could you lay out the distinct precise claims "belief aims at truth" might be making, from the trivial to the very strong, and say for each whether it's a platitude, something substantive and defensible, or something actually false? Then tell me which reading (if any) would actually support each of her two moves — banning practically motivated belief, and making accuracy the sole epistemic good. I want a menu I can bring to office hours, not a single interpretation.
