I've taught Eichmann in Jerusalem too many times to count, and I keep coming back to Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day — the novel, though the film works fine too. I want to read Stevens through Arendt's banality of evil, but not in the cheap way where he just becomes an Eichmann in a tailcoat. So here's what I'm after. Arendt's Eichmann isn't a monster; he's thoughtless, a joiner, someone who processed his part in genocide through clichés and careerist categories and couldn't really think from the standpoint of anyone else. Stevens builds a whole account of dignity around not abandoning your post and inhabiting your role so completely that your own feelings never surface. Is that the same phenomenon Arendt is describing, or is it importantly different? Where does the banality of evil lens actually illuminate Stevens's complicity — the dismissal of the two Jewish maids, his refusal to see what Lord Darlington's appeasement work really was — and where does the lens start to lie about him? Because Stevens isnt exactly thoughtless; he's actively refusing to think, and the novel is full of the thoughts he wont let himself finish. I want a reading that takes both the novel and Arendt seriously, and that says at the end what Ishiguro shows that Arendt's categories don't capture — or the reverse, if that's where you come out. I'm not asking whether Arendt got the historical Eichmann right; take her account as given and use it.
