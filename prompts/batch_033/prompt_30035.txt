I've recently started working as a risk analyst, and I keep bumping into a pattern I don't have a name for — though I suspect philosophers have already mapped this terrain.

Whenever I estimate the probability of something happening to a particular case — say, the chance that a specific building will flood this year — the number I get depends entirely on which broader group I compare it to. If I treat the building as "buildings in this postal code," I get one probability. If I treat it as "buildings of this construction type in this flood zone," I get another. If I treat it as "buildings owned by clients with this claims history," I get a third. There's no obvious rule for which grouping is correct, because the building belongs to all of them. And I've started noticing the same move outside of work: in policy debates where someone cites "the historical success rate" of some intervention (but which past interventions count as the same kind?), in arguments about whether a given startup will "probably" fail, and in discussions of what a medical test result "means" for a particular patient. People just reach for whichever grouping gives the number they already wanted, and their opponent reaches for a different one, and nobody acknowledges that the number was manufactured by the choice of grouping in the first place.

Does this have an established name in philosophy? I'm less after a label than a precise characterization: what exactly the problem is, what it's not, how it differs from nearby issues like the problem of induction or ordinary disagreement about priors, and where its boundaries are. If there's no standard name, I'd be grateful for a clear definition and some test cases.

Please reason through this step by step as an expert in the philosophy of probability. I'd also like you to state how confident you are in each major claim you make and to flag the point you're least sure about.
