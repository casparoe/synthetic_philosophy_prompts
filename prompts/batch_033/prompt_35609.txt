I've been going down a predictive processing rabbit hole for a paper and I've hit something that feels like it might be a reductio of the whole framework, but I can't tell if I've made a mistake or if the view really has this problem. I'd love someone to walk through it with me.

Here's the argument as I understand it. (1) On the predictive processing account, the brain's core imperative is minimizing prediction error — the mismatch between top-down generative-model predictions and bottom-up sensory input. (2) There are exactly two ways to do this: you can revise the model to better fit the signals (perceptual inference, roughly what we call "learning" or "seeing things as they are"), or you can act on the world so the signals come to match the model (active inference — moving your eyes, shifting your posture, or in more elaborate cases rearranging the environment). (3) These two strategies are formally equivalent from the system's perspective; which one gets deployed in a given moment depends on estimated precision — roughly, how much the system "trusts" its predictions versus the incoming error signals. (4) So there is no in-principle difference, within the architecture itself, between revising a belief to fit the evidence and acting to make the evidence fit the belief. (5) If that's right, then the distinction we normally draw between "responding to evidence" and "engaging in motivated reasoning" is not one the system can draw on its own terms — both are just prediction error minimization by different routes, and the route taken is a matter of precision-weighting, not a matter of one process being truth-tracking and the other being self-deceptive. (6) This seems to imply that a predictive processing system has no built-in preference for truth; what we call "truth" is just whichever set of predictions ends up dominating given the precision assignments at the time. (7) But predictive processing is itself advanced as a true theory, supported by evidence. If the theory is correct, then the act of arguing for it is just one more precision-weighted error-minimizing maneuver, no different in kind from someone doubling down on a delusion because the cost of updating their model is too high relative to the precision they assign to disconfirming input. (8) So the theory seems to undermine the very distinction between good and bad reasoning that it needs in order to claim its own superiority over rival theories. That looks like a reductio.

I'm most reluctant to give up (1)–(3), since those seem to be the core commitments of the view as I read it in Clark and Hohwy and Friston. But if the trouble starts at (4) or (5), I want to know exactly how and what I'd be sacrificing by rejecting that step. And please tell me what would change your mind about your diagnosis — what kind of argument or consideration would make you locate the flaw somewhere else in the chain.
