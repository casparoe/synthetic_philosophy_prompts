Please design a decision tree that sorts a reader into one of the main positions in the debate over whether practical factors—stakes, the cost of being wrong, and salient error possibilities—can affect whether someone knows something. The tree is meant as a handout for an advanced epistemology seminar. It should sort readers by their answers to concrete cases rather than by which label they prefer, so no question should simply ask something like "are you a contextualist?" or "do you think knowledge depends on stakes?"

Build the tree from the logical space of possible answers rather than from a list of names. First work out the diagnostic questions and which combinations of answers they allow; then locate the named positions in that space; then mark any combination of answers that no position occupies, and say for each empty cell whether it is empty because the view would be incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Label each branch with the question that separates it from its siblings, and phrase every question so that a reader can answer it without already knowing which position it leads to.

The tree must include at least the following diagnostic cases:

(1) A bank-style case in which a person has the same evidence for a proposition in two versions, but far more turns on being right in one version than in the other. Ask whether the person knows in both versions, in neither, or only in the low-stakes version; keep separate the question whether the sentence 'she knows' is true from the question whether it is merely appropriate or inappropriate to say.

(2) An ascriber/subject dissociation case: a low-stakes speaker evaluates a high-stakes subject, and a high-stakes speaker evaluates a low-stakes subject, with the evidence held fixed. Ask whether the truth of the knowledge ascription tracks the speaker's practical situation, the subject's practical situation, both, or neither.

(3) A salience-only case in which nothing practically turns on the proposition, but someone in the conversation raises a far-fetched error possibility. Ask whether that alone can make 'she knows' false or deniable.

Organize the tree around at least these three splits: (i) whether the apparent effect of practical factors is genuinely truth-conditional—a difference in whether the knowledge ascription is true, or in whether the subject stands in the knowledge relation—rather than merely pragmatic; (ii) if it is truth-conditional or knowledge-constituting, whether the relevant practical context is the ascriber's, the subject's, or the assessor's; and (iii) whether the operative practical feature is stakes and costs, the salience of error possibilities, or both.

The leaves must include at minimum: classical invariantism with a pragmatic explanation of the shifting intuitions; epistemic contextualism, subdivided into stakes-based and salience-based variants if your tree supports it; subject-sensitive invariantism, also called pragmatic encroachment or interest-relative invariantism; and relativism about knowledge ascriptions, on which the truth of an ascription is relative to an assessor's context. Each leaf should carry the position's name and one sentence stating the commitment its defenders generally find most awkward to accept. If the tree cannot separate two of these positions, do not force a separation; instead flag explicitly which pair it cannot distinguish and say what further case or argument would be needed to separate them.

Aim for roughly six to nine leaves. End with a final line consisting of a single sentence beginning 'Summary:' that states the taxonomy's top-level structure.
