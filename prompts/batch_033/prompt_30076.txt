A friend walked me into this and now I cant find the exit. Here's the setup, in case you need it. Urn A has 50 red balls and 50 black balls. Urn B has 100 balls, each one red or black, but you dont know the proportion. You get $100 if you draw the color you bet on, nothing otherwise. Almost everyone prefers betting red on A to betting red on B, and black on A to betting black on B. That's the Ellsberg pattern: the known urn beats the unknown urn even though your best guess at the odds is 50% either way.

The friend's argument runs like this.

(1) If your choices are rational, they can be represented by some single probability assignment over how urn B is composed, plus a ranking by expected payoff.

(2) Under any such assignment, the chance of drawing red from B is some number p, and the chance of drawing black is 1 - p.

(3) At least one of p and 1 - p has to be at least 1/2.

(4) So by (1), at least one of the two bets on urn B is at least as good as the matching bet on urn A.

(5) But the setup is symmetric between red and black: nothing you've been told distinguishes the two colors in urn B.

(6) So if you prefer red-on-A to red-on-B, label symmetry forces you to prefer black-on-A to black-on-B.

(7) That pair of preferences is exactly the Ellsberg pattern.

(8) But (4) says at least one urn-B bet is at least as good as its urn-A counterpart, while (7) says both urn-A bets are strictly better. Contradiction.

(9) So at least one of three things is false: the single-probability representation in (1), the symmetry move in (5)-(6), or the rationality of the Ellsberg pattern itself.

He says the right move is to reject (1): ambiguity aversion is rational, a single probability is too demanding, and that's the whole lesson of Ellsberg. But I've also heard that once you reject (1), you can be led to pay not to learn the composition of urn B even when the information is free and would let you pick the better color under every probability you took seriously. That sounds like a reductio all on its own. I'd like to keep (1) and (5)-(6), but then I'm stuck calling the Ellsberg pattern a mistake, and that feels wrong too. The argument seems to prove too much: either the foundations of expected utility go, or a principle as trivial as red and black being just labels goes, or one of the most robust findings in the experimental literature is one big error.

Where does the argument actually go wrong, if it does? If it doesn't, which premise is the least costly to give up, and what exactly do you pay? Name the step, don't give me a vibe.
