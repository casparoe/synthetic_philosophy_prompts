I'm teaching an undergraduate seminar on philosophy of mind, and we've just spent two weeks on Daniel Dennett's *Consciousness Explained* — specifically the chapter where he introduces the multiple drafts model and dismantles the Cartesian theater. My students have grasped the argument in the abstract, but I want to give them something that makes the view feel like more than a thesis to be evaluated. I'd like to close the unit by giving them a poem — not a poem *about* the multiple drafts model, but a poem that performs it, that makes the reader inhabit the very thing Dennett is describing.

Here's what I have in mind. The poem should exist in three parallel versions — presented as three columns side by side on the page, or as three numbered "drafts" arranged sequentially — each describing the same brief episode of conscious experience: perhaps the moment of waking from a dream and trying to place a sound, or the instant of recognizing a face in a crowd before you know why you recognize it. The three versions should differ in small but consequential ways. In one draft, the sound is identified before the feeling of familiarity arises; in another, the feeling comes first and the identification follows; in a third, the two are simultaneous. A sensory detail present in one draft might be absent from another, or might appear in a different temporal position. The point is that none of the three is the "correct" version, the one that captures how the experience *really* went. There is no canonical draft, no place where all the threads converge for a master audience — and the poem should make that felt rather than stated.

The poem needs to work as poetry: it should be vivid, precise, and worth reading on its own terms, not a piece of versified theory. But a student who has read Dennett should recognize, in the form itself, why there are three versions and not one, why the variations are not mistakes, and why the poem refuses to tell you which draft to trust. The title should do some work too — perhaps alluding to the theater without naming it, so that the recognition lands after the reading rather than before it. I'm open to meter and line length, but the form should feel deliberate and the language should be concrete and sensory, because the whole dispute is about what it is for experience to seem a certain way.
