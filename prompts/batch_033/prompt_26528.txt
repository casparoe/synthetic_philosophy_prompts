I've been working through my intuitions about the Liar paradox by going case by case through self-referential and related sentences, and I want to know whether there's a single principle behind my verdicts. Here they are:

(a) "This sentence is false." Verdict: neither true nor false. Calling it true forces it to be false, calling it false forces it to be true. It fails to express a proposition at all.

(b) "This sentence is true." Verdict: also neither true nor false, but the flavor is different. Assigning it true or false doesn't produce a contradiction — there's simply no fact of the matter to ground either assignment. It's like a variable that was never given a value.

(c) Two sentences: (1) "Sentence (2) is true" and (2) "Sentence (1) is false." Verdict: both lack truth values, and for the same reason as (a) — the pair jointly yields a contradiction on any assignment.

(d) A sentence that says "The only sentence on the whiteboard in room 302 is false," and as it happens it is the only sentence on that whiteboard. Verdict: paradoxical, just like (a), even though its self-reference depends on a contingent physical fact. If someone later wrote "Paris is in France" on the same whiteboard, my verdict would flip: the sentence would become straightforwardly false. And that shift — driven purely by a change in the world rather than in the sentence's meaning — feels correct to me.

(e) "Most of Nixon's assertions about Watergate are false," where this very sentence is itself one of Nixon's Watergate assertions. Verdict: unproblematic. It has a truth value determined by the facts about Nixon's other assertions. The accidental self-inclusion creates no paradox at all.

(f) "This sentence is either false or neither true nor false" (the strengthened liar). Verdict: paradoxical — and worse, it seems to show that simply introducing a third category like "neither" doesn't solve anything, because the liar can reformulate itself using whatever categories you offer.

(g) An infinite sequence S₁, S₂, S₃, ... where each Sₙ says "For every m > n, Sₘ is false." Verdict: every sentence in the sequence is paradoxical, even though no sentence refers to itself. This makes me doubt that self-reference is the real culprit.

(h) "This sentence contains five words." Verdict: straightforwardly true. Self-reference alone doesn't cause trouble.

Is there a single theory of truth — or of the conditions under which a declarative sentence succeeds in expressing a truth-evaluable proposition — that delivers all of these verdicts? I can feel tensions in my own pattern: my verdicts on (b) and (f) pull against each other, since I want to say both lack truth values, yet the strengthened liar seems to weaponize its own lack of truth value into something paradoxical in a way the truth-teller doesn't. I also don't know how to square my verdict on (d) — where contingent facts about the world determine whether a sentence is paradoxical — with my verdict on (e), where contingent self-reference is harmless. What distinguishes the two?

I want a committed answer that takes a definite position rather than surveying options. If no single principle captures all my verdicts, tell me which ones I should overturn, and give me the cleanest account that handles the rest. Then apply your account to a case I haven't listed: "This sentence is paradoxical."
