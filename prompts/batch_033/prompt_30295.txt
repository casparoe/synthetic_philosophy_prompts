I run a small reading group that has spent the last few weeks going back and forth on Aumann's agreement theorem, and we've split into two camps that keep talking past each other. Camp A (the convergence camp) thinks the theorem shows something deep: rational Bayesians with a common prior can't "agree to disagree," so when two honest people persist in disagreeing after a long discussion, at least one of them is being irrational or they have different priors, and that's a useful diagnostic for real arguments. Camp B (the triviality camp) thinks the theorem is basically just a restatement of its assumptions: it says that if you have a common prior and your posteriors are common knowledge, then the posteriors are equal — and since common knowledge of posteriors almost never happens in real life, the theorem liscenses no conclusions about actual disagreement.
