I spent thirty years in atmospheric physics and retired a couple years back. Since then I've been reading a lot of philosophy—never had the time before—and I've gotten particularly interested in the philosophy of probability. I just finished Keynes's *Treatise on Probability* and it's been gnawing at me, because it's making me rethink something I used to take for granted without a second thought.

The IPCC has this whole calibrated language system for communicating uncertainty in its assessment reports. They've been using it since the Third Assessment Report, and it's still in place. The scale goes: "virtually certain" means 99–100% probability, "very likely" means 90–100%, "likely" means 66–100%, "about as likely as not" is 33–66%, and then "unlikely," "very unlikely," and "exceptionally unlikely" on the low end. Every key finding in the summary for policymakers gets one of these labels. The stated goal is to make uncertainty communication consistent and transparent across working groups, and I've sat in enough IPCC meetings to know how seriously they take this.

Now, Keynes's big move in the *Treatise* is that probability is a logical relation between evidence and conclusion, and he argues that not all probabilities are numerically measurable or even comparable. Some are just not on a single scale—you can have good grounds for believing something without being able to stick a number on it. He distinguishes situations of "risk" (where you can calculate probabilities from known frequencies or symmetries, like a dice roll) from genuine "uncertainty" (where the basis for numerical judgment simply isn't there), and he thought a lot of the most important decisions we face fall into the second category. Climate projections would seem to be a textbook case of the second category: a one-off system, no ensemble of identical Earths to sample from, deep structural uncertainties about feedbacks and tipping points. And yet here's the IPCC, assessing the most complex system on the planet, assigning precise numerical probability ranges to essentially every major conclusion.

So here's what I can't quite work out on my own. What theory of uncertainty does the IPCC's calibrated-language practice actually presuppose? Does it implicitly assume that all climate-relevant uncertainties are quantifiable—that they're all in Keynes's "risk" bucket? Or is the practice compatible with Keynes's view, maybe because the probability ranges are meant as subjective degrees of belief rather than frequencies? And if Keynes is right that some genuinely important knowledge is non-probabilistic—that a conclusion can be well-supported by evidence without a number attached—what would the IPCC's uncertainty communication practice have to look like instead? Would the whole thing collapse, or could it be reformed to accomodate Keynesian uncertainty without losing the consistency they're after?
