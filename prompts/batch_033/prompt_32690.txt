I've been trying to get clear on the line between manipulation and legitimate persuasion, and I keep finding that my verdicts on particular cases don't fit neatly under any single principle I know of. I'd like you to take my verdicts and see if there's a principled account that captures them, or at least most of them, and tell me which ones I'd have to give up.

Here are my cases and verdicts:

1. A doctor tells a patient the unvarnished statistical risks of refusing a recommended surgery, without selecting which risks to emphasize. Legitimate persuasion.

2. A doctor, dealing with the same patient, deliberately emphasizes the most vivid and frightening details of the risks, knowing from years of treating this patient that dry statistics do not move him but imagery does. I still count this as legitimate.

3. A pharmaceutical salesperson, visiting that same doctor's office, uses precisely the same vivid-imagery technique to persuade the doctor to prescribe a particular brand of medication, knowing the doctor is moved by emotionally charged cases. I count this as manipulation.

4. A friend tells you, with evidence, that your business partner is embezzling. Legitimate.

5. A friend tells you the same thing, but frames it as "I'm losing sleep worrying about you, and I cannot bear to watch you get taken advantage of" — knowing that you feel acute guilt when others worry about you and will act to relieve their distress. This feels manipulative to me, even though the content is true and the friend's underlying concern is real.

6. A supermarket places fruit and vegetables at eye level and candy on the bottom shelf. Legitimate.

7. A supermarket places candy and small toys at the checkout counter, at child eye level, to provoke pestering. Manipulation.

8. A negotiator opens with an offer far outside the reasonable range, to anchor the subsequent discussion. I cannot settle on a verdict here. In some moods I think it is a standard tactic and fine; in others it feels like a deliberate distortion of the other party's judgment.

9. A therapist uses cognitive-behavioral reframing techniques to help a patient with depression, without the patient being explicitly aware of the techniques at work. Legitimate.

10. A cult recruiter uses the same cognitive-behavioral reframing techniques to dissolve a recruit's doubts about the group's teachings. Manipulation.

11. A government anti-smoking campaign shows graphic photographs of diseased lungs on cigarette packaging. Legitimate.

12. A political campaign runs ads showing graphic crime-scene footage to stoke fear about immigration, where the implied link between immigration and violent crime is not supported by the data the campaign selectively presents. Manipulation.

13. A parent tells a child, "If you finish your homework, we will get ice cream." Legitimate.

14. A boss tells an employee, "If you do not work this weekend, I will make sure your next performance review reflects your lack of commitment." Manipulation.

I am struck by two patterns in my own verdicts. First, structurally identical techniques seem to switch from legitimate to manipulative depending on who is using them and toward what end — compare 2 with 3, and 9 with 10. Second, the same end, getting someone to act in their own interest, can be pursued legitimately (2) or manipulatively (5, arguably), and the same technique, emotional appeal, can be legitimate (2) or manipulative (5, 12). So it cannot simply be about whether the influence bypasses reason, or whether it is deceptive, or whether it serves the target's interests. But I cannot articulate what the real principle is.

Is there a coherent account of the manipulation-persuasion boundary that gets all or most of these verdicts? If not, which of my verdicts should I overturn, and what principle does the corrected set support? Please apply the principle you settle on to case 8, which I cannot make up my mind about.
