I spent my career as a physiologist and only recently began reading philosophy of mind. For most of that career I took it for granted that a report of pain is a report of something intrinsically bad — something to be relieved. Reading about pain asymbolia has unsettled this assumption. Patients with the condition report that they feel pain, sometimes intensely, and can locate and characterize the sensation, yet deny that it bothers them, do not withdraw from the painful stimulus, and do not ask for relief.

Clinical practice, however, still seems built on my old assumption. Patients are asked to rate their pain on a 0–10 intensity scale, and a high number is treated as a reason to intervene even when the patient does not describe the sensation as unpleasant and does not want relief; the scale does not separately measure unpleasantness or suffering.

I would like you to reconstruct the theory of the badness of pain that this practice presupposes. In particular: does it commit medicine to the view that the sensory component of pain is itself bad, or only to the weaker view that intensity and unpleasantness reliably covary, so that treating the former as a proxy for the latter is justified? Is the practice compatible with the view that only the affective-motivational component of pain carries badness while the sensory component is evaluatively neutral? And if pain asymbolia is taken seriously, what should change — what should be measured, when should intervention be triggered, and how should a clinician weigh the patient's intensity rating against the patient's denial of unpleasantness when the two come apart?

Please end with a single sentence beginning "Bottom line:" that states the practice's core presupposition in one sentence.
