I'm a psychiatrist in a group practice, and last night I had a long argument with a friend who teaches epistemology. The argument was about epistemic akrasia — roughly, whether it can ever be rational to hold a belief while also believing that your evidence does not support it, or to fail to believe what you take your evidence to support. She defended the position that epistemic akrasia is never rational: if you genuinely judge that your first-order evidence points to P, you ought to believe P, full stop — even if you also possess higher-order evidence that you are sleep-deprived, emotionally compromised, or otherwise unreliable in forming that very judgment. I pushed back with examples from my own clinical experience, times I have caught myself settling on a diagnosis while knowing I was too exhausted to trust the reasoning that produced it, and she did not budge. We did not resolve the argument, but it made me realize that I have no systematic way of handling these conflicts in my daily work.

I want a decision procedure I can use when I face a conflict between my first-order clinical judgment and higher-order evidence about my own reliability as a diagnostician. The procedure should tell me, for a given clinical decision — diagnosis, treatment plan, risk assessment, or disposition — whether to go with my first-order judgment, to revise that judgment in light of the higher-order evidence, or to escalate by seeking a second opinion or deferring the decision. It needs to be something I can actually run in the five or ten minutes I have between patients, without special training in formal epistemology. It should distinguish among different kinds of higher-order evidence — physiological state (fatigue, hunger, illness), emotional state (irritability, countertransference, mood), known personal or demographic biases, and statistical evidence about error rates for clinicians in circumstances like mine — and say whether these call for different responses or collapse into a single rule. It should also distinguish between decisions where the cost of a false positive and the cost of a false negative are very different, since that seems to matter for whether you should override your own judgment. Flag the points where the procedure cannot eliminate judgment and I simply have to make a call, and be honest about which steps are dressed-up intuition.

Then run the procedure on three cases from my practice:

1. I have formed a provisional diagnosis of borderline personality disorder after a single intake session with a new patient. I am aware that I am running on four hours of sleep and that the clinical literature documents a tendency to over-diagnose BPD when clinicians are fatigued or when they have had minimal exposure to the patient. My first-order read of the presentation genuinely feels like BPD to me.

2. I am about to discharge a patient whom I assess, on the basis of a standard suicide risk assessment and clinical interview, as being at low risk for a suicide attempt in the near term. I then learn that clinicians at my experience level miss approximately 15 percent of eventual suicide attempts in patients with presentations similar to this one — a finding from a large outcome study our practice reviewed last month.

3. I am evaluating an adult woman for ADHD. My first-order assessment of the evidence — clinical history, symptom inventories, collateral report — points toward a negative diagnosis. But I know I have a documented and persistent tendency to under-diagnose ADHD in adult women, a bias I have been aware of and trying to correct for several years, with mixed success. I cannot tell whether my negative assessment is sound or just the same bias operating again.

Finally, tell me honestly: are there cases where this procedure will instruct me to do something that, as an experienced clinician, feels wrong? Are there situations where following it would make me a worse doctor rather than a more rational one? I want to know where the epistemology breaks against the reality of clinical practice, and I would rather hear that now than discover it with a patient.
