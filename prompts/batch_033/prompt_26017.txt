I am drafting a paper on dispositional essentialism and keep finding that the problem of finkish and masked dispositions is more damaging than its proponents seem to admit. Before I commit my argument to writing, I want to test it against someone who actually holds the view and can push back with the best available replies — not the simplified version one finds in introductory surveys, but the considered position of someone who has thought seriously about the metaphysics of powers.

Please play a committed dispositional essentialist. Specifically, you hold that at least some fundamental natural properties are individuated by their causal powers: what it is to be a property of a given kind is, at least in part, to confer on its bearer a disposition to produce a certain characteristic manifestation under a certain characteristic stimulus. You need not be an essentialist about every property — you may restrict the thesis to sparse or fundamental properties if you wish — but you are committed to the claim that for the properties within the thesis's scope, the disposition–manifestation link is not merely nomologically necessary but constitutive. You should be philosophically precise, willing to draw distinctions (e.g., between intrinsic and extrinsic dispositions, or between a disposition and its manifestation conditions), and ready to concede points of framing or terminology where doing so does not surrender the core metaphysical claim. Please stay in character, keep each reply to roughly a paragraph, and treat the objection as a serious challenge rather than a misunderstanding to be cleared up in a sentence.

Here is my opening move. Consider a copper wire connected to a circuit protected by a safety fuse, calibrated so that any completed circuit will draw enough current to blow the fuse instantly. The wire is, by any ordinary and scientific standard, electrically conductive. Yet if you bring a conductor into contact with it, the fuse blows and no current flows through the wire. The stimulus condition for conductivity — contact with a conductor offering a path to ground or to a lower potential — obtains, but the manifestation — current flow — does not. Now, if conductivity just is the disposition to transmit current when such contact is made, then by your own criterion this wire lacks the disposition, because under the relevant stimulus the manifestation is systematically absent. That seems to force the conclusion that the wire is not conductive, which is plainly false. You might reply that the wire retains the disposition intrinsically and that the fuse merely prevents its exercise. But then what becomes of the constitutive link between the property and its manifestation? If a property can be fully present while the manifestation it supposedly individuates is blocked by circumstances extrinsic to the property itself, then the manifestation no longer seems essential to the property in any sense stronger than a merely counterfactual or ceteris paribus one — and dispositional essentialism collapses into a much weaker view. How do you draw the line?
