I run a behavioral design consultancy that builds commitment devices and self-binding features into consumer apps — savings platforms, fitness trackers, productivity tools, habit trackers. A product team comes to us with a request like "our users keep setting goals and abandoning them; can you build in a commitment mechanism?" and we need to decide what to do. Sometimes the right answer is a hard commitment device; sometimes it is a soft nudge; sometimes the real problem has nothing to do with hyperbolic discounting, and adding a commitment device would make things worse.

I want a reusable decision procedure, grounded in the theory of hyperbolic discounting and what it actually implies about preference reversal and akrasia, that someone on my team can run on any such request. The person running it will have read some behavioral economics but is not a specialist in the philosophy of action or the formal models. The procedure should be a sequence of questions we ask, in order, with guidance on what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure stops with a "no" and we tell the client not to build a commitment device, and where it hands the decision to a human because the question is one of values rather than facts.

The procedure should be honest about the points where the theory itself is ambiguous — for example, where it is genuinely unclear whether a particular pattern of preference reversal counts as irrational, or where two different models of discounting make the same behavioral prediction but different normative ones. It should also flag the cases where the procedure might misfire systematically, and it should say what the procedure cannot do.

Then run the procedure on two cases. First: a savings app where users set aggressive monthly savings targets and then, partway through the month, disable the automatic transfer because they want the money for a specific near-term purchase — not an emergency, just something they want now. Second: a fitness app where users purchase a six-month gym commitment, stop attending after three weeks, report regretting the commitment when surveyed, but then six months later purchase the same commitment again. For each case, walk through the procedure step by step and tell me where the answer turns. If the procedure gives an answer the product team will not like, do not soften it; say what it says and explain why.

I want the entire response in continuous prose, the way you would write a memo to a colleague — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. And I want a committed response: make the calls, give your reasons, and do not simply lay out options for me to choose among.
