One of my colleagues in a philosophy of science reading group keeps insisting that Nelson Goodman's grue problem is not just a disguised case of model selection, and I want to know whether he is right. My own background is machine learning: I think fluently in terms of hypothesis classes, training and test distributions, out-of-distribution generalization, regularization, and inductive bias, but I have no formal training in philosophy.

Here is the puzzle as I understand it. Goodman defines a predicate "grue" that applies to things examined before some future time t just in case they are green, and to other things just in case they are blue. Suppose all emeralds examined so far have been green. Then those same emeralds have also been grue. So the available evidence appears to support both the generalization that all emeralds are green and the generalization that all emeralds are grue, even though the two make incompatible predictions about emeralds first examined after t. The philosophical problem is to say what makes "green" a legitimate, projectible predicate and "grue" a gerrymandered one, without simply appealing to the fact that green is the predicate we happened to have used.

I would like you to restate this problem in the vocabulary I actually work in, as faithfully as you can: two hypotheses with identical training loss that diverge on held-out or future data, where the choice between them comes down to inductive bias, regularization, simplicity, or a choice of feature representation, and where "green" and "grue" may be symmetric under a change of coordinates. Be precise about what maps cleanly and what has to be fudged. Then give me the residue report: which parts of the philosophical puzzle get distorted in translation, which have no counterpart in machine learning at all, and whether the leftover shows that my vocabulary is missing something or that the philosophical problem is less substantive than it looks. I want to know whether "it's just model selection" is a real insight or a way of not seeing the problem.
