And here's the student's answer:

"The difference between indicative and counterfactual conditionals is most clearly illustrated by the Oswald-Kennedy pair:

(1) 'If Oswald didn't kill Kennedy, someone else did.'
(2) 'If Oswald hadn't killed Kennedy, someone else would have.'

Both share the same antecedent and consequent, but (1) is clearly true while (2) is very likely false (assuming Oswald acted alone). The key difference is in what we hold fixed when evaluating them. For the indicative (1), we hold fixed everything we know — including that Kennedy was assassinated — and simply add the supposition that Oswald didn't do it. Since we know Kennedy was killed, someone must have done it, so (1) comes out true. For the counterfactual (2), we imagine a world where Oswald didn't kill Kennedy, and we change whatever else needs to change to make that supposition consistent with the past, evaluating what would have happened in that world. So we might imagine a world where no one else stepped in, and Kennedy survived.

This difference suggests that indicative and counterfactual conditionals probably need different semantic theories. Indicatives are naturally handled by Adams' thesis, which says that the probability of an indicative conditional just is the conditional probability of the consequent given the antecedent — so P(If A then C) = P(C|A). This explains why (1) is true: P(someone else killed Kennedy | Oswald didn't) is essentially 1. Counterfactuals, on the other hand, need a possible-worlds semantics with a similarity ordering, like Lewis's or Stalnaker's, because we need to evaluate what happens in the closest A-worlds, not just conditionalize on our background knowledge. The fact that these two kinds of conditionals behave so differently under negation and embedding also supports the idea that they have different underlying logics and can't be unified under a single theory."
