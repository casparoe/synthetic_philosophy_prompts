I spent thirty years in experimental physics before retiring — mostly optics, some condensed matter — and I've been reading philosophy of mind for the last couple years. Dennett, Clark, Hohwy, Seth, the whole crew. I've gotten pretty taken with the predictive processing framework and the free energy principle. The basic idea, as I understand it, is that the brain is fundamentally a prediction engine: it builds generative models of the world, compares incoming sensory data against top-down predictions, and minimizes prediction error either by updating the model (perception) or by acting to make the prediction come true (action). The free energy principle says all of this is just minimizing variational free energy, which is an information-theoretic bound on surprise. What I really like about it is that it gives you a single, unified story about perception, action, attention, even some psychopathology — all falling out of one optimization principle. It's the kind of elegant unifying framework that reminds me of why I went into physics in the first place: you take a messy phenomenon and show its just a system doing gradient descent on a single quantity.

But here's the thing nagging at me. I've been around long enough to know that a theory that explains everything can easily end up explaining nothing. I've come across the criticism — I think it's out there in the literature, though I'm not sure who said it first — that the free energy principle is unfalsifiable: that any organism doing literally anything can be described as minimizing free energy under some model, and that the principle doesn't stick its neck out in a way that lets you cut it off. I'm not sure I buy that, but I want to take it seriously. So here's my question: I hold the view that predictive processing and the free energy principle are on the right track as a fundamental account of brain function. What would count against it? I want the defeaters — empirical results, conceptual problems, alternative frameworks that do better — ranked from most damaging to least. I want an honest assessment of whether the framework has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if it has, whether that's a problem for a framework of this kind (as opposed to a specific hypothesis). I'm especially interested in stuff that would have made me, as a working experimentalist, say "alright, that hypothesis is dead." Give me exactly five, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion, ranked from fatal to merely awkward.
