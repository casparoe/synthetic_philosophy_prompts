I retired from a career in molecular biology a couple years ago and started sitting in on this philosophy discussion group at the communtiy college. Mostly I just listen, but last Tuesday two of the regulars — Miriam and Doug — got into a proper argument about Frankfurt cases and I've been turning it over in my head ever since. I took some notes after, so this is pretty close to verbatim.

Miriam started by saying she doesn't think Frankfurt actually refuted the principle of alternative possibilities, and that the cases are tricking us somehow. Doug said that's wild — the whole setup is designed so the agent acts on their own, nobody forces them, but there's a fail-safe that would have forced them if they'd tried to do otherwise. Since the fail-safe never activates, the agent couldn't have done otherwise, yet they're clearly responsible. That seems like a clean counterexample.

Miriam said: but they *could* have done otherwise — they could have shown some tiny prior sign of hesitation or wavering, and *that* would have triggered the fail-safe. So there's still an alternative possibility, it's just been relocated to an earlier moment. Doug said that's the "flicker of freedom" move and it's been chewed over for decades — the flicker isn't the kind of alternative that could possibly ground moral responsibility. Being responsible for a murder doesn't depend on whether you could have blinked differently right beforehand.

Miriam said okay, but there's a bigger problem. She said Frankfurt cases face a dilemma. Either the actual sequence of events — the agent's deliberation and decision — is deterministic, or it isn't. If it's deterministic, then the agent couldn't have done otherwise *anyway*, regardless of the fail-safe. The mechanism is irrelevant; determinism already closed off the alternatives. If it's indeterministic, then the would-be intervener can't know with certainty what the agent will do, so the fail-safe can't *guarantee* the agent does the thing — there's always some possible world where the agent wavers, the mechanism fires, and the agent does it only because forced. In that world they're not responsible, and in the worlds where they do it freely, they *could* have done otherwise. So either way the case doesn't land.

Doug pushed back on the second horn. He said the intervener doesn't need certainty — they just need a reliable indicator. If the agent was going to do otherwise, the indicator would fire and the intervention would happen. The fact that there's some remote possible world where the indicator misfires doesn't mean the agent could have done otherwise in the actual world. Counterfactual control doesn't require determinism.

Miriam said that's too quick — if the connection between the indicator and the decision is only probabilistic, then in the actual world there was a real chance the agent would have done otherwise, and "could have done otherwise" just means there was a real chance. Doug said that conflates epistemic possibility with the metaphysical kind, and anyway Frankfurt's point is about the *principle* that responsibility requires alternative possibilities — even if the cases aren't airtight, they show the principle isn't obviously true.

They went around once more — something about whether the dilemma is really a dilemma or a trilemma, I lost the thread a bit — but that's the core of it.

I'm a biologist by training, not a philosopher, and I honestly can't tell who's got the better arguments here. Can you go through their exchange point by point and say who's right about what? And if there's something important both of them are missing, I'd like to know that too.
