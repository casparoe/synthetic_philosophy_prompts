I'm running a small philosophy meetup and we're doing the two-envelope paradox next week. I want to make a handout with a decision tree that helps people diagnose *where* exactly they think the reasoning goes wrong — or whether they think it goes wrong at all.

The setup: Two envelopes, one contains twice as much money as the other. You pick one at random, open it, and find $100. You're offered the chance to switch to the other envelope. The argument for switching: the other envelope must contain either $50 or $200, and since you picked randomly, these are equally likely. So the expected value of switching is ½($50) + ½($200) = $125, which is more than the $100 you're holding. So you should switch. But notice: this argument works no matter what amount you see when you open the envelope. So you'd switch regardless — which means you should just pick the other envelope to begin with, and then the same argument would tell you to switch back. Something's off.

The tree should start from "do you think there's a flaw in this argument?" and then branch through diagnostic questions that narrow down *what kind* of flaw, or whether the person thinks it's not a flaw at all. The leaves should include at least these positions:

- The improper-prior diagnosis (the 50-50 assignment is unjustified without a proper probability distribution over the possible amounts)
- The infinite-expectation diagnosis (the expected values aren't well-defined)
- The equivocation diagnosis (the argument uses X inconsistently across the two scenarios)
- The no-paradox position (switching really is rational, and that's fine)

Each question should be phrased so someone who just heard the paradox for the first time can answer it — no jargon, no asking "do you hold the Bayesian diagnosis?" Also, if there are logically possible combinations of answers that nobody actually defends, flag them and say briefly *why* (incoherent? just unexplored?).

Finally: could you note which branch point in your tree is the most philosophically contentious, and what argument or empirical result would most likely lead you to restructure the tree? Like, what's the thing that would change your mind about how the positions shoud be carved up?
