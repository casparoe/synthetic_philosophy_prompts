Last weekend I was at a barbecue and ended up stuck between two friends who got into a genuinely interesting argument about Occam's razor. I took some notes on my phone because I wanted to think about it later, and now I'm hoping you can help me sort through it.

Dan kicked it off by saying he'd finally found a mathematical justification for Occam's razor that actually works — Solomonoff induction. He said the idea is that you consider all possible computable hypotheses, assign each a prior probability proportional to 2^{-K}, where K is the length of the shortest program that generates the hypothesis, and then update on evidence. Simpler hypotheses get exponentially more prior weight, so Occam's razor falls right out.

Mira said that's sleight of hand. The length of the shortest program depends on your programming language — your universal Turing machine. Pick a different UTM and you get different lengths, different priors, different verdicts about which hypothesis is simpler. She said the whole thing is language-relative and therefore arbitrary.

Dan said the invariance theorem handles this: switching UTMs only changes the Kolmogorov complexity by a constant, and that constant gets absorbed as you get more data. In the limit, the choice of UTM doesn't matter.

Mira said "in the limit" is doing a lot of work. The constant can be as large as you like, and with any finite amount of evidence — which is all we ever have — the constant can swamp the data. So for any real inference problem, the UTM choice is doing all the normative work, and there's no UTM-independent fact about which hypothesis is simpler.

Dan paused and then shifted ground a bit. He said maybe the real insight of Solomonoff isn't about simplicity per se, but about computability. The result shows that any Bayesian with a computable prior will converge to the truth in the limit, regardless of what that prior is. Simplicity is just one way to have a computable prior, but what's really doing the justificatory work is that you're not ruling out any computable hypothesis in advance. Occam's razor is vindicated not as a unique principle but as a member in good standing of the class of computable priors.

Mira said that's a much weaker claim than what Dan started with. If the justification is just "simplicity is one computable prior among many," then Occam's razor doesn't get any special status. You could use an anti-Occam prior that assigns higher probability to more complex hypotheses, and as long as it's computable, you'd still converge to the truth in the limit. So Solomonoff induction doesn't tell us to prefer simplicity — it tells us that simplicity is not ruled out, which is a completely different thing.

Dan said that's still important, because it means Occam's razor isn't just some vague aesthetic preference — it's a coherent strategy with a convergence guarantee. The anti-Occam prior also converges, sure, but it converges slower, and in practice we care about finite-sample performance.

Mira said now we're back to the UTM problem. Whether a simplicity prior converges faster than an anti-simplicity prior depends on the UTM and on what the actual world is like. There's no guarantee that simplicity wins in finite samples. And if the world happens to be complex, the anti-Occam prior might even do better.

They went back and forth a bit more after that but I had to leave to get more food. I've been turning it over since. Can you go through their exchange and adjudicate move by move — who's right about which specific point, where each of them is on strong ground and where they're overreaching, and what the overall upshot is? I'm not asking who "wins" exactly, but I want to know which claims survive scrutiny and which don't.
