I'm a journalist covering AI and the ideas behind it for a general-interest magazine. Lately I've been running into a problem I can't solve on the fly, and I need a procedure I can reuse across stories.

When an AI lab, startup, or research group claims that its approach to alignment is "prosaic"—meaning it relies only on extensions of current deep learning methods and does not require fundamental conceptual breakthroughs about agency, values, or intelligence—I need to know whether the claim is substantive. The label matters. It tells readers, funders, and regulators that a group's safety work is grounded in the engineering we already know how to do, rather than banking on insights nobody has had yet. But I've seen the term used loosely, and I don't have a systematic way to tell the difference between a genuine extension of the current toolkit and a research agenda that quietly assumes the hard part will get solved later.

I'd like you to write a decision procedure I can run when I encounter one of these claims. It should be a sequence of questions, ordered so that I can stop early if the answer is clearly no, with criteria for what counts as a sufficient answer to each. Flag the points where the procedure can't settle the question and I need to call an outside expert, and the points where the judgment is substantive rather than mechanical—where reasonable people applying the same question could disagree. The procedure should be grounded in what the prosaic-versus-esoteric distinction actually tracks: the difference between methods that demonstrably work within the current ML paradigm and methods that would require a new kind of theory to become reliable.

Practical constraints: I'm on a deadline of a day or two. I can read a technical paper and parse its claims, but I'm not going to run code or reproduce experiments. I can reach out to one or two outside researchers for a sanity check, but I can't commission a full external review. The procedure needs to be something my editor would accept as a defensible part of my reporting—it can't be a hunch dressed up as a flowchart.

Please walk the procedure through at least two worked examples, drawn from real categories of alignment work if you can, and make one of them a case where the procedure returns an answer I'd rather not get—for instance, a company I'd be inclined to take seriously whose claim doesn't survive the test. Then tell me plainly what the procedure cannot do: what kinds of alignment claims it's blind to, where the prosaic/esoteric distinction itself breaks down, and what a journalist using it would still be guessing about.

If the whole premise of operationalizing "prosaic alignment" this way is confused—if the term is too contested, or the distinction it tries to draw collapses under scrutiny, or the real action is elsewhere—say so. I would rather learn that the question is ill-posed than get a procedure that looks clean and misleads me.
