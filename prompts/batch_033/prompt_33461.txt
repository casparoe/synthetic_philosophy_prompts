I've been going back and forth on whether "qualia" is a coherent posit at all, and I want help figuring out what my verdicts on cases add up to. Here's where I land.

Chase. A coffee taster who's drunk the same brand for twenty years and loved it. One day he says the coffee itself tastes different to him now—same coffee, but the very taste changed. I believe he's sincere, and I'm sure something in his experience changed. But I dont think his report gives me reason to posit a "taste quale" that changed; his standards, or his gustatory system, could have shifted and he'd report the same thing.

Sanborn. Same story, except he insists the taste is exactly the same and only his liking changed—he just doesn't enjoy it anymore. My verdict: also not decisive. He could have the same taste with a changed reaction, or a changed taste with a changed reaction that he misdescribes as a pure change in reaction. Him saying "it tastes the same" doesn't settle it.

Inverted spectrum, two people. Two people from birth, behaviorally identical about color, and the question is whether red and green look to one the way they look to the other, or are swapped. Here I find myself wanting to say there is a fact of the matter—each of them has something it's like to see a stop sign—and "same or swapped" is a real question even if we can never answer it. I cant shake that.

Inverted spectrum, one person over time. I wake up after a neural procedure and grass now looks the way red used to look to me, and red things look green. I remember grass looking green before. My verdict: I'd say my color experience genuinely changed, but I'd also have to admit I cant tell whether the "green quale" is now attached to grass or whether my memory of how green looked has been altered. The report feels authoritative that something changed, but not about what changed.

Phantom limb. Someone feels a burning pain in a hand that was amputated. Verdict: the pain is real, and there's a felt quality to it that isn't just the disposition to wince, take painkillers, or say "ouch." There's something it's like, and it doesn't reduce to those dispositions.

My working principle, which I'm not sure survives any of this: a quale is whatever is left over after you subtract all the dispositions, functional roles, memories, and reports from an experience—the intrinsic, private, directly-known something that the reports are reports of. But my Chase and Sanborn verdicts seem to say the reports don't latch onto that thing, and my inverted-spectrum verdicts seem to say there's a fact about it anyway.

So: is there a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that gets all or most of my verdicts? If there is, what does it say about a case I haven't listed—say, someone with a red afterimage versus someone who merely knows the wall is red? If there isn't, which verdicts should I give up, and what does the surviving principle say about the cases I'd then have to reclassify? And is the leftover-after-subtraction idea itself coherent, or is that where the whole problem lives?
