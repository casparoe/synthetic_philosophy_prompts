I'm teaching a four-week adult-education course called "What's Wrong with Dying?", and in the second session I have seventy-five minutes to get a room of thoughtful non-philosophers — a retired nurse, a software engineer, a high-school history teacher, a few others — to genuinely feel the force of the Epicurean argument that death is not bad for the one who dies, before we spend the next two weeks on responses to it. They'll have read the short Epicurus and Lucretius selections, but they won't have understood why anyone took the argument seriously; they'll arrive thinking it's obviously sophistry.

Plan how I should use that seventy-five minutes. I need the order in which the pieces must be introduced so that the argument lands as something that has to be answered rather than dismissed, the one example or scenario that carries the whole thing and what it must have and must lack, the misconceptions they'll form — I'm guessing at least 'the argument confuses the state of being dead with the process of dying,' 'it only works if you think nothing can be bad for you unless you feel it at the time,' and 'the symmetry argument about prenatal nonexistence just shows the whole thing is a trick' — and exactly where in the sequence each should be headed off, plus the one question I can pose at the end that will tell me whether they've grasped the argument's structure rather than just found it creepy. Also tell me what I should openly refuse to settle at this level and why leaving it unsettled is pedagogically better than resolving it. And I'd like the response to flag which step in the reasoning is most vulnerable — the point where, if a student pushed hard enough, the plan would need to change — and what kind of pushback would force that change.
