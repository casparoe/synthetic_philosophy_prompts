Take the Lucas–Penrose argument — the claim, roughly, that Gödel's incompleteness theorems show human mathematical understanding cannot be captured by any Turing machine — as a case study in what the theorems do and do not show outside mathematics. I know the standard one-sentence caveat that the theorems are about formal systems; I want the disciplined mapping. Work through exactly which properties of a formal system the first and second incompleteness theorems require (consistency, recursive axiomatizability, the relevant amount of arithmetic) and what the conclusions actually assert; then isolate each step by which Lucas or Penrose moves from those conclusions to a claim about the human mind, and mark each step as valid, invalid, or valid only under a hidden premise. In particular, address the difference between a given formal system's inability to prove its own consistency and the claim that no mechanical procedure could match a given human's output, and say what the argument needs to assume about the consistency, soundness, and formalizability of the relevant human reasoning. Close by saying what this episode tells us about the wider practice of exporting Gödel's theorems to debates about minds, AI, or social systems: is the export ever legitimate, and what conditions would a responsible use have to satisfy?
