I'm a novelist, and I've been going down a philosophy of language rabbit hole for a book I'm working on — it's about a character who discovers they're not who they think they are, partly through the way names track or fail to track identity across different scenarios. I've ended up convinced by the Kripkean picture: proper names are rigid designators, they pick out the same thing in every possible world, and there are these necessary truths — like that water is H2O — that you can only discover empirically. Its become the backbone of how I'm thinking about the metaphysics of my novel.

But I've been around the block enough to know that when a view feels this neat, I'm probably missing something. So I want to know: what would count against the rigid-designator / necessary-a-posteriori package? I don't mean the obvious objections Kripke already addressed in the lectures — I mean the things that, if they turned out to be true or if you thought about them long enough, would genuinely undermine the view. What are the problem cases, the bad consequences the view is committed to, the empirical results in linguistics or psychology that would be awkward, the places where the apparatus of possible worlds does more harm than good? I especially want to know which of these a committed Kripkean would themselves concede as counting against them, and which they'd dismiss as not engaging the view properly.

Rank them from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me honestly whether the view has been insulated by qualifications to the point where nothing could really touch it — and if so, whether thats a problem for a view of this kind or just how philosophy of language works.
