I'm a PhD student in philosophy of technology, and I'm organising a session of our department's ethics reading group later this term. We've been working through the literature on responsibility gaps for autonomous systems — Matthias, Sparrow, Danaher, Nyholm, and the surrounding discussion — and I want to move beyond seminar discussion into something experiential. We have read the arguments, but I'm not convinced we have felt the problem.

The group is about 14 people, mostly philosophy PhD students with a couple from computer science and law. We have 90 minutes. I'd like you to design a structured role-play activity that makes participants feel the force of a responsibility gap: the sense that when an autonomous system causes serious harm, it can be genuinely unclear who, if anyone, is morally responsible, even though everyone in the development and deployment chain acted reasonably by ordinary standards — and that this unclarity is not merely epistemic but may reflect a structural feature of the situation.

The activity should not begin with any lecture or framing of the concept. Participants should be thrown into roles and discover the problem though the structure of the scenario and the deliberation itself. The debrief should then map what happened onto the philosophical literature, but only after participants have already inhabited the problem.

Here's what I need:

1. A scenario: a concrete case of harm caused by an autonomous system, detailed enough that role cards can be written from it. The scenario should be constructed so that (a) the harm is serious (a death or life-altering injury), (b) every role in the chain made decisions that were defensible given their information, incentives, and the norms of their professional context, (c) no single decision was clearly negligent when assessed in isolation, and (d) the harm emerges from the interaction of many small, individually reasonable decisions across different parts of the chain. I'd prefer a civilian context (e.g., an autonomous delivery drone, a medical diagnostic system, or a self-driving vehicle) rather than an autonomous weapons case, since weapons scenarios let participants off the hook too easily — they can blame the very idea of the system rather than grapple with the distribution of responsibility within a system they are generally inclined to accept.

2. Role cards: for about6–8 roles spanning the development and deployment chain. The roles should include actors at different distances from the harm: e.g., someone involved in algorithm design, someone responsible for training data curation, a safety tester or QA engineer, a product manager who made a feature-prioritisation decision, a fleet operator or dispatcher, an end user or proximate human supervisor, and a regulatory compliance officer. Each card should specify the role's perspective, what they knew and when, what decisions they made, what constraints they were under (time pressure, budget, information asymmetries, organisational incentives), and what they did not know. The cards should be written so that each role can sincerely defend their decisions — no role should be written as obviously culpable, and no role should be written as a straw man. The cards should also include one or two pieces of information that the role holder knows but other roles do not, to create genuine information asymmetries in the room.

3. A procedure: a timed structure for the90--minute session. Include how to assign roles (random draw? matching to temperaments?), how to run the within-role preparation, and how to structure the group deliberation. The deliberation should have enough constraints that it does not become a free--for-all — for instance, rounds of opening statement followed by structured cross--questioning, or a requirement that the group produce a tentative allocation of responsibility shares (percentages summing to100%) by the end. This last constraint is important: forcing a numerical allocation often surfaces the gap more sharply than open--ended discussion does, because participants discover that their assignments do not sum naturally to100% or that they are assigning shares for reasons they cannot defend.

4. A debrief guide: map specific moments or impasses in the activity onto specific moves in the responsibility--gap literature. For instance: if participants end up concentrating blame on the proximate human operator because they need someone to blame, that maps onto the retribution gap vs.. the moral gap (Danaher); if they try to distribute responsibility in percentages but find the exercise frustrating or arbitrary, that maps onto the problem of many hands and the conditions for distributed moral responsibility; if they appeal to forward--looking or regulatory responsibility when backward--looking desert runs out, that maps onto the distinction between kinds of responsibility gaps that Nyholm and others draw. Name the authors and arguments for the facilitator's use, but the debrief should be structured as a guided discussion that starts from what participants experienced, not as a lecture that overwrites it.

5. A troubleshooting section: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and how to handle it. Address at least three failure modes — e.g., a participant who rejects their role and insists the whole system is illegitimate; the group reaching a consensus too quickly and missing the gap; a law student citing legal doctrine that seems to settle the question and short--circuiting the moral inquiry; or participants conflating causal responsibility with moral responsibility thoughout.

6. An honest note on what the activity cannot capture: which feature of responsibility gaps the role--play necessarily distorts or omits, and what the facilitator should supply afterward — briefly — to complete the picture. (For instance, the activity may not capture the way that genuine gaps depend on the system's capacity for genuine learning and behaviour that outruns its programmers' specifications; participants may treat a scripted scenario as fuly predictable in hindsight in a way that real--world autonomous systems are not..)
