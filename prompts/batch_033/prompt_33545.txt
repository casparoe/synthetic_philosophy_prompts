I was arguing with a buddy about this philosophy thought experiment — it's from a guy named Black. Imagine a universe with nothing in it except two iron spheres. They're exactly the same size, same temperature, same composition, perfectly smooth, and the whole universe is perfectly symmetrical so they even have all the same relational properties (each is the same distance from the other as the other is from it). The question is whether there are really two distinct things there, or just one thing that's somehow in two places. My friend says obviously two, they're in different places. But if they share literally every property, including spatial relations relative to each other, it's not clear what makes them "different places" in the first place — the whole setup is symmetric.

Anyway, here's what I can't stop thinking about. The law seems to take a side on this without knowing it. Picture a grocery store with two cans of tomato soup on the shelf, same brand, same batch, absolutely indistinguishable in every physical respect. I pick one up, pay for it, and leave. If I'd picked up the other can, the state of the world afterward would be identical in every observable way — same inventory, same money, same soup in my hand. But the law definitely treats these as two different cans. If a friend swaps the can I paid for with the unpaid can while I'm not looking, and I walk out with the latter, I've technically shoplifted — even though the can in my possession is physically identical to the one I bought. The law is tracking which specific token object is involved, not just the type.

What metaphysical theory of identity does this practice presuppose? It seems to require something like haecceity — a bare "thisness" or primitive distinctness that makes can A different from can B even before anyone labels them or assigns them different spatiotemporal coordinates. But is the practice actually committed to that, or could it be justified on purely pragmatic grounds (we need to track ownership, and spatiotemporal continuity is the cheapest proxy) without taking a stand on the metaphysics? And here's the part that bothers me: in other contexts the law treats identical items as completely interchangeable. In commercial law for fungible goods — grain in a silo, oil in a pipeline — you own a quantity, not specific tokens. Nobody tracks which wheat kernels are yours. So the practice seems to flip between presupposing haecceity and presupposing something closer to the identity of indiscernibles, depending on the context. I want to know: is the implicit theory actually inconsistent, or is there a coherent principle that handles both kinds of case? And if we decided the identity of indiscernibles was true — that genuinely indiscernible things can't be numerically distinct — what would have to change in the legal system?
