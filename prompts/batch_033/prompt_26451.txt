I teach a graduate seminar in democratic theory—fourteen students, mostly from philosophy and political science, a couple from law. We have a three-hour session coming up on the boundary problem: the question of who properly belongs to the demos, the "people" that is supposed to govern itself democratically. The core difficulty is that this question cannot itself be settled by democratic procedures without circularity, since you need a demos already constituted in order to run a democratic procedure that would decide who is in it. I want the students to feel the force of this circularity, not just read about it. I want them to walk into the trap themselves and only recognize where they are once they are stuck.

Design a structured in-class activity that does this. The students should not have read anything on the boundary problem before the session; the activity is their introduction to it. The name "boundary problem" should not be uttered until the debrief.

Here are the parameters. The activity should be built around a concrete scenario: a territory with overlapping populations that have different kinds of claims on a decision that must be made—for instance, a watershed that crosses several existing jurisdictions and needs a new governance body, or a newly settled region whose constitutional convention is about to be convened. The scenario should be detailed enough that different principles for drawing the boundary (all-affected-interests, all-subjected to the laws, residency, citizenship, stakeholder, etc.) pull in genuinely different directions and no single principle obviously dominates.

Split the class into small groups—you decide how many and of what size. Give each group a role or a brief that pushes them toward a different principle for who should count. The briefs should make each group's principle feel intuitively compelling from inside that role, so that the conflict is real and not merely academic.

The activity should have three phases. In the first phase, each group works internally to design its preferred demos for the decision, using the principle embedded in its brief, and prepares a justification. In the second phase, the groups come together and must collectively agree on a single procedure for determining who gets to vote—but they cannot yet vote, because they have not agreed on who the voters are. In the third phase, the facilitator intervenes: whatever procedure a group proposes for settling the disagreement, the facilitator asks who gets to participate in that procedure, and on what basis that meta-boundary is drawn. The aim is to back them into the circularity—every answer either presupposes the very boundary it is supposed to justify or appeals to a non-democratic principle (force, tradition, nature, an external authority) that the group finds uncomfortable. The activity ends at the point of maximum frustration, before the debrief.

Provide: the full scenario and role briefs; rules for each phase; a timeline for the three-hour session (including setup, the three phases, a break, and the debrief); materials needed; and a detailed debrief script that maps what happened in each phase onto the philosophical structure of the boundary problem. The debrief should distinguish the constitution of the demos from decision-making within the demos, explain why democratic theory cannot supply its own foundations here, and connect the students' impasse to the theoretical literature without naming texts they haven't read—the concepts should land because they just lived them. The debrief should also surface the genuine attractions of each principle they were given, so that no group feels its position was merely a setup.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong when I run this: which group will try to win by procedural fiat, where students will attempt to dissolve the problem rather than feel it (e.g., by appealing to an existing constitution, by saying "we just take a vote of everyone in the room," or by claiming the scenario is artificial), and how I should handle each of these moves in the moment without defusing the tension too early. Finally, say how confident you are in the overall design and single out the point you are least sure of.
