ok so me and a buddy were working through the principal principle and we built this argument that seems to show its flat-out inconsistent with a humean account of chance, and now we're stuck because the argument looks airtight but the conclusion is absurd. can you tell us which step is bad and what we'd have to pay to reject it?

here's the argument.

(1) suppose the humean best-system account of chance is right: the chances at a world are whatever the best systematization of the mosaic of local facts says they are. let H be the complete true chance theory at some world — the full set of chance claims that hold there.

(2) now consider a possible future F such that (i) H assigns F a positive chance, but (ii) if F occurs, H is false. this isn't hard to construct. imagine H says a coin is fair, so Ch(heads) = 0.5. now let F be an enormously long run of heads — long enough that if it happened, the best system would instead say the coin is biased, making H false. so Ch(F | H) > 0, but H entails not-F.

(3) the principal principle says: for any rational initial credence function Cr and any admissible evidence E, Cr(A | H & E) = Ch(A | H). take E to be empty and A to be F. then Cr(F | H) = Ch(F | H) > 0.

(4) but by the probability calculus, Cr(F | H) = Cr(F & H) / Cr(H). since H entails not-F, Cr(F & H) = 0, so Cr(F | H) = 0.

(5) so Cr(F | H) > 0 and Cr(F | H) = 0. contradiction.

(6) therefore the principal principle is incompatible with humeanism about chance (or with standard probability, but that's a nonstarter).

the conclusion seems way too strong — lewis knew about this kind of worry and still endorsed something like the principal principle, so there's gotta be a fix. but i can't tell whether the problem is in (2) (maybe H can't coherently assign positive chance to its own falsity?), or in (3) (maybe the principal principle needs a restriction that rules out F as inadmissible even when E is empty?), or in (1) (maybe humeanism has to go), or somewhere else entirely. where does this break, and what does fixing it cost you?
