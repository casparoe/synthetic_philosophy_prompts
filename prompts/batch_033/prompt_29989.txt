In my epistemology seminar we've been doing norms of assertion, and one of my classmates keep a running list of cases with her verdicts about whether the person was in a position to assert what they said. She won't state the rule, so we've been trying to reverse-engineer it. Her verdicts so far: (1) looking out the window, seeing rain, and saying "it's raining" is fine; (2) saying "the coin landed heads" on a hunch with no evidence is not fine, even if the coin did land heads; (3) saying "it's raining, but I don't believe it's raining" is not fine even when both halves are true; (4) an experienced birdwatcher who identifies a distant bird as a cedar waxwing in good light is fine to say so, even though she'd admit a small chance she's misidentified it; (5) telling a tourist the museum closes at five because a friend who works there just told you is fine, assuming it's true. Next class she's going to rule on this one: a man's mechanic, who is usually reliable, tells him his car needs a new alternator; the mechanic is wrong — the real problem is a bad battery cable — but the man tells his wife "the car needs a new alternator." We're supposed to predict her verdict before class.

Can you predict what she'll say about the alternator case? More usefully, identify the rule or rules her earlier verdicts are consistent with, say which earlier cases rule out which candidate rules (truth norm, belief norm, certainty norm, knowledge norm, reasonable-belief norm, or whatever else you think is live), what each remaining rule predicts about the alternator case, and then give your best guess with the reasons for it. Also tell me what further case would settle which rule she's actually running, and what would change your mind about the prediction — which argument or case would make you flip, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
