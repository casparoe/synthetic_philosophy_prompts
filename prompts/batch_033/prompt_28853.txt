At my job I'm the person who writes up incident reports for a large-language-model assistant, and we have the same fight every single week. Half the team writes things like "the model believed the user was asking for medical advice, so it tried to hedge," and the other half says that's anthropomorphic nonsense and we should only ever say things like "the model assigned high probability to tokens consistent with hedging." The reports now go to people who make real decisions and sometimes to regulators, so I can't just let each engineer use whatever language feels right to them. I recently read a bit about Dennett's intentional stance — the idea, roughly, that you're allowed to attribute beliefs and desires to a system when treating it as a rational agent is the best way to predict what it will do, rather than describing it only in terms of its design or its physical workings. I think that's the principle we need, but I don't know how to turn it into something my coworkers will actually follow at 2 a.m. during an incident.

Could you write me a reusable decision procedure for when an engineer on my team may use mental-state language about the model (believes, wants, expects, is trying to, etc.) in an incident report, eval writeup, or runbook, and when they have to fall back to non-mental language? It should be a checklist or a flowchart in words that an on-call engineer can run in about twenty minutes without a philosopher in the room. For each step, say what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it has to hand the decision to a person because the underlying question is one of live philosophical dispute rather than settled fact. Then run the procedure on two or three concrete cases: (1) the model confidently told a user a false medical claim — should we say it "believed" the claim? (2) the model refused a request, and in the transcript it looks like it "tried" several different excuses; and (3) a case where the temptng mental description is very natural but your procedure says not to use it, because I want to see where the procedure gives me an answer I won't like. Please also be explicit about which parts of this are settled among people who actually work on the intentional stance and which parts are genuinely contested, and don't write as if one side of a live dispute is the consensus. American English, please.
