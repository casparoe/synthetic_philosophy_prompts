I recently spent a long evening arguing with a friend about which computations matter morally — about what it is in a computational system that makes it sentient, capable of being harmed, or otherwise a fit object of moral concern. I defended a substrate-neutral view: what matters is the system's causal-functional organization, so any physical realization of the right kind of computation — the kind that in us underlies pain, fear, and desire — deserves the same moral consideration, whether it is realized in neurons, silicon, or some other substrate. My friend defended an implementation-sensitive view: moral status attaches to concrete physical processes, not to abstract computational descriptions, so two systems can compute the same function while differing morally, because what matters includes the substrate's intrinsic causal powers, its history, and its connection to a self-maintaining body. We argued for hours and neither of us conceded.

Write a dialogue between two characters, equally competent and arguing in good faith, who hold these two positions. Neither character should be a strawman; each should be able to state the other's position fairly before responding to it. The dialogue should be a genuine back-and-forth in which the characters respond to each other's actual arguments rather than talking past each other. Have them test their positions against hard cases: a perfect neuron-by-neuron simulation of a suffering animal, a lookup table that reproduces the same input-output behavior, and a simple homeostatic system with little or no cognition. The exchange should end without a tidy resolution, but with both positions sharpened.
