The argument seems to prove that if every truth is knowable, then every truth is actually known — which is obviously bonkers. But the steps all look airtight to me, and none of my classmates' objections landed.

Here's the argument as our professor laid it out. (1) Assume the knowability thesis: for any true proposition p, it's possible that someone knows p. This sounds like a modest, anti-dogmatic claim — it just says there are no truths that are in principle unknowable. (2) Now consider the proposition: "p is true and nobody knows that p." If there are any unknown truths out there, this proposition is true for some p. (3) Plug it into the knowability thesis: it's possible that someone knows that (p is true and nobody knows p). (4) Knowledge distributes over conjunction: if you know (A and B), then you know A and you know B. (5) Knowledge is factive: if you know A, then A is true. (6) So if someone knows that (p is true and nobody knows p), then they know that p (by distribution) and they also know that nobody knows p (by distribution). (7) Since knowledge is factive, "nobody knows p" must be true in that situation. But wait — the knower themselves knows p at that very moment. So someone knows p and nobody knows p. Contradiction. (8) So it's impossible for anyone to know that (p is true and nobody knows p). (9) But by the knowability thesis, if that proposition is true, it must be knowable. So it can't be true. (10) Therefore, for any p, if p is true then someone knows p. All truths are known.

I want to keep the knowability thesis — it feels like the kind of thing anyone who isn't a radical skeptic about inquiry should accept. I also want to keep the obvious fact that there are unknown truths (like, how many grains of sand were on this one beach in Crete in 400 BCE).
