I've been reading the philosophy of consciousness literature on the side, specifically higher-order theories, and I keep having the intuition that there must be a clean way to state what these theories are claiming in computational terms. But every time I try the translation myself, something slips — either I end up with a claim so thin it's trivial, or I catch myself importing architectural assumptions the philosopher never signed up for.

Here's what I'd like you to do. Take higher-order theories of consciousness — primarily the higher-order thought (HOT) theory associated with Rosenthal, but flag differences for the higher-order perception (HOP) variant and the self-representational variant where they matter — and restate each theory's core claims in the vocabulary I work with. Use concepts like: representations, meta-representations, generative models, training objectives (supervised, unsupervised, self-supervised), attention, recurrence, architectural levels, readout, reportability, and anything else from that toolkit that does honest descriptive work. Don't force a mapping where there isn't one, and don't smuggle in commitments from the target vocabulary (e.g., that representations must be learned from data, or that consciousness is a functional kind) that the original theory doesn't make.

Then give me a residue report, organized into these four sections:

**1. What translates cleanly** (roughly 250 words). Claims that have a natural computational counterpart — for instance, "a first-order representation of red" mapping onto a feature in a sensory encoder, "a higher-order representation of that representation" mapping onto a meta-representation in a hierarchical architecture. Be specific about the mapping and about which architectural commitments it does and doesn't entail. If the translation suggests a particular computational mechanism (e.g., that the higher-order representation must be a readout from the first-order one, or that it must be available to a downstream module), say whether the philosophical theory actually requires that mechanism or merely allows it.

Claims you can force into the vocabulary but only by stretching a term or dropping a distinction the philosophers treat as load-bearing. **3. What has no counterpart at all** (roughly 300 words). Claims that simply cannot be expressed in the computational vocabulary. This is the part I most want to get right. For each such claim, tell me whether the failure is because (a) the computational vocabulary is genuinely missing a concept it needs — something about phenomenology, about what-it's-likeness, that no amount of architectural refinement will capture — or (b) the philosophical claim, when you try to make it computationally precise, turns out to be less clear than it looked, perhaps trading on an ambiguity that dissolves under pressure.

**4. Diagnosis** (roughly 300 words). Given the residue, what should someone in my position conclude?
