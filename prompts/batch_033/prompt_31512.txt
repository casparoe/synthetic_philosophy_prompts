I've spent the past few months reporting on what happens to medical data once it leaves the hospital, and I keep running into a practice that seems to rest on a theory of privacy that no one states explicitly. Under the American health-privacy rules, a hospital or insurer may pass a dataset of patient records to researchers, a data broker, or a technology company without obtaining anyone's consent, so long as the data has been de-identified in the prescribed way: a fixed list of fields—names, addresses, telephone numbers, full dates, Social Security numbers, and similar identifiers—is stripped out or coarsened. Once that list has been removed, the dataset is legally treated as no longer being information about identifiable persons, and the privacy rules simply stop applying to it. The privacy officer I interviewed presented this as obvious: remove the labels and no one is exposed.

The computer scientists I speak with keep insisting that this is not how the world works. They describe studies in which supposedly de-identified health records were relinked to named individuals using nothing more exotic than public voter rolls, and they argue that modern statistical tools are getting better at turning apparently innocent fields into fingerprints. I can see the empirical problem, but what interests me is the philosophical one. The practice seems to presuppose a particular account of what privacy is and where it resides: that a privacy violation consists in the presence of certain identifying fields, that identifiability is a stable property of a dataset rather than a relation between a dataset and the rest of the world, and that once those fields are gone the record is no longer about anyone in a morally relevant sense. I would like a reconstruction of the implicit theory of privacy that the de-identification practice presupposes—what view of privacy it only makes sense under, which rival views it quietly rules out, and where the practice is philosophically underdetermined because more than one theory would license the same rule. I would also like an assessment of whether the practice is coherent with the rest of the same legal framework, which elsewhere treats individual consent as the core protection, and a concrete account of what would have to change in the governance of health data if the presupposed view were replaced by a theory on which privacy concerns the appropriateness of information flows in context, or by one on which privacy protects people from inferential harm rather than from the presence of labels.
