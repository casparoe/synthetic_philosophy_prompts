I'm teaching a grad seminar on decision theory this semester and we're about to hit the Pasadena paradox. I want to get my own story straight before I walk into the room, because last time I taught this I realized mid-sentence that I was papering over a crack in my own view and had to bluff my way through the last ten minutes. Here's where I've landed, more or less:

(1) I'm committed to expected utility maximization as the right normative theory of choice — not as a description of what people do, but as what they ought to do, modulo the usual caveats about state-dependent utility and act-state dependence.

(2) I think the Pasadena game (and the Altadena game, and the rest of the Nover and Hájek cases) should be rejected: you shouldn't pay to play them, and you shouldn't accept them even for free if there's any opportunity cost. They're not rational gambles.

(3) I think the St. Petersburg paradox is not a deep problem for expected utility theory. Bounded utility, or a finite resource constraint, or something in that ballpark, handles it fine.

(4) I think that when an expected value is genuinely undefined — as in the Pasadena game, where the series converges conditionally but not absolutely — the rational thing is to refuse the gamble. This is a diffrent kind of refusal than when EV is defined and negative, and it needs a different justification.

(5) I hold that dominance reasoning (if A beats B in every possible state, choose A) is a more fundamental constraint on rational choice than expected utility maximization, and it can settle some cases where EV is silent.

I can feel that some of these are pulling against each other but I'm not sure which ones. Specifically, I'm worried that (1) and (2) might be in tension if I can't give a principled story about what EU maximization actually says when EV is undefined. And I'm worried that (3) and (4) might be using different frameworks without my noticing — one appeals to the structure of utility, the other to the structure of the gamble itself. But I might also be missing a deeper tension, or worrying about something that's only apparent.

Give me a committed audit: rank the genuine tensions in this set by severity, flag any apparent tensions that dissolve, and tell me what the cheapest single revision would be. I want the answer as a numbered list of exactly five items — each a sentence or two, no intro, no conclusion — where each item identifies one tension or apparent tension and gives a definite verdict on it.
