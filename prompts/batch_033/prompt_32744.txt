Question: Explain the backward induction paradox as it arises in the centipede game. What does the paradox reveal about the assumption of common knowledge of rationality?

Student's answer:

The centipede game, introduced by Rosenthal (1981), is a two-player extensive-form game in which players alternate choosing whether to take a larger share of a growing pot — ending the game — or to pass, allowing the pot to grow and the other player to choose. The payoffs are structured so that at each decision node, the player whose turn it is would prefer to take now rather than pass and have the other player take at the next node; however, both players would prefer that the game continue for many rounds. Backward induction begins at the final node, where the last player to move faces a simple choice between taking and passing, and since taking yields a higher payoff, she will take. Working backwards, each player knows that if she passes, the next player will take at the subsequent node, so each player prefers to take at her own node. The chain of reasoning unravels all the way to the first move, where player 1 takes immediately, even though both would be better off if the game continued. This is the paradox: a seemingly impeccable chain of reasoning leads to a counterintuitive and Pareto-inefficient outcome.

The paradox reveals that common knowledge of rationality is an unrealistic assumption. In experimental settings, players routinely pass in the early rounds, and this behaviour is not irrational once we relax the assumption that rationality is common knowledge. If player 1 assigns even a small probability to player 2 being an altruistic type who might pass, then it can be rational for player 1 to pass as well. The paradox thus shows that the backward induction solution is the unique Nash equilibrium of the game — it is the only strategy profile from which no player has a unilateral incentive to deviate — but this equilibrium rests on assumptions that are seldom satisfied in practice.
