I help run a reading group on AI alignment, and we've been spending this semester on the embedded agency literature. One phrase keeps surfacing in discussion — "the agent and the environment are not cleanly separable" — and I've noticed that different people invoke it to do very different kinds of argumentative work. Last week alone it came up three times: once to argue that we cannot draw a principled boundary between a language model and its training data when attributing generated content to the model; once to claim that standard expected utility theory is fundamentally misguided because it assumes a Cartesian boundary between decision-maker and world; and once to dismiss the whole idea of boxing an AI system as conceptually incoherent, since "you can't separate agent from environment."

I am supposed to lead the next session, and I want to begin by forcing us to get clear on what we are actually asserting when we say this. I suspect the phrase covers several distinct claims that range from the anodyne to the radical, and that some of the arguments above rely on conflating a weak reading (which is true) with a strong one (which would do the work, but is not obviously true).

Could you lay out the distinct precise theses that "the agent and the environment are not cleanly separable" could be expressing? I want them ordered from weakest to strongest, with a verdict for each: is this reading defensible as stated, and which (if any) of the three arguments from last week does it actually license? In particular, I want to know whether there is a coherent reading on which the slogan is both philosophically defensible and strong enough to genuinely threaten the foundations of standard decision theory — or whether that combination is asking too much from a single claim.
