People have been arguing about Wigner's unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics for decades and the debate still seems to run on the same rails: one side treats it as a deep fact about the world that abstract mathematics invented for pure reasons — group theory, complex numbers, Riemannian geometry — keeps turning out to be exactly what physics needs; the other side says its selection bias, or that the math in question was often developed from physical problems in the first place, or that we never keep count of all the pure math that didn't get used. I've read a fair bit of this and nobody has moved. My hunch is that the two sides don't really disagree about the history of mathematics; they disagree about what would make that history surprising, because they're using different baselines for how often pure math should be expected to apply. Is that the right diagnosis? If not, what kind of disagreement is this actually — an empirical one about the historical record, a verbal one about what unreasonable means, a difference in burden of proof, or something else? And given your diagnosis, is there anything that could settle it, or is the only progress dissolving the question?
