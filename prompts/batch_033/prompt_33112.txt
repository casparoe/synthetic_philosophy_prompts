At a recent departmental workshop, two of my colleagues got into a dispute about what the clinical phenomenon of pain asymbolia shows about the badness of pain, and I have been turning it over ever since. Pain asymbolia is the condition, reported after certain cortical lesions, in which a patient will acknowledge that a stimulus hurts, and can locate and roughly characterize it, but shows none of the usual aversive responses: no withdrawal, no wincing, no complaint, sometimes even laughter, and little or no tendency to avoid the stimulus afterward.

Eleanor argued that pain asymbolia shows that the badness of pain is not in the pain sensation itself. If the same sensation can occur with and without the aversion, then the sensation and its badness are separable; the badness must be supplied by something else — an evaluative or motivational response to the sensation, or an evaluation carried by a distinct affective component.

Marcus replied that this is too quick. Pain is not a bare sensation but a complex state with sensory, affective, and motivational dimensions. What asymbolia shows is that the affective-motivational dimension can be selectively impaired while the sensory-discriminative dimension survives. A state with the aversive dimension stripped away is not an intact pain with a normal attitude subtracted; it is a partial or degraded pain. So the case does not establish that the badness of ordinary pain is detachable.

Eleanor answered that the same dissociation appears in less dramatic cases. Patients given morphine for postoperative pain sometimes report that the pain is still there, in the same place and of the same character, but that it no longer bothers them. If we credit those reports, pain and its badness come apart even without asymbolia's behavioral oddities, which undercuts the claim that asymbolic pain is not really the same pain.

Marcus came back that the morphine reports are weaker evidence than Eleanor allows. A patient's saying that the pain no longer bothers her is compatible with a less intense pain, with a dampened affective dimension alongside an unchanged sensory signal, or with a calm attitude toward an unpleasant state. Moreover, asymbolia is a stable, lesion-based condition marked by an absence of protective behavior, whereas the morphine report is a transient, drug-altered self-description; the two kinds of evidence should not be treated as interchangeable. So, he argued, the selective damage in asymbolia is best read as removing a constituent of pain, not as removing an attitude toward an otherwise unchanged pain.

Neither would move, and each accused the other of reading the clinical details selectively. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: assess the strength of each move in turn, say which specific claims each establishes or fails to establish, and whether the replies actually answer what they purport to answer. Then say who is right about what overall, and identify the important considerations — empirical or conceptual — that both parties leave out.
