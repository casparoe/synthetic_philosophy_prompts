I recently came across Parfit's hitchhiker case in a philosophy discussion online. As I understand it: a hitchhiker stranded in the desert knows that a driver who picks him up has no guarantee of being paid once they reach safety, so no driver has any reason to stop — unless the hitchhiker can somehow make himself the sort of person who keeps promises even when it goes against his own interests. The idea seems to be that it can be rational to make yourself irrational, because the "irrational" disposition to keep your word when it costs you is what gets you rescued.

But this is bothering me. If it's rational to adopt an irrational disposition, then the disposition isn't really irrational — it's just rational at one remove. And if that's right, then the very distinction between rational and irrational seems to dissolve: any apparently irrational behavior could be reframed as the product of a rational choice to be that kind of person. Where exactly does the argument go wrong, or does it? And if it doesn't go wrong, what work is the word "irrational" still doing?
