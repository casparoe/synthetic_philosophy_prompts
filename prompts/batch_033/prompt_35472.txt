I hold a broadly Humean view about laws of nature: what we call laws are not extra entities that govern or produce the unfolding of events, but rather are summaries — the axioms of the deductive system that best systematizes the totality of particular, non-modal facts (the "Humean mosaic"), trading off simplicity against informativeness. On this picture, laws describe the mosaic; they don't make it go. I find this attractive partly because it avoids having to posit primitive modal glue in the world, and partly because it seems to fit how we actually arrive at laws in science: we observe regularities and systematize them.

I want to know what would count against this view. Not just the standard objections I've already heard (that Humeanism can't account for the direction of causation, or that it makes laws inexplicably contingent), but a fuller map of its exposure. What observations, arguments, formal results, or developments in physics or metaphysics would a Humean have to regard as counting against their position? Which of these would a committed Humean concede as genuine costs, and which would they dismiss as begging the question? I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I'd like to know whether the view has been qualified over time in ways that insulate it — and if so, whether that insulation is a problem for a view that claims to be an account of what laws actually are.
