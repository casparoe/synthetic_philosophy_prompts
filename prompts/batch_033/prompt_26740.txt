I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I've recently started reading through some discussions on the Effective Altruism Forum and the rationalist blogosphere. A phrase that keeps appearing in arguments about impartial beneficence is "one thought too many." The way I've seen it deployed is roughly this: someone proposes that we should apply the same cost-benefit or impartial-maximization framework to decisions involving our nearest relationships — family, close friends — that we apply to charitable giving or career choice. The reply comes back that this would involve "one thought too many," and the implication is that this settles the matter: the proposal is not just wrong but somehow misses the point of personal relationships entirely.

I've gathered that the phrase originates with Bernard Williams and an example about a man who saves his drowning wife, but I haven't read the original essay. What I'm interested in is the phrase itself, as a piece of argumentative currency. It does a lot of work in a very small space, and I suspect it's doing several different kinds of work depending on who's wielding it.

Could you lay out, as systematically as possible, the distinct precise claims that "one thought too many" could be making? I want a menu of readings, ordered from the relatively modest to the quite radical. For each reading, I'd like to know:

- What exactly is the claim — what is the extra thought, at what point in the agent's psychology does it occur, and what exactly is wrong with having it?
- Whether the reading is something an impartialist utilitarian could simply accept without revising their view (i.e., something that sounds like a criticism but isn't), or whether it genuinely challenges the impartialist framework.
- Whether the reading is independently plausible — that is, would it be compelling even to someone who doesn't already share Williams's sensibilities about the case?

And then, crucially: which of these readings actually support the use I've been seeing — the move from "one thought too many" to "therefore we should not apply impartial cost-benefit reasoning to decisions involving close personal relationships"? My suspicion is that some readings of the slogan support that conclusion and others don't, but the slogan's rhetorical force comes from sliding between them. I'd like to know whether that suspicion holds up, and if so, which reading is the one the argument actually needs.
