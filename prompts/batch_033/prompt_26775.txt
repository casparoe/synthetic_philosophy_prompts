Your view: if our best theory of sets cannot pin down even the cardinality of its own objects — if it admits models in which the "uncountable" sets are in fact countable — then mathematical terms lack determinate reference in the way realism requires. You are not a full-blown anti-realist, but you think the realist owes an account of how terms like "the power set of the natural numbers" pick out a unique intended object when the theory itself is compatible with radically different interpretations. Here is my opening. I grant that the downward Löwenheim-Skolem theorem is a striking result, but I do not see why it is a paradox. What it shows is that countability is not absolute — a set can be uncountable relative to one model and countable relative to another. That is a fact about the expressive limitations of first-order logic, not a fact about sets. If I am a realist, I simply say that the intended model is the one in which the real numbers really are uncountable, and the countable models are unintended artifacts of the first-order formalism. Why is that not a perfectly adequate reply?
