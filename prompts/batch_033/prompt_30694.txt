I'm writing a paper on whether the bootstrapping problem and the problem of easy knowledge undermine externalist accounts of justification, and before I commit to a diagnosis I want a full specification of what an adequate treatment must accomplish. Write the desiderata for a theory of how a basic source—perception, memory, testimony, an instrument—can yield justification or knowledge without licensing vicious bootstrapping: a subject who already knows a gas gauge has been reliable reads F, infers the gauge is accurate on this occasion, and then uses such instances to confirm its reliability. The account must respect the difference between that reasoning and legitimate track-record reasoning of the sort used to validate instruments, and must address the easy-knowledge inference in the red-table case—I know by looking that the table is red, so I know it is not a white table illuminated by red light—saying whether, and why, that inference is too easy. For each desideratum, give the case or argument behind it and mark it as non-negotiable or as a camp-specific preference (internalist, externalist, closure-denier, and so on). The part I most want is the conflict structure: say which subsets of desiderata cannot be jointly satisfied—in particular whether a view can keep closure under known entailment, block bootstrapping, and avoid an unacceptable skepticism about our basic sources while returning a principled verdict on the easy-knowledge inference—and which single requirement does the most to exclude the standard reliabilist, dogmatist, and skeptical treatments. Keep the scorecard brief; the criteria and their conflicts are the deliverable.
