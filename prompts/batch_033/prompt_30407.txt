I'm a PhD student working on the ethics of political violence, and I keep getting stuck on how peace settlements handle atrocity crimes. A lot of post-conflict agreements include amnesty clauses: the people who ordered massacres or ran torture sites get immunity in exchange for laying down arms or stepping aside, and the same document often has a preamble condemning the very acts it is shielding people from being prosecuted for. At the same time, the international system has spent decades building courts to prosecute exactly those crimes, and in practice it sometimes prosecutes the foot soldiers while leaving the architects of the violence alone. I can't tell wether this is just an ugly compromise forced by circumstances, or whether the practice actually presupposes a distinctive picture of political morality — the kind of thing people call dirty hands, where a leader can be required to do something that remains genuinely wrong, and the wrongness doesn't get canceled by the good outcome but leaves a kind of moral remainder.

So here is what I'd like you to do. Reconstruct the implicit theory of political morality that amnesty-for-peace practices presuppose, if they presuppose one at all. Does the practice need the dirty-hands view — that some morally wrong acts can nonetheless be the right or necessary thing for a political agent to do — or is it fully compatible with a more straightforward view on which amnesty is just the lesser evil and the wrongness is completely accounted for by the tradeoff? Are the parts of these agreements consistent with each other, or does condemning the acts while immunizing the actors only make sense if you think the wrongness persists even when the act is authorized? And what would have to change in how negotiations and post-conflict courts operate if we replaced the dirty-hands picture with, say, the view that torture and massacre may never be authorized no matter the consequences, or with the view that there is no special political morality and leaders have exactly the same permissions as ordinary people? I'd rather you take a stand than just map the options — tell me what the practice strictly presupposes, what it merely fits with, and where it's genuinely underdetermined between rival theories.
