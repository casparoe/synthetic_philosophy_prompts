Consider the following exam question and a student's answer from an upper-division undergraduate course on free will and moral responsibility.

**Question:** In "Alternate Possibilities and Moral Responsibility," Harry Frankfurt presents a counterexample to the Principle of Alternative Possibilities (PAP). First, state PAP as Frankfurt formulates it. Then reconstruct the Jones-and-Black case and explain precisely how it is meant to challenge PAP. Finally, evaluate whether the challenge succeeds: discuss at least one objection from the subsequent literature and assess whether Frankfurt has the resources to answer it.

**Student's answer:**

The Principle of Alternative Possibilities, as Frankfurt states it, is the thesis that a person is morally responsible for what they have done only if they could have done otherwise. This principle has been widely accepted in the free will debate because it captures the intuitive idea that "ought implies can" applies to retrospective judgments of responsibility: if someone literally could not have avoided doing something, it seems unfair to hold them responsible for it.

Frankfurt challenges PAP with his Jones-and-Black case. In this scenario, Black wants Jones to perform a certain action — say, voting for a particular candidate. Black has implanted a device in Jones's brain that allows him to monitor Jones's neural activity and, if necessary, intervene to ensure Jones votes as Black wants. However, Black prefers not to intervene; he will only trigger the device if Jones shows some sign that he is about to decide to vote differently. As it happens, Jones decides on his own to vote for the candidate Black wants, and Black never intervenes. Frankfurt argues that in this case, Jones could not have done otherwise — Black's device would have prevented any alternative action — yet Jones seems clearly morally responsible for his vote, since he made the decision himself without any interference. If this is right, then PAP is false: moral responsibility does not require alternative possibilities.

Frankfurt's argument, if sound, directly establishes that determinism poses no threat to moral responsibility. Since determinism is just the thesis that no one could ever have done otherwise, and Frankfurt shows that responsibility does not require the ability to do otherwise, it follows straightforwardly that determinism and moral responsibility are compatible.

The most influential objection to Frankfurt's argument is the "flicker of freedom" strategy. Proponents of this objection point out that even in the Jones-and-Black case, there remains an alternative possibility: Jones could have shown the neural sign that would have triggered Black's intervention. This sign — a flicker of an alternative decision — means that Jones did, after all, have an alternative possibility at the moment just before his choice. The flicker strategist argues that this thin alternative is enough to preserve PAP, because PAP only requires that there be some alternative, no matter how slight. Frankfurt's response, developed in his later work, is that the flicker is not the kind of alternative that matters for moral responsibility. What PAP was supposed to capture was the idea that the agent could have acted differently in a robust sense — could have done otherwise in the way that makes blame or praise appropriate. An involuntary neural sign is not that. Most philosophers now regard this response as successful, and the flicker strategy is widely considered a dead end.

In my view, Frankfurt's challenge to PAP succeeds. The Jones-and-Black case makes it intuitively clear that what matters for responsibility is the actual sequence of events — how the agent's decision came about — rather than whether alternative possibilities existed. The flicker objection, while clever, tries to save PAP by shrinking the required alternative to something trivial, which drains PAP of its original moral significance.
