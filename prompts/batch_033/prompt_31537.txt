I recently listened to an episode of a philosophy podcast in which the host offered what he called a definitive summary of Sharon Street's evolutionary debunking argument against moral realism. I took the trouble to transcribe the passage, and I'd be grateful if someone with a solid grasp of the metaethics literature could walk me through what it gets right and where it goes wrong. Here is what the host said:

"The core of Street's argument is really quite simple. Evolution shaped our basic moral intuitions — the sense that incest is wrong, that we ought to keep promises, that harming the innocent is bad — not because these intuitions track objective moral truths, but because they helped our ancestors survive and reproduce. The evolutionary explanation for why we hold these beliefs is complete in its own terms; it doesn't need to posit any mind-independent moral facts. So by Ockham's razor, the realist's claim that our moral beliefs are responsive to a realm of objective value is a redundant hypothesis — it adds nothing to the explanation. And if the best explanation of why we believe what we believe makes no reference to moral facts, then those beliefs are unjustified. Street thus demonstrated that moral realism is incompatible with an evolutionary account of human psychology, full stop."

The host then turned to what he called the main realist comeback — the 'third factor' response, according to which some independent feature (say, the fact that certain behaviours promote survival) both shaped our evolutionary trajectory and also happens to be what makes right actions right. He dismissed this in roughly thirty seconds, saying it simply begs the question, because you'd need to already be a moral realist to identify which evolutionarily advantageous traits are the genuinely moral ones. He moved on without discussing more nuanced versions of this response — for instance, the thought that a realist might appeal to a pre-established moral epistemology, or that the third factor needn't be identified from within the evolutionary story at all.

I'm not a metaethicist, and while I've read a bit of the secondary literature, I don't trust my own judgement about whether this summary is fair to Street's actual argument, whether it conflates distinct claims that need to be kept apart, or whether the host's sweeping conclusion actually follows from the accurate parts. Could you go through the transcribed passage — claim by claim, or at least the major moves — and tell me what a careful philosopher working on evolutionary debunking would accept, what they'd flag as an overstatement or a conflation, and whether the final remark about incompatibility is justified? I'd also be particularly interested in whether the host's dismissal of the third-factor response is as devastating as he made it sound, or whether there's more to be said on that front.

Please write your answer in British English.
