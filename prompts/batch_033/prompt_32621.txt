I teach an undergraduate course on probability and philosophical methodology, and last week two of my stronger students got into an extended argument during office hours about anthropic reasoning. I stayed out of it at the time, but I'd like to understand exactly where each of them went right and wrong, because I suspect there's a genuine philosophical insight buried in the exchange that neither quite articulated. I'll recount the core of their dispute below.

Mira opened by arguing that the Self-Indication Assumption is obviously untenable, and she offered the Presumptuous Philosopher thought experiment to make her case. She laid it out as follows: suppose we have two cosmological theories, T1 and T2, that are equally well-supported by all the ordinary physical evidence. T1 says the universe contains roughly two hundred billion observers in total; T2 says it contains roughly two hundred trillion. Now imagine a philosopher arrives and proposes T3, according to which the universe contains two hundred trillion trillion observers. T3 has no independent evidence going for it — it is simply T1 with the number inflated beyond all reason. But under SIA, the likelihood of your own existence given a theory scales with the number of observers that theory says exist, so your existence is vastly more probable under T3 than under either T1 or T2. SIA would therefore require you to shift the bulk of your credence to T3. Mira said this is a reductio: no sound principle of reasoning should let you purchase credibility simply by cranking up a number.

Leo pushed back. He said that the Self-Sampling Assumption — the main alternative to SIA — has an embarrassment of its own, namely the Doomsday Argument. Under SSA you reason as if you are randomly selected from the set of all observers who will ever live, which entails that you are probably not exceptionally early in that sequence. That forces you to conclude that humanity is more likely to go extinct relatively soon, independent of any empirical evidence about specific existential risks. Leo argued that if you reject SIA because of the Presumptuous Philosopher, you have to accept the Doomsday Argument as a cost of switching to SSA, and that cost is at least as steep as the one Mira is trying to avoid.

Mira replied that the two problems are not symmetric. The Doomsday conclusion is existentially unsettling, but it is not formally absurd in the way the Presumptuous Philosopher is — it does not let you multiply your credence by fiat. The Presumptuous Philosopher gets something for nothing: his theory has zero independent motivation, yet SIA treats it as epistemically superior to theories that actually fit the data. The Doomsday Argument, by contrast, at least uses a real empirical fact — your birth rank — to shift probabilities. So the Doomsday Argument might be a bullet you have to bite, whereas the Presumptuous Philosopher is a genuine reductio.

Leo countered that Mira was smuggling in a prior that treats T3 as intrinsically less plausible than T1 or T2, and that SIA only tells you how to update on the evidence of your existence — it does not say you abandon all other considerations. If you have independent reason to think the universe is not teeming with observers on that scale, your prior already penalizes T3, and SIA's likelihood boost may not be enough to overcome it. The Presumptuous Philosopher only looks like a reductio if you assume the competing theories start with equal priors, but in any realistic cosmology they would not.

Mira said that was exactly the problem: SIA's boost for T3 is so enormous that it would overwhelm any prior you could place on T3 without gerrymandering. The only way to avoid the conclusion is to deliberately rig your prior to cancel out the SIA likelihood boost, which is just cooking the books. At that point you are not letting the evidence speak — you are tailoring your priors to protect the conclusions you already favor.

They went back and forth a while longer, but that is the core of it. I would like you to adjudicate their exchange move by move. Who is right about which specific claims, and where do their arguments go wrong? Is there a way to dissolve the apparent tension between the two problems, or is one of them more fundamentally correct than the other?
