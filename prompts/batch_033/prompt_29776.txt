I recently read a popular neuroscience book that had a section on pain asymbolia and its philosophical implications. The author summarizes things this way:

"People with pain asymbolia — a condition caused by damage to the insula — show us that pain isn't essentially unpleasant. These patients feel the sensation of pain perfectly well: they can tell you where it is and how intense it is, but they just don't mind it. They might even laugh while their hand is being pricked with a pin. This has led philosophers to conclude that pain is just a neutral sensory signal, like seeing red or hearing a tone, and that the 'badness' we normally associate with pain is merely a learned response. If we could train ourselves out of that learned response, pain would stop being a problem — which is why some philosophers now argue that the elimination of pain should not be a priority for medicine."

Something about this feels too sweeping to me, but I don't know enough about the philosophy of pain to say where the problems are. Is this a fair summary of what pain asymbolia shows and what philosophers have made of it? Go through the claims one by one and tell me what's well-supported, what's contested, and what a philosopher working on pain would push back on.
