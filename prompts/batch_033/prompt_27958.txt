I am a senior legislative drafter at a federal agency. My office writes and reviews the text of regulations before they are published in the Federal Register. Over the past five years, we have had two incidents in which a regulation contained a provision that turned out to be truth-value indeterminate — a liar-like paradox buried in the text — and it caused genuine legal confusion that took months and a formal interpretive rule to untangle. My director has now tasked me with developing a pre-publication review procedure to catch these before they become law.

Here are three provisions from our past work that illustrate the kind of thing that worries us:

(a) "Any rule in this part may be waived by the Administrator for good cause, except this rule."

(b) "For purposes of this part, 'regulation' means any rule, order, or guidance document issued under this statutory authority, including this definition."

(c) "This provision shall take effect only if a court of competent jurisdiction determines it to be invalid."

Please write a decision procedure my team can run on any proposed regulatory text to identify and flag provisions that may be paradoxical or truth-value-indeterminate due to self-reference. The procedure should satisfy the following constraints:

- It must be runnable by a trained drafter — not a logician — in under an hour per regulation. Our regulations average about 40 pages of text.
- It must distinguish benign self-reference (e.g., "this section does not apply to Class II devices" or "the authority granted by this section terminates after five years") from self-reference that creates a genuine risk of paradox or a truth-value gap.
- For each flagged provision, it must indicate whether the problem can be repaired by a simple rewrite (and if so, what kind of rewrite) or whether it must be escalated to our general counsel's office because it raises a substantive question that cannot be resolved by drafting technique alone.
- It should include a small set of diagnostic patterns or test sentences that a drafter can apply to a suspect clause to determine whether it is problematic, without requiring the drafter to reconstruct the underlying theory of truth or self-reference from scratch.

After laying out the procedure, please run it on each of the three examples above, showing exactly how the procedure classifies and handles each one.

Then construct a fourth case of your own — a regulatory provision that is not obviously paradoxical on a casual reading — and run the procedure on it, choosing the case so that the procedure yields a result that a typical drafter would find surprising or would resist (for instance, a seemingly innocuous clause that the procedure flags as dangerous, or a clause that looks suspicious but that the procedure correctly clears).

Finally, tell me honestly: what kinds of self-referential paradoxes will this procedure miss? Where will it generate false alarms that waste our time? And at what points in the procedure does a judgment call get disguised as a mechanical step?
