I'm preparing a handout for a graduate seminar in value theory, and I want a decision tree that takes a reader from their reaction to a spectrum argument to a named position on whether 'all-things-considered better than' is transitive. The spectra I have in mind run from, say, a barely-worth-living life to a flourishing one, or from mild discomfort to torture, through a long series of steps each of which seems only trivially different from the last. Start the tree from this question: when you judge the first item clearly better than the last but judge no single adjacent step worse than its predecessor, which of those judgments gives way, and why? Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of labels, so each branch node is a concrete case or a claim the positions genuinely come apart on — for example, whether 'not worse than' is transitive; whether a difference can be value-relevant without being noticeable at any single step (an unknowable sharp cutoff versus a genuine threshold or lexical priority); whether adjacent items can be merely on a par or incomparable rather than one being worse; whether 'better than' shifts its standards across the chain so the argument equivocates. Include at least these leaves: transitivity preserved by an unknowable sharp cutoff; transitivity preserved by thresholds or lexical priorities; intransitivism about 'not worse than' (and perhaps about 'better than' itself); parity or incomparability between adjacent items; and a contextualist or equivocation response. Also flag any combinations of answers that no one occupies, saying briefly whether each is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored, and flag any two leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate. Aim for roughly seven to nine branch questions, each answerable by someone who has never heard the position labels.
