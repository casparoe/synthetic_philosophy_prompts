I'm a retired physiologist — spent my career in a motor control lab — and I've recently fallen down the philosophy of mind rabbit hole, so its likely I'm going to garble the metaphysics. The thing I can't stop turning over is the old Libet work and whether it shows conscious intentions don't do any causal work. Then I read Kim's causal exclusion argument, and it struck me that the armchair argument and the electrode data might be pointing at the same conclusion, and I want to know whether they actually fit together or just look like they do.

Take the Libet controversy as a case study for the causal exclusion argument. The salient facts as I remember them: the readiness potential starts roughly half a second before a self-initiated wrist flex; subjects report the urge to move around 200 ms before the muscle fires; later fMRI studies claimed they could predict a left/right choice several seconds before the subject was aware of deciding; and more recently people have argued the readiness potential is largley an artifact of averaging the signal and applying a threshold, not a trace of an unconscious decision. The philosophical side: if every physical event has a sufficient physical cause, and mental properties are distinct from physical properties, then the conscious intention can't be a distinct cause of the movement without massive overdetermination.

I want a careful mapping, not a sermon. Who is the player here and what are the options — the conscious intention, the readiness potential, the motor command, the subject's verbal report? What exactly does the exclusion argument say the experimenter should conclude from the timing gap, and what does it say the timing gap can't even in principle show? Which features of the Libet controversy does the argument genuinely illuminate, and where does it go silent or misdescribe what the neuroscientists were actually arguing about?
