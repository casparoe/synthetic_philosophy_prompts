I'm in a metaethics seminar and we've spent the last three weeks on the Frege–Geach problem. Last session I tried to reconstruct the objection to expressivism as cleanly as I could, and I ended up with an argument that seems to have a much wider scope than I intended. I'm worried I've made a mistake somewhere, but I can't locate it, and the seminar discussion didn't settle things.

Here's the argument as I laid it out:

(1) According to expressivism, the meaning of a moral sentence like "Lying is wrong" is constituted by the non-cognitive attitude (e.g., disapproval of lying) that it conventionally expresses.

(2) In a conditional like "If lying is wrong, then getting your brother to lie is wrong," the antecedent "lying is wrong" occurs unasserted — the speaker does not express disapproval of lying in uttering it.

(3) If the antecedent does not express the attitude, then by (1) its meaning in the conditional context is not the same as its meaning when it stands alone as an assertion.

(4) If "lying is wrong" means one thing in the antecedent and another thing in the standalone premise, then the following argument commits the fallacy of equivocation: "If lying is wrong, then getting your brother to lie is wrong. Lying is wrong. Therefore, getting your brother to lie is wrong."

(5) That argument is a valid instance of modus ponens and does not commit any fallacy.

(6) Therefore, expressivism cannot account for the validity of simple moral reasoning, and is false.

So far this is just the standard objection. But here's what's bothering me. The argument's structure doesn't seem to turn on any feature specific to moral language. You could run the same argument against imperatives: "If it rains, shut the window. It's raining. So shut the window." If imperatives express directives rather than propositions, then "shut the window" in the consequent of the conditional is not issued as a directive, so it means something different, and the inference equivocates — which is absurd, because we all recognize that inference as valid. The same goes for interrogatives in disjunctions, optatives embedded under negation, and so on. So the argument I've constructed, if sound, would prove that no non-propositional semantics can work for any domain of discourse — imperative logic, erotetic logic, the whole lot. That seems like a wildly excessive conclusion, and it makes me suspect that something has gone wrong in the way I've set the argument up, even though each step felt plausible as I wrote it.

Which premise or step in (1)–(6) should a defender of expressivism reject, and why? In your answer, explain why that step looked plausible in the first place, what rejecting it costs, and whether the cost is unique to moral expressivism or is shared by non-propositional theories of imperatives, questions, and other non-assertoric speech acts. End with \answer{the number of the premise you think should be rejected, or 'none' if you think the argument is sound and the conclusion must be accepted}.
