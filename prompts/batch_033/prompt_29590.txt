I spent my career as an evolutionary biologist — empirical work, mostly on morphological evolution — and since retiring I've been working through the philosophical literature on adaptationism. I've read Gould and Lewontin's "The Spandrels of San Marco," the exchanges with Dennett and others, and more recent work on optimality models, constraint, evolvability, and the burden of proof in adaptive explanations.

What strikes me is that, more than four decades later, the debate hasn't been resolved so much as it has diffused. Different subfields seem to operate with different implicit standards for what counts as an adequate adaptive explanation, and there is surprisingly little cross-talk between the philosophical literature and what working biologists actually do when they propose — or criticize — an adaptive hypothesis. I'm considering writing something aimed at biologists rather than philosophers, trying to clarify what is genuinely at stake and what methodological lessons have actually survived the debate. But before I commit to a direction, I want to get clear on the intellectual landscape.

If you had to identify the three or four open conceptual questions in the adaptationism debate that most deserve sustained philosophical attention over the next five to ten years, what would they be? I am not asking for a literature review. I want a defended prioritization. For each question: why does it matter for how biologists actually do their work, rather than being a purely intramural philosophical dispute? How tractable is it — are the conceptual tools already available, or would real progress require new formal or methodological machinery? And what would count as genuine progress, as opposed to another round of position papers?

I would also like you to identify questions that people still argue about but that you would deliberately deprioritize — either because they have been talked out, because resolving them would not change much in practice, or because they rest on confusions that clearer thinking could dissolve rather than solve.
