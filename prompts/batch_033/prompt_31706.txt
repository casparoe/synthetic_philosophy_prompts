I've been working through some eighteenth-century moral philosophy on my own, and I keep returning to Hume's essay "Of Suicide." A piece I read in a popular philosophy magazine summarized the argument in a way that struck me as too tidy, but I don't know the essay well enough to say where the strain is. The summary ran:

"Hume's central argument is that if God governs everything through general laws of nature, then suicide cannot be singled out as a transgression of our duty to God: a death one brings about by one's own hand is just as much part of God's providence as a death from disease. He also argues that once a person's life is no longer useful to others, society has no remaining claim on them, since their departure leaves society no worse off. And he treats the question whether to end a life that has become painful as a matter of prudence and courage rather than of sin. The conclusion of the essay is therefore that suicide is always permissible, and that anyone who thinks otherwise is merely dressing up religious scruples as moral argument."

I suspect the last sentence in particular overreaches, but I also worry that the earlier sentences sound more solid than they are, and I would like to know what to trust. Could you go through this summary sentence by sentence and tell me what a careful reading of the essay would accept, what it would qualify, and what it would reject? In particular: does the argument from general providence establish what the summary says it establishes, or does it depend on premises Hume is taking over from his opponents for the sake of argument? Is the slide from "society's claim on me is limited" to "once I am no longer useful, society has no claim on me" one Hume would endorse? And does the strong conclusion in the last sentence follow from the parts that are accurate, or does Hume draw a weaker conclusion that the summary has quietly upgraded?
