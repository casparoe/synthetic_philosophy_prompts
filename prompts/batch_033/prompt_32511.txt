I am a novelist by trade, though I read philosophy as an undergraduate, and I have been asked to lead a session of our monthly writers' salon — a group of roughly fifteen novelists, poets, and essayists, all of them serious about ideas but none with formal training in philosophy. I want to introduce them to the wrong kind of reasons problem. My aim is not to canvass the literature but to make them feel the pull of the puzzle: why it is genuinely hard to say what distinguishes a reason that bears on whether an attitude is fitting from a reason that merely makes the attitude advantageous to hold. Do not explain the problem to me. Instead, plan how I should explain it to them in about forty minutes. I need: the order in which the components must be introduced and what depends on what; the single concrete example that will carry the whole discussion, and why it is better than the alternatives; the misconceptions this audience will predictably form — I suspect they will try to assimilate the problem to the distinction between instrumental and intrinsic reasons, or to the idea that some reasons are simply bad reasons — and the precise point in the sequence at which each should be headed off; what I should openly decline to settle at this level; and one question I can pose near the end that will tell me whether they have grasped the problem itself rather than merely memorised my examples.
