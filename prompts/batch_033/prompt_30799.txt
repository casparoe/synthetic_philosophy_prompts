I am a sixth-form student preparing a talk for my school's philosophy society, and I have been reading about Libet's experiments on the neuroscience of free will. I find that my own reactions to different cases do not add up. I would like to know whether there is a single principle that captures them, and if not, which of my verdicts I should revise.

Here are the cases and my verdicts.

First, the original Libet-style case. A participant is asked to flex their wrist whenever they feel the urge to do so. Recordings from the scalp show that a readiness potential begins before the moment at which the participant reports having decided to move. My verdict: this does not show that the decision was made unconsciously. Reporting the exact moment of an urge is unreliable, and a trivial wrist movement is a poor model of ordinary choice.

Second, a modern prediction case. Researchers record brain activity whilst a person decides which of two buttons to press, and they can predict the choice several seconds before the person reports deciding, with accuracy well above chance but far from perfect. My verdict: this is more unsettling, but it still does not undermine free will. Prediction is not compulsion, and less-than-perfect accuracy leaves room for the person to have done otherwise.

Third, a stimulation case. An electrode in a person's brain is used to produce an urge to raise one hand, and the person reports that the urge felt like their own and that they acted on it freely. My verdict: this does undermine the person's freedom in that action, or at least the reliability of their report. The action has the wrong kind of causal history.

Fourth, a clinical case. A person with a tumour that affects impulse control suddenly becomes aggressive and strikes someone. My verdict: the person's responsibility is diminished, because the usual mechanisms of self-control are damaged.

Fifth, a deliberation case. A person spends weeks weighing whether to accept a job in another city, and finally chooses. A neuroscientist claims that the choice was already determined by neural states present at the start of the deliberation. My verdict: the choice is still the person's own, and the earlier neural states do not undermine responsibility, even though I cannot fully explain why.

Sixth, a perfect-prediction case. Imagine a scanner and a complete account of the laws of nature that together allow every decision to be predicted from prior brain states. My verdict: I find this genuinely threatening to free will, even though it seems to be the second case taken to its limit.

The tension I cannot resolve is this. I treat the stimulation and tumour cases as undermining freedom because the action bypasses the person's normal deliberative capacities. That suggests the Libet and prediction cases should be harmless, since they do not obviously bypass anything, and I accept that. Yet the perfect-prediction case bothers me for exactly the reason I said does not bother me in the prediction case. The only principle I can half-articulate is that an action is free when it is produced by the person's ordinary deliberative capacities working as they usually do; but that principle seems to say the perfect-prediction case is not a threat, and I do not believe it.

Is there a principle, or a small set of candidates, stated precisely, that gets all or most of these verdicts, and what does it say about a further case I have not settled: a person whose choices are made slightly more likely by a subliminal prime, but who can still resist the influence and sometimes does? Which of my verdicts would the principle force me to abandon?
