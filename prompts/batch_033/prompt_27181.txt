In Book IV of The Methods of Ethics, Henry Sidgwick considers what population size utilitarian principles would recommend. He argues, contrary to what he takes to be a widespread Malthusian assumption, that the relevant optimum is not the point at which average happiness is greatest, but rather the point at which the product of the number of persons and the amount of average happiness is maximized. This commits him to the view that, at least in principle, a sufficient increase in population can outweigh a loss in average well-being. Derek Parfit later gave this kind of implication a canonical formulation in the Repugnant Conclusion: for any population of at least ten billion people all with a very high quality of life, there is some much larger imaginable population whose existence would be better even though its members have lives that are barely worth living.

Suppose Sidgwick had lived to encounter Parfit’s Reasons and Persons. Predict what his considered response to the Repugnant Conclusion would have been. In your answer, distinguish carefully between the following: what his stated utilitarian criterion directly implies about the conclusion; how he might weigh Parfit’s appeal to the intuitive repugnance of the conclusion against Sidgwick’s own methodological preference for systematic reflection over untutored common-sense intuition; and whether his broader view about the possibility of an ultimate conflict between utilitarian and egoistic practical reason would lead him to treat the Repugnant Conclusion as a practical problem, a theoretical problem, or both.
