I am a novelist, currently researching a book about a mathematician in the early twentieth century, and I have been reading around the foundations of mathematics in a spirit of cheerful amateurism. The other evening I described to a friend — a logician with constructivist sympathies — a proof I had come across which establishes the existence of a certain object by assuming that no such object exists and deriving a contradiction. He interrupted me with the words ‘the law of excluded middle doesn’t hold’, as if that settled the matter; but I could not tell exactly what he was claiming, nor why it should bear on that particular proof.

Could you lay out the distinct precise claims that one-liner might be used to express — from a modest observation about what is provable in some formal system, through a claim about which methods of proof are legitimate, to a substantive thesis about truth or about what it is for a mathematical object to exist — and say, for each reading, whether it is plausible, whether it is trivial or contentious, and whether it would actually license rejecting the proof I described? I should like to know which reading my friend was most likely relying on, and whether that reading really does the work he wanted it to do.
