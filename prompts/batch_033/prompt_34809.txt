Two members of my political philosophy reading group got into an argument about a city ordinance that would ban cashless businesses, requiring all retail establishments to accept cash.

(1) Elena said the ordinance is a straightforward restriction on liberty: a shopkeeper should be free to decide what forms of payment to accept, and customers who prefer cash can take their business elsewhere.

(2) David replied that this is exactly the impoverished conception of liberty that Isaiah Berlin warned against when he distinguished negative from positive liberty — but even Berlin's framework doesn't capture what's going on here. The real issue isn't interference but domination: when every grocery store in a neighborhood goes cashless, unbanked residents have no meaningful choice. They aren't being physically coerced, but they live at the mercy of decisions made by others who don't have to take their interests into account. That's unfreedom in the republican sense — the sense Philip Pettit and Quentin Skinner recover from the Roman tradition.

(3) Elena pushed back: you're conflating having few options with being unfree. No one has a right to a grocery store that accepts cash, just as no one has a right to a grocery store that stocks their preferred brand. The shopkeeper isn't dominating anyone; she's simply not offering a service the way you'd like. If we call every instance of "I can't get what I want" a liberty violation, the concept loses all boundaries.

(4) David answered: the difference is that the shopkeeper's decision, multiplied across a market, creates a condition where a whole class of people cannot participate in basic economic life on terms they can meet. That's structural domination, not mere preference-frustration. The republican tradition has always distinguished between mere interference and domination: domination is the capacity to interfere arbitrarily, even if you don't exercise it. A cashless business may not intend to exclude anyone, but it exercises arbitrary power over those who, through no fault of their own, lack bank accounts.

(5) Elena came back: but then you've defined "arbitrary power" so broadly that any market outcome you dislike counts as domination. If a business stops selling vinyl records and I'm a vinyl enthusiast, am I dominated? You need a principle for when an option's disappearance is a liberty problem and when it's just life. I don't think the republican framework gives you one without smuggling in a positive-liberty ideal of what people need to flourish — at which point you've abandoned the claim to be offering a distinct third concept.

(6) David replied: the principle is whether the power in question is forced upon people without tracking their interests. The vinyl case fails because no one is forced to buy records to live a decent life. But the unbanked are forced to buy food, and when the market systematically eliminates the payment method they can use, they face a choice between going hungry and incurring costs they can't afford. That's not a market outcome they can walk away from, and the businesses creating that condition don't have to consider their interests at all. That's the republican definition of domination.

They asked me to referee and I found myself sympathizing with both. Can you adjudicate this move by move? For each of the six moves, assess whether the point is sound, what it gets right, and what it misses. Then say who has the better overall case and whether there's a way to resolve the dispute that neither side has articulated.
