My philosophy of language professor has given us her verdicts on which identity statements are informative — where by "informative" she means the statement conveys something non-trivial to a competent speaker, as opposed to being a mere logical truth that anyone who understands the words already knows. Here are her verdicts across nine cases:

- "Hesperus is Phosphorus": informative.
- "Hesperus is Hesperus": trivial.
- "Cicero is Tully": informative.
- "Cicero is Cicero": trivial.
- "The morning star is the evening star": informative.
- "The morning star is the morning star": trivial.
- "The author of Waverley is Sir Walter Scott": informative.
- "The author of Waverley is the author of Waverley": trivial.
- "Water is H2O": informative.

Next week she will give us a new case, drawn from a well-known puzzle. Peter has heard of Paderewski the celebrated pianist and also of Paderewski the Polish prime minister, but he does not realise they are the same man — he keeps two entirely separate mental dossiers. Someone who knows the full story says to Peter: "Paderewski is Paderewski." Is this statement informative for Peter, or is it trivial?

I want your prediction of her verdict, but more importantly I want the analysis. Identify the two or three most plausible rules about informativeness that fit all nine of her earlier verdicts. Show how each rule handles the Paderewski case and whether they converge or diverge on it. Give your best guess about which rule she is actually running, with your reasons for preferring it. Then propose a further case — not on her list and structurally different from Paderewski — that would cleanly discriminate between the candidate rules, and explain what each rule would say about it.
