Two of my colleagues in the department got into it at a reception last week about the reference class problem, and theyre both still annoyed about it. I ended up playing referee and honestly I'm not sure I got it right. Can you adjudicate their exchange?

Here's roughly how it went:

(1) Miriam said the reference class problem shows that single-case probabilities are basically meaningless. She gave the example of a marriage: what's the probability this couple gets divorced? If you put them in the class "all marriages" you get one number; if you narrow it to "marriages between college-educated people over 30" you get another; if you add "whose parents stayed married" you get a third. There's no principled stopping point, so there's no fact of the matter about the probability.

(2) Dan pushed back and said some reference classes are obviously more relevant than others. If we know factors that correlate with divorce — age, education, premarital cohabitation — we should use the narrowest class that includes those. That's just good statistical reasoning, not a philosophical puzzle.

(3) Miriam said that just pushes the question back: why stop there? Why not include astrological signs? Or the couple's attachment styles? The narrowest possible reference class is a singleton — just this couple — which gives you no base rate at all. And "relevance" has no non-arbitrary definition here.

(4) Dan said relevance is determined by actual correlation with the outcome. Astrology doesn't correlate with divorce; age does. The problem only bites if you pretend all partitions are equally legitimate, which they plainly aren't.

(5) Miriam said correlation itself is defined relative to a reference class — whether some factor correlates with divorce depends on what population you're measuring it in. And even granting we can identify genuinely predictive factors, there's still no unique answer about which combination to include. Include too many and your sample vanishes; include too few and you're ignoring relevant info. Where's the principled line?

(6) Dan said that's a practical estimation problem, not a conceptual one. Statisticians deal with the bias-variance tradeoff all the time using cross-validation and regularization. The fact that there's no unique answer doesn't mean there's no answer — it means the answer is context-dependent and comes in degrees.

(7) Miriam said "context-dependent" is exactly her point: there's no objective probability of this marriage ending in divorce, only probabilities-relative-to-this-or-that-reference-class. So when someone says "the probability is 60%," they're not stating a fact — they're making a choice about how to carve up the world.

(8) Dan said she's conflating epistemology with metaphysics. The fact that we have to make judgment calls about reference classes doesn't mean there's no fact about how likely the event is — it just means we're uncertain about the right model, which is true of all empirical reasoning.

They asked me to settle it and I gave some hand-wavy answer about how they might both be partly right depending on what you mean by "probability." But they want a real verdict.
