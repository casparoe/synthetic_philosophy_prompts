I'm teaching a unit on the philosophy of death, and I want to use Margaret Edson's play Wit (the film adaptation works too) next to the Epicurean argument that death is nothing to us. The main character, Vivian Bearing, is a John Donne scholar dying of ovarian cancer, and the play keeps circling back to Donne's Holy Sonnet X, Death, be not proud. That sonnet isnt actually Epicurean — it's a Christian argument that death isn't really an end, because after a short sleep we wake eternally, so death itself ends up dead. But the play seems to undercut the sonnet's bravado: Vivian spent her whole life mastering Donne's wit, and it doesnt save her from the fear and humiliation of dying.

Read the play through the Epicurean argument and also through the deprivation account, the view that death is bad because it deprives you of goods you would otherwise have had. Does the play end up closer to Epicurus — the fear of death is a confusion that should dissolve, and once Vivian lets go death stops being terrifying — or closer to the deprivation view, by showing concretely what her death cuts short? I especially want you to sort out this: the Epicurean argument is about the state of being dead, not the process of dying, and the play is almost entirely about the process. Does Edson blur that distinction on purpose, and if she does, what does that blurring show? Also, what do I make of the ending, where her old professor reads The Runaway Bunny to her instead of Donne — does the play just swap one consolation for another, and where does that leave the philosophy? I'd like a reading that takes both the play and the philosophy seriously, and that's honest about the places where the play resists the philosophy and the philosophy flattens the play.
