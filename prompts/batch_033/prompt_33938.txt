"The hard problem of consciousness, as Chalmers formulates it, is the problem of explaining why and how physical processes in the brain are accompanied by subjective, phenomenal experience—the 'what-it-is-like-ness' of being in a mental state. This is distinct from the so-called easy problems of consciousness, which are the problems of explaining particular cognitive and behavioural functions, such as the capacity to discriminate stimuli, integrate information, and verbally report on mental states. The hard problem persists even after all the easy problems are solved, because it seems we can conceive of a system that performs all the relevant functions without any phenomenal experience at all—a philosophical zombie.

The meta-problem of consciousness, introduced by Chalmers in his 2018 paper, is the problem of explaining why we are disposed to make the judgments we do about consciousness—in particular, why we judge that there is a hard problem in the first place. The meta-problem is itself an easy problem, because it asks us to explain a set of behavioural and cognitive dispositions (our tendency to say things like 'consciousness seems non-physical' or 'I can imagine a zombie'), which can in principle be addressed by standard scientific methods. The meta-problem is therefore just a special case of the easy problems of consciousness.

According to Chalmers, solving the meta-problem would essentially dissolve the hard problem. If we can fully explain why we are disposed to judge that there is a hard problem—in terms of our cognitive architecture, introspection mechanisms, and so on—then we would see that the hard problem was never a real problem at all. This is the illusionist position that Chalmers endorses: once we understand the cognitive mechanisms that produce our sense of a 'hard problem,' we realise that phenomenal consciousness is an illusion generated by those mechanisms."
