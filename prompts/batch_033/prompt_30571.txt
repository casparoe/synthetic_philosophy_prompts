I've been working through Goodman's *Fact, Fiction, and Forecast* for a reading group and I've ended up convinced by the grue story in a way that's starting to bug me, because I can't tell if I've been argued into something unfalsifiable or if the view is just actually right. Here's the position I've landed on: the grue paradox shows that the problem of induction isn't just Hume's old one about justifying the step from observed to unobserved. Even if you somehow solved *that*, you'd still face the new riddle — which regularities do you project? "All emeralds are green" and "all emeralds are grue" are both perfectly supported by all evidence gathered before time t, but they make contradictory predictions after t. So you need a principled way to rule out grue-like predicates, and the only thing that seems to work is that "green" is entrenched in our actual history of inductive practice while "grue" is a gerrymandered predicate we just invented for a philosophy paper. Projectibility isn't a syntactic property and it isn't a metaphysical one either — it's a matter of which predicates we've actually used and built successful practices around. Induction works when we project entrenched predicates, and the reason we can't give a deeper justification is that there isn't one: entrenchment is where the buck stops.

I find this picture pretty compelling, but I want to know what I'm signing up for. What would count against it? I mean this in a serious Popperian way — what observations, arguments, or formal results would or should make me abandon it? I want the defeaters sorted from fatal to merely awkward, and I want to know which ones a committed Goodmanian would actually concede as counting against them (as opposed to what critics say from the outside). I'm especially interested in whether there are empirical findings — from developmental psychology about how kids actually generalize, from linguistics about how we learn color terms, from machine learning about how models pick up natural categories — that bear on whether entrenchment really does the work Goodman says it does. And I want an honest assessment of whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability: if "entrenched" just ends up meaning "whatever we happen to project successfully," then the whole thing is circular and I'd like to know whether that's a bug or just the feature of a view that denies there's anything deeper to say. If there's a live alternative that handles grue without leaning on contingent facts about our linguistic history — something like a natural-kind-based account or a Bayesian story where grue predicates get lower prior probability for principled reasons — tell me how seriously to take it and what it would take for it to win.
