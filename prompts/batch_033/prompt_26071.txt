So I'm a neurologist, and I recently had a patient with pain asymbolia — she could feel the location, intensity, quality of pain perfectly well, but it just didn't *bother* her. She'd say things like "oh yes, that's a sharp 7 out of 10 in my left hand" with the same tone she'd use to describe the weather. It's honestly made me rethink everything I thought I knew about pain.

I know philosophers have this whole debate about whether pain is intrinsically bad, or whether the badness is separable from the sensation. There's evaluativism, imperativism, the idea that pain's phenomenology just *is* aversive, stuff about motivational internalism — I've tried to read up but I get lost in the jargon. Meanwhile I think in terms of the sensory-discriminative pathway (lateral spinothalamic, VPL, S1/S2) vs. the affective-motivational pathway (medial spinothalamic, medial thalamus, insula, ACC), and I think of pain as this composite thing where the "ouch" and the "this is bad" might actually be dissociable systems.

Can you restate the main philosophical positions on the badness of pain in my neuroscientific vocabulary — as faithfully as you can — and then give me the residue report? I want to know which philosophical claims map cleanly onto the separation between sensory and affective pain pathways, which ones you had to fudge or distort, and which ones have no counterpart at all in how we neuroscientists talk about pain. And the real question I'm trying to get at: does the neuroscience actually settle any of these debates, or are the philosophers arguing about something that the lesion data just can't touch?
