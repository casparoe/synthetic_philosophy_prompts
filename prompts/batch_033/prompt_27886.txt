I'm trying to get straight what I actually think about what it takes to be alive, and I've been collecting my gut reactions on cases. You're a philosopher of biology who's thought a lot about definitions of life and organismality; think through this carefully, maybe brainstorm a bit before settling, but I want the final version to be something I can actually use. Heres where my gut is:

- An E. coli doubling in a flask of glucose: alive, obviously.
- A mule: alive, even though it can't reproduce. I don't even blink at that one.
- A bacterial endospore that's been dormant in permafrost for thousands of years, no metabolism to speak of: alive, just paused.
- A tobacco mosaic virus inside a plant cell, redirecting the cell to churn out more virus: I keep saying not alive, but this one bugs me, because it's doing something awfully lifelike in there.
- A Rickettsia bacterium, which can only reproduce inside a host cell and can't be grown on its own: alive. I don't hesitate, even though it's also stuck inside a host.
- A prion that propagates by converting normal proteins into more prion: not alive.
- A mitochondrion inside a muscle cell: not alive, even though it has its own DNA and ribosomes and divides on it's own schedule. It's an organelle, not an organism, I guess.
- HeLa cells dividing in a culture flask: alive, but then I'm stuck saying Henrietta Lacks has been dead for decades while parts of her are alive and still dividing.
- A brain-dead patient on a ventilator whose organs are kept working for transplant: the person is dead, but I can't bring myself to say there's nothing alive in that bed.
- A synthetic minimal cell built from scratch, with a membrane and a stripped-down genome, that grows and divides in the lab: alive.
- A self-replicating RNA molecule in a test tube, no membrane: not alive, though it feels closer than the prion does.

The closest principle I've been carrying around is something like: alive iff it's a bounded system that runs its own metabolism, or at least has the machinery for it. But that seems to say mitochondria are alive, and maybe that the brain-dead body is alive in the only sense that matters, and I don't like either result. Is there a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that gets all or nearly all of my verdicts? State it precisely, say which case forces each clause, and tell me which of my verdicts it can't accommodate. Also: I keep going back and forth on a lab-made protocell — a lipid vesicle with a self-replicating RNA and a few enzymes inside, but it can't make its own lipids and has to be fed them.
