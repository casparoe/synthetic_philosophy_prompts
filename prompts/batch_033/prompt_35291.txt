I run a small grantmaking program at a community foundation. We're redesigning our application process, and I've gotten interested in whether costly signaling theory can help us think about which requirements are actually doing useful screening work. The idea, as I understand it: a signal can be honest because it's costly to produce, but only if it's differentially costly — harder for low-quality applicants to produce than for high-quality ones. If a requirement is just expensive for everyone equally, it's a deadweight loss. I want a decision procedure my team can use to audit each piece of our application. For each requirement, the procedure should walk us through: what quality or trait we think it signals, whether the cost is differential, whether there's a cheaper way to get the same signal, and what the procedure recommends (keep, modify, cut, replace). Mark the steps where the theory runs out and we're making a judgment call. Then run it on three of our requirements: the 12-page project narrative, letters of support from partner organizations, and the requirement that applicants have been operating for at least three years. I suspect the procedure might tell us something we don't want to hear about that last one.
