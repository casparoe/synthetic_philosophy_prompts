You are a formal epistemologist who works on anthropic reasoning, self-locating belief, and the foundations of probability. I want your considered credence in a precisely stated claim, with the reasoning decomposed. Please think through the problem slowly, and begin by brainstorming the distinct sub-questions on which your answer depends before you commit to any numbers.

The claim to be assessed, which I will call the Doomsday Claim, is this:

For a rational agent whose background knowledge includes ordinary empirical information about human population history, existential risk, and the long-run future, but who has not yet taken into account their own position in the sequence of human births, learning that their birth rank is approximately 1.2 × 10^11 should at least double that agent's credence that the total number of humans ever born will be less than 10^12. In other words: the doomsday argument's central inference is sound in the specific sense that self-locating birth-rank information, after the standard objections are taken into account, does at least this much evidential work.

Give me your actual credence that the Doomsday Claim is true, as a number between 0 and 1. Do not answer that it merely depends on one's choice of anthropic theory and stop there; the point is to make those dependencies explicit and assign probabilities to them.

Decompose the claim into at least the following sub-questions, adjusting or adding to them if you think the structure is wrong:

1. The self-sampling assumption, or some suitably precisified principle in its vicinity, is the correct rule for self-locating credence, rather than the self-indication assumption, a no-update view, or the view that the relevant probabilities are indeterminate.
2. Conditional on sub-question 1 coming out in favor of self-sampling, there is a non-arbitrary reference class of observers relative to which the inference can be run, so the reference-class problem does not make the doomsday argument hopelessly indeterminate.
3. Conditional on sub-questions 1 and 2, the standard objections — that you could not have observed your own nonexistence, that the argument lacks the right kind of outside observers, and that your own existence is old evidence — do not cancel or reverse the update.
4. Conditional on the inference surviving those objections, the resulting posterior shift is large enough to meet the quantitative threshold in the Doomsday Claim: at least a factor of two for the specified threshold of 10^12 total births, for the kind of broad and reasonable empirical prior described in the claim, rather than only for a narrow or contrived one.
5. The Doomsday Claim itself has a determinate truth value, because the notions of birth rank, total human births, and rational self-locating credence are well-defined enough for the claim to be evaluated as true or false.

For each sub-question, give a rough probability and one line of justification. Then show how these probabilities combine into your final credence in the Doomsday Claim; if the sub-questions are not independent, state the dependency structure you are assuming and combine them accordingly.

Finally, tell me which sub-question you are least confident about, and what evidence or argument would most move your credence in the overall claim. If your final number is far from what you take to be the mainstream view among philosophers working on this material, say where you think the mainstream error lies.
