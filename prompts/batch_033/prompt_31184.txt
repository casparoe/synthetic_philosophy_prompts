I run an undergraduate political philosophy seminar, 18 students, a 80-minute session, on Burke and Chesterton. Design an in-class simulation that makes them feel the force of Chesterton's fence before I ever name it. The thing I want them to run into themselves is the asymmetry: a rule can look like pointless friction because the reason for it isn't visible to the people now looking at it, and you can repeal it on perfectly good reformist grounds and only find out later what it was holding up. I'd like the session to split them into small groups, give each group a rulebook for a small institution (a housing co-op, a student society, whatever works) with maybe 8–10 rules, some load-bearing for non-obvious reasons and some genuinely obsolete, and give them a modernization mandate plus a limited number of repeals. Then run a second-round scenario — a disputed election, a budget shortfall, a membership surge, something like that — where the rules they repealed turn out to matter. Please give me the actual rules, the scenario, a timeline, a debrief that maps what happened to Chesterton's fence and to where Burke's caution differs from just never changing anything, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and which feature of the real idea the simulation can't reproduce. And no lecture up front; the name should only come up at the end.
