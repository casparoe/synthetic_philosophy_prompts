I've been teaching a module on decision theory and self-locating belief this term, and the absent-minded driver problem has been eating at me. I've got a set of verdicts on the classic case and a bunch of variants, they feel right to me, but I can't for the life of me articulate the principle that ties them together. I'd like you to extract it, or else tell me which of my verdicts I have to give up.

Here they are, numbered for reference.

(1) The classic absent-minded driver: exit at the first exit gives 0, exit at the second gives 4, continuing past both gives 1, and the driver can't remember whether he's passed an exit. I say the uniquely rational strategy is to exit with probability 2/3 at each exit — the planning-stage solution. The action-stage argument that says you should continue at each exit (because, conditional on being at an exit, continuing seems to have higher expected value if you assume you'll exit at the next one) strikes me as a mistake. It's a mistake about what you're allowed to condition on when your presence at a decision node depends on your own past decisions.

(2) Same setup but the payoffs are 0, 100, and 1. I still think the planning-stage solution is correct — exit with very high probability at each exit. But I'll admit the action-stage reasoning feels more seductive here, and I'm not entirely sure why it should be easier to resist when the stakes are lower.

(3) Symmetrical payoffs: exit at the first gives 10, exit at the second gives 10, continuing past both gives 0. Here I think the obvious answer is to exit at whichever exit you reach, and the planning-stage and action-stage reasoning agree. No puzzle.

(4) The driver can write a perfectly reliable note at the first exit and read it at the second. My verdict: he should write "first exit," continue, read the note at the second exit, and exit. The puzzle dissovles because the driver can now distinguish the exits. This seems to confirm that the original puzzle is about memory, not about anything deeper.

(5) Same as (4) but the note is unreliable: with probability 50%, whatever the driver writes is replaced by a random message from {"first exit", "second exit"}, and the driver can't tell whether the message he reads is his own or the replacement. I think the driver should ignore the note entirely and stick with the 2/3 strategy. The note is useless. But I'm not entirely confident about this — maybe there's a clever way to use a partially reliable signal.

(6) The driver has a co-pilot who is perfectly attentive and shares the driver's goals. At each exit, the co-pilot says "exit" or "continue." The driver can't remember the co-pilot's past advice. My verdict: the co-pilot should say "continue" at the first exit and "exit" at the second. The driver, knowing the co-pilot's strategy, can infer from "continue" that he's at the first exit and from "exit" that he's at the second. The puzzle dissolves because the co-pilot is basically external memory. This feels like it should tell us something important about what the puzzle is really about, but I'm not sure what.

(7) Sleeping Beauty. I'm a thirder: Beauty should assign credence 1/3 to Heads upon waking. I'm aware that there's a formal connection to the absent-minded driver — both involve decision-making under imperfect recall and self-locating uncertainty — but I've never been able to articulate a single principle that generates both "exit with probability 2/3" and "credence 1/3". If your principle doesn't speak to Sleeping Beauty, fair enough, but I'd prefer one that does.

(8) A variant I haven't made up my mind about. Classic payoffs, but there's a 50% chance that at the second exit, the driver is replaced by a twin who has the same preferences, memories, and decision rule. The driver knows this, and the twin doesn't know whether he's the original or the twin. What does my implicit principle say about this case?

So: what principle, if any, captures (1)–(7) and gives a verdict on (8)? State it precisely, walk me through each case, and tell me what it says about (8). If no single principle gets all of them, tell me which verdicts I should overturn and why. I want a committed answer — take a definite position, defend it, and don't just lay out the options and leave me to pick.
