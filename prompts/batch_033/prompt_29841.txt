Last week we discussed counterfactual mugging and XOR blackmail, and afterward a friend in the group sent around a summary of what he took the main lessons to be. I have a nagging sense that a couple of things in his summary are off, but I can't put my finger on which parts, and I don't want to push back until I'm sure. Here is what he wrote:

"Counterfactual mugging and XOR blackmail are both decision-theoretic puzzles designed to show that causal decision theory gives the wrong answer. In counterfactual mugging, Omega flips a fair coin. If it comes up tails, Omega asks you for $100. If it comes up heads, Omega gives you $10,000, but only if it predicted that you would have paid had the coin come up tails. You know all this, and you're being asked for $100. CDT says don't pay—your choice can't causally reach back and change the coin flip. EDT also says don't pay, since paying is evidence you're in the tails branch where you just lose $100. But the real lesson is that both CDT and EDT are wrong: the correct answer is to pay, because you should act as if you're choosing for both branches at once. That's what updateless decision theory captures. XOR blackmail makes the same point from the other direction. Omega sends you a message iff your house has termites XOR you will pay $1,000. Termites cost a million. CDT says don't pay, since the termites are already there or not, and ends up losing the million. EDT says pay, since paying makes it more likely the message was sent because you pay rather than because of termites—so EDT agents get money-pumped, paying $1,000 in a world where they might have termites anyway. Both problems are really just variations on Newcomb's problem, and they all show that you need a decision theory that cares about logical rather than merely causal or evidential connections."
