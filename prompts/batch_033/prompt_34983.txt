You are an expert in philosophy of biology with a strong background in both information theory and the conceptual foundations of molecular genetics. Please think through the following carefully before answering.

Two members of my philosophy of biology reading group got into a long argument last week, and neither would concede anything. I'd like you to go through their exchange and adjudicate it move by move — say who is right about what, where each goes wrong, and who has the better overall position.

Here's roughly what they said, reconstructed from my notes:

(1) Elena: "DNA is literally a code. The genetic code maps sixty-four nucleotide triplets to twenty amino acids and stop signals. That mapping is arbitrary in the same way a linguistic code is — there's no chemical reason CAG has to code for glutamine. It's a convention of the molecular machinery, not a law of chemistry."

(2) David: "It's not a convention — it's a frozen historical accident. Conventions are adopted by agents who could have chosen otherwise. The genetic code is the product of natural selection and chemical constraints, not anyone's choice. Calling it a 'code' is a useful metaphor, but it's still a metaphor."

(3) Elena: "You're smuggling in an intentional requirement that isn't part of the concept. Shannon's theory of information doesn't require an agent — a communication channel carries information whenever the sender's state reduces uncertainty about the receiver's state. DNA reduces the ribosome's uncertainty about which amino acid to add. That's information transfer, and the mapping is the code."

(4) David: "Shannon information is just statistical correlation. By that standard, tree rings carry information about past rainfall, but nobody says the tree encoded the climate. The word 'code' implies encoding and decoding — operations performed by an agent following rules. The ribosome isn't following rules; it's a macromolecule undergoing conformation changes driven by free energy. You're projecting agency onto chemistry."

(5) Elena: "Then your view has a strange consequence: if we synthesize a DNA sequence, transcribe it in vitro, and produce a protein, we've decoded it — so there must have been a code. But if the same sequence produces the same protein inside a cell, suddenly there's no code? The physical process is identical. The difference is only in whether a human designed the sequence. That makes 'code' depend on the history of the molecule, not on what it does."

(6) David: "Yes, exactly — and that's not strange at all. If I scratch marks in the sand and the wind later rearranges them into a pattern that happens to spell 'HELP,' nobody sent a message. The same physical pattern can be a message or not depending on its history. DNA isn't a message from anyone; it's a molecule that covaries with phenotype because of selection. The 'code' framing makes us imagine a sender where there is none."

(7) Elena: "But there is a sender — the parent organism. DNA literally transmits information from one generation to the next. That's the whole point of the Central Dogma. Information flows from nucleic acid to protein, and from parent to offspring. You can measure it in bits."

(8) David: "Now you're using 'information' in yet a third sense — not Shannon's statistical sense, not the intentional sense, but something like 'inherited biological specification.' I grant that DNA specifies phenotype in some sense. But that doesn't make it a code. A blueprint isn't a code either, and we don't call it one."

They went back and forth a while longer but these eight moves capture the core of it. Please adjudicate: for each move, say whether the claim is mostly right, mostly wrong, or mixed, and briefly justify your rating. Then say who has the better overall position and what each would need to concede to make progress.
