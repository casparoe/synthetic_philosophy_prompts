I'm recently retired after a career in environmental systems modelling — flood risk, coastal erosion, that sort of thing — and I've started reading philosophy properly in the last couple of years. Something that's bugged me for decades has started to look like a proper philosophical problem rather than a technical annoyance, and I'd like you to help me get it straight.

In the cost-benefit analyses I used to contribute to, a death avoided or a tonne of carbon avoided in fifty years time was discounted at something like 3% a year, so a death avoided in fifty years counted for only about a fifth of one avoided today. The Treasury guidance we worked under built that in as a matter of course; nobody in the room thought twice about it. But if you'd suggested discounting a life saved by the person's postcode, or discounting a life saved in another country relative to one saved here, you'd have been called some names and probably sacked. So the practice seems to say: where someone is in space doesn't matter morally, but when someone is in time does.

What theory of value, or of the relation between present and future people, does this practice presuppose? I gather philosophers distinguish pure time preference from discounting that reflects uncertainty or economic growth — is the standard practice actually committed to pure time preference, or can it be reconstructed as no pure time preference plus something else, or as just a technical consequence of how we value consumption? Does the exponential shape matter, or would a declining discount rate change the philosophical story? And if the practice were forced to drop pure time preference, what would actually have to change in the cost-benefit framework, and would it survive? I'm after the implicit theory, not a policy essay — tell me where the practice is genuinely committed and where its underdetermined.
