I am a doctoral student in the philosophy of law, and I have been trying to work out what Anglo-American legal practice actually presupposes about the ethics of lying, because the practice looks as though it is operating with two inconsistent theories at once.

Consider the pattern. If I make a knowingly false statement to a federal investigator, I can be prosecuted for a false-statement offense; if I lie under oath, I have committed perjury; if I induce a contract or a payment by misrepresenting a material fact, I am liable for fraud. The law justifies these prohibitions by appealing to the importance of truthful communication and the integrity of fact-finding. Yet the very same legal system licenses police officers to lie to suspects during interrogation — inventing physical evidence that does not exist, falsely claiming that an accomplice has confessed, misrepresenting the strength of the case — and courts routinely admit confessions obtained through such deception. The same system also licenses undercover officers to assume false identities, make false promises, and cultivate relationships that depend on a sustained pretense. These are not treated as marginal violations; they are ordinary investigative tools.

What view of lying, and of what makes lying wrong, does this pattern presuppose? Does it presuppose that the wrongness of a lie lies not in the false assertion itself but in the breach of a role-specific duty of truthfulness, so that a police officer interrogating a suspect simply lacks the duty an ordinary citizen owes to an investigator, or has it suspended? Or does it presuppose a consequentialist view on which lies are wrong only when their expected consequences are bad? Or does it presuppose the narrower thesis that what is wrong is not deception as such but only certain uses of it, such as lying under oath, where a special institutional trust is in place? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, and say whether the practice is actually coherent under that theory.

I am particularly interested in how the reconstructed theory compares with the rigorist position associated with Augustine: that every lie is a sin, that the purpose of speech is to convey what is in the mind, and that no end — not even saving a life — can make a lie permissible. Could the investigative practices I have described be reconciled with that rigorist view, or would adopting it force the law to abandon them? Where is the legal practice philosophically underdetermined, such that more than one theory of lying would license the same pattern, and what further features of the practice would decide among those theories?
