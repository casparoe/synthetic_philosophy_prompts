ok so i've been trying to figure out what i actually think about when a bunch of things compose a further thing — the special composition question. here are my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases, and i want to know if there's a principle that gets all or most of them, and if not, which ones i should give up.

1. the atoms that currently make up my coffee mug: they compose a mug. obviously yes.

2. those same mug-atoms plus one random atom in the andromeda galaxy: they don't compose anything. obviously no.

3. two soap bubbles that touch for half a second and then drift apart: during that half-second, they don't compose a new object. no.

4. a hammer with its handle firmly attached to its head: the handle and head compose a hammer. yes.

5. the same handle and head, detached and sitting six inches apart on my workbench: they don't compose anything. no.

6. a cloud: the water droplets compose a cloud, even though the boundary is vague. yes.

7. all the water droplets that were in the cloud a minute ago, now dispersed across the sky: they don't compose anything. no.

8. a house of cards: the cards compose a house. yes.

9. the same cards after the house collapses into a heap: they compose a heap. yes — but this feels different from the house case. the heap is just the cards arranged heap-wise, whereas the house felt like a genuine new thing. am i just confusing composition with kind-membership here?

10. two people shaking hands: they don't compose a new object. no.

11. conjoined twins who share a liver and a circulatory system: they don't compose a single organism. no. but i'm less confident than in the handshake case, and i can't articulate why.

12. a living mouse: its fundamental particles compose a mouse. yes.

13. the same particles, an instant after the mouse dies: they compose a mouse corpse. yes. but something changed — the mouse is gone and the corpse is there. so composition survived the change but identity didn't? or composition is the same but what's composed is different?

14. all the left shoes currently on earth: they don't compose anything. no.

15. my pair of shoes (left and right, in my closet): they compose a pair. yes. is a pair a real object? i feel like it is, but i can't really defend it.

16. here's the one that bothers me most: a living mouse, and then i replace one cell with a functionally identical synthetic cell. still a mouse. replace another. still a mouse. after replacing 30% of its cells, still a mouse. after replacing all of them with synthetic duplicates, i want to say it's still a mouse — the particles compose a mouse. but now none of the original particles are there. so composition isn't about which particles, it's about arrangement and causal continuity. but then why did i say the dispersed cloud-droplets don't compose anything? they have causal continuity with the cloud too.

so: is there a principle that captures all or most of these? if you have to overturn some of my verdicts to get a clean principle, which ones go and why? and what does the principle say about a case i didn't list: a dry stone wall where the stones are stacked without mortar — do the stones compose a wall, and if i remove one stone and the wall still stands, do the remaining stones compose a (slightly different) wall?

also, please tell me which of my verdicts you're least confident about and what argument or consideration would flip it — i want to know where the reasoning is shakiest.
