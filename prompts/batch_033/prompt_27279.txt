Got into it with my roommate the other night, who took a couple econ classes and now acts like he's cracked human nature. His line is that there's no point asking people what they care about, you just watch what they do. The way he puts it, preferences are like the temperature in a room and choices are the thermometer. You can't see the temperature itself, but the reading is all you've got, and if the thermometer says 70 you don't sit there insisting it "really feels" 65. So if someone says they care about their health but spends every weekend on the couch eating chips, the couch-and-chips choices are the preference, and the health talk is just noise. He calls it revealed preference, and he thinks it means surveys and self-reports about values are basically worthless; you should only look at how people spend their money and their time.

I'm not sure its that clean. I want to know how far the thermometer analogy actually holds. What maps onto what (the room, the temperature, the thermometer, the reading, the calibration)? Where does the picture break down, and which breakdowns actually matter for his conclusion? And if the disanalogies do matter, does the conclusion—that you should ignore what people say and only trust what they choose—survive in some weaker form, or was the thermometer doing all the work?
