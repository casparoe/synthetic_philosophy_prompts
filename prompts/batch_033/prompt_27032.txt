I'm teaching a unit on promissory obligation this semester and I keep thinking about The Merchant of Venice, which I'm also rereading. The standard philosophy-of-promising positions all locate the binding force somewhere different: in the social practice or convention (Hume, Rawls), in the expectations the promise creates in the promisee and the wrong of disappointing them (Scanlon), or in something like the promisor voluntarily transferring a right or invoking a relationship of assurance. The play seems to me to be running it's own version of this argument. Shylock's bond is a contract entered voluntarily, and everyone in Venice treats it as binding even though what it obliges Antonio to hand over is a pound of his own flesh. Portia never denies that the bond binds—she enforces it hyper-literally and then uses the no-blood reading to flip it on Shylock. And then the ring subplot is basically a string of broken promises that the play treats as comic and almost weightless.

So here's what I'd like. Read the trial scene and the ring scenes through the question "why do promises bind?" and tell me what theory of promising, if any, the play actually dramatizes. Where does the play treat a promise as binding because of the words, where because of the expectations it sets up, and where because of some deeper relationship or virtue like mercy or love? Does the play think a promise to do something monstrous still binds, or does the courtroom resolution suggest the bond was somehow void from the start? And where does the play resist the theories—for instance, is the ring subplot a genuine counterexample to the idea that all promises bind, or is it just comic business that we shouldn't press too hard? I'm not after a bibliography or citations, just a careful reading that takes both the philosophy and the play seriously, and that's willing to say when the play won't fit the theory.
