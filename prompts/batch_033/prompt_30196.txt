Last night I had a long argument with a friend about whether the conceivability of philosophical zombies puts any real pressure on physicalism. We ended up stuck on what I now think is the actual crux: the argument seems to require an inference from conceivability to metaphysical possibility, but the standard physicalist reply is that this inference is known to be unreliable, and yet the zombie argument is clearly designed to survive exactly that reply. We never settled whether it does. It struck me afterward that students in my course tend to reach the same impasse, and that a well-designed exam question could force them to work through it rather than recite the familiar labels.

I teach an upper-level undergraduate philosophy of mind seminar, and I am writing the final exam. Please draft one essay question on the zombie conceivability argument against physicalism. The question should not be answerable by merely defining 'philosophical zombie,' 'physicalism,' or 'conceivability,' and it should not reward an answer that simply asserts that conceivability does not entail possibility. It should force students to explain what work the conceivability premise is supposed to do, why the physicalist's appeal to a posteriori necessity may not be enough to block the argument, and what a physicalist would have to say about the relevant kind of possibility, or about the relevant concepts, in order to resist the conclusion. Then provide a model answer of roughly 700 words and a marking scheme that separates an A from a B by naming the specific moves each answer must contain and the specific mistakes that should cost marks. Students will have about 50 minutes for this question, and it should be answerable from the argument itself rather than from recall of particular texts.
