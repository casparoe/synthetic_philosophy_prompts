I've been teaching the standard coherence arguments for expected utility theory in my graduate seminar on rational choice, and I have come to suspect that they prove too much. Let me present the argument as I understand it, and then explain where I think the overreach lies.

The argument:

(1) If an agent's preferences over gambles violate the axioms of expected utility theory — completeness, transitivity, continuity, and independence — then there exists a finite set of gambles, each of which the agent would accept if offered in isolation, such that the agent is guaranteed a net loss if they accept all of them. This is the Dutch book theorem.

(2) An agent who is vulnerable to a Dutch book is pragmatically incoherent: they are committed, by their own evaluative standards, to a set of choices that leaves them worse off in every possible state of the world.

(3) Pragmatic incoherence is a decisive mark of irrationality — indeed, it is the strongest case one can make that a pattern of preferences is rationally impermissible.

(4) Therefore, any agent whose preferences violate the expected utility axioms is irrational.

So far, this is the standard line. Now here is my concern. If the argument is sound, it condemns as irrational a great many preference patterns that strike me, and many others, as not merely widespread but defensible on reflection. Consider three:

- The Allais preferences: an agent prefers a certain £1,000,000 to a gamble offering an 89 per cent chance of £1,000,000, a 10 per cent chance of £5,000,000, and a 1 per cent chance of nothing, while also preferring a 10 per cent chance of £5,000,000 to an 11 per cent chance of £1,000,000. This pair violates the independence axiom.

- The Ellsberg preferences: an agent prefers to bet on a draw from an urn known to contain fifty red and fifty black balls over a bet on a draw from an urn with an unknown proportion of red and black balls, and does so regardless of which colour they are betting on. This pattern violates the same axiom, or else forces a representation that abandons the idea that the agent's beliefs can be captured by a single probability function.

- Incomplete preferences: an agent has no settled view about whether they prefer one academic post to another — say, a research fellowship at a prestigious institute versus a permanent lectureship at a less distinguished but congenial department — because the two options are good along incommensurable dimensions. This violates completeness.

If the argument is sound, each of these agents is irrational. I can accept that the Allais preferences reveal a kind of mistake, though I am not sure it rises to the level of irrationality. But the Ellsberg preferences seem to me like a sensible response to ignorance about the probabilities, and the incomplete preferences seem not like a mistake at all but like a recognition that some values do not reduce to a common scale.

So: where does the argument go wrong? I see several possibilities, and I want to know which is correct, or whether some other diagnosis is needed.

(a) The Dutch book argument assumes that the agent evaluates each gamble in isolation and cannot see the package. If the agent is permitted to consider the whole set before accepting, they might refuse some of the individual gambles, and the bookie loses their leverage. But does this merely shift the problem to a different representation theorem, or does it genuinely defang the argument?

(b) The argument may equivocate between two senses of 'irrational': one meaning 'has preferences that are not representable by a probability and utility function', and another meaning 'is foolish or unreasonable to hold those preferences'. The Dutch book establishes only the former, but the argument slides into the latter.

(c) The argument may be valid, and the real work is in deciding which premise to reject. I am most inclined to reject (3): pragmatic incoherence is not the sole mark of rationality, and an agent can be coherent in the Dutch book sense while still being irrational, or vulnerable to a Dutch book while still being reasonable. But I want to know what I would be giving up — if coherence is not the standard, what is?

(d) The argument may be valid but narrower in scope than it appears: it shows that an agent with certain preferences can be exploited by a clever opponent who knows their preferences, but exploitability is not the same thing as irrationality. A trusting person can be exploited; that does not make trust irrational.

I would like a diagnosis of the argument: which step fails, and why it looked plausible. And I would particularly like to know whether the argument can be repaired so that it condemns the genuinely problematic cases — cyclical preferences, for instance — without condemning the Ellsberg and incomplete-preferences cases. Or do they stand or fall together?
