A recurring argument in my decision-analysis group goes like this: we are deciding whether to run a small, costless pilot study before a larger product decision, and someone ends debate by saying we should obviously take the free information, because "free information never hurts." The claim is offered as if it were a theorem — and I know there is a result in the neighborhood, Good's theorem on the value of information, which is often summarized in roughly that way. But I suspect the slogan covers several different claims, some true, some false, and some that do not actually license the conclusion being drawn from it. I want a disambiguation I can bring to the next meeting.

Lay out the distinct precise claims that "free information never hurts" (or "the value of information is never negative") could be taken to express. For each reading: state it as a precise thesis in one or two sentences; say whether it is a theorem, a plausible but non-trivial claim, or false; and specify the assumptions it needs. I especially want the following distinctions kept track of: ex ante versus ex post value; expected versus realized utility; expected-utility maximizers versus agents with non-expected-utility preferences such as ambiguity aversion, anticipatory feelings, or regret; costless acquisition versus the downstream costs of knowing, using, and disclosing the information; accurate evidence versus information one can update on versus information one can simply "ignore"; instrumental versus intrinsic value; and decision-relevance. Then say which reading or readings would actually license the move from "the pilot study is free" to "we should run it," and identify which readings are doing silent work when the slogan is used that way. Also flag any reading that is false even under the theorem's own assumptions because it confuses ex ante with ex post.

Keep the answer self-contained and do not include a bibliography or citations; I want the structure of the claims, not a literature review. End with a final line beginning "Summary:" followed by one sentence stating which reading of the slogan survives scrutiny and which reading the original argument actually needed.
