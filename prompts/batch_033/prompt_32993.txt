I am a retired chemist who, after forty years in the lab, has turned to philosophy and now attends a fortnightly reading group at my local university. We have been working through writings of the Harlem Renaissance, and last week we read W. E. B. Du Bois’s 1926 essay "Criteria of Negro Art." The session pivoted on a single sentence, which Du Bois sets down in the essay’s closing section:

"Thus all Art is propaganda and ever must be, despite the wailing of the purists. I stand in utter shamelessness and say that whatever art I have for writing has been used always for propaganda for gaining the right of black folk to love and enjoy."

One member of the group—a painter who has grown skeptical of "art for art’s sake"—seized on this line to argue that any attempt to prize aesthetic experience apart from political effect is not merely naïve but dishonest. On her reading, Du Bois’s slogan shows that there is simply no such thing as apolitical art: every work, however private it feels, is propaganda in virtue of belonging to a social world, and the only choice is whether one’s propaganda serves liberation or oppression. She used this to dismiss a question I had raised about whether a formally beautiful poem could be valuable even if its explicit subject matter is intentionally trivial and politically quietist.

I am not convinced that the sentence will bear the weight she put on it, but I want to be precise rather than merely skeptical. The slogan "all art is propaganda" can be heard in very different registers. What I need is not a single interpretation but a systematic menu of the distinct precise claims the slogan might express, graded from the trivial to the radical. For each candidate thesis, please tell me:

- whether it is obviously true (a platitude), substantively controversial but defensible, or demonstrably false;
- whether it is plausible that Du Bois himself was asserting it in this essay; and
- whether that particular reading would, if true, actually license my fellow reader’s conclusion that any appeal to aesthetic autonomy is self-deception and that a quietist poem is, by its nature, a failure.

I am particularly interested in the gap between a sociological claim (that all art has political conditions or consequences) and a normative claim (that art ought to be judged by its political effects, or that art’s only worthwhile function is political). I also suspect the word "propaganda" is doing unacknowledged work, ranging from "any expression that carries ideas about how the world is or should be" to "deliberate messaging that treats its audience as a means." Lay out the readings so I can bring them back to the group and we can see exactly where our agreements and disagreements lie.
