I'm a retired evolutionary biologist and I've only recently started reading philosophy of biology, so apologies if I'm using terms loosely. I've been through the Gould and Lewontin spandrels debate a few times now, and before I start repeating my views to people I want a consistency check on where I've landed. Here are the committments I seem to hold:

(1) Natural selection is the only known process that can build complex functional design, and a great many traits really are adaptations shaped by it.
(2) Gould and Lewontin were right that the adaptionist style has a systematic bias: it treats adaptation as the default hypothesis and makes constraint, drift, allometry, and history earn their way in, when those should be treated as coequal alternatives from the start.
(3) But their San Marco example bugs me enough that I don't fully trust the rest. The triangular things under the dome that they keep calling spandrels are actually pendentives; if they couldn't get the architecture right in the very example the paper is named after, I'm inclined to think the constraint story is just as sloppy as the adaptationist stories they attack.
(4) I also think constraint and drift explanations are just as easy to spin after the fact as adaptationist ones, so the just-so story problem doesn't give me a reason to prefer non-adaptive explanations over adaptive ones.
(5) In practice, I still teach that the right method is to start with an adaptive hypothesis and only fall back on drift or constraint when the adaptive story fails or makes no sense.

Do these hang together, or am I holding a contradiction somewhere? I'd like the tensions ranked by how serious they are rather than a list, and for each one tell me what I'd have to give up and what it would cost. If some of these only look like they conflict, say so.
