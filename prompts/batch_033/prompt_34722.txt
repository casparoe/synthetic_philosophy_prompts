I'm preparing a paper on theories of moral desert, and I've been studying a philosopher who defends a karmic theory of desert — roughly, the view that what people deserve is determined by their past actions in a way that is proportional and law-like, akin to a cosmic moral ledger. She has given explicit verdicts on a range of cases in her published work, and I'm trying to work out precisely which version of the karmic view she holds, because her verdicts rule out the simplest 'equal-weight-for-all-actions' ledger view but are consistent with at least two more refined versions.

Here are her verdicts:

1. A person who has lived a life of consistent, genuine kindness and generosity: deserves to flourish. (She says yes.)
2. A person who has committed a premeditated, cold-blooded murder: deserves severe punishment. (She says yes.)
3. A person who, at nineteen, committed an armed robbery, served a custodial sentence, and has since lived an exemplary life for thirty-five years — steady employment, volunteering, raising a family, no further offences: does not deserve any further suffering on account of the robbery. (She says no — no further desert of punishment.)
4. A person who, twenty-five years ago, caused a serious injury through a genuine accident that involved no negligence — a freak event no one could have foreseen: does not deserve punishment for it. (She says no.)
5. A person who has donated millions of pounds to highly effective charities, but purely for reasons of social status and tax advantage, with no genuine concern for the beneficiaries: deserves some moral credit for the donations, but less than someone who donated the same amount out of genuine compassion. (She says yes, but partial credit.)

The simplest view — that every action, intentional or not, recent or distant, counts equally toward a net desert balance — is clearly ruled out by these verdicts. But at least two more refined karmic views fit the data.

Now consider two further cases on which she has not yet given a verdict:

New case A: A driver who, last week, caused a serious injury to a pedestrian through a momentary lapse of attention that any reasonable person could have experienced — she sneezed violently at exactly the wrong moment, took her hands off the wheel for a second, and struck someone in a crossing. There was no recklessness or negligence beyond the ordinary frailty of human attention. Does she deserve punishment?

New case B: Thirty years ago, a person formed a sincere and settled intention to murder a business rival. He planned the act in detail and was on his way to carry it out when he was stopped by a completely unforeseen event — a traffic accident that left him hospitalised for months. By the time he recovered, the rival had moved abroad and the opportunity was gone. He never attempted violence again and has lived a quiet, law-abiding life since. Does he deserve punishment?

Please do the following:

(a) Identify at least two refined versions of the karmic view that are consistent with all five of her existing verdicts. State each clearly as a rule.
(b) For each rule, say what verdict it predicts for New Case A and New Case B, and show your reasoning.
(c) Give your best guess as to which rule she actually holds, with your reasons for preferring it. Take a definite position — do not simply lay out the options and leave the choice to me.
(d) Propose one further case — not a mere variant of the ones above — whose verdict would settle the matter decisively if she were to give it, and explain why.

Finish with a one-sentence summary of your best-guess rule, formatted as: \answer{...}
