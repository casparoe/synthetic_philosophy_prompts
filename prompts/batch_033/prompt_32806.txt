I've been thinking about what it takes for something to be genuinely about something else—for it to have intentionality, in the philosopher's sense—and I find that my snap judgments about cases pull in different directions. I'd like you to help me figure out what principle, if any, unifies my verdicts, and whether some of them need to go.

Here are the cases and my verdicts on each:

1. My conscious thought that it's raining outside. Verdict: original intentionality. This thought is about the rain in a basic, underived way—no one had to assign it that content.

2. The sentence "It is raining" printed in a novel. Verdict: derived intentionality. It's about the rain because the author meant it to be, and we readers inherit that. The marks on the page don't have aboutness on their own.

3. The same sentence, "It is raining," produced by a random-letter generator that happened to output exactly those characters. Verdict: no intentionality at all. Physically identical to case 2, but the causal history is different, and that seems to matter.

4. A cat's belief that there's food in its bowl. Verdict: original intentionality. Cats have minds, and this mental state is genuinely about the state of the bowl.

5. A thermostat's bimetallic strip bending when the room cools. Verdict: no intentionality. It's just a physical response to temperature. The thermostat doesn't detect anything; we merely use it as if it did.

6. A tree's rings indicating how old the tree is. Verdict: no intentionality. A causal trace, not a representation.

7. A person's implicit racial bias, as measured by an IAT, where the person sincerely disavows the bias and is unaware of it. Verdict: I'm pulled toward original intentionality, since it's a mental state. But it's unconscious, and calling it "about" race in the same way a conscious belief is about something feels like a stretch.

8. A large language model outputting "Paris is the capital of France" in response to a question. Verdict: derived intentionality. It's a sophisticated pattern-matcher; its outputs are about the world only because we designed and trained it to track worldly facts.

9. The firing of a single neuron in my visual cortex that responds selectively to vertical edges. Verdict: I'm torn. It's part of a system that has original intentionality, but the neuron itself doesn't seem to be about vertical edges—it's just causally linked to them.

10. A stop sign at an intersection. Verdict: derived intentionality. It means "stop" because of the traffic conventions we've established.

What principle best captures these verdicts? I notice in particular that I treat the thermostat (case 5) as having no intentionality, the neuron (case 9) as a borderline case, and the implicit bias (case 7) as probably having original intentionality—even though all three are subpersonal states that are causally correlated with something. Is there a principled distinction here, or do I need to revise one of these verdicts? And what does the principle you arrive at say about a case I haven't listed: a bee's visual state that tracks the color of a flower it's approaching?
