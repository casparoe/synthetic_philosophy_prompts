I am a PhD student in metaphysics, and I have been asked to prepare the written output of an adversarial collaboration between two colleagues who have been arguing past each other for most of the term. One defends the identity of indiscernibles as a necessary truth and maintains that no counterexample to its qualitative form has ever been described; the other maintains that Max Black's two-sphere universe is exactly such a counterexample and that the principle therefore fails in every interesting version. Draft the joint statement these two could both sign.

The statement should have two main parts. First, a numbered section of agreed claims. These should be substantive rather than merely diplomatic, and should include at least the following: a formulation of the principle that both accept, with the trivial reading involving identity-involving properties such as being identical with a explicitly set aside and the qualitative reading made precise; an agreed description of the two-sphere universe (two spheres of the same material, size, mass, and intrinsic profile, at a fixed distance from one another, in a world containing nothing else and providing no orientation or external landmark by which one sphere could be singled out); and an agreed statement of what the scenario is supposed to establish, namely that the spheres are numerically distinct while sharing all their qualitative properties, so that the qualitative principle fails in that world if the scenario is possible. Include any further claims both would sign, such as claims about where the burden of proof falls once the possibility of the scenario is asserted, or about what would count as trivializing the principle rather than defending it, and make those claims specific enough that they are not vacuous.

Second, a disagreements table. Each row should state one residual issue in neutral terms, then give each side's position in wording that side would accept for itself. The rows should go well beyond the headline disagreement and should include at least: whether the spheres' spatial relations give them distinct qualitative properties or only symmetric ones; whether appealing to the distinctness of the regions or points of space solves the problem or merely relocates it to the points themselves; whether the scenario is genuinely metaphysically possible or only superficially conceivable; and whether some restricted version of the principle, for example one restricted to intrinsic properties or to perfectly natural properties, survives the case and still does interesting work. After the table, state explicitly which disagreements, if any, are at bottom verbal. I suspect at least one is, and that it concerns the scope of the term indiscernible, in particular whether relational and identity-involving properties are silently included or excluded; say whether that suspicion is right and whether fixing the scope dissolves anything.

Finally, identify the cruxes: the sub-questions such that a settled answer to them would move at least one side. For each crux, specify a case, argument, or test that both parties would accept as genuinely bearing on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. For example, one crux is likely to be whether the spheres can be individuated by their locations without already relying on the numerical distinctness of those locations; a test both might accept is to run the same argument for the points or regions of space themselves and see whether the defender of the principle is forced to accept primitive distinctness among indiscernible points, with each side stating in advance what they expect that exercise to show. Produce the document itself, not a commentary on the debate.
