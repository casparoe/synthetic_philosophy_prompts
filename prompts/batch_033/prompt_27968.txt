I am a novelist at the research stage of a book whose central character is a working mathematician, and I have been reading my way through the arguments that purport to show, from Gödel's incompleteness theorems, that the human mind cannot be a machine. One move in particular keeps recurring, and I want to understand it well enough that I do not simply put a garbled version of it into my character's mouth. Someone will say, in more or less these words, that Gödel's incompleteness theorems show that the mind is not a machine, and then treat the matter as settled. But the move seems to be made at very different levels of ambition. Sometimes it amounts only to the observation that no fixed formal system can capture all mathematical truths; sometimes it is the stronger claim that no mechanical procedure could reproduce human mathematical insight; sometimes it is the still stronger claim that the mind has some non-mechanical access to truths that no formal system can prove. Most recently I saw it used to dismiss, in a single sentence, the idea that a machine could ever be said to grasp a proof.

I am fairly sure that one sentence is doing several different jobs at once, and that some of the things it could mean are nearly trivial while others are highly contentious or simply false. The book is not meant to be a tract, so I want the character to be able to defend the strongest version of her position and to notice when she is overclaiming. Before I let her say anything in this neighborhood, I would like the sentence taken apart. What are the distinct precise claims that the slogan Gödel's theorems show the mind is not a machine might be expressing, arranged from the weakest or most nearly trivial to the strongest? For each reading, tell me whether it actually follows from the incompleteness theorems, whether it is independently plausible, and whether it would support the conclusion that the human mind could not in principle be realized by a machine. Then, with that menu on the table, tell me which reading a person has to be relying on when they use the slogan to close down a debate about whether a machine could do mathematics the way a human mathematician does.
