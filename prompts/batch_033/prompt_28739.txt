I cover the science and ideas beat for a general-interest magazine, and over the past couple of years I've noticed a pattern in arguments about minds, machines, and causation that I can't find a satisfactory name for, though I'm sure philosophers must have mapped this terrain. The pattern goes like this. I'll be interviewing a neuroscientist who tells me, with some passion, that it's a mistake to say "your brain decided" — you decided, and your brain activity is the vehicle of that decision, not a replacement for it. When I ask whether this means a decision is something over and above the neural activity, they say no, of course not, they're not a dualist; the decision just is the neural activity, described at a different level. Fair enough. But then, later in the same conversation, they'll say that a depressed person's negative beliefs cause them to withdraw from social situations, and when I ask whether those beliefs are causing anything beyond what the underlying neural events cause, they look at me as if I've asked whether the wetness of water causes anything beyond what the H₂O molecules cause. The wetness metaphor does a lot of work here — it makes the question sound faintly ridiculous — but I can't tell whether it's actually answering it or just making it uncomfortable to press.

I've seen the same move in arguments about whether large language models "really" understand language or merely statistically approximate it, and in debates among physicists and philosophers about whether thermodynamic regularities cause anything or merely describe. The structure is always: a strong causal claim about a higher-level phenomenon, a retreat to the claim that the higher level is nothing over and above the lower level, and then a return to strong causal language as soon as the pressure is off, often lubricated by an analogy — software and hardware, wetness and molecules, patterns and substrate — that seems to dissolve the problem without quite solving it. What I find striking is that the people making this move are often sophisticated thinkers who would reject dualism without hesitation; they are not confused about the physics, and yet the oscillation persists.

I'm not asking whether the strong causal claim is true. What I want to know is whether this specific argumentative oscillation — the advance, the retreat, the unacknowledged return — has been identified and named as a distinct phenomenon, and if so, what its exact boundaries are. Does it reduce to a known problem like the causal exclusion argument, or is it something that sits in the gap between that debate and the literature on the intentional stance? And if it doesn't have a name, what should it be called, and how would you distinguish it from ordinary equivocation on one side and from the substantive claim that higher-level causation is simply real on the other?
