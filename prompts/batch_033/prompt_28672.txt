I cover the science and ideas beat for a general-interest publication, and over the past few years I've developed a set of views about what the increasing reliance on large language models and similar systems is doing to our collective epistemic life. I'm drafting a long essay that pulls these views together, and before I commit to print I want to know whether the position I've arrived at is internally consistent. Here is where I stand:

(1) There is a genuine phenomenon of epistemic erosion: when a person or a community systematically offloads a cognitive task to an AI system, their capacity to perform that task without the system degrades, and with it their capacity to assess the outputs of the system for that task.

(2) This erosion is not, in itself, a reason to avoid using AI systems. Calculators erode mental arithmetic, and search engines erode unaided recall of facts, but neither is a serious objection to using them. The objection, if there is one, must turn on something distinctive about what is eroded, not on the bare fact of erosion.

(3) What is distinctive about the current generation of AI systems is that they encroach on tasks that involve judgment, not merely computation or retrieval. Using an LLM to summarize a scientific paper, draft an argument, or assess the strength of evidence is different in kind from using a calculator to multiply two numbers. Judgment eroded is a more serious loss than computation eroded.

(4) Nonetheless, I think the epistemic division of labor — the fact that we routinely and legitimately rely on experts without ourselves understanding their reasoning — shows that dependence on an external cognitive resource does not automatically entail a loss of understanding. A patient does not understand oncology by trusting their oncologist, but the patient's epistemic position is not thereby defective.

(5) I hold that genuine understanding requires something like being able to give an explanation in one's own words, to answer "what if things had been different" questions, and to discriminate between better and worse reasons for a conclusion. Call this the generative view of understanding.

(6) I also hold that there is a distinctive epistemic value in widespread understanding across a population — that it matters for democratic deliberation, for the resilience of institutions, and for the kind of accountability that requires citizens to grasp the reasons behind decisions that affect them.

(7) Finally, I hold that the appropriate response to epistemic erosion from AI is not to restrict use of the technology but to redesign the interfaces and practices around it so that use builds understanding rather than replaces it — what some have called intelligence augmentation rather than intelligence outsourcing.

I suspect there are tensions in this set, but I'm not sure where they are or how deep they run. Go through these commitments and identify which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to be, and rank the tensions by severity. For each genuine tension, tell me what would have to give to resolve it and what the resolution would cost the overall position. I am especially interested in whether (4) and (5) pull against each other in a way that matters, and whether (7) is doing real work or just gesturing at a solution that the rest of the commitments rule out.
