I'm a high school student, and I run a very small philosophy podcast — mostly interviews with teachers and, once in a while, a professor who is willing to talk to a seventeen-year-old with a microphone. Next month I'm interviewing a philosopher of mind who has written extensively about Douglas Hofstadter's notion of tangled hierarchies and the inviolate level, especially as these ideas bear on theories of self-reference and consciousness. I first encountered the concepts while reading *Gödel, Escher, Bach* for my school's philosophy club, and I've been turning them over ever since: the image of two hands drawing each other into existence, and the claim that somewhere there must be an inviolate level — a vantage point outside the loop — that keeps the whole structure from collapsing into paradox.

I want to avoid the kind of interview where the guest simply re-explains the basics. My listeners have already heard me ramble about strange loops for half an episode, and they're ready for something sharper. Can you help me draft eight to ten interview questions that genuinely press on the tensions in the framework? In particular, I want to probe three areas. First, the status of the inviolate level: must it always be external to the tangled system, the way Escher stands outside the drawing hands, or could a system contain its own inviolate level — something internal that is nevertheless protected from the tangle? Second, the application to consciousness: if the mind is a tangled hierarchy, does the framework commit us to the existence of something like an inviolate level in mental life, and if so, what would that be — a higher-order monitoring capacity, a pre-reflective self-awareness, or something else entirely? Third, the relationship to Gödelian incompleteness: is the inviolate level best understood as a formal-logical necessity, or is it a psychological requirement — something about how we as observers need to position ourselves to make sense of self-reference? For each main question, please include a follow-up that anticipates the reply the guest is most likely to give — the kind of nuanced response someone who has thought about this for decades would offer to deflect a superficial version of the question, and then what I should ask next to get past that deflection and into the harder territory.
