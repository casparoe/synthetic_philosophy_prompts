A friend and I spent most of yesterday evening arguing about whether Aristotle's account of money and exchange has any real traction on contemporary finance, and I came away less sure of my own position than I would like. My friend's line was that Aristotle's framework is too tied to the material conditions of fourth-century Athens to say anything determinate about modern financial practices: he kept insisting that Aristotle had no experience of credit markets, secondary trading, or money detached from any underlying commodity, so any "Aristotelian" verdict on those practices would just be us projecting our own commitments onto him. I resisted, because Aristotle does seem to have a principled distinction between acquisition that serves a household's needs and acquisition pursued for its own sake, and a principled view that some ways of getting wealth are contrary to nature because they treat wealth as an end rather than as a means. But I could not say, with any confidence, how those principles would apply to something like high-frequency trading, where there is no obvious final good being served but also no obvious deception or coercion, or to a fixed-supply digital token that people buy only in the hope of selling it later.

What I would like you to do is reconstruct, as carefully and charitably as you can, the position Aristotle would most plausibly take on three modern phenomena, assuming he were given an accurate, non-question-begging description of how each works: (1) central-bank fiat money and the creation of money through fractional-reserve lending, (2) high-frequency algorithmic trading in equities, and (3) holding a cryptocurrency such as Bitcoin as a speculative asset. For each, please distinguish what his stated commitments clearly imply from what they leave underdetermined, and say where his framework would need to be extended or would simply break down. I would also like your judgment on whether his distinction between natural and unnatural acquisition can survive contact with a financial system in which nearly all exchange is mediated by credit and nearly all money is created by lending. Please stay close to Aristotle's own views rather than later Aristotelian or Thomist developments, and flag explicitly any point at which you are filling a gap rather than reporting a settled reading. Please end with a one-sentence summary of your overall verdict, as the very last line, beginning with "Summary:".
