I am drafting a short position statement for a graduate seminar in comparative ethics, and before I circulate it I would like an audit of whether the view I have come to hold actually hangs together as a package. I have settled on the following five claims about Buddhist ethics:

(1) The most defensible systematic reading of Buddhist ethics is consequentialist: the criterion of right action is the reduction of dukkha, understood as suffering, unease, or unsatisfactoriness, across all sentient beings.
(2) At the same time, an action's karmic quality — and therefore the moral quality of the action itself, not merely the agent's blameworthiness — is fixed principally by the agent's intention, cetanā, rather than by the outcome the act produces. A well-intentioned act that causes harm is not morally equivalent to a malicious act with identical consequences.
(3) The precepts against killing, stealing, lying, sexual misconduct, and intoxication are not mere rules of thumb for beginners; they bind even when a case-by-case reckoning would conclude that breaking one would yield better consequences.
(4) The bodhisattva path can nevertheless require transgressing the precepts out of compassion, through skillful means, upāya: a realized bodhisattva may kill a would-be mass murderer or lie to save a life where this prevents far greater suffering.
(5) Nirvana is not simply the top of the consequentialist ordering; it is the cessation of craving and the ending of the round of rebirth, and its value is of a different order from that of ordinary well-being.

I would like to know which pairs among these are in genuine tension and which only appear to be. Please rank the tensions by severity, say for each what the cheapest repair would be and what it would cost, and identify the single revision that would do the most to make the set cohere. Do not give me a general survey of Buddhist ethics; I want an audit of this particular profile, with the tensions ordered rather than listed as equally serious.
