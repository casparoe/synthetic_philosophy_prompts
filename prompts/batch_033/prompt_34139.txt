I read a chapter that summarizes Rousseau's quarrel with d'Alembert over whether Geneva should get a theater, and the summary goes something like this. Rousseau thought a theater would corrupt the city because plays teach immorality by example: audiences watch characters commit vices and then go imitate them. He didn't buy the old idea that tragedy purges the passions; instead he thought the pity we feel for tragic heroes is a cheap, self-congratulatory emotion that evaporates as soon as we leave the building, so it can't make anyone better. But he also thought the damage would be much worse in a small, still-virtuous republic like Geneva than in an already-corrupt city like Paris, which is why he wanted theater suppressed anywhere a society still takes republican virtue seriously. As an alternative he proposed public festivals where citizens would be participants rather than spectators, so the imitation problem wouldn't arise. Something feels off to me about the first claim — I have a vague memory that Rousseau thought the real problem with stage pity was subtler, and that he didn't actually want a general ban — but the chapter is by a professional and i don't trust my memory. Is this a fair summary? Go through the claims and tell me which bits a careful Rousseau scholar would accept, which ones they'd wince at, and whether the conclusion that he wanted theater banned everywhere survives once the errors are fixed.
