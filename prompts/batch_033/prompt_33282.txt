Hey — I'm a PhD student in decision theory, and I've been sitting in on a formal epistemology reading group this term. A friend in the group gave me his one-paragraph gloss of van Fraassen's Reflection principle, and I've been trying to figure out weather it's actually fair to the view. He put it like this:

Reflection says your current opinion has to defer to your future opinion. If you know that tomorrow you'll assign probability .8 to A, then you should assign .8 to A today. The idea is that future-you has strictly more evidence than current-you, so current-you should treat future-you as an expert and adopt whatever credences future-you ends up with. On this view you can't rationally know that you'll believe A later while not believing it now. And the argument for it is a Dutch book: if your current credence in A, conditional on the supposition that future-you assigns r to A, is anything other than r, then a bookie who knows your future credences can sell you bets today and tomorrow that guarantee a loss no matter what happens.

I know the official statement is something like P(A | P_tomorrow(A) = r) = r, so I get that it's a conditional credence. But his gloss makes it sound like an evidential-humility principle, and that seems off to me: my future self could be sleep-deprived, or drunk, or just badly calibrated, so why would I automatically treat them as an expert? I'm worried I'm pushing back on the gloss rather than the principle itself. Can you go through his summary bit by bit and say what a formal epistemologist would accept, what they'd push back on, and whether the Dutch book argument actually gets you the strong version he stated?
