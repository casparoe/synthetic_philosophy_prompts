I'm taking an upper-level epistemology seminar this term, and we've spent the last several sessions working through the dogmatism paradox. The basic shape of it is familiar: if you know something, you seem entitled to dismiss counterevidence without even looking at it, but that looks like exactly the kind of epistemic stubbornness we're supposed to avoid. I've been trying to get my own commitments clear before I start writing my term paper, and I've settled on five claims that all feel right to me individually. The problem is that I can't tell whether they actually fit together, and I'd rather find out now than have my professor find out for me.

Here are the five:

(a) Knowledge is closed under known entailment. If I know P, and I know that P entails Q, then I am at least in a position to know Q — I don't need to do any further empirical investigation to get there.

(b) If I genuinely know that P, then I can know in advance, before examining any particular piece of alleged counterevidence, that any such evidence is misleading. Since P is true and I know it, anything that purports to show not-P has to be leading away from the truth, and I can recognize that without inspecting it.

(c) It is sometimes rationally required to examine counterevidence even to propositions one takes oneself to know. Flatly refusing to look at a study that challenges something I believe — even something I'm confident I know — seems like a failure of intellectual responsibility, not a virtue.

(d) You cannot lose knowledge merely by examining new evidence. If I know P and then read a paper arguing for not-P, the bare act of reading cannot by itself destroy my knowledge. If it could, knowledge would be pathologically fragile — you'd lose it every time you opened a journal.

(e) Epistemic justification is defeasible. New evidence can, in principle, undermine what was previously justified belief, and can even turn what was knowledge into mere belief, provided the evidence is strong enough.

I have a vague sense that there's friction somewhere in this set, but I'm not confident I've located it. Maybe (b) and (e) are the problem, because (b) seems to make knowledge oddly immune to counterevidence while (e) insists that justification is always vulnerable. Or maybe the real trouble is between (a) and (c), because closure might force me from something I know to the conclusion that counterevidence isn't worth examining, which (c) rejects. I'd like a systematic audit: which pairs are genuinely in tension, which pairs only appear to be but dissolve on closer inspection, and how would you rank the tensions by severity? For each genuine tension, what would have to give to resolve it, and what is the cheapest single revision that would restore consistency across the whole set?
