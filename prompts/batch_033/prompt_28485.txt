Okay so I'm on my university's honorary degrees committee — not something I ever expected to be doing — and we've hit a wall on one nomination and its my turn to write the memo. The nominee is a former Director of National Intelligence. The facts aren't really in dispute: after a major terrorist attack, he authorized a warrantless surveillance program that swept up domestic communications on a huge scale. Multiple courts later ruled it unconstitutional. There are internal memos from the time showing he knew the legal basis was shaky. But — and here's the part that's splitting the committee — the intelligence community's own after-action assessments say the program almost certainly prevented a second attack on a major city, plausibly saving thousands of lives. No one's really challenging those assessments.

So the committee is basically 50/50. One camp says: you can't give an honorary degree to someone who deliberately authorized an illegal program that violated citizens' rights for years. It would signal the university is fine with "the ends justify the means" as long as the ends are big enough. The other camp says: refusing to honor him signals that we'd rather have a public servant who keeps his hands clean while people die than one who gets his hands dirty doing the necessary thing. They're framing our refusal as a kind of moral cowardice — we get to feel righteous, and he's the one who had to make the actual call.

The chair has asked for a short memo (call it a thousand words) that recommends a clear course of action and actually works through the moral question, not just a "on balance" hedge. We vote next week. I'm supposed to treat both sides seriously and come down somewhere definite. I need something the committee can actually deliberate over and vote on — and honestly I could see myself going either way, so I want the reasoning laid out clearly enough that the memo itself might change my mind.
