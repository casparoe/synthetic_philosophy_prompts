I teach a bioethics seminar, and every year we hit the same wall with the therapy/enhancement distinction. The thought is that medical interventions aimed at restoring normal human functioning—vaccines, insulin, prosthetic limbs—are morally unproblematic or even obligatory in ways that interventions aimed at surpassing normal functioning—cognitive enhancers, genetic engineering for height or IQ, mood brighteners for the already-well—are not. But every attempt to draw the line seems to either collapse under pressure or smuggle in normative assumptions it was supposed to ground. Species-typical functioning sounds biological until you ask whether it means the statistical norm (in which case curing aging would become an enhancement once most people are old), or an idealized notion of proper function (in which case you've already decided what a human being ought to be like). Evolutionary accounts face the problem that evolution has no purposes, only history. And even if you could draw the line cleanly, it's not obvious why it should matter: if an intervention is safe and improves someone's life, why should it matter whether it restores or surpasses?

I'd like your credence that the therapy/enhancement distinction can be drawn in a principled, non-arbitrary way that does genuine normative work. By "genuine normative work" I mean: there exists some formulation of the distinction that (a) is extensionally adequate—it classifies the cases most people feel sure about in the way they feel sure about them, (b) does not simply encode prior moral judgments under a different description, and (c) can bear the weight of differential moral evaluation that its defenders want to place on it—it's not just a label but a reason. Please decompose this: what probability do you assign to there being a defensible account of normal human functioning, to that account successfully distinguishing paradigm cases of therapy from paradigm cases of enhancement, to the distinction tracking anything of moral relevance even if it can be drawn, and to the possibility that the whole framing is a mistake and we should evaluate interventions on entirely different grounds? Give a number and a sentence of reasoning for each term, show how they combine, and tell me which term you'd most want to see further philosophical or empirical work on before updating substantially.
