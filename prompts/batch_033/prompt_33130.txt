I recently spent an evening arguing with a close friend about whether we have stronger moral obligations to our compatriots than to strangers abroad. He defended the nationalist view; I pressed the cosmopolitan case. The argument went deep enough that we set aside the standard moves — the veil of ignorance, the drowning child, the analogy to family — and got into the structure of his position.

What I noticed, and what I have been unable to stop thinking about, is a particular move he made when I asked him to justify the boundary. He had been arguing that shared political institutions, a common public culture, and a history of mutual reliance generate special obligations among citizens. I asked: why do those considerations stop at the national border? Your city has shared political institutions, a common public culture, and a history of mutual reliance. Your continent has some of these too. If the considerations are genuinely what ground the obligation, shouldn't the obligation be strongest at the city level and weakest at the continental level, with the nation being just one arbitrary point on a gradient? He conceded he could not give a principled answer to why the boundary sits at the nation rather than somewhere else. But — and this is the move — he treated this inability as a gap in our current theoretical understanding, not as a reason to doubt that the boundary is real. He said something like: "We clearly do have special obligations to compatriots; the fact that we can't yet explain exactly why the boundary falls where it does just means we need a better theory."

I have since recognised this pattern elsewhere: in debates about the moral status of animals (why is sentience the boundary, and not, say, a certain kind of self-awareness?), about the scope of free speech (why protect political speech but not commercial speech?), and about when a fetus acquires moral standing. In each case, someone holds a moral distinction with complete confidence, cannot give a principled demarcation of its boundary, and treats the lack of a principle as a promissory note — something theory will eventually deliver — rather than as a strike against the distinction itself. The direction of fit is one-way: theory must accommodate the intuition; the intuition need not accommodate the theory.

Is there a name for this phenomenon? I am not asking about the general idea that people cling to intuitions — that is too broad. I am asking about this specific configuration: a confidently held moral distinction whose boundary conditions lack principled justification, where the subject treats the justificatory gap as a problem for theory rather than as a problem for the distinction. The nearest concepts I can think of are reflective equilibrium (but that involves mutual adjustment, not one-way deference), intuitionism (but that is a meta-ethical theory, not a description of a dialectical pattern), and the notion of a promissory note in science (but that applies to empirical theories, not moral boundaries). If there is no established term, what would you call it, and how would you distinguish it from those neighbours?
