I'm teaching an intro ethics unit and I want to use Ursula Le Guin's "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" to get at Bernard Williams's integrity objection to utilitarianism, specially that bit about "one thought too many." You know the story: Omelas is this perfect utopian city, but its whole happiness depends on one child kept in abject misery in a basement. Everyone knows. Most people, once they learn about it, are horrified at first but then come around to accepting the tradeoff — the misery of one for the flourishing of thousands. But some people, after seeing the child, just walk away from the city and never come back.

I want to use the story to get students to feel what Williams was on about with integrity and the idea that some justifications are themselves corrupting. Here's what I'm chewing on: Williams says that if you save your wife from a burning building instead of a stranger because you love her, and then you stop to justify it by saying "well, in situations like this it's actually optimific for each person to look after their own loved ones," you've had one thought too many. The justification itself is alienating — it shows you're not really in the relationship in the right way. Now in Omelas, the people who stay have to do a lot of justifying. They tell themselves the child is too degraded to really suffer, or that the child's suffering makes possible a civilization of art and love and kindness, or that freeing the child would destroy everything and help no one. Those justifications feel like they have the same structure as Williams's one thought too many — but scaled up to an entire society rather than one person.

But here's where I get stuck, and what I want help with: the people who walk away — are they actually the ones with integrity in Williams's sense? They don't offer a justification at all. They just leave. Is that what integrity looks like at the social level? Or is walking away just a different kind of failure — a refusal to even engage with the moral calculus? And does Le Guin's story actually vindicate Williams, or does it show the limits of his view? Because Williams's objection is supposed to be about the individual agent's projects and commitments, but Omelas makes it about complicity in a system. The people who stay aren't the ones torturing the child — they're just beneficiaries. Does Williams's framework have the resources to say what's wrong with staying, or does it only work for the person whose own hands are dirty?

I'd love a reading that takes both the story and Williams seriously, points me to the moments in the story that matter most for this, and is honest about where the analogy breaks down. Also, if you think there's a better Williams essay or concept to pair with Omelas than the one-thought-too-many idea, say so — I'm not wedded to it.
