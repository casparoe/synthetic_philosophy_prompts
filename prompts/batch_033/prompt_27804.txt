Since retiring from a career in the physical sciences, I have been working through some contemporary philosophy of mind, and I recently encountered the causal exclusion argument for the first time. A book I am reading, a general introduction to consciousness aimed at nonspecialists, offers an account of the argument that I will paraphrase from memory. As the book presents it, Jaegwon Kim reasons roughly as follows. The physical domain is causally closed, meaning that every physical event has a sufficient physical cause. Now consider a mental event, such as a decision to raise one's arm, and suppose it causes a physical event, the arm's rising. If the mental event is simply identical with a physical event, then there is only one cause described twice, and no special problem arises. But if the mental event is distinct from the physical cause of the arm's rising, then that physical cause is already sufficient on its own, and the mental cause is left with nothing further to contribute; at best the effect would be overdetermined, and Kim rules out systematic overdetermination. The book then reports that Kim, being unwilling to identify the mental with the physical, concludes that mental events are real but causally powerless epiphenomena, and that as a consequence our ordinary talk of acting for reasons describes nothing that actually does any causal work.

Something about the final step struck me as too quick, and I suspect the book has quietly moved from expounding an argument to endorsing a particular interpretation of it, but I am too new to this literature to say exactly where the missteps are. I would be grateful for a careful, sentence-by-sentence audit: which parts of the paraphrase are accurate, which are subtly distorted, and what would a defender of Kim's actual position say in response. In particular, I would like to know whether Kim's own considered view really is epiphenomenalism, whether the argument's force depends on premises the summary omits, and whether the claim about ordinary reason-talk being a fiction follows from anything Kim actually argued.
