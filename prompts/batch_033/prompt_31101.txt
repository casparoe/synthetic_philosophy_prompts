I'm a journalist working on a piece about cost-benefit thinking in medicine, and last week I ended up sitting in on a long argument between two people I was interviewing together — one a health economist, the other a medical ethicist. It started over a proposed population screening program: a cheap blood test that would catch a rare but deadly condition early. The economist's model said it would save several hundred lives a year in expectation. The problem is the test has false positives, and the follow-up biopsy is invasive and occassionally causes serious complications, so on current numbers a handful of healthy people each year would be badly harmed, a few maybe fatally. The ethicist said you can't green-light that, because you'd be knowingly sacrificing a few people for the many. The economist said refusing it sacrifices the several hundred instead. Here's roughly how the back-and-forth went.

(1) Ethicist: You can't just add up saved lives and subtract harmed ones. Each person you harm is a whole life, not a cell in a spreadsheet. The fact that more people benefit does nothing for the person who gets the complication.

(2) Economist: That logic rules out every tradeoff you already accept. You drove here, which raises someone's risk of being hit. You got vaccinated, knowing a tiny number of people react badly. If we couldn't impose small harms on some for large benefits to many, we couldn't run a society. So the principle can't be right as stated.

(3) Ethicist: Those are risks spread across millions of choices, not a policy that says "we will run a lottery where someone loses." A screening program with a known complication rate is closer to knowingly making a few identifiable people worse off for the rest. My objection isn't to aggregation as such; it's to knowingly using a few as a means.

(4) Economist: But at the population level your driving example is also a lottery — we know roughly how many people will be hurt by cars this year, just not which ones. So the difference can't be certainty. And the people harmed by the screening aren't identifiable in advance either. So why does it matter that a policy "points at" someone, if it can't point at who?

(5) Ethicist: Because a person is the unit of experience. Within one life, I can accept a biopsy risk because I'm the one who might get the early diagnosis; the cost and the benefit land in the same life. Across lives, the person who suffers is just sacrificed for strangers — there's no one who experiences the compensation. That's the difference between trading off within a life and trading off between lives.

(6) Economist: But you keep accepting cross-person tradeoffs — driving, vaccines, the whole background risk of modern life. So either there's a principled line between acceptable and unacceptable ones, or your view is just "it's fine when I'm used to it." You haven't drawn the line, and until you do, I don't think you've given me a reason to reject the program.

They went around on this for another hour and I honestly couldn't tell who was winning.

Can you adjudicate the exchange move by move? For each numbered move, rate it from clearly wrong to clearly right, say who has the better of that move, and then tell me who's more right overall and what, if anything, both of them are missing.

Give me the answer as a JSON object with these fields:

- "overall_verdict": a string, one or two sentences on who is more right overall.
- "moves": an array of objects, one per numbered move, each with:
  - "move": the number (1 through 6)
  - "speaker": "ethicist" or "economist"
  - "rating": one of "clearly right", "mostly right", "mixed", "mostly wrong", "clearly wrong"
  - "who_has_the_better_of_it": "ethicist", "economist", or "tie"
  - "rationale": one or two sentences justifying the rating

- "least_confident_about": a string naming the single rating or point you're least sure of and why.
