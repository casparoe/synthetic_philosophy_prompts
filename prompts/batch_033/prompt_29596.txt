So I've been going down a rabbit hole on modal metaphysics — yeah, I know, this is what happens when you read too much Lewis and then try to explain it to your friends at a party and they slowly back away. Anyway, I built this argument and now I can't tell if it's genuinely devastating or if I've smuggled something in. It seems to show that de re modality — the whole idea that *I myself* could have been otherwise, not just that the world could have been different — is unintelligible. But that conclusion is insane, right? We say things like "I could have been a doctor" all the time and we clearly mean something by it. So the argument must go wrong somewhere. Here it is:

(1) For any claim of the form "I could have been F," there has to be some fact about reality that makes it true or false — truth doesn't float free.
(2) If transworld identity is correct, the truthmaker for "I could have been F" is the existence of a possible world containing me — this very person, numerically identical — and in that world I am F.
(3) But what makes some person in another possible world *me*? It can't be my actual properties, since ex hypothesi I'm F in that world and not-F in the actual world, so the properties differ.
(4) If we say it's a bare haecceity — a primitive "thisness" that I carry across worlds — we've invoked something occult and unobservable, the metaphysical equivalent of a soul. That's a big bullet to bite.
(5) So transworld identity is too mysterious to do the job.
(6) If counterpart theory is correct instead, the truthmaker is that I have a counterpart — someone in another world who resembles me closely enough in the relevant respects — and that counterpart is F.
(7) But "resembles me closely enough" has no objective answer. Similarity is relative to a choice of respects and a threshold, both of which shift with conversational context. Whether some otherworldly individual counts as my counterpart depends on what we happen to care about right now.
(8) So whether "I could have been F" comes out true depends on our conversational interests. But my modal properties — what I could have been — shouldn't depend on what anyone is talking about. That seems to make modality subjective in a way it clearly isn't.
(9) So counterpart theory fails too.
(10) Transworld identity and counterpart theory are the only serious candidates for making sense of de re modal claims; if both fail, de re modality has no coherent truth conditions.
(11) So de re modality is unintelligible — all those "could have been" claims about particular things are, strictly speaking, either false or meaningless.
(12) But (11) is absurd. We rely on de re modal claims constantly, and no amount of philosophical argument should convince us to stop. So something has gone wrong.

I'm torn. My gut says the weakest link is either (7) — maybe the counterpart theorist can insist that the counterpart relation is vague but still objective, the way "bald" is vague without being subjective — or (10) — maybe there's a third option I'm missing, like some kind of modal fictionalism or primitivism about modality. But I don't know what those cost. And I also worry that rejecting (7) just pushes the bump under the rug: if the counterpart relation is objective, what *are* the objective similarity metrics, and where do they come from? Meanwhile, if I reject (4) and swallow haecceities, am I just doing theology?

Where does this argument go wrong? If it doesn't — if it's valid — which premise should I give up, and what do I have to accept instead?
