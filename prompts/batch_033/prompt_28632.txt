Write a dialogue between two philosophers of science who disagree about verisimilitude — that is, about what it would take for one false theory to be closer to the truth than another. One of them holds the Popperian content view: a theory gets closer to the truth by gaining true consequences (truth content) and shedding false ones (falsity content). The other holds the likeness view: a theory is closer to the truth when the possible worlds it permits are nearer to the actual world, so you need a distance or similarity measure over worlds rather than a tally of consequences. Have them really go at each other over the standard sticking points. The likeness theorist should press the Popperian on the familar result that on Popper's own comparative definition, no false theory comes out closer to the truth than another false theory, which makes the notion useless for the one job it was meant to do. The Popperian should press back on where the metric on worlds is supposed to come from, and why the resulting orderings shouldn't just be artefacts of whatever language or set of predicates you choose to describe the worlds in. Neither should be a strawman: each should have real replies to the other's objections, and each should have to concede something specific before the dialogue ends.
