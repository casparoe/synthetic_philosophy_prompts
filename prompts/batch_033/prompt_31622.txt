I run a small discussion circle that meets every other week at a public library — about a dozen people, mostly adults with day jobs and a stubborn curiosity about puzzles that won't leave them alone. We've spent the last few sessions on probability, working through coin flips, Monty Hall, and the two-envelope problem using nothing more than scratch paper and conversation. I want to devote one evening to Bertrand's paradox: the fact that asking for "a random chord of a circle" gives you three different answers for the probability that the chord is longer than the side of an inscribed equilateral triangle, depending on how you generate the chord. Most of the group has never studied probability formally, and I want them to feel the paradox in their hands before we talk about it — to discover that their own reasonable procedures give incompatible numbers, and to sit with that discomfort long enough that the principle of indifference stops feeling like an innocent default and starts feeling like a problem.

The session runs ninety minutes. We have a whiteboard, string, rulers, compasses, and I can print handouts. I am looking for a structured activity — ideally something where participants work in pairs or small groups, generate chords by different methods without being told in advance that the methods will diverge, pool their results on the whiteboard, and only then see three different proportions emerge from their own data. The debrief should walk them from the raw numbers to the principle of indifference and why it underdetermines the answer, without requiring them to have heard of Jaynes, measure theory, or anything they would need a syllabus for. Please give me rules, materials, a timeline, a debrief script that maps what happened in the activity to the conceptual issue, and an honest note on what will predictably go wrong when I run this — including which part of the paradox the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply myself. As a last line, please give a one-sentence summary of what the activity is meant to leave participants with.
