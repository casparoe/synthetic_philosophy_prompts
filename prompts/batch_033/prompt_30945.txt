Two friends of mine, Miriam and Paul, recently argued at length about a third friend, Dana, who runs a small animal-welfare organization. Dana has just accepted a paid seat on the board of a large grocery chain that buys heavily from factory farms. In exchange, the chain has agreed to phase in cage-free eggs over several years and to fund plant-based meal programs in public schools, and Dana has agreed not to campaign publicly against the chain while she serves on the board. Miriam thinks Dana has sold out; Paul thinks the arrangement is a textbook example of a justified moral compromise. I tried to referee and found I couldn't, so I'm hoping you can adjudicate the exchange move by move.

Miriam began by saying that the seat is payment for Dana's silence: you cannot take money from the very industry you claim to oppose and still be an advocate. Paul replied that Dana did not trade away her voice for nothing; she used the seat to extract concrete welfare improvements, and without taking it she would have had no leverage, so refusing would have kept her hands clean while the hens got nothing. Miriam answered that campaigns win concessions all the time without anyone joining the board, and that as a director Dana now has a fiduciary duty to the chain, which means she will sometimes be required to act for the company rather than for the animals. Paul said that is true of any compromise: you take on obligations to the other side, and the right question is whether the whole package is better for what Dana cares about than no deal at all. He added that gains from trade do not require getting everything; they require that each side gives up what it values less and receives what it values more — the chain gets a credible director, and Dana gets cage-free eggs and school meals. Miriam replied that this treats moral commitments as if they were divisible goods; if Dana regards factory farming as a serious wrong, she cannot trade pieces of that commitment away for unrelated benefits, because there are things one must not do even for good results. Paul said that line would forbid nearly every real-world improvement, since almost all of them require dealing with wrongdoers, and the hens do not care about Dana's purity. Miriam closed by saying it is not about purity; by accepting the seat, Dana lends the movement's credibility to the chain, and in the long run an animal-welfare movement that looks purchasable will lose more for animals than this deal ever gains.

Please go through this exchange and say who is right about which specific point, where each argument goes wrong or overstates, what if anything both of them are missing, and who is more right overall.
