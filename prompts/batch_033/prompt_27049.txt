I want a single number from you, not a survey of positions, and I want the reasoning that produces it laid out in the open. The question is whether the Dutch book strategy actually succeeds in establishing its intended conclusions about rational credence.

To fix the target, let the proposition be this: at least one of the two canonical Dutch book arguments—the synchronic argument that any credence function violating the probability axioms is rationally defective, and the diachronic argument that any update policy violating conditionalization is rationally defective—is, after the most charitable reconstruction its defenders could reasonably offer, a sound argument for its conclusion, where the conclusion is read as a defect of epistemic rationality and not merely as a pragmatic liability to being exploited. My own credence in that proposition is around 0.35; a colleague whose judgment I respect is at roughly 0.7.

Now decompose your credence. Assign a probability to each of the following sub-claims, give a one-line justification for each, state how they combine, and then give the final number:

(a) The mathematical core: for any non-probabilistic credence function, and for any determinate update plan that violates conditionalization, there really is a set of bets, each fair by the agent's own lights at the stated odds, that jointly guarantee a loss.

(b) The bridge premise: an agent's degrees of belief are faithfully measurable by the betting odds she accepts, so that the bets in (a) are a genuine readout of her credences rather than an artifact of risk attitudes, decreasing marginal utility of money, or other non-epistemic factors.

(c) The normative step: vulnerability to a sure loss of the kind exhibited in (a) is a mark of epistemic irrationality—or at least reliably indicates an epistemic defect—rather than a merely pragmatic or prudential flaw.

(d) The diachronic argument specifically: the bookie's strategy does not illegitimately presuppose the agent's future credences or update policy in a way that begs the question or misdescribes an agent who is genuinely uncertain about her own future opinions.

(e) The absence of a decisive undercutting consideration: no surviving objection shows that the argument, charitably reconstructed, proves too much or condemns agents who rationally refuse the relevant bets for reasons unrelated to their credences.

Then tell me which term your final number is most sensitive to: if you were forced to revise that one probability, how much would the final number move? What argument or evidence would most shift that term?
