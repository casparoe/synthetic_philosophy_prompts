I am a novelist, and at a dinner last month I overheard two guests — let me call them Daniel and Mira — arguing about a philosophical puzzle I had never encountered before. They called it "Pascal's mugging." The argument has stayed with me, and I would be grateful for a careful adjudication.

As I reconstruct it, the exchange went roughly as follows:

Daniel laid out the scenario: a mugger stops you on the street and claims to possess supernatural powers. If you hand over your wallet now, he will use those powers to grant you, tomorrow, a reward equivalent to a billion lives lived in perfect bliss. The probability that he is telling the truth is vanishingly small — but the promised reward is so enormous that the expected value of complying still exceeds the certain loss of your wallet. Expected utility theory therefore instructs you to hand over the wallet. Daniel's claim was that this is a reductio: no sane person would comply, so expected utility theory is defective.

Mira objected that the problem is easily blocked by recognizing that utility is bounded. Any real agent's utility function has a finite ceiling; beyond some point, additional utility is meaningless. With a bounded utility function, the mugger's astronomical promise no longer swamps the certain loss, and the problem evaporates.

Daniel replied that bounding utility introduces difficulties of its own. If there is a finite upper bound, then there exists some outcome that hits it, and the agent must be indifferent between that outcome and a strictly better one — which looks irrational. Moreover, he said, even granting a bound, one can construct a mugging that operates just below the bound, and the structural problem remains: you are still being asked to accept a deal whose expected value is positive only because of an enormous payoff multiplied by a minuscule probability you cannot reasonably discriminate from zero.

Mira shifted ground. She said the real solution lies not in bounding utility but in the structure of our priors. We assign probabilities to hypotheses in a way that penalizes complexity; the mugger's claim is astronomically complex, and a proper prior — she invoked something called a Solomonoff prior — would assign it a probability so low that no finite promised reward could overcome it. The expected value of complying is effectively zero, and the problem dissolves without artificial bounds.

Daniel answered that this merely relocates the difficulty. For any prior you specify, he said, there exists a possible mugger whose offer is calibrated to overcome it: a mugger who promises a reward just large enough, given your prior, to make the expected value positive. If expected utility theory together with your prior tells you to accept that deal, and you still would not, then the conjunction of the theory and the prior is giving the wrong answer. The problem, he insisted, is structural.

At that point the conversation moved on, and I was left with the sense that neither had landed a decisive blow but that both had said something true. I would like you to go through their exchange move by move and tell me: who is right about which specific claims? Are there important considerations neither raised? And, if you had to judge, which of them has the stronger overall position?
