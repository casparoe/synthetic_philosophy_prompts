I am a PhD student in political theory, and next month I am presenting a paper at my department's PPE workshop. The paper defends the claim that Oskar Lange's trial-and-error solution to the socialist calculation problem answers Ludwig von Mises's original objection but cannot answer F. A. Hayek's later objection about dispersed, tacit, local knowledge — and that the standard defenses of Lange on this point equivocate between an equilibrium allocation and the process by which an economy discovers that allocation.

The audience matters. The workshop is run by a PPE center, and the regulars are mostly normative political philosophers, several of whom are sympathetic to market socialism and treat Lange as having shown that socialism can in principle be efficient. There are also two economists who work in general equilibrium and mechanism design and who tend to regard the whole debate as a loose way of talking about incentives and implementation; and there is one historian of economic thought who works on the interwar calculation debate and will be quick to flag anachronism, especially any reading of Hayek's mid-1940s knowledge argument back into the early 1930s exchange.

The argument in brief: (1) Mises's original objection is that without private ownership of the means of production there are no factor prices, and without factor prices there is no rational cost accounting; (2) Lange's answer — a Central Planning Board that announces parametric prices, instructs managers to treat those prices as given, to minimize average cost, and to set output where marginal cost equals price, and then adjusts prices in response to shortages and surpluses — is a genuine answer to that objection, because it shows that a socialist economy can contain a price system and a trial-and-error movement toward equilibrium; (3) but Hayek's later objection is that the knowledge such a Board would need is not the kind that can be centralized: it is dispersed across agents, local in character, often tacit, and produced only through the act of competitive discovery; (4) so the Board in Lange's model is being asked to do something impossible in principle rather than merely difficult in practice, and defenders of Lange who claim the Board could gather the data or run the trial and error on paper are assuming away precisely what Hayek says cannot be assumed away.

Please give me a Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually get from this audience — not the objections a room of Austrian economists would give — ordered by a combination of how likely they are to be raised and how much damage they would do. Phrase each objection the way the person in the room would actually put it, and identify who it comes from. For each, give me a reply I can deliver in two or three minutes, a note on where that reply is weakest, and say what I should concede up front. Finally, include the question I should be hoping nobody asks.
