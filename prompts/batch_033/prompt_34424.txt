I've been teaching Dennett's *Consciousness Explained* in my graduate seminar on the philosophy of mind, and we've been grappling with the methodological heart of the book: heterophenomenology. The central move, as Dennett presents it, is that the scientist studying consciousness should adopt the stance of an anthropologist encountering an alien tribe. The subjects' verbal reports about their inner lives — their avowals of what it is like to see red, to feel pain, to be the subject of a stream of consciousness — are treated as a body of texts to be interpreted, transcribed, and explained, but not as incorrigible revelations of a private, authoritative realm. The scientist takes these reports seriously as data, yet remains neutral about whether the entities they appear to posit (qualia, a Cartesian theater, a self as inner witness) really exist — just as the anthropologist faithfully records what the tribe says about their gods and their rain dances without thereby committing to the existence of those gods or the causal efficacy of those dances. Dennett draws this analogy explicitly and repeatedly, and it does a great deal of argumentative work for him. It licenses the slide from "subjects say they experience a red afterimage" to "the data are that subjects judge that they experience a red afterimage," and it ultimately supports his broader claim that there is no fact of the matter about consciousness beyond what can be extracted from such reports combined with third-personal investigation of the brain and behavior.

I want to give my students a proper audit of this analogy. What corresponds to what in the mapping between the anthropologist's situation and the heterophenomenologist's? Where does the mapping break down, and which disanalogies are philosophically consequential rather than merely superficial? In particular, I'm interested in the fact that the anthropologist studying an alien tribe cannot themselves inhabit the tribe's perspective — their position is one of radical exteriority — whereas the heterophenomenologist is a human being who has their own first-person experience of consciousness and is studying fellow humans. Does that asymmetry matter for the neutrality the method claims to enforce? And does the conclusion Dennett wants to draw survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or has the analogy been quietly importing assumptions about the nature of the data that the argument itself was supposed to establish?
