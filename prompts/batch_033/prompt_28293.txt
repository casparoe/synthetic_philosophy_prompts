I'm in a fourth-year seminar on weakness of will and practical rationality, and I've gotten myself tangled up in an argument I can't find my way out of. We've been reading about hyperbolic discounting — the idea that people discount future rewards with a hyperbolic function rather than an exponential one, which means our preferences reverse over time in predictable ways (the classic one-marshmallow-now-vs-two-later thing). And we've also been covering the standard Davidsonian picture of akrasia, where you act against your better judgement.

Here's the argument I built, and it seems to show that if hyperbolic discounting is an accurate description of how we value things, then most ordinary cases of akrasia aren't really akratic at all. That can't be right, but I can't spot were the reasoning goes wrong.

(1) An agent acts akratically only if, at the moment of action, they do X while judging that Y is the better thing to do, all things considered.

(2) In a typical supposed case of akrasia — say, staying on the sofa scrolling my phone instead of going to the gym — I judge, the night before, that going to the gym at 7am is better than staying on the sofa, all things considered.

(3) But if my time preferences are hyperbolic, then my all-things-considered judgement about what to do is sensitive to temporal proximity: the nearer a cost or benefit is, the more heavily it's weighted, and this weighting shifts as time passes.

(4) So at 6:55am, when the cost of getting up is imminent and the benefit of exercise is still hours or days away, my hyperbolic discount function yields a different all-things-considered ranking: staying on the sofa now ranks higher than going to the gym.

(5) If (4) is correct, then at the moment of action my all-things-considered judgement actually favours the sofa, not the gym.

(6) So by (1), I am not acting akratically when I stay on the sofa. I'm simply acting on my actual, occurrent better judgement.

(7) The same reasoning applies to nearly every standard example of akrasia: procrastination, breaking a diet, drinking too much, you name it. In each case, if hyperbolic discounting describes the agent's real preference structure, the agent's judgement at the moment of action favours the akratic-seeming option.

(8) So either hyperbolic discounting is false as a descriptive account of human preference, or akrasia (as philosophers standardly understand it) barely ever occurs.

(9) But hyperbolic discounting has a lot of empirical support, and akrasia is a central explanandum in the philosophy of action — we can't just declare it an illusion.

So something has to give. My instinct is that premise (1) is too demanding — maybe akrasia doesn't require that your better judgement be your *occurrent* judgement at the exact moment of action. But then what does it require? A judgement you hold "stably" across time? One you'd endorse on reflection? And does that mean the hyperbolic discounter's night-before judgement has more authority than their morning-of judgement, and if so, why?
