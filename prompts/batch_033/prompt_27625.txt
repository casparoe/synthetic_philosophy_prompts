I'm a philosophy professor whose own work is mostly in ethics, but I've been asked to cover a metaphysics unit on material constitution next term, and while prepping I've discoverd that my gut verdicts on the standard cases don't obviously add up to any view I can name. I realize this is the kind of thing I should have sorted out in grad school, but here I am.

Let me just report the verdicts. A lump of clay is molded into a statue of a horse and then squashed flat: the statue is destroyed, the lump persists, so I want to say the statue is not identical to the lump, since one can survive what destroys the other. But before the squashing, looking at the table, I can't bring myself to say there are two material objects in the same place, the statue and the lump; I want to say there's one thing there. Tibbles the cat has a tail; let Tib be all of Tibbles except the tail. Before the accident Tib is a proper part of a cat, not a cat. After the tail is severed, Tibbles survives, and Tib now occupies exactly the same place as Tibbles and is made of the same flesh, so it seems Tib has become a cat too. I don't want to say there were two cats before the accident, and I don't want to say a mere part becomes a cat just because the tail around it was cut off, since nothing about Tib itself changed. Ship of Theseus: planks are replaced one by one until no original plank remains, and then the original planks are reassembled into a ship. My gut says the continuously maintained ship is the original and the reassembled one is a replica — but I can't see why the reasoning that made me treat the surviving clay as the thing that persists in the statue case doesn't push me to say the reassembled ship is the original. And a gold ring: if I melt it, the ring is destroyed and the gold persists, so again I seem committed to the ring not being identical to the gold — yet if you'd asked me beforehand I'd have said the ring just is the gold, shaped.

So I seem to endorse distinctness in the statue and ring cases while also insisting there's only one object in front of me, and in the Tibbles case I want to avoid two cats without letting a proper part become a cat for free. Is there a principle of material constitution that gets all or most of these, and which of my verdicts does it force me to give up? What would the principle you favor say about a case I haven't listed — say, a bronze statue that has one of its bronze parts replaced and is then melted down?
