I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, but I fell down this hole while trying to write a character—call him Daniel—who is serially unfaithful and yet, when a friend betrays him in a much smaller way, is genuinely, not comically, outraged. I wanted to know whether Daniel's outrage is just wrong, or true but somehow not his to voice, and I ended up with an argument that seems to prove to much.

(1) Blame has a standing condition: you can't appropriately blame someone for a wrong if you are up to your neck in the same kind of wrong yourself. (2) Daniel, then, is in no position to blame his friend for betrayal. (3) But Daniel isn't special. For almost any wrong W that anyone actually blames, the blamer is guilty of something of the same broad kind under some description—or has waived away near-identical wrongs by friends and allies, or quietly benefits from arrangements that make W profitable. (4) So nearly everyone lacks standing nearly all the time. (5) And if nearly everyone lacks standing, then nearly every act of blame is inappropriate. (6) But that can't be right: a victim can blame her unrepentant abuser even if she is no saint herself, and a practice built on truth-telling and accountability shouldn't collapse just because the messengers are flawed.

I'd most like to keep the idea that hypocrisy matters—I built a whole novel around it—but I can't see how to keep it without sliding into (5). Which step should I reject, and what do I pay for rejecting it?
