Two people in my department got into it at a seminar about discounting the future, and I've been turning their argument over ever since. I'll try to reproduce the exchange as best I remember it.

(1) Mark said it's obviously rational to discount future costs and benefits: a hundred quid today is worth more than a hundred quid in ten years, and the same logic applies to well-being. We all act this way, and interest rates reflect it.

(2) Claire replied that interest rates reflect the productivity of capital and people's impatience with their own consumption, not the idea that future well-being matters less simply because it's later. She said if you think a child's suffering in 2050 matters less than a child's suffering today just because it's futher off, you need a reason that isn't about money.

(3) Mark said the reason is uncertainty: the further out something is, the less certain you can be that it'll happen, or that the people involved will even exist, or that your actions will have the effects you intend. Discounting captures that.

(4) Claire said uncertainty is already priced in when you calculate expected value — you multiply by the probability. Discounting on top of that is double-counting. She said pure time preference is the claim that you should discount future well-being even when you're certain it'll occur, and that's what she finds indefensible.

(5) Mark said that's still too quick. He said we have stronger obligations to people we can interact with now — our contemporaries — than to future people who don't exist yet, and temporal distance is part of what makes those relationships thin. You don't owe the same duties to someone in 2225 as to your neighbour.

(6) Claire said that's a substantive claim about the content of obligations, not about the discount rate on well-being. She said you can think we have special duties to contemporaries without thinking that their well-being literally counts for less in the moral calculus. And she added that if you really believe future people's well-being counts for less, you'd have to accept that a catastrophe that kills a billion people in 2125 is less bad than one that kills a billion people next year, which she thinks is absurd.

(7) Mark said he does think it's less bad, and that anyone who says otherwise is just refusing to follow their own principles to the conclusion. He said if you offered most people the choice between a disaster next year and an identical disaster in a century, they'd pick the later one, and that fact counts for something.

They went back and forth a bit more but that's the core of it. Could you go through this move by move and say who's right about what? I'm especially interested in whether Claire's distinction between pure time preference and uncertainty-based discounting holds up, and whether Mark's point about relationships and special duties actually supports his position or undermines it. Oh, and please write your answer in British English.
