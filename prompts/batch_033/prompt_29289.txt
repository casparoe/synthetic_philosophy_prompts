I'm in a philosophy of biology course and we've been going through the units of selection debate, and I've found myself pretty convinced by the gene's-eye view — the idea that natural selection is ultimately about replicators, and organisms and groups are just vehicles (or interactors) that genes build around themselves. I get that saying selection acts on genes isn't supposed to mean little chunks of DNA are literally being yanked around; it's that alleles are the things whose frequencies change across generations, and everything else is causal machinery. What I want to know is: what would actually count against this view? Not the standard organism-is-the-real-unit-of-selection handwave, but the evidence, formal results, or cases that should genuinely move me.

In particular, I want to know where its genuinely exposed. Things like: cases where an allele's fitness depends on what other alleles are around (epistasis, frequency dependence), meiotic drive and other selfish genetic elements where what spreads at the gene level and what's good for the organism come apart, the Price equation and contextual analysis stuff that supposedly shows group selection is a seperate component of change, and the major transitions (like the evolution of multicellularity) where selection at lower levels had to be suppressed. For each, I'd like to know whether a committed gene's-eye person can absorb it as just bookkeeping, and whether at some point the redescription gets so strained that the view stops saying anything distinctive. Also, is the view in danger of being unfalsifiable — if every higher-level selection result can be re-expressed in gene-frequency terms, has the position insulated itself into triviality? I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and an honest take on whether the gene's-eye view is a substantive empirical thesis or just a useful notation.
