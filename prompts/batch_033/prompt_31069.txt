Criminal law in most jurisdictions grades the same physical act very differently according to the defendant's state of mind: an act done intentionally is punished more harshly than one done knowingly, recklessly, or negligently, and a killing that would otherwise be murder may be no crime at all if the defendant lacked the required belief or intent. Yet the same legal system insists that it punishes conduct, not bare thoughts. What theory of mind does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit psychology behind mens rea doctrine, being careful to separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with. Does the practice presuppose that beliefs, desires, and intentions are real internal states that cause behavior, or could its mental-state language be read more instrumentally? Is the treatment of recklessness and negligence, where there is no conscious aim at the harm, consistent with the rest of the doctrine? Finally, what would have to change in the practice if eliminative materialism about beliefs and desires turned out to be true — which elements of mens rea could survive reinterpretation, and which would lose their rationale?
