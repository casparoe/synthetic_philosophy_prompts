Since 1988, the United States has closed unneeded military bases through the Base Realignment and Closure (BRAC) process. Under the ordinary legislative rules that preceded it, closing any particular base was effectively impossible: each member of Congress faced a concentrated local loss from a closure in their own district while the savings were spread across the whole country, so the same body that agreed the military had too much infrastructure could not bring itself to close any specific base. BRAC changed the decision procedure: an independent commission draws up a single list of recommended closures, and Congress votes only once, on the whole package, which takes effect unless Congress passes a resolution of disapproval rejecting it in its entirety. In effect, the institution imposed a precommitment device on itself to escape the kind of escalation trap modeled by the dollar auction, in which a sequence of locally rational moves locks the participants into a collectively ruinous outcome.

What theory of rational agency, and of the relation between individual and collective rationality, does the existence and use of a mechanism like BRAC presuppose? If the same legislators who could not close one base under the ordinary rules could vote to bind themselves through the all-or-nothing package, were they irrational before, akratic, or simply rational agents in a decision environment where individually sensible moves aggregate badly? Does the success of BRAC suggest that escalation of commitment is better understood as a structural feature of certain sequential decision environments than as a cognitive bias, and what would that imply about institutional design more generally? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and answer in American English.
