I'm in a reading group working through G. E. Moore's Principia Ethica, and we've been trying to pin down his views on what has intrinsic value by testing them against concrete cases. One of our members has taken on the role of "Moore's spokesperson"—she's read the relevant chapters closely and gives verdicts on cases that she claims follow from Moore's position. So far she's been completely consistent, and here are the cases she's ruled on:

1. A beautiful painting locked in a vault that no conscious being will ever see: negligible intrinsic value.
2. A person who stands before a genuinely beautiful painting, correctly perceives its beautiful qualities, and feels the emotion appropriate to those qualities: very high intrinsic value.
3. A person who stands before a genuinely ugly painting, correctly perceives its ugliness, and feels the revulsion appropriate to it: negative intrinsic value.
4. A person who studies a beautiful mathematical proof, grasps its elegance, and feels genuine aesthetic delight: very high intrinsic value.
5. A friendship where one person loves the other for that person's actual kindness, wit, and courage: very high intrinsic value.
6. A friendship where one person loves the other for qualities they believe the other has, but the other actually lacks those qualities entirely: little intrinsic value.
7. A person who correctly identifies all the beautiful formal features of a poem but feels absolutely no emotion while doing so: little or no intrinsic value.

This week we're discussing a new case: a person reads a novel and is deeply moved by the beauty of a fictional landscape described in it. The reader is not especially struck by the quality of the prose; what moves them is the beauty of the imagined landscape itself—its colors, shapes, and atmosphere—which they vividly picture in their mind's eye. The reader knows the landscape is entirely imaginary and feels genuine aesthetic emotion. What would our Moore spokesperson say about the intrinsic value of this reader's state of consciousness?

I'm less interested in the bare answer than in identifying the rule or rules that fit all seven earlier verdicts and would generate a prediction for this new case. Please lay out the candidate rules—including any plausible ones I might not have noticed—explain what each predicts about the fictional-landscape case, give your best guess about which rule the spokesperson is actually running (with your reasons for preferring it over the alternatives), and tell me what further case would most cleanly distinguish the candidate rules from one another.
