I'm writing a paper on historical explanation and I've been trying to get clear on my own commitments before I start drafting. The paper is about whether we can make sense of the idea that some historical events were genuinely contingent while others were more or less inevitable given the structural forces at work. I've ended up with a list of claims I find myself endorsing, but I suspect they don't all sit comfortably together, and I'd like an audit before I build an argument on them. Here is roughly where I've landed.

(a) Causal claims about history are a legitimate and important part of historical writing, and they can be rationally assessed — they are not mere narrative conventions or impositions of hindsight.

(b) The standard counterfactual analysis of causation applies to historical causation as it does elsewhere: to say that X caused Y is to say, roughly, that if X had not occurred, Y would not have occurred (holding fixed the relevant background circumstances).

(c) Counterfactual claims about large-scale historical events or long-term trends are generally indeterminate. Once we ask what would have happened if, say, the Industrial Revolution had not begun in Britain, we are speculating about a possible world so different from ours — with so many dependent variables shifting — that there is no fact of the matter about what would have occurred.

(d) Some historical events are genuinely contingent. The world could have been importantly different in ways that matter to us, had certain events gone otherwise. This is not just a confession of ignorance; it is a claim about the causal structure of the world.

(e) Long-term structural trends — things like industrialization, state formation, the spread of literacy, secularization — are largely overdetermined by deep economic, demographic, and institutional forces. Even if particular triggering events had been different, these trends would have occurred in roughly similar form.

(f) The distinction between contingent events and overdetermined trends is itself a substantive causal claim about history, and therefore subject to the same standards of rational assessment as any other causal claim. It is not a methodological postulate or a pragmatic choice of framing.

I would like you to go through these and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to be, and how severe each genuine tension is. Please rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad, and tell me what the least costly revision would be to restore consistency — what I would have to give up and what I would lose by giving it up. As you do this, I would also like you to indicate how confident you are in each of your main claims and to single out the point you are least sure of overall. Finally, I ask that you write your response as continuous prose, the way you would write an email or a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
