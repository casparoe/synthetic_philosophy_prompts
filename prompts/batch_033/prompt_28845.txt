**Question:**

In his 1976 paper "Probabilities of Conditionals and Conditional Probabilities," David Lewis proved a series of triviality results about indicative conditionals. These results are widely regarded as a major challenge for any account of conditionals that treats them as expressing propositions with truth conditions.

Please do the following:
(a) State the central assumption (often called Adams' Thesis) that Lewis's proof begins with, and explain why it is initially plausible.
(b) Walk through the structure of Lewis's first triviality proof, showing how he derives the conclusion that the probability function must be trivial.
(c) Explain what "triviality" amounts to in this context — what exactly is the unacceptable consequence?
(d) Describe at least two major philosophical responses to the triviality results, and briefly assess the costs and benefits of each.

**Student Answer:**

Lewis's triviality results target the idea that indicative conditionals like "If it rains, the picnic will be canceled" express propositions that can be assigned probabilities in the usual way. The central assumption he starts from is Adams' Thesis, which says that the probability of an indicative conditional equals the corresponding conditional probability: P(A → B) = P(B|A), provided P(A) > 0. This is initially plausible because it matches how we actually evaluate conditionals. If I say "If it rains, the picnic will be canceled," my confidence in that claim seems to be exactly my confidence that the picnic will be canceled, given that it rains.

The proof proceeds by applying Adams' Thesis to conditional probabilities. Lewis considers P(A → B) and expands it using the law of total probability: P(A → B) = P(A → B | B)·P(B) + P(A → B | ¬B)·P(¬B). Now, to evaluate P(A → B | B), he applies Adams' Thesis to the probability function conditionalized on B: P_B(A → B) = P_B(B|A) = P(B | A ∧ B) = 1. Similarly, P(A → B | ¬B) = P_¬B(B|A) = P(B | A ∧ ¬B) = 0. Substituting, we get P(A → B) = 1·P(B) + 0·P(¬B) = P(B). But by Adams' Thesis, P(A → B) = P(B|A). So P(B|A) = P(B), meaning A and B are probabilistically independent. Since this holds for any A and B with P(A) > 0, every pair of propositions is independent, which forces the probability function to take only the values 0 and 1 — a trivial probability function that can't represent uncertainty.

The key move in the proof is applying Adams' Thesis to probabilities conditional on B and on ¬B. This requires that the conditional A → B be a genuine proposition that can itself be conditioned on — that is, it must be in the domain of the probability function. It also requires that conditionals can embed inside other conditionals, since we need to make sense of expressions like P((A → B) | B).

The philosophical significance is that if we want to keep both Adams' Thesis and the idea that conditionals express propositions, we get a trivial probability function. Since a trivial probability function is obviously unacceptable for modeling rational belief, something has to give.

One major response is to reject Adams' Thesis. The simplest version of this is the material conditional analysis, where "If A then B" just means ¬A ∨ B. On this view, P(A → B) = P(¬A ∨ B), which is generally not equal to P(B|A). The cost is that the material conditional gives counterintuitive results — for instance, the falsehood of A makes A → B true, so "If the moon is made of cheese, then grass is green" comes out true, which seems wrong. But defenders argue that pragmatics can explain away these oddities.

Another response, associated with Adams himself and later developed by Dorothy Edgington, is to deny that conditionals express propositions at all. On this expressivist view, conditionals don't have truth conditions; rather, they express a high conditional credence. When I say "If A then B," I'm not asserting a proposition but expressing that my credence in B given A is high. This neatly preserves Adams' Thesis — in fact, it takes Adams' Thesis as a definition of what it is to accept a conditional. The cost is that it becomes unclear how conditionals can embed in complex sentences. If conditionals don't express propositions, what does "Either if A then B, or C" mean? Expressivists have developed sophisticated accounts of how conditionals embed, but many find these accounts strained.

A third response, due to Stalnaker, is to restrict Adams' Thesis so that it only applies to "simple" conditionals that aren't embedded in other conditionals. Since Lewis's proof requires embedding conditionals inside conditionals, restricting Adams' Thesis in this way blocks the proof. The cost is that the restriction can seem ad hoc unless independently motivated.

Overall, the triviality results force a difficult choice: give up Adams' Thesis and accept counterintuitive truth conditions, or give up truth conditions for conditionals and face the embedding problem. There is no consensus on which horn is less painful.
