I'm in a political philosophy seminar and we've been doing luck egalitarianism — Arneson, Cohen, Temkin, that crowd. Last week someone in my group laid out an argument that's been gnawing at me, and I can't figure out if it's sound or where exactly it comes apart.

The argument runs like this. (1) Luck egalitarianism says that inequalities are unjust when and because they trace to brute luck — unchosen circumstances, native endowments, accidents — but not when they trace to option luck, i.e., the outcomes of gambles you knowingly and voluntarily took. (2) So if someone ends up badly off because of a choice they freely made, they have no claim of justice to be compensated for the resulting disadvantage. (3) A lot of the cases that actually move people — the gambler who lost his family's savings, the mountaineer who ignored the forecast and now needs an expensive rescue, the lifelong smoker with lung cancer — involve disadvantages that, on any ordinary understanding, trace to option luck. (4) If someone has no claim of justice to compensation, then society has no obligation of justice to assist them. (5) So luck egalitarianism implies that society has no obligation of justice to assist the destitute gambler, the dying smoker, or the stranded mountaineer. (6) But that's obscene. Whatever the metaphysics of free will, we plainly have obligations to help people in dire need, even when their need results from their own choices. (7) Therefore luck egalitarianism is either false or it has to gerrymander "voluntary choice" so tightly that almost nothing counts as option luck — at which point the brute-luck/option-luck distinction collapses and the view loses everything that made it interesting.

I can see a natural reply: deny (4). Luck egalitarianism is a theory of distributive justice, not a complete moral theory. Maybe we have humanitarian duties or duties of beneficence to rescue people from bad option luck, even if those people have no claim of justice to be rescued. But then I worry the view becomes a paper tiger. If we always have independent moral reasons to prevent people from suffering the full consequences of their choices, what actual distributive work is the brute-luck/option-luck distinction doing? It starts to look like luck egalitarianism is just a complicated way of saying "people shouldn't be worse off through no fault of their own, but also we shouldn't let people be too worse off even when it is their fault" — which is just the pre-theoretical common sense we started with, dressed up in jargon.

So my question: where does this argument go wrong, if it does? Which premise do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? And in particular, can the "justice isn't all of morality" move actually save the view, or does it just relocate the embarrasment?
