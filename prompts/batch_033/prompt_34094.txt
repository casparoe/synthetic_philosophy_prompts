I am a novelist at work on a cycle of stories set in a single institution, a selective residential conservatory, across four decades. In scene after scene I find myself reaching for the same two images of institutional power: a figure of authority issuing commands that characters obey or defy, and a watcher in a central position observing rooms full of people. I am told by friends who know the literature that this is a caricature of what Foucault means by power, knowledge, and discipline, and I am prepared to believe them, but I do not yet know how to do better on the page. The stakes are genuine: I do not want to write thesis fiction, but I also do not want my scenes to be politically naive, and whatever procedure I adopt must survive a skeptical editor who is impatient with theory.

I would like you to turn Foucault's account of disciplinary power into a reusable procedure I can run each time I draft a scene set inside or around the conservatory. The procedure should take the form of a checklist or ordered set of questions I can work through in under an hour per scene, without formal philosophical training, and for each question it should state what counts as a sufficient answer before I may move on. It should help me decide, before I write, what kind of power is operating in the scene — for example, sovereign prohibition and spectacle as against disciplinary training, observation, and normalization, or a mixture — where knowledge is being produced and fed back into the exercise of power (records, examinations, classifications, expert reports), and how the scene shapes bodies, routines, spaces, and the ways characters come to monitor themselves. It should tell me which of Foucault's distinctions must survive the simplification into a working checklist and which can be collapsed without loss, and it should mark the points where the procedure stops and hands the decision back to me as an aesthetic or political judgment rather than an application of the theory. It should also be willing to tell me that a planned scene rests on a mistaken picture of power and should be reworked rather than patched.

Then apply the procedure to three scenes I am planning: an admissions audition in which a panel takes notes while a nervous applicant performs a prepared piece; a dormitory inspection in which a resident adviser works through a list of infractions; and a faculty meeting at which instructors review a struggling student's file and agree on a development plan. For each scene, show me what the procedure would lead me to write, and include at least one case in which the procedure tells me to write something contrary to my dramatic instinct. Finally, tell me plainly what the procedure cannot do: the kinds of power or the kinds of scenes it will misdescribe, and where it would let me put a theoretical label on a scene whose actual drama comes from somewhere else.
