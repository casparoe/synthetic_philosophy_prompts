I've been working through Bernard Williams's essay "Internal and External Reasons" for a seminar on metaethics, and I keep returning to a worry that I'm having trouble stating precisely. As I understand the argument, Williams holds that all genuine reasons for action must be "internal" — by which he means that a consideration counts as a reason for an agent to φ only if that consideration can be reached through a sound deliberative route starting from something already present in that agent's subjective motivational set. If a consideration fails to connect to what the agent actually cares about, it simply isn't a reason for them. Now, Williams is explicit that the deliberative route can involve things like correcting factual errors, resolving inconsistencies among one's existing desires, and exercising imagination about what satisfying a desire would actually involve. But none of this, it seems to me, can generate a reason where there is genuinely nothing in the agent's motivational set to work with — no seed of concern, no latent impulse that deliberation could draw out. And this seems to land us in a deeply uncomfortable place. If someone is indifferent to the suffering of others, and this indifference is not based on any factual mistake or inconsistency, then on Williams's view there simply is no reason for that person to refrain from causing harm. Moral criticism of such a person would then amount to something like expressing our own motivational set, or perhaps trying to causally reshape theirs, but not to identifying a reason that genuinely applies to them. But this seems to hollow out the very idea of moral normativity — the thought that some reasons bind us irrespective of what we happen to want. Am I reading Williams too uncharitably, or is this really where the internal reasons thesis leads? And if he is right, what remains of the practice of holding one another morally accountable?
