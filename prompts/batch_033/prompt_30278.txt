For the past several months I have been reporting on how research institutions oversee the use of laboratory animals, and I keep running into a tension I cannot quite articulate. The committees that must approve animal protocols at United States research institutions — institutional animal care and use committees — behave as though animal suffering matters in its own right. Working within the replacement–reduction–refinement framework, they will send a protocol back because the investigator has not justified the number of animals, has not refined a procedure to reduce pain and distress, or has not specified a humane endpoint, and they require these changes even when the science would be equally valid without them. At the same time, the system treats the animals as replaceable research resources, and the committees are not asked to rule on whether animal research as such is permissible, only on whether a particular proposed use is sufficiently careful.

I would like you to reconstruct the theory of moral status that this practice presupposes, if it presupposes one at all. In particular, does the practice commit its participants to the view that animals are direct objects of moral obligation, or could it be underwritten by the older Kantian position that our duties regarding animals are indirect — duties owed ultimately to ourselves and to other human beings, because cruelty toward animals corrodes the sympathetic dispositions that make us humane? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely happens to fit with, and say where it is philosophically underdetermined. Then tell me what would have to change in the day-to-day workings of such a committee if it were forced to make its implicit theory explicit under each candidate view.
