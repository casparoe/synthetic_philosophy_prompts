Write a sonnet about the probability problem in the many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics. The octave should present the appeal of the view: unitary evolution without collapse, a single world-vector branching cleanly under decoherence, no measurement postulate tacked on. The volta should bring in the difficulty — if every branch is equally actual, in what sense does the Born rule make some outcomes more probable than others, and why should a rational agent about to branch care about the amplitude-squared measure rather than branch-counting? The closing couplet should capture the dilemma precisely: that the view seems to require either a primitive probability postulate that sits awkwardly with its parsimonious metaphysics, or a decision-theoretic derivation that risks presupposing the very norm it sets out to ground. The poem should be accurate enough that a philosopher of physics would recognize the structure of the debate, and it should make the problem felt rather than merely named.
