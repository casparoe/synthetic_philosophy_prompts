Berkeley’s *The Querist* is composed as a long series of numbered queries, and its form matters as much as its content. The passage below is the opening portion of the work. I would like help understanding what Berkeley is doing philosophically in this stretch, and in particular how the interrogative form itself is supposed to work on the reader.

Here is the passage:

Query 1. Whether there ever was, is, or will be, an industrious nation poor, or an idle rich?

2. Qu. Whether a people can be called poor, where the common sort are well fed, clothed, and lodged?

3. Qu. Whether the drift and aim of every wise State should not be, to encourage industry in its members? And whether those who employ neither heads nor hands for the common benefit deserve not to be expelled like drones out of a well-governed State?

4. Qu. Whether the four elements, and man's labour therein, be not the true source of wealth?

5. Qu. Whether money be not only so far useful, as it stirreth up industry, enabling men mutually to participate the fruits of each other's labour?

6. Qu. Whether any other means, equally conducing to excite and circulate the industry of mankind, may not be as useful as money.

7. Qu. Whether the real end and aim of men be not power? And whether he who could have everything else at his wish or will would value money?

8. Qu. Whether the public aim in every well-govern'd State be not that each member, according to his just pretensions and industry, should have power?

I would like you to help me work through this passage with attention to the following questions. First, what exactly is Berkeley claiming or presupposing here about the nature of money, wealth, industry, and the proper aim of the state? In particular, how do the normative-sounding premises interact with the more descriptive-sounding claims about what money does? Second, how should we understand the rhetorical function of the query form? Is Berkeley disguising assertions as questions, or is the form doing some real philosophical work that a treatise format could not do as well? Third, where precisely in this opening sequence does Berkeley commit himself to the view that money is not itself wealth, and how strong is the argument for that view as it emerges here?

Please do not treat this as merely a request to paraphrase the lines. I am interested in the conceptual structure of the passage: what Berkeley thinks he is entitled to take as obvious, what he is arguing for, and whether the sequence of queries is best read as an argument, a diagnosis of Irish economic life, or something else. Also, the Querist was published in three parts and revised by Berkeley over time; where the historical facts about publication matter, please be explicit about what is firmly established and what is uncertain or controversial. In your answer, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among scholars of Berkeley and of eighteenth-century political economy, and avoid presenting a contested reading as if it were the consensus.
