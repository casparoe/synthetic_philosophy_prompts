You are a professional philosopher specializing in animal ethics and the moral status of invertebrates. Think carefully, and begin by laying out the candidate positions before you commit. I am a PhD student in environmental philosophy, and I want help reconstructing the theory of moral status that our actual pest-control practices presuppose. Consider the pattern, described schematically rather than as legal doctrine. To register a new insecticide, regulators require toxicity data on birds, mammals, fish, and often bees, yet the deaths of the target insects are treated as a measure of efficacy rather than as a welfare cost. Municipal mosquito-control programs model risks to humans, pets, and non-target vertebrates, while the millions of mosquitoes killed are the program's success criterion rather than an itemized harm. In agriculture, integrated pest management sorts insects into "pests" and "beneficials" by their diet, so two nearly identical beetles are conserved or poisoned depending on what they eat. And organic standards permit naturally derived insecticides that kill insects as thoroughly as synthetic ones, as if the molecule's origin mattered morally. What view of insect moral considerability does this pattern presuppose? Does it presuppose that insects have no moral status, that their status is routinely outweighed, or that status attaches to functional roles such as pollination and predation rather than to the individual? Are these commitments mutually consistent, and which familiar views—capacity-based, sentientist, biocentric, relational—are quietly ruled out or left underdetermined? Finally, what would actually have to change in regulation, mosquito control, and organic certification if we instead held that an insect's capacity to suffer counts directly?
