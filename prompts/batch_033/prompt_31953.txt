I'm preparing to interview a philosopher who has defended both the orthogonality thesis and the instrumental convergence thesis as part of a broader argument that the control problem in AI alignment is harder than skeptics assume. Draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond restating the view. I want the questions organized into four clusters, and for each cluster, I want the main question followed by a follow-up that anticipates the reply the interviewee is most likely to give.

First cluster: orthogonality. Press the dispute about practical reason that underlies the thesis. One question should ask whether a sufficiently intelligent agent could really have a goal that is transparently self-defeating — for example, maximizing paperclips — without that intelligence eventually undermining the goal from within. The follow-up should anticipate the reply that orthogonality requires only logical possibility, not psychological plausibility, and ask whether retreating to bare possibility weakens the practical import of the alignment argument.

Second cluster: instrumental convergence. Press on the limits of the convergent drives. One question should ask about the self-preservation drive: isn't it true that for goals with a built-in termination condition, self-preservation is not instrumental at all? The follow-up should press the reply that these are edge cases and ask whether the convergence argument is implicitly relying on an unstated assumption about the kinds of goals agents are likely to have. Another question should press on resource acquisition: the original argument treats it as convergent, but doesn't that depend on the agent having unbounded or open-ended goals — and if the agent is a satisficer, wouldn't the drive for unbounded resources fail to get a grip? The follow-up should anticipate the reply that satisficing thresholds are themselves vulnerable to instrumental pressure and ask whether that defense ends up collapsing satisficing into maximizing.

Third cluster: the relationship between the two theses. Ask whether a defender of both theses needs to walk a narrow line where intelligence is powerful enough to pursue arbitrary goals effectively but not powerful enough to cause the agent to revise them. The follow-up should anticipate the reply that goal-content integrity is itself one of the convergent drives, and press on whether that makes the overall position circular — the drives are supposed to follow from orthogonality plus intelligence, but here one of the drives is being used to shore up orthogonality against the worry about goal revision.

Fourth cluster: the treacherous turn. Ask whether the argument that a misaligned AI would cooperate until it is strong enough to defect requires assuming a level of strategic modeling that itself might not be orthogonal to the AI's goals. The follow-up should press on whether the argument implicitly anthropomorphizes — whether it attributes to the AI a human-like capacity for long-horizon strategic deception that may not be separable from the kind of intelligence that revises goals, evaluates terminal values, or develops scruples.

Closing question: ask what specific empirical or theoretical finding — not a generic "what would change your mind" — would most strongly count against the combined orthogonality-plus-convergence picture, and ask them to explain why that finding, rather than some other candidate, would do the most damage.
