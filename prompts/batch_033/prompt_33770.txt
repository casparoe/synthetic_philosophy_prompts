I'm trying to work out what I actually think about moral luck, going by my gut on cases. I write novels, so I spend a lot of time thinking about how my characters get judged—and I've noticed my own verdicts are a bit all over the place.

Here's where I land on a few:

Two drivers both glance at their phones for the same three seconds. Driver A hits a child who darted out between parked cars; Driver B's road is empty. I think they're equally blameworthy. The fact that a child happened to be there is just luck, and it shouldn't change the moral judgement. I know the law disagrees, but I'm asking about blame, not liability.

A woman who grew up in a virulently racist family and community makes a vile remark at a dinner party. Another woman, raised in a progressive, diverse enviroment, makes the exact same remark. I think the first is less blameworthy. Her upbringing wasn't her fault, and it shaped her in ways that are hard to undo. The second women should know better.

A man with a strong genetic predisposition to alcoholism drinks himself into wrecking his marriage. His neighbour, with no such predisposition, does the same. I think the first man is less blameworthy. He was dealt a worse hand, constitutionally.

A soldier conscripted into a just war kills an enemy combatant. Another soldier conscripted into an unjust war of aggression does the same. Neither chose to be there. I think the first is less blameworthy, even though neither had any say in which side they fought for.

A novelist unconsciously plagiarises a passage she read a decade ago and forgot. Another novelist lifts the passage deliberately. The first is much less blameworthy, though I'm not sure she's entirely off the hook.

Now here's the case that keeps me up. Consider someone who, had they been born in 1930s Germany, would almost certainly have become a Nazi collaborator. But they were born in 1970s England, and they've lived a perfectly decent life. I don't think they're blameworthy for what they would have done. But something about this verdict bothers me, because it seems to imply that whether I'm a good person is itself a matter of luck, and yet I can't help feeling something like pride in being decent, and something like contempt for those who weren't, in circumstances where I might have been just as bad.

Is there a principle that captures all these verdicts? If there isn't one that gets all of them, which should I give up? And what does the principle say about the collaborator-in-another-life case? I'm especially interested in whether there's a principled distinction between using luck to mitigate blame (which I seem happy to do in most of these) and using it to ground blame (which I'm not doing in the Nazi case, but maybe I should be).
