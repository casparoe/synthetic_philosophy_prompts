I run a philosophy discussion group, and at our last meeting two regulars got into a long argument about wireheading and what it shows about theories of well-being. I took notes because I thought the back-and-forth was unusually sharp, but I'm still not sure how to adjudicate it. Here is roughly how it went:

(1) First speaker: "The standard wireheading thought experiment is a decisive objection to hedonism. If you plug someone into a machine that directly stimulates their pleasure centers, they will lie there in bliss until they starve. Hedonism says that is the best possible life — maximal pleasure with zero pain. But any reasonable person recognizes that is a wasted life, not a good one. So hedonism is false."

(2) Second speaker: "That is too quick. Preference-satisfaction theories face a parallel problem. Imagine a machine that does not stimulate pleasure directly but instead modifies your desires and then satisfies them — or, even more directly, stimulates whatever neural mechanism constitutes the experience of having a preference satisfied. You would get exactly the same result: a person lying there experiencing desire-satisfaction, unwilling to leave. If the wireheading objection kills hedonism, it kills desire theories too."

(3) First speaker: "No, there is a crucial asymmetry. Desires have external satisfaction conditions. If I desire that my children are happy, then directly stimulating my brain does not make my children happy — the desire remains objectively unsatisfied. The machine is giving me the feeling of satisfaction without the fact of it. A desire theory that cares about actual satisfaction, not felt satisfaction, is not threatened by this."

(4) Second speaker: "Fine, but the machine can target the belief-forming system instead. It can make me believe, truly and deeply, that my children are happy. If I never find out otherwise, the subjective state is identical to the real thing. And if I am a desire theorist who says well-being consists in the satisfaction of desires as evaluated from the agent's own perspective, then I have to say the machine has genuinely improved my life — which seems just as counterintuitive as the hedonist's predicament."

(5) First speaker: "But the belief is false. The desire is not actually satisfied — the children are not actually happy. Any plausible desire theory builds in a connection to reality: it is the satisfaction of the desire in the world, not just in the agent's head, that matters. That is the whole point of moving from hedonism to desire theories in the first place."

(6) Second speaker: "Now you are just drawing a line around the specific causal path you find objectionable and calling it 'reality.' But once you admit we need to rule out certain causal paths to satisfaction — no direct brain stimulation, no false-belief implants — you have opened the door for the hedonist to do the same thing. The hedonist can say: pleasure is the good, but only when it arises through the right kinds of engagement with the world. You have both got the same problem: specifying which causal histories make a mental state genuinely valuable."

At this point they were going in circles and we had to end the meeting. Could you adjudicate this exchange move by move? For each of the six numbered moves, identify what is right, what is wrong, and what is being missed. Then say who has the stronger overall position, and whether the wireheading objection ultimately lands harder on hedonism or on desire theories — or whether it is equally problematic for both.
