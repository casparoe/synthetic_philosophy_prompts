I've been working through a version of negative utilitarianism, and an argument I keep returning to seems to show that the view's central intuition proves too much. I can't find the step that fails.

(1) Lexical threshold negative utilitarianism is motivated by the thought that there is some level of suffering so severe that no amount of pleasure could counterbalance it.
(2) If that thought is right, then somewhere along the continuum of pain intensity there is a threshold: pains below it can be counterbalanced by enough pleasure, while pains at or above it cannot be counterbalanced by any amount of pleasure.
(3) Pain intensity varies continuously, so for any such threshold there are pains just below and just above it that are arbitrarily close in felt intensity.
(4) Two pains that close in intensity cannot differ in the radical way the view requires — one fully commensurable with pleasure, the other lexically beyond it — unless the line between them is morally arbitrary.
(5) So the motivating intuition commits us to an arbitrary line.
(6) The only way to avoid the arbitrary line is to accept full commensurability: for every pain, however severe, there is some amount of pleasure that counterbalances it.
(7) But then even torture is counterbalanced by enough mild pleasure, which is exactly what the original intuition denied.
(8) So the intuition forces a choice between arbitrariness and its own falsity.

I would most like to keep (1). Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? If the fault is in (4) or (6), I would want to know exactly how the slide happens and what a stable middle position would look like.
