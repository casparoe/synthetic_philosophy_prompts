I spent most of my working life as a geologist, and I've only started reading political philosophy in the last couple years, so forgive me if I don't have the vocabulary down. I just finished Simone Weil on political parties, and it's made me want to check my own gut reactions against some actual cases instead of just nodding along with her. She seems to think there's something wrong with the party form itself, not just with particular nasty parties. I'm not sure my instincts line up with that.

Here are my verdicts, as honestly as I can report them.

A small neighborhood chapter of a socialist party that meets in a library basement, argues about zoning policy, and occassionally endorses a candidate: totally fine, no problem at all; it feels no different to me than a birding club.

A national party with a whip system, where members who vote against the leadership lose committee assignments and get primary challengers funded against them: this bothers me a lot. It seems to manufacture agreement instead of earning it.

An open primary where anyone can vote to choose a party's nominee: fine, mostly, though I don't love how it pushes candidates toward slogans.

A closed primary where you have to be a registered party member to vote on the nominee: this bugs me more, but I can't say why, since it's the party's own buisness who it nominates.

A single-issue committee formed to pass a library levy, which is legally classified as a political party for election-law purposes and runs ads: seems completely fine to me; it's just an advocacy group with a weird legal label.

A party platform written by activists at a convention, which no candidate is actually bound to follow: harmless, maybe even useful as a public statement of values.

A coalition government formed by two parties under a written agreement: doesn't bother me at all; that just looks like normal compromise.

A workers' party in a one-party state that controls candidate lists, the press, and access to government jobs: obviously not fine, but I worry that's just authoritarianism, not the party form as such.

A judge elected on a party ticket: this one really gets me; I think judges shouldn't carry party labels at all, even though I can't say exactly what changes between a judge and a governor.

And here's the case I keep going back and forth on: a scientific professional association — the kind I used to belong to — that votes to endorse candidates for office and spends dues money on them. Part of me thinks it's just like the birding club again; part of me thinks it's worse because it lends scientific authority to a faction.

So my question: is there a principle that captures these verdicts, or close to them? Does that principle end up agreeing with Weil that the party form itself is the problem, or does it just pick out some narrower thing like coercion or capture? And what does the principle say about the scientific association case, and about the closed primary, since those are the two I can't settle? If some of my verdicts have to go to make the whole thing cohere, tell me which ones and why.

Please write the answer in American English.
