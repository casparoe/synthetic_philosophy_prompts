I've been working through Timothy Williamson's anti-luminosity argument from *Knowledge and Its Limits*, and I've reconstructed it in a way that seems to prove something absurd, but I can't locate the mistake. Here's the argument as I understand it:

(1) Suppose there is a condition C that is luminous: whenever C obtains, you are in a position to know that C obtains. Paradigm candidates include being in pain, feeling cold, or seeming to see red — the kinds of occurrent mental states we ordinarily take ourselves to know we are in just by having them.

(2) Consider a sorites series of cases α₀, α₁, …, αₙ, where α₀ is a clear case of C (e.g., intense pain), αₙ is a clear case of not-C (no pain at all), and each adjacent pair αᵢ and αᵢ₊₁ is so similar that you could not reliably discriminate between them. The change is gradual and everywhere below your threshold of discriminability.

(3) Assume a safety condition on knowledge: if you know that p, your belief could not easily have been false. More precisely, in all nearby cases where you believe p on the same basis, p is true.

(4) Now take any case αᵢ where you are in a position to know that C obtains. Given the indiscriminability of αᵢ and αᵢ₊₁, αᵢ₊₁ is a nearby case. If C did not obtain in αᵢ₊₁, then your belief in αᵢ would be unsafe: you could easily have been in αᵢ₊₁, believed C obtains on the very same basis, and been wrong. So, by safety, C must obtain in αᵢ₊₁.

(5) By luminosity, since C obtains in α₀, you are in a position to know that C obtains in α₀. By (4), C obtains in α₁. By luminosity again, you are in a position to know that C obtains in α₁. By (4) again, C obtains in α₂. Iterating through the whole series forces C to obtain in αₙ — the clear case of not-C. Contradiction.

(6) Therefore, no non-trivial condition is luminous. In particular, you are never in a position to know that you are in pain, that you feel cold, or that you seem to see red.

I find (6) impossible to accept. It seems to prove that I cannot know my own occurrent sensations, which feels like a reductio of the argument itself rather than a discovery about the limits of self-knowledge. Yet each of (1) through (5) looked individually compelling when I first encountered them. I am especially suspicious of the safety condition in (3): perhaps it is too strong, or perhaps the notion of a "nearby case" is doing more work than it can bear when we are talking about phenomenal states rather than external-world beliefs. But I am also drawn to the idea that the real problem lies with (1): maybe luminosity sets the bar for self-knowledge in a way we do not need to meet, and we can know our sensations without their being luminous in Williamson's sense. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If I want to keep the claim that I can know I am in pain, what else must I give up, and which alternative picture of self-knowledge does that commit me to?
