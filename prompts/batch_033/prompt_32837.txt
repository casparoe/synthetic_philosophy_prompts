I've been working through Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach on my own — I don't have any formal training in logic or philosophy — and I keep circling back to the MU-puzzle and the idea of "jumping out of the system." I understand the surface mechanics well enough: you begin with the string MI, you have four typographical rules for producing new strings, and the problem is to derive MU. And I follow the meta-level argument for why MU is unreachable: the rules all preserve the property that the number of I's in the string is not a multiple of three, so any derivable string must share that property, and MU — with zero I's — doesn't. So far so good.

What I'm stuck on is what the act of jumping out actually amounts to. Hofstadter presents JOOTSing as a deep insight: to see the limits of a formal system, you sometimes have to step outside it and reason about it from a higher vantage point. But here's the difficulty. The higher-level reasoning — the divisibility-by-three argument — is itself a perfectly rigorous piece of mathematical reasoning. It could be formalized inside a richer system, like Peano arithmetic. So was I ever really "outside" anything, or did I just trade one formal system for another? If the latter, then JOOTSing seems to be less like escaping formalism and more like climbing a ladder from a weaker system to a stronger one — and the ladder itself is just another rung in the formal hierarchy. But then the whole image of "jumping out" starts to feel misleading. If every meta-level insight can be absorbed into a more powerful formal system, is there ever a genuine outside, or is it just systems all the way up?

And if that's right, I'm left wondering what it does to the larger ambitions of the book. Hofstadter seems to be building toward something about consciousness, meaning, and self-reference — the idea that minds somehow arise from or relate to these kinds of formal limits. But if JOOTSing is just moving between systems rather than transcending them, does the argument still have the force he wants it to have? I'd like an explanation that takes this tension seriously rather than just re-explaining the puzzle's rules.
