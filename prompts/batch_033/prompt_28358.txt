In my pragmatism seminar we have spent the term on the dispute between Peirce and James about what it is for a belief to be true, and I have come away with a strange impression: the argument never seems to move, and not because either side misunderstands the other. Peirce, as I understand him, takes truth to be a property a belief has independently of what you or I happen to think now — roughly, the opinion that would be converged upon by an indefinitely extended community of inquirers who push investigation as far as it will go. James, as I understand him, starts from a different place: he asks what difference it makes in practice to call a belief true, and answers that true beliefs are the ones that fit with the rest of our experience, guide us successfully from one part of experience to another, and prove their worth in use. These do not sound like conflicting descriptions of the same thing so much as answers to different questions, yet the secondary literature treats the exchange as a genuine standoff. The familiar charges are that James reduces truth to whatever happens to satisfy or verify well enough here and now, while Peirce's convergence account is an empty regulative ideal that says nothing about what we should believe before inquiry is finished. James's defenders reply that he was not defining truth at all but describing the role the concept plays in our cognitive lives; Peirce's defenders reply that only his account explains why truth is objective and why inquiry is answerable to something beyond present usefulness. Peirce himself eventually adopted the label pragmaticism precisely to mark his distance from James and others who, he thought, had made pragmatism too subjective.

My working hypothesis is that the debate has stalled because it is partly a verbal disagreement about the word true and partly two research programs answering different questions — one about the semantics of truth, the other about the function of truth-talk in inquiry — but I distrust my own diagnosis because calling a debate verbal is always the easy way out. So I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is the disagreement substantive, verbal, or two projects talking past each other under a single heading? Where exactly does the argument stall, and is the impasse a sign that the question is ill-posed? And given your diagnosis, say what would count as progress: a distinction, a reframing, a formal result, or an argument that no progress should be expected.
