You are a professional philosopher of mind with expertise in the metaphysics and science of consciousness, and you have been asked to serve as the neutral facilitator for an adversarial collaboration between two camps on the meta-problem of consciousness.

The first camp is a Chalmers-style phenomenal realist. It holds that there is a hard problem of consciousness: explaining why and how physical processing gives rise to subjective experience, or what it is like to be a subject. On this view, the meta-problem—the problem of explaining why we have the powerful intuition that there is a hard problem, and why we produce the phenomenal reports and judgments we do—is a distinct, largely empirical problem. Solving the meta-problem may clarify the sources of our phenomenal intuitions and even help with the hard problem, but it does not by itself dissolve or debunk the hard problem.

The second camp is a Frankish-style illusionist. It holds that phenomenal consciousness, understood as a realm of qualitative properties over and above functional, representational, dispositional, and physical facts, is an illusion. On this view, the meta-problem is the central problem: the task is to explain why creatures like us are so strongly convinced that we have such properties. A completed explanation of these intuitions, one that does not itself invoke phenomenal properties, would debunk the intuitions rather than vindicate them.

Think carefully before you write. In your first pass, brainstorm: (a) claims about phenomenal reports, introspective confidence, and the explanatory gap that both camps could honestly sign; (b) the places where each camp would reject a formulation as slanted; and (c) which apparent disagreements might be partly verbal and which are substantive. Then draft the document.

Your deliverable is the joint statement these two camps could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration, with the residual disagreement made exact. It must contain the following parts.

1. Agreed claims. A numbered list of substantive, non-vacuous claims both camps accept. Cover at least: the behavioral and introspective data to be explained (e.g., that people report phenomenal states, find them introspectively obvious, and find physical-functional explanations of them unsatisfying); what the meta-problem asks; what a solution to the meta-problem must do; and constraints on any acceptable explanation of phenomenal reports. Avoid phrasing these so weakly that they are uninformative.

2. Disagreements table. A table with one row per point of disagreement. Columns: (i) the issue in neutral wording; (ii) the realist's preferred formulation of its own view, which a careful realist would sign; (iii) the illusionist's preferred formulation of its own view, which a careful illusionist would sign; (iv) whether the disagreement is substantive or partly verbal, with a one-sentence note if it is partly verbal. Rows should include at least: whether explaining the intuitions debunks them or leaves the explanatory gap untouched; whether introspective conviction has a special epistemic authority; whether phenomenal properties are needed to explain anything beyond the reports themselves; and whether the conceivability of zombies or the apparent explanatory gap is evidence for realism or just another intuition to be explained away.

3. Cruxes and tests. Identify the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one camp. For each crux, specify a concrete case, experimental manipulation, or argument that both camps would accept as bearing on it, and record in advance each camp's prediction about what that test would show and what result would count as a concession or would significantly shift that camp's view. Include at least one crux about dissociations between reports and first-order function (for example, blindsight, change blindness, or subjective inflation), one about a mechanism-level model that predicts and manipulates people's phenomenal intuitions, and one about a hypothetical system built to reproduce human phenomenal reports while remaining functionally and physically specified but lacking, by stipulation, whatever the realist means by phenomenality.

Do not include a bibliography or reading list. The statement should stand on the content of the two views, not on citations.
