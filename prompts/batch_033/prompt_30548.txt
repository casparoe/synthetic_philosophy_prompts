Here is the question. In The Descent of Man (1871), Darwin offered a naturalistic account of how the human moral sense could have arisen through evolutionary processes, rooted in social instincts that were then extended by reflection, habit, and cultural reinforcement. In recent decades, a number of philosophers have advanced what are called evolutionary debunking arguments in metaethics. The core claim is roughly this: if our moral intuitions were shaped by natural selection to promote reproductive fitness rather than to track objective moral truths, then we have reason to doubt the reliability of those intuitions — and perhaps reason to doubt the existence of objective moral facts altogether. The evolutionary story, on this view, explains why we hold the moral beliefs we do without needing to invoke any actual moral properties, thereby undercutting our justification for those beliefs.

If Darwin were alive today and encountered these debunking arguments, how do you think he would respond? Would he regard them as a natural extension of his own naturalism about morality, or would he push back against their skeptical implications? In framing your answer, please distinguish carefully between two questions: (a) the descriptive question of whether morality has an evolutionary origin, and (b) the normative question of whether that evolutionary origin undermines morality's authority or our justification for moral beliefs. Ground your reasoning in what we know of Darwin's intellectual temperament, his evolving views on religion, his remarks about the moral sense in The Descent of Man and elsewhere, and his general approach to philosophical questions. Where the evidence admits of more than one plausible answer, acknowledge the uncertainty and explain which reading you find best supported and why.
