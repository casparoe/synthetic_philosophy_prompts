I am a legislative drafter at a state agency. We write regulations that carry the force of law, and I have noticed that a surprising number of interpretive disputes turn on sentences containing definite descriptions — phrases like "the director," "the applicant," or "the report submitted under subsection (b)." Courts sometimes read these as carrying an implicit claim that exactly one such thing exists, and when that claim fails (no director has been appointed, two reports were submitted), the whole provision becomes ambiguous or unenforceable. Other times the problem is scope: "The agency shall not approve the application if it is incomplete" could mean there is a unique application and the agency shall not approve it if it is incomplete, or it could mean the agency shall not approve any application that is incomplete — and the difference matters when there are multiple applications.

I understand there is a philosophical literature on this — Russell's theory of descriptions and its descendants — that analyzes the logical form of definite descriptions and distinguishes existence, uniqueness, and scope. But I need something I can use at my desk, not a logic textbook.

Please write me a procedure — a checklist or decision tree — that I can apply to any regulatory sentence containing a definite description to determine whether it is vulnerable to one of these interpretive failures. The procedure should: (1) tell me how to spot a definite description in the wild, including disguised ones; (2) walk me through the three checks (existence, uniqueness, scope) in order, with a clear test for each; (3) give me a rewrite rule for each kind of failure; and (4) flag the cases where the procedure cannot settle the issue and I need to consult a senior attorney.

Then apply the procedure to three examples from our actual work: (a) "The commissioner shall revoke the license of any facility that fails inspection"; (b) "The report prepared under section 4(c) is deemed conclusive"; and (c) "The agency shall not disclose the identity of the complainant." For each, show me what the procedure flags and what the rewrite would be.

Finally, tell me honestly what this procedure cannot catch — what kinds of definite-description problems will still slip through, and where the Russellian framework itself might mislead a drafter.
