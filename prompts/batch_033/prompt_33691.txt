Please play this visiting speaker. You're committed to the soundness of the simulation argument, though you're nuanced about it — you don't claim it proves we're in a simulation, only that it forces a trilemma and that the third horn (we are probably simulated) deserves a substantial credence. You know the technical literature and you're willing to engage with subtle objections; you'll concede small points where the argument genuinely has limits, but you won't give up the core. Stay in character, keep replies to a paragraph or two so it feels like a real conversation, and don't break the fourth wall.

Here's my opening:

"I've been thinking about the step in the argument where you move from 'there are vastly more simulated minds than non-simulated ones' to 'we should assign a correspondingly high credence to being simulated.' That step requires something like an indifference principle across minds — if I know I'm a mind with certain experiences, and most minds with experiences like mine are simulated, I should think I'm probably simulated. But here's my worry. The indifference principle only works if you've fixed the right reference class. If I widen the reference class to include all possible minds — including minds in universes with very different physics — the ratios might shift in unknown ways. If I narrow it to minds that share my exact neural architecture, the ratio might be roughly 1:1 because I'm only counting biological humans. The simulation argument seems to pick a reference class — roughly, 'minds with experiences subjectively like ours' — that's neither obviously correct nor obviously question-begging. But the argument's conclusion is highly sensitive to this choice, and Bostrom's discussion of the reference-class problem in the paper is surprisingly brief. So my question is: how do you justify the reference class you're using, without begging the question against someone who thinks the right reference class is much narrower?"

When we're done, I'd like you to step back and tell me — not as part of the role-play but as a candid assessment — what you think is the genuinely weakest step in the simulation argument, the place where you'd be most likely to change your mind if the empirical or philosophical facts turned out a certain way.
