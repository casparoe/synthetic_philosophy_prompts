I was reading one of those popular "how to think about probability" pieces and it gave this run-down of Dutch book arguments:

"A Dutch book argument shows that if your degrees of belief don't obey the probability axioms, you're vulnerable to exploitation: a bookie can offer you a series of bets, each of which you'll accept because it's fair by your own lights, but which together guarantee you lose money no matter what happens. Say you're 60% confident it'll rain and 60% confident it won't. The bookie sells you a bet that pays $1 if it rains for 60 cents, and a bet that pays $1 if it doesn't for 60 cents. You pay $1.20 total and get back $1 either way — a sure 20-cent loss. So the argument is meant to show that anyone whose credences aren't probabilities is irrational, since rational people don't take sure losses. Defenders of Dutch book arguments also say this shows credences just are betting odds: to have credence 0.6 in rain is nothing more than being willing to pay 60 cents for a bet that pays $1 if it rains. And once your credences are probabilities, you're safe: no Dutch book can be made against you."
