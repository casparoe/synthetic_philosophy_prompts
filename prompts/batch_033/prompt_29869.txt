I'm a novelist working on a book about how people make up their minds—or fail to. One of my characters is an economist who keeps explaining everything in terms of information cascades, and I want to get inside his head. But the more I think about it, the more I realize my own intuitions about when it's okay to follow the crowd are kind of a mess. I've jotted down a bunch of cases with my gut reactions, and I'd love it if you could help me find the principle behind them—or tell me which verdicts I need to walk back.

Here goes:

1. I'm in a new city, hungry, weighing two ramen shops. One has a line out the door, the other is empty. My phone tells me the empty one has better reviews. But I figure all those people can't be wrong—they've presumably eaten here before, or at least made the same calculation I'm making. I join the line. Verdict: perfectly rational.

2. A novelist friend of mine just had his manuscript rejected by twenty agents. A single editor friend who read it thinks it's extraordinary. My friend shelves the book. I think this is rational, and honestly I'd do the same—but there's a part of me that hates it. Verdict: rational but regrettable.

3. Same friend, different scenario: he's in a writing group and five people all tell him the third-act twist doesn't land. He privately thinks it's the best thing he's ever written. He changes it anyway. Verdict: rational. Five independent readers can't all be missing the point.

4. But now suppose the five people in the writing group discussed the twist before giving their feedback. My friend knows they talked, so they might have influenced each other. Should his verdict change? I feel like it should, but I'm not sure why. I think if he knows they talked, he should discount thier agreement somewhat. Verdict: less rational to defer to them, maybe not rational at all.

5. Here's a nasty one. A critic I know—a good critic, someone whose judgment I trust—privately loved a novel. Then the big reviews came out and savaged it. Her review ended up panning it too. When I asked her about it, she said she'd re-read it and realized she'd been wrong. But I don't believe her. I think she cascaded. Verdict: not rational, and also kind of spineless.

6. But swap the details: same critic, same novel, but this time she reads five brutal takedowns by critics she deeply respects, each making specific arguments she hadn't considered, and she genuinely changes her mind. Verdict: rational. So the difference isn't the outcome, it's something about the process.

7. I buy a novel because it won the Booker. I read it. I think it's medicore, honestly kind of a slog. But I assume I'm missing something—these are serious judges, they must have seen something I didn't. Verdict: rational for a reader, though it makes me a little sad about myself.

8. Now flip it. I'm on a prize jury. I privately think Book A is the best, but the other four jurors are passionately for Book B. I've been wrong before. I switch my vote. Verdict: this feels like a dereliction—I was put there to exercise my judgment, not to defer. Not rational.

9. Last one, and this is the one that confuses me most. I'm at a dinner party and everyone is praising a novelist I privately think is a fraud—glib, manipulative, all style. I say nothing. Later, two people quietly tell me they agree with me but didn't want to say so in the moment. We were all waiting for someone else to go first. My silence felt rational in the moment—why alienate people over a take that might be wrong?—but looking back it seems like a textbook cascade that wouldn't have happened if any one of us had spoken. Was my silence rational or not?

So that's the pile of cases. I'm trying to figure out: is there a single principle—or maybe two or three—that separates the cases where following the herd is rational from the cases where it isn't? Something about independence of signals, or about the stakes involved, or about whether you're an observer or a participant? I'd also love to know what your principle says about case 4—the one where the writing group talked to each other first—because I feel like that one might force some clarity.

Oh, and if you could write your answer in British English that'd be great—my editor's in London and I'd like to run it by her without having to translate spelling.
