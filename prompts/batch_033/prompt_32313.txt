I work in trust and safety at a mid-sized video platform. I write the content rules and also handle appeals, so I'm making maybe twenty calls a week about whether a specific video violates something like "no glorification of violence" or "no hateful conduct." The stakes are real: a wrong call either keeps a bad actor up or kills a creator's channel. I've got about ten minutes per case, I can't pull in a lawyer or a philosopher each time, and my boss wants the calls consistent and defensible when someone appeals.

The problem is the cases keep outrunning the rule. The rule plus every past decision we've made just doesn't settle whether this new video is a violation, and asking "what does the rule really mean here" makes my team argue in circles. I've read a little of the Kripkenstein rule-following stuff — the claim that there's no fact about the rule that fixes what counts as the right next application, and that what saves us is agreement in practice rather than the rule itself. I don't need the exegesis about whether Kripke got Wittgenstein right; I need the operational version.

Give me a reusable procedure for deciding whether a novel case falls under a content rule, written for someone who has to act on it, not theorize about it. It should be a checklist of questions in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with "the rule doesn't settle this" and hands the case to a person, where it says "yes, apply the rule" even when my team's intuition squirms, and which calls I can make myself versus which need the policy lead's sign-off. Mark the steps where judgment can't be eliminated, and say what the procedure as a whole can't do. Then run the procedure on two cases: (1) a rule against "glorifying violence" applied to a documentary clip of a real bombing that the channel celebrates; (2) a rule against "hateful conduct" applied to a minority creator using a slur for themselves in a comedy sketch. One of those should come out in a way I'm not going to like.

Format: exactly seven numbered items, each a sentence or two, no introduction and no conclusion. The first five items are the procedure; the last two are the worked examples.
