I'm trying to get clear on what exactly Williams's 'one thought too many' is supposed to target, and I keep going in circles. Here's what's bugging me.

The standard story: Williams gives the example of a man who has to choose between saving his wife and saving a stranger, and he saves his wife. Williams says that if the man's motivation involves the thought 'it was morally permissible to save her,' that's one thought too many — the right motivating thought is just 'she's my wife.' So far so good.

But here's what I don't get. Is this supposed to be an objection to consequentialism as a criterion of rightness, or as a decision procedure, or to something else entirely? Because a consequentialist can easily say: look, consequentialism is a theory of what makes actions right, not a guide for how to deliberate moment-to-moment. The guy should just act on his love for his wife, and consequentialism will (in most cases) say that's the right thing to do. The fact that a consequentialist justification is available in principle doesn't mean anyone has to think it in the moment. So what's the objection actually hitting?

I've heard people say the point is deeper: it's not about when you have the thought, but about the kind of relationship you have to your own reasons. That if you even think of your wife as 'someone I am permitted to save,' you're already one step removed from the actual relationship — like the consequentialist justification is always there in the background, and that fact itself changes something. But then what exactly does it change? Is the claim that the agent's motives are corrupted, or that the agent's relationship to their spouse is damaged, or that there's something incoherent about the agent's overall stance?

And then there's the separate question of how this connects to the integrity objection — the stuff about ground projects and commitments and how utilitarianism alienates you from them. Is the 'one thought too many' just an illustration of the integrity objection, or is it a distinct argument with its own target? I've seen people treat them as basically the same thing, and I've seen people insist they're different, but I haven't seen a clear map of what the relationship actually is.

If you could walk me through the architecture here — what each objection is aimed at, what kind of theory each is supposed to rule out, and whether the 'one thought too many' survives the 'criterion, not decision procedure' move — I'd appreciate it. And at the very end, give me a one-sentence summary of your answer as the last line.
