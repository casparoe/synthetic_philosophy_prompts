I've been working through the literature on epiphenomenalism lately — specifically the "paradox of phenomenal judgment" that gets leveled against it. A friend and I have been going back and forth about this for weeks, and I keep finding that prose arguments hit a wall: you can state the paradox, you can state the epiphenomenalist's reply (usually something about evolutionary coincidence or about the judgment being constituted by rather than caused by the quale), but the sheer weirdness of the position never quite lands. I want something that makes the self-referential knot feel visceral rather than merely stated.

For context: epiphenomenalism holds that conscious mental states are caused by physical processes but have no causal powers of their own; they're like the steam from a locomotive whistle, accompanying the work without doing any. The paradox, pressed most famously by William James (though he didn't use the term "epiphenomenalism") and later sharpened by critics of the view, is that if epiphenomenalism is true, then my judgment that I am in pain cannot be caused by the pain itself — the quale is causally inert. So my belief that I'm conscious would have to be explained entirely by non-conscious physical processes that happen, miraculously, to produce a judgment that tracks the very qualia that, by hypothesis, play no role in producing it. It looks like an incredible coincidence, or worse, a self-undermining position: the epiphenomenalist's own assertion of the view would be a physical event caused by something other than the truth of the view itself.

I want a poem — a sonnet, specifically — that does justice to this paradox. The octave should lay out the epiphenomenalist picture with some care: consciousness as a shadow, a byproduct, real but powerless. The volta should be the moment the paradox snaps into focus: the realization that the very act of describing consciousness as epiphenomenal is itself a judgment about consciousness, and if the theory is true, that judgment wasn't caused by the thing it's about. The closing couplet should leave the tension unresolved — not resolving in favor of epiphenomenalism or against it, but making the reader feel the force of the self-referential knot. It should be accurate enough that someone who knows the literature (James, Chalmers on the paradox of judgment, the back-and-forth with property dualists) would recognize the argument, not just the buzzwords.
