In my research group there's a running argument about how to treat two quantities when you don't have any specific evidence about their correllation. One of the postdocs keeps saying the sensible default is to treat them as independent and only update when the data forces otherwise — he calls it the presumption of independence and leans hard on the legal analogy: in a court you don't assume the defendant is guilty, you make the prosecution prove it. I can't tell how much of that is doing real work or just rhetoric. Can you do an actual audit of the analogy? I want the correspondence table — defendant, crime, trial, standard of proof, burden of proof, all of it — and then the places where the mapping breaks. Specifically I'm suspicious about the asymmetry: the legal rule is built to be lopsided because convicting an innocent person is worse than letting a guilty one go, but I don't see what the matching lopsidedness would be in estimation, where treating dependent things as independent can be just as costly as the reverse depending on what you're using the estimate for. Does the "start independent, move only on evidence" norm survive the disanologies, or is it mostly the legal glow doing the work? And if the analogy is bad, is there a better one for what this heuristic actually is?
