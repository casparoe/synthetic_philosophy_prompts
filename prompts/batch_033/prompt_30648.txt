Please give me your credence that the question "Why is there something rather than nothing?" has a correct and informative answer — where "informative" means the answer does more than reject the question's presuppositions or declare the existence of the world a brute fact requiring no further explanation. Decompose your credence along the following lines, adding or removing terms as you see fit:

(a) The probability that the question is well-formed — that "absolute nothingness" is a coherent default or null state, and that the demand for an explanation of the totality of contingent things is not based on a confusion about what an explanation of a totality could even consist in.

(b) The probability that some version of the principle of sufficient reason holds strongly enough to require an answer — that the existence of the concrete world is the kind of thing that must have an explanation, even if that explanation is not causal in any ordinary sense.

(c) The probability that, conditional on (a) and (b), the correct answer is broadly metaphysical (e.g., a necessary being, the impossibility of there being nothing, the identity of existence with some evaluative or modal fact) rather than broadly scientific (e.g., a cosmological model in which spacetime emerges from a zero-energy quantum state, or a multiverse framework in which the question is displaced onto the laws).

(d) The probability that, conditional on a metaphysical answer being correct, we can in principle grasp it — that we are not cognitively closed to the relevant facts or concepts, and that the answer would not consist in a gesture toward something ineffable.

(e) Any other term you think carries significant weight.

Give a number and a one- or two-sentence justification for each term, then show how they combine to yield your overall credence. Finally, identify the term your credence is most sensitive to: if you could learn the truth of exactly one sub-question, which would shift your overall number the most, and in which direction?
