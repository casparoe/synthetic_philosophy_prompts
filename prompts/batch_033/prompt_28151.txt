Im a novelist, not a philosopher, so forgive the framing. I've been reading Machiavelli's The Prince because the novel I'm writing turns on exactly the question he worries about in chapter 25: how much of a person's life is luck and how much is something they actually do. His argument there rests on the river image. Fortune is like a river that, when it floods, tears up trees and buildings and ruins everything; but in calm weather people can build embankments and channels, so the flood only destroys whoever was too lazy or short-sighted to prepare. From that he concludes fortune controls maybe half of what we do, and that boldness plus preparation — virtù — can master the other half. A few paragraphs later he switches to the image of fortune as a woman who is better mastered by the bold than by the cautious, which already seems to be doing different work.

I keep wanting to use the river picture as the spine of my book: the protagonist either builds her dikes in time or gets swept away, and whether she does is a matter of virtù, not luck. But the more I stare at it, the less sure I am that the river actually supports that conclusion. So audit the analogy for me. Give me the correspondence table: what plays the role of the river, the flood, the calm spell, the embankments, the builders. Then the places it breaks. In particular, I suspect it quietly imports regular hydrology and engineering — a river behaves according to predictable physical laws and embankments work for the same reason — while fortune, as Machiavelli himself seems to think, doesnt behave like that; and a flood is one external event, whereas fortune in politics is mostly a lot of other people acting. Does the conclusion — that preparation and boldness buy you real control over roughly half of life — survive once those disanalogies are on the table, or was the river doing all the argumentative work? And if it breaks, what image would actually carry what he's claiming, if any?
