I'm a postgraduate student in political theory and I keep noticing a pattern in activist and institutional circles that I don't have a word for, and I'm hoping you can help me pin it down.

Here's the sort of thing I mean. Two advocacy groups — say, a climate group and an animal welfare group — realise they have overlapping membership but different priorities. They make an explicit deal: the climate folks will show up to the animal welfare protest and vice versa, or they'll endorse each other's policy demands, even though neither group's core members actually think the other's cause is anywhere near as important as their own. So far this just sounds like coalition-building. But here's the thing that nags at me: in the cases I'm thinking of, each side privately believes the other's cause is not merely less important but actually misguided in some respect — the climate people think the animal welfare people are sentimentalising creatures that lack the relevant kind of consciousness, and the animal welfare people think the climate people are obsessing over abstract temperature targets while ignoring the concrete suffering happening right now. And yet they go through with the deal anyway, because they reckon the strategic benefit to their own cause outweighs the cost of advancing something they quietly think is wrongheaded. Nobody is lying exactly, but nobody is being fully candid either.

I've seen the same structure in academic politics: two departments facing budget cuts agree to defend each other's funding in senate meetings, even though each department chair privately thinks the other department produces work that is, frankly, intellectually bankrupt. They don't say this aloud during the deal, but everyone sort of knows.

I've heard the phrase "moral trade" floating around, and I know there's some philosophical literature on it, but I'm not sure this is quite the same thing. In the examples of moral trade I've come across, the two parties at least respect each other's moral frameworks — the vegan and the pro-life activist each think the other's cause is genuinely morally weighty, even if they disagree about priorities. In my cases, there's something closer to quiet contempt. So my question is: does this phenomenon have an established name, or is it just a subspecies of moral trade? If it does have a name, what distinguishes it from standard moral trade, logrolling, and straightforward hypocrisy? And if it doesn't, can you coin one and define its boundaries — for instance, does it matter whether the contempt is private or publicly known, or whether the deal is explicit or tacit?

I'd love it if you could approach this as a professional philosopher working in moral and political philosophy. Take your time, think through the distinctions carefully — maybe start by brainstorming the nearby concepts and then narrow down — and please write your answer in British English.
