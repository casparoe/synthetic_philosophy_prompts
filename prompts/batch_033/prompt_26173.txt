I've been working on a long-form piece about self-referential systems — constitutions that contain their own amendment procedures, games whose rules govern how the rules themselves may be changed, and the strange logical terrain where a system's operations can turn on its own foundations. The centerpiece of the piece is Peter Suber's game Nomic, in which changing the rules is a move permitted by the rules, and the rule-changing rules are themselves subject to change. What fascinates me is how quickly the game generates the same paradoxes that legal theorists worry about: a rule that governs its own amendment can be used to amend itself out of existence, or to alter the voting threshold by which it is altered, or to entrench itself against any future change, raising hard questions about legal continuity, the identity of a rule across revisions, and whether there is any Archimedean point outside a self-amending system from which its validity can be assessed.

I'm running a workshop for about 20 people — a mix of legal scholars, philosophers, and game designers, none of whom need to have played Nomic before — and I want them to encounter these problems directly rather than hear about them. I have 90 minutes. What I'm after is a stripped-down, playable version of Nomic: something that fits on a single sheet of rules, runs three or four rounds at tables of five, and is deliberately engineered so that the self-referential trouble surfaces within the first few turns. Participants shouldn't be told what the paradoxes are in advance; they should discover the instability themselves when someone proposes a rule that redefines what counts as a valid vote, or a rule that makes certain kinds of proposal unpassable, or a rule that declares itself immune to repeal. The debrief should then map what happened at each table onto the philosophical issues — the distinction between amendment and repeal, the analogy to constitutional entrenchment and parliamentary sovereignty, the way self-amending systems can drift into inconsistency or paralysis, and the question of whether there is a stable distinction between "ordinary" rules and "constitutional" rules when both are mutable by the same procedure. I'd also like an honest note on what the activity cannot reproduce: real legal systems have enforcement mechanisms, interpretive traditions, and institutional actors with interests in continuity, and a 90-minute game will have none of those.

Please give me the rules, the materials, a minute-by-minute timeline for the session, a debrief script that draws out the philosophical content without lecturing, and a prediction of what will go wrong the first time I run it. Also, tell me how confident you are in the design overall and which part of it you're least sure about.
