I've been going in circles on the finks vs masks distinction for dispositions and I need someone to build me a case that really isolates what's at stake. Here's what I want:

Construct a single ordinary scenario — no wizards, no sci-fi, something you could set up in a lab or a kitchen — where the very same physical intervention counts as a mask under one natural description of the stimulus condition and as a fink under another natural description of the stimulus condition. "Natural" here means: neither description is gerrymandered or cooked up just to get the result; both are ways a reasonable person would describe what triggers the disposition.

Then walk me through:
(a) What the disposition is and what the two candidate stimulus descriptions are.
(b) Why, under description 1, the intervention is a mask (the disposition is still there, the stimulus occurs, but the manifestation is blocked).
(c) Why, under description 2, the intervention is a fink (the disposition is removed or altered when the stimulus occurs, so the stimulus happens but the object no longer has the disposition).
(d) What this means for the simple conditional analysis — you know, "x is disposed to M when S iff x would M if x underwent S." Does your case show that the conditional analysis can't even get off the ground without a principled way to pick the right level of description for S? Or can the conditional analyst just say that one of your two descriptions is the right one for capturing the disposition, and if so, what makes it the right one?
(e) Your own take: is there a principled way to settle which description is correct, or does the finks/masks distinction turn out to be partly conventional — a matter of how we chose to describe the stimulus?

The point isn't to reproduce a case from the literature (I've read the standard ones). I want something that makes the conceptual pressure vivid in a new way.
