I've spent the last couple of years reading about animal cognition and the evolution of minds — not in any formal way, just following my curiosity through whatever I could find at the library and in essays online. I've gradually settled into a set of views that feel right to me one by one, but lately I've had the nagging sense that they might not all hang together, and I'd like someone who really knows this terrain to take a look.

Here's where I've landed:

(1) Minds are entirely the product of evolution by natural selection. We don't need to invoke anything non-physical to explain cognition or consciousness.

(2) The capacity to learn from experience through trial and error — the kind of learning we see across a huge range of animals — is already a real form of mindedness. A creature that can adjust its behavior based on what worked and what didn't has a mind in a meaningful sense.

(3) Some animals go well beyond that: they can try things out internally, simulating possible actions and anticipating outcomes without having to actually perform them. This is a qualitatively different kind of mind, and it's what underwrites the flexible problem-solving we see in corvids, primates, dolphins, and octopuses.

(4) Humans add yet another layer. We inherit not just genes but an accumulated culture — language, writing, social practices, tools for thinking — and this external scaffolding is what makes full self-consciousness and reflective thought possible. A human child raised without language and culture would still have a mind, but not the kind of mind that can step back and examine its own thinking.

(5) At least some non-human animals — certainly the ones I gestured at in (3) — are conscious in a way that matters morally. Their suffering and enjoyment count for something, independently of their usefulness to us.

(6) Genuine thought, as opposed to mere information processing, requires the ability to reflect on one's own thinking. And that reflective capacity depends on having some kind of symbol system or inner language in which to represent one's own mental states to oneself.

I can feel tensions among these but I'm not confident I've identified the right ones or how serious they are. I'd be grateful for an audit that goes through these point by point, flags the genuine tensions, ranks them by severity, and suggests what the least costly revisions would be. I'd also appreciate it if you could separate what's broadly settled among people who work on these questions from what's genuinely contested, so I know which of my views are controversial and which are relatively uncontroversial.
