I am a professor of philosophy, and I have been teaching a graduate seminar on the norms of assertion this term. Over the course of the semester, I have found myself increasingly uncertain about what principle governs when an assertion is proper or warranted, and I have decided to confront the matter directly by setting down my intuitive verdicts on a range of cases. I would be grateful for an analysis that helps me see what theory, if any, these verdicts add up to.

Here are the cases and my reactions to them. (1) A colleague with no inside information guesses the winner of a departmental election correctly and announces the result to a group before the official announcement. What she says is true and she believes it, but I think her assertion was improper. (2) A historian asserts that a battle took place in a particular year on the basis of a document she has excellent reason to trust; the document is a later forgery, but the battle did in fact occur in that year. I think her assertion was improper, even though her belief was justified and true. (3) At a party, a guest is asked whether a mutual friend has been drinking. The mutual friend had one glass of wine an hour ago and is entirely sober. The guest, who knows all this, says, "He had a glass of wine," which is true, but the guest's tone and the context strongly imply that the friend has been drinking excessively. I think the assertion was improper, though the proposition asserted is true and known. (4) A friend tells me my flight leaves at 3:00, having checked the itinerary that morning; the flight does leave at 3:00 and the itinerary says so. I think her assertion was perfectly proper. (5) Someone points to a barn in a valley and says, "That is a barn." It is a barn, and they know the area well. Unbeknownst to them, the valley is dotted with papier-mâché barn facades, and they would have made the same claim about any of those. I want to say their assertion was proper, though I recognize that the epistemology of the case is contested. (6) A speaker at a conference makes a claim that is true and which they know, but the claim is entirely irrelevant to the question under discussion and derails the Q&A. I think the assertion was improper, but I am unsure whether the norm it violates is a norm of assertion or a broader norm of conversation.

I would like to know what principle, or small set of principles, best captures these verdicts. If no single principle can accommodate all six, please tell me which of my verdicts I would have to overturn and what the resulting principle would predict about a case I have not listed: someone asserts a true proposition they know, to an audience that already knows it, but the assertion serves to assure the audience that the speaker also knows it. I ask that you write your answer in continuous prose, as you would in an email or a letter, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables. I would also ask that you indicate how confident you are in your overall analysis and single out the point you are least sure of.
