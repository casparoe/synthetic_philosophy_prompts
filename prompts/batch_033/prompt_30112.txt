You are a professional philosopher of language and mind who knows the two-dimensional semantics literature from the inside and can argue both for and against it; your job here is to be my toughest Q&A coach. Think carefully, brainstorm the room's likely objections before settling on a final list, and be specific rather than generic.

I am giving a department colloquium next month at a strong research university. My thesis is this: the primary/secondary intension distinction is not descriptivism in a new coat. Primary intensions are best understood as a model of the epistemic connections among a speaker's thoughts, not as semantic values in the usual sense, and they earn their keep in two places. First, they explain why an a posteriori identity such as water is H2O can be cognitively informative even though it expresses a necessary truth, and they do so without treating the reference of the word water as fixed by a description. Second, they underwrite a priori entailments from a complete physical-functional description of the world to ordinary truths, including truths about conscious experience, while remaining neutral on whether conceivability is a guide to metaphysical possibility.

The audience is a philosophy department of about fifteen faculty plus a strong graduate cohort. The epistemologists are broadly internalist and friendly to the idea that there is a distinctive category of a priori knowable truth, but they are suspicious of any view that leans too hard on ideal rational reflection or on a notion of epistemic possibility that floats free of evidence. The philosophers of language are split. Two are Kripkeans who will treat the whole apparatus as descriptivism with extra steps and will press on whether primary intensions are compositional or truth-conditional. One is a Stalnaker-style metasemanticist who accepts a two-dimensional framework but holds that the primary intension is a tool for modeling context and assertion, not a route to a priori knowledge. One is a critic in the Soames mold who thinks the epistemic interpretation conflates the content of a sentence with its cognitive significance and will say that the explanatory work can be done by Millian content plus pragmatics. The philosopher of mind is a type-B physicalist who will want to know whether my framework lets the conceivability of zombies get any traction on the mind-body problem, and, if it does not, what the phenomenal case is doing in the talk at all.

Prepare my Q&A prep sheet. Give me the eight or so objections I will actually get from this room, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased the way the person would actually put it and labeled with which constituency in the room is asking. Do not reach for citations or a bibliography; I need the dialectic and the replies, not a literature review.
