The Allais paradox has been at the center of debates about the normative status of expected utility theory since the 1950s. The empirical pattern is not in dispute: when presented with the two choice problems, most people — including, famously, Savage himself when Allais first presented them to him — prefer the sure thing in the first problem but switch to the riskier gamble in the second, in violation of the independence axiom. What is in dispute is what this pattern shows. One camp holds that the independence axiom is a requirement of practical rationality, so the Allais preferences are simply a mistake — a cognitive illusion that people should correct upon reflection, as Savage did. Another camp holds that the robustness and reflectiveness of the Allais preferences show that the independence axiom is not a genuine requirement of rationality, and that a normatively adequate decision theory must accommodate them — hence the development of alternatives like regret theory, rank-dependent utility, and cumulative prospect theory. A third camp argues that the paradox is an artifact of how the problems are described and that, when the choices are framed in a way that makes their structural equivalence transparent, the violation disappears or loses its normative force.

Seventy years in, the debate looks stalled. The same experimental results are cited by both sides, the same arguments about the normative force of axioms versus intuitions are rehearsed, and nobody seems to move. I want a diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement rather than a summary of the positions. Is this a disagreement about what counts as evidence in normative theory — whether reflective intuition is data or whether axioms are to be judged by their plausibility independent of cases? Is it a disagreement about the correct description of the choice problem — about whether the two problems are genuinely equivalent under the agent's own conceptualization of the outcomes? Is it a verbal dispute about the word "rational," where one side means "consistent with axioms that seem compelling on reflection" and the other means "what a reasonable person would actually choose after careful deliberation"? Or is there a hidden empirical claim that could resolve it — for instance, about whether people who violate the axiom would, upon having the structural equivalence explained to them, revise their preferences?
