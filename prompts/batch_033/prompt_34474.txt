I'm a PhD student in environmental ethics, and i've been following this debate in wild animal welfare / EA circles about whether habitat destruction is net good or bad, and I'm getting frustrated because it seems completely stuck. The basic argument on one side (call them the pessimists) is: most wild animals live short, brutal lives—starving, diseased, eaten alive. If you destroy a habitat, you prevent a bunch of those lives from happening, which is a net reduction in suffering. The other side says: hold on, you don't actually know the net welfare of wild animals, ecosystems are complex, and destroying habitat could concentrate suffering in the survivors or eliminate the few animals that do have positive lives. Also, there's something hubristic about thinking you can improve the world by destroying ecosystems.

But here's the thing: both sides seem to agree on the factual claims we actually have (which are basically none), they agree suffering is bad, and yet nobody moves. After five years of this, the same arguments just get swapped around. So I want a diagnosis: what kind of disagreement is this, actually?

Is it a hidden empirical question about the sign of average wild animal welfare that we just can't measure yet? Is it a disagreement in population ethics—total vs. average, or about whether nonexistence is comparable to a bad life? Is it about the burden of proof when you're radically uncertain? Or is it really just a clash of temperaments—optimism vs. pessimism about nature—that's been dressed up in philosophical clothes?

And given your diagnosis, what would actually move it? A better empirical study? A conceptual breakthrough? Or is this the kind of disagreement where no amount of evidence will resolve it because the two sides are just answering different questions under one heading?
