ok so we read about stoicism in my philosophy club and the whole "dichotomy of control" thing from Epictetus — some stuff is up to us, some isn't, and we're supposed to only care about the stuff that's up to us. i've been trying to apply it to actual situations and my gut keeps giving me different answers. here's where i'm at:

- bombing a test i studied hard for: the grade isn't up to me, the studying was. feels right to say i should only be bothered if i slacked off, not if the test was unfair. this one fits.

- a friend spreads a rumor about me: i can't control what they say, only how i react. but it feels wrong to say i shouldn't be upset — like, they betrayed me, that matters.

- my parents getting divorced: totally not up to me. but if someone told me "don't be disturbed, it's outside your control" i'd want to punch them. this case makes the whole idea feel heartless.

- trying out for the school play: i can control my preparation but not whether i get cast. the line between what's "up to me" and what isn't is blurry here in a way the dichotomy doesn't seem to handle — like, my audition performance is partly me and partly how the director sees it.

- getting randomly shoved in the hallway: not up to me. but my anger just happens, i don't choose it. epictetus would say my judgment is up to me, but honestly the anger shows up before i even think.

- my little brother being born with a disability: obviously not up to me. but caring about it feels not just natural but good? like it would be worse if i didn't care.

so some cases fit the dichotomy perfectly, some feel like they're stretching it, and some make it seem actively wrong. is there a better principle that gets all or most of these verdicts? or do i need to give up on some of my reactions? also what would the fixed-up principle say about a case i haven't listed — like, feeling anxious about climate change?
