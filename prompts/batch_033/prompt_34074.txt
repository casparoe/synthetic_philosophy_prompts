Here are my gut reactions on what counts as a species, and I'm trying to figure out if there's a coherent principle behind them — or if I have to give some up.

California Ensatina salamanders: the coastal form and the inland form can't interbreed where they meet, but they're connected by a chain of populations around the Central Valley, each of which can interbreed with its neighbors. I want to say the endpoints are different species. But I also want to say each adjacent pair is the same species. That can't both be true if "same species" is transitive, so something's gotta give.

Lions and tigers: different species, even though they produce ligers. No hesitation.

Dogs and wolves: I'm wobblier. They interbreed freely, fertile offspring, but they look and act different and have been separate for thousands of years. I lean toward "different species" but it feels shakier than the lion/tiger case.

E. coli and Salmonella: different species, obviously. They're bacteria, so the whole interbreeding criterion doesn't even apply. But I'm fine with that — I don't think bacteria get a free pass out of being classified.

Neanderthals and Homo sapiens: different species, even though we now know they interbred. If I'm consistent with the lion/tiger case, this should be fine.

But here's the problem case: there's a continuum of fossil hominins from Australopithecus to Homo erectus to us. Where do you draw the lines? I want to say there are real species boundaries in there, but I also know the fossil record is just snapshots of a gradual process. I'm not sure what my verdict is, and that's the one I keep going back and forth on.

Is there a species concept that gets most of my verdicts right, and what does it say about the fossil continuum case? And which of my verdicts would I have to abandon to make it work? Also, please tell me what argument or evidence would change your mind about which species concept is best — where's the reasoning most likely to fail?
