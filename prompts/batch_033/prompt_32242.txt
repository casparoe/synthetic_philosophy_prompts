I've been trying to get clear on what I actually think about the St. Petersburg paradox by checking my gut on a bunch of games rather than doing theory. I don't have a principle yet, just prices. If it helps, the story I keep telling myself is something about diminishing marginal utility plus not taking astronomically tiny probabilities seriously, but a couple of the verdicts below seem to fight that story, and I want to know which ones I should give up.

Here are the games, and the most I'd pay to play each one (single play unless I say otherwise):

1. The classic game: a fair coin is flipped until it lands heads, and I get 2^n dollars if the first heads is on flip n. I'd pay about $10, maybe $12 on a good day.
2. Same game, but the house has a bankroll cap: it pays the usual schedule only up to $1,048,576, and any longer run just pays that amount. The expected value is $21, and I'd pay about $12.
3. Fair coin, but the payoff is 10^n dollars instead of 2^n. I'd pay about $20 — more than the classic, mostly because the ordinary outcomes are better (50% chance of $10 instead of $2).
4. Fair coin, but the payoff is just n dollars. Expected value is $2, and I'd pay $2, maybe $2.50.
5. A package of 100 independent plays of the classic game, where I keep the total of all 100. I'd pay about $1,500 for the package — so $15 a play, more per play than I'd pay for a single one. That feels right but I can't quite justify it.
6. A plain lottery ticket: a 1 in 1,000 chance of $10,000, otherwise nothing. Expected value is $10, and I'd pay $15, because $10,000 would actually matter to me.
7. The classic game again, but suppose I already had $100 million in the bank. Then I'd pay about $40 for a single play.

What principle, if any, captures all of this? Please state it precisely, say what it predicts for a case I haven't listed — the classic game with a coin biased toward heads, probability 0.6 heads on each flip, payoff still 2^n — and tell me which of my verdicts it can't accomodate and which ones I should overturn.
