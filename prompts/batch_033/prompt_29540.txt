Write a dialogue between two friends, Maya and Jordan, arguing about whether the Kelly criterion reflects a genuinely different normative principle — *geometric rationality* — or whether it's just expected utility maximization with a logarithmic utility function and no deeper lesson.

They're at a bar. Maya has been reading Ole Peters on ergodicity economics and thinks it fundamentally reorients decision theory. Jordan thinks Peters is repackaging old ideas and that Samuelson basically settled this in the 70s. Neither is a strawman. Maya knows the formal arguments for expected utility theory and can anticipate Jordan's moves. Jordan understands the ergodicity point and doesn't just dismiss it.

The dialogue should have real back-and-forth — Maya should respond to Jordan's specific objections, and Jordan should engage with the strongest version of Maya's case, not dodge it. They should push each other into territory where the disagreement becomes sharp: What does it mean to "care about" the time average vs. the ensemble average? Is the normative force of Kelly tethered to the empirical claim that life is a long sequence of multiplicative gambles, and if so, what happens when you face a genuinely terminal one-shot decision? Does the Peters-style argument sneak in a preference and then pretend it derived it? And does the expected-utility defender owe an account of why ensemble averaging is the right way to aggregate over possible worlds, or is that just a stipulation?

Write this casually — they're friends talking at a bar, not giving a seminar. Feel free to include a minor typo or two.

End the dialogue with a one-sentence summary as the last line, set off as "Summary: ..." — something that captures the crux of the disagreement as it stands at the end of the conversation.
