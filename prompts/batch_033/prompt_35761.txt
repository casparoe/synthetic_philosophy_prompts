The interpretive debate over Dostoevsky's Grand Inquisitor chapter has been running for more than a century, and as far as I can tell the basic positions were all on the table by the mid-twentieth century and have barely moved since. The chapter presents Ivan Karamazov's poem in which Christ returns to sixteenth-century Seville during the Inquisition, is arrested, and is told by the Grand Inquisitor that he has no right to return because the church has corrected his work: Christ offered freedom, but human beings cannot bear freedom, and what they actually want is miracle, mystery, and authority — bread, not choice. The Inquisitor claims that the church, in taking up the three temptations Christ rejected in the wilderness, has given people the happiness they actually need at the cost of the freedom they cannot use.

The interpretive dispute I have in mind is not about the literary merits of the chapter but about what Dostoevsky himself was doing with it philosophically. One camp reads the Inquisitor's case as so powerful that Dostoevsky must have felt its force and meant it as a genuine challenge to Christian faith — a challenge the novel does not fully answer. Another camp reads the chapter as a dialectical setup: the Inquisitor's argument is meant to be refuted by the lived example of Father Zosima and by Alyosha's kiss, which echoes Christ's kiss at the end of the poem, and the refutation works if you read the novel as a whole. A third camp argues that the chapter is not really about Christianity at all but about socialism and the political temptation to replace freedom with material security, and that reading it as a theological argument misses the point. A fourth camp says the chapter is deliberately unresolved and that Dostoevsky's own position is irreducibly ambiguous — that he saw the force of both sides and refused to settle it.

These readings have been restated, refined, and defended against one another for decades, but nobody seems to change their mind. I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this really is. Is it a disagreement about textual evidence that could in principle be settled by closer reading? Is it a disagreement about Dostoevsky's authorial intentions, and if so, is that a question that biographical or historical evidence could ever resolve? Is it a disagreement about what counts as a philosophical refutation — about whether a literary character's way of life can answer a discursive argument? Or is it something else: a disagreement about what the novel is for, such that different readers are effectively asking different questions of the same text? I want the diagnosis supported by what each interpretive camp actually says about the text, and I want an honest answer about whether anything could move this debate, and if so what.
