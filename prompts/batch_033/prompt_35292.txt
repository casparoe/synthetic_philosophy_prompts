I was at a philosophy of cosmology workshop last month — I was covering it for the magazine — and I ended up in the hotel bar after the last session, overhearing two guys going at it for like an hour. They were both postdocs, I think, one in physics and one in philosophy, and they were arguing about anthropic reasoning and the "presumptuous philosopher" thought experiment. I took some notes on my phone because it was genuinely the most interesting thing I heard all week. Here's roughly how it went:

The physics guy (call him M) was defending the Self-Sampling Assumption — roughly, the idea that you should reason as if you're randomly drawn from the set of all observers in your reference class. The philosophy guy (call him N) was defending the Self-Indication Assumption — the idea that you should reason as if you're randomly drawn from all possible observers, but weighted by the number of observers each hypothesis predicts, so hypotheses that predict more observers get a boost just for that reason.

(1) M kicked things off by saying SIA leads straight to the presumptuous philosopher: if you take SIA seriously, then all else being equal, a cosmological theory that predicts a trillion trillion observers is a trillion times more likely than one that predicts a trillion, purely because it gives you more chances to exist. Which means a philosopher armed with SIA can walk into a physics department and tell the cosmologists their theory of cosmic structure is wrong because it doesn't predict enough conscious beings. That's absurd, so SIA is false.

(2) N said that's not absurd at all, it's just Bayesian updating. If theory A says you should exist with probability P and theory B says you should exist with probablity a trillion times P, then finding yourself existing is a trillion times more likely under B. That's not cheating — that's how evidence works. We don't call it "presumptuous" when a fossil forces us to revise our geology.

(3) M said the fossil case is different because the fossil is an observation we went out and gathered. But with SIA, the "observation" is just the fact of your own existence, which you already knew before you ever heard of cosmology. How can a fact you knew before you even formulated the theories discriminate between them?

(4) N said that's exactly how old evidence works in Bayesianism — you can have evidence E that you knew before you formulated hypothesis H, and yet E can still confirm H if H predicts E better than the alternatives. The fact that you exist is old evidence, sure, but it's still evidence.

(5) M pivoted: fine, but SIA has a worse problem. If you accept SIA, you have to be absolutely certain that there aren't infinite observers, because if there are, then every hypothesis that predicts a finite number of observers gets zero probability. And we don't know the universe is finite. So SIA forces you to have a very strong, unjustified cosmological opinion.

(6) N said that's a fair worry but it cuts both ways — SSA has the reference class problem, where you can't say what counts as an "observer like you" without making arbitrary choices that swing the probabilities around wildly. At least SIA's problem is about the actual world, not about how we carve up concepts.

(7) M came back: but the reference class problem is a problem for SIA too — you still need a reference class to know what counts as an observer for the weighting. And anyway, SSA at least gets the right answer in the doomsday argument: finding yourself among the earliest hundred billion humans is evidence that humanity won't last for trillions of people. SIA washes that evidence out entirely.

(8) N said that's a feature, not a bug. The doomsday argument is a probabilistic trick — it's exactly the kind of reasoning that would have told a medieval peasant that the world was about to end because most people who ever lived were alive at the time. SIA correctly blocks that.

They went back and forth a bit more after that but by then the bar was closing and I had to file my piece. I honestly couldn't tell who was winning. Can you go through their exchange move by move and say who's right about what — and who ends up in a stronger position overall?
