I've been teaching social choice theory in my political philosophy course, and I keep tripping over something with the discursive dilemma. The way List and Pettit set it up, you've got a panel of judges deciding a case on three connected propositions — say, the defendant did the act, the act was prohibited, and therefore the defendant is liable. Each judge is perfectly consistent, but when you take a majority vote on each proposition separately, the group can end up affirming the premises but denying the conclusion. So majority voting produces an inconsistent group judgment even from consistent individual judgments.

Here's what's nagging at me. Isn't this just Condorcet's paradox with a legal face-lift? In Condorcet, you've got transitive individual preferences that aggregate into intransitive group preferences via majority rule. In the discursive dilemma, you've got logically consistent individual judgments that aggregate into an inconsistent group judgment via majority rule. Structurally, it seems like the exact same phenomenon — cycling — just applied to beliefs instead of preferences. And if that's right, then the lesson is the same too: there's no such thing as a coherent "group mind," and talk of corporate or institutional beliefs is at best a useful fiction. Right? Or am I flattening something important here? I'd like a committed take — don't just give me the menu of possible views, tell me where I'm going wrong if I am.
