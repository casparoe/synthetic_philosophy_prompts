I'm a science and ideas journalist preparing a piece on the moral philosophy of pest control, and I want to think through Kafka's *The Metamorphosis* as a kind of limit case. The story is common knowledge: Gregor Samsa wakes up as a giant insect, his family recoils, they gradually treat him as vermin to be contained and, ultimately, eliminated. What interests me is what the story reveals about the ethics of insecticide and pest control more broadly — the justifications we use to kill creatures we classify as pests.

Read the novella through the lens of the ethics of pest control. I want a reading that takes both the fiction and the philosophical literature seriously, and that doesn't reduce one to an illustration of the other. Specifically: what criterion for moral considerability does the story put under pressure, and how? The Samsa family's treatment of Gregor shifts over the course of the plot — does the story suggest that their behavior becomes unethical at a particular threshold, or that it was unethical from the start, or that it never becomes unethical because Gregor truly is vermin? What would a philosopher working on the ethics of pest control say the story gets right that the standard arguments miss, and where does the story itself resist a clean philosophical reading? I'm especially interested in whether the story exposes a tension in views that ground moral considerability in cognitive capacities — since Gregor retains human cognition — and whether that tension maps onto real-world disagreements about the justifiability of insecticide use.
