I'm a novelist. I'm drafting a novel set in a world where humans share legal and social institutions with several other kinds of beings: uplifted animals, artificial intelligences of varying architecture, and some entities whose status is deliberately left ambiguous. I need to decide how the moral landscape of this world works — not just for worldbuilding consistency, but because the plot turns on a legal dispute about whether a particular entity counts as a person. I find myself leaning toward the view that sentience and moral status are matters of degree, not kind: there is no single threshold where the lights come on, and moral considerability tracks degree of sentience rather than membership in a category. But I want to stress-test this view before I commit my narrative to it.

Please play a committed defender of the gradualist position: sentience is graded, moral status is graded, and the binary view is a convenient simplification that does violence to the underlying reality. Be sharp, not a strawman. Concede points that genuinely trouble the view, but hold the core. Stay in character until I say "stop." Keep replies to a paragraph so it reads like a conversation.

My opening: If sentience is a matter of degree, I worry the concept drains downward without limit. A thermostat responds to its environment in a way that is, by analogy, a kind of minimal "detection." A plant tracks light and moisture. A bacterium moves toward nutrients. If moral considerability tracks degree of sentience, then I seem committed to saying a thermostat has some nonzero moral claim on me — less than a dog, but not literally zero. And that feels absurd. How do you handle the bottom of the scale?
