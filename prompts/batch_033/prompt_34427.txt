I'm a journalist covering the intersection of psychology and epistemology, and I recently sat in on a conversation between two researchers at a conference that I haven't been able to resolve on my own. I'd like you to walk through it and tell me who's right about what.

The argument concerned a third colleague, a climate scientist named Dr. Kapoor, who has publicly stated that she believes a particular direct-air-capture technology will be ready to deploy at scale within fifteen years. The evidence base is genuinely split: some modeling and pilot results suggest the trajectory is plausible, while other work identifies serious engineering obstacles that may not be solvable on that timeline. Kapoor acknowledges the uncertainty openly. She says she does not claim to know the technology will succeed; she claims to believe it, and she says that belief is not merely a summary of the evidence. She argues that maintaining that belief sustains the motivation of her research group, attracts funding to a field that might otherwise be abandoned, and makes the favorable outcome more likely by keeping talented people working on the problem. She is fully transparent about the state of the evidence in her publications and talks; she just holds that belief and evidence can serve different functions.

One researcher — call her A — argued that Kapoor is violating a basic epistemic norm. Her opening move was this: we have an obligation to proportion belief to the evidence, and believing beyond what the evidence warrants is a kind of intellectual dishonesty regardless of the practical benefits. She added that if Kapoor wouldn't accept the same reasoning from a researcher on the other side of the debate — someone who believes the technology will fail because that belief galvanizes work on alternatives — then Kapoor is making a special exception for her own cause, which is epistemically arbitrary.

The other researcher, B, pushed back. He said that A's principle treats belief as though it has only one function — tracking truth — when in fact belief also serves a practical function by guiding action. When the evidence is genuinely inconclusive and yet the question is forced and momentous, he argued, it can be legitimate to let the stakes influence what you believe. He said this is not a license for wishful thinking; it is an acknowledgment that some questions cannot be settled by evidence alone within the timeframe that action demands, and that in such cases suspending judgment is itself a practical choice with consequences just as real as believing.

A replied that this is a recipe for epistemic disaster. Once you permit belief to be guided by anything other than evidence, she said, you have opened the door to motivated reasoning everywhere. The history of science is partly a history of researchers believing in their own theories for practical and emotional reasons, and that has slowed progress at least as often as it has enabled it. She said the principle B is proposing would license believing anything you desperately need to believe — a spouse's fidelity, a political ideology, a religious doctrine — and the history of human misery is full of sincere beliefs formed on exactly those grounds.

B answered that A's position, if taken seriously, would make it impossible to commit to any research program at all, since the evidence for a new idea is always insufficient at the start. He said the demand to proportion belief strictly to evidence is a regulative ideal suited to settled science, where the evidence is in and the practical stakes of belief are low, but it is not a workable rule for science in the making, where the evidence is still accumulating and the decision to pursue one line of inquiry rather than another cannot be deferred.

I'd like you to adjudicate this exchange. Identify the distinct argumentative moves — I count at least four, possibly more depending on how you individuate them — and assess each one for soundness. Where a move is partly right and partly wrong, say so precisely. Then give an overall assessment: which position is closer to being correct, and what, if anything, are both parties failing to see?

Finally, on its own line at the very end of your response, summarize your overall verdict in a single sentence beginning with "Verdict:".
