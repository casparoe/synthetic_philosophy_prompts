I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I've been trying to get a handle on the frame problem because it surfaces in debates about what makes AI hard. One move I see repeatedly is the claim that the frame problem isn't really a narrow technical issue about representing action effects in a logical formalism — it's fundamentally the problem of relevance. The analogy, as I've seen it drawn, goes like this: an AI system trying to determine what changes and what stays the same after an action faces the same underlying difficulty as a person who knows, without explicit reasoning, that opening a door doesn't alter the color of the walls or the distance to the moon. In both cases, the system has to pick out, from an indefinitely large set of background facts, the ones that bear on the current situation and ignore the rest. The analogy is then used to argue that the frame problem can't be solved by better knowledge representation or more efficient algorithms — it's a deep philosophical problem about relevance itself, and any agent, human or artificial, has to confront it.

I want an audit of this analogy. What exactly maps onto what? What corresponds to the frame axioms in the human case? What corresponds to common sense or background knowledge in the AI case? Where does the mapping break — and which disanalogies actually matter for the conclusion being drawn? Once the disanalogies are on the table, does the conclusion survive (that the frame problem is a fundamental obstacle rather than a technical one), or was the analogy carrying more weight than it can bear?
