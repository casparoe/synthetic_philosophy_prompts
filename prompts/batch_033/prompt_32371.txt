The debate over Boltzmann brains in cosmology and the foundations of statistical mechanics has been running for more than two decades now, and I cannot tell whether it is actually making progress or just getting more elaborate. The basic setup, as I understand it: in certain cosmological models — particularly those with eternal inflation or an extended de Sitter phase — the universe spends most of its history in a high-entropy, near-equilibrium state. In such a state, statistical fluctuations will occasionally produce all sorts of low-entropy configurations, including, if you wait long enough, a disembodied brain that briefly flickers into existence with a full set of illusory memories, sensations, and beliefs about inhabiting a coherent external world. That is a Boltzmann brain. The worry is that if our best cosmological theories imply that Boltzmann brains vastly outnumber ordinary observers like us, then we should conclude that we ourselves are far more likely to be Boltzmann brains — which is absurd, because if we were Boltzmann brains, we would have no reason to trust the very reasoning that led us to that conclusion. So the argument goes: any cosmological model that predicts Boltzmann brain dominance is self-undermining and should be rejected on epistemic grounds.

Here is where the debate seems to me to stall. One camp treats the Boltzmann brain problem as a straightforward reductio ad absurdum: a theory that predicts we are almost certainly Boltzmann brains is empirically ruled out by the fact that we take ourselves to be ordinary observers with reliable cognitive faculties. The other camp — call them the skeptical camp — argues that this reasoning is too quick. To say that Boltzmann brains "vastly outnumber" ordinary observers, you need a measure over the set of all observers, and the measure problem in cosmology is notoriously unresolved. Different measures give different answers about which kinds of observers are typical. Without an agreed-upon measure, you cannot even state the claim that Boltzmann brains dominate, let alone treat it as a decisive objection. And then there is a third position, or maybe a refinement of the skeptical one, that says the whole framing is confused: the very idea of "typicality" across an infinite or near-infinite ensemble may not be well-defined in the way the reductio camp needs it to be.

The result is that the debate seems to loop: the reductio camp says "this is clearly crazy and no serious theory can survive it," the skeptical camp says "but your reasoning presupposes a solution to the measure problem that nobody has," the reductio camp replies that the measure problem is a separate issue and that any reasonable measure will yield Boltzmann brain dominance in the offending models, and then everyone goes back to arguing about the details of particular inflationary scenarios, which changes the production rates but does not address the underlying conceptual impasse. Meanwhile, the whole thing feels increasingly scholastic: smart people with the same physics keep disagreeing about what the disagreement is even about.

What I want is a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement itself, not a verdict on who is right. Specifically: is this a factual disagreement about the physics (how many Boltzmann brains are actually produced in various models, and whether those models are independently viable), is it a formal disagreement about the measure problem (which is unsolved but in principle solvable), is it a disagreement about what counts as a legitimate epistemic principle (can we reject a theory because it makes our own observations infinitely atypical, or does that kind of anthropic reasoning require a justification that nobody has provided), or is it a deeper disagreement about whether anthropic reasoning is coherent at all — and if it is not, whether the Boltzmann brain argument is really just a vivid way of exposing that incoherence? And given whichever diagnosis you defend, tell me what would actually count as progress: a solution to the measure problem, a new empirical constraint, a sharper conceptual distinction, or an honest acknowledgment that the question is ill-posed and the debate should be dissolved rather than resolved.
