So I'm working on this novel — it's about a handful of people orbiting the same terrible idea over a decade, and I've gotten weirdly obsessed with how ideas actually move through populations, mutate, catch fire, go dormant. The thing is, every time I bring this up with friends who are into philosophy or evo-bio, someone eventually says "oh, so you're writing about memes," and then they trot out the whole Dawkins thing: memes are the cultural analogue of genes, they replicate, they vary, they get selected, culture is just Darwinian processes operating on a different substrate. And I get the appeal — it's tidy, it's generative, it gives you a vocabulary. But I'm a novelist, not a biologist, and I keep suspecting the analogy is doing a lot of work under the hood that I'm not tracking.

So here's what I want: walk me through the meme-as-gene analogy properly. Give me the correspondence table — what's the phenotype? what's the selection environment? what plays the role of drift, of recombination, of epistasis? — and then tell me where the mapping breaks. I'm especially interested in the disanalogies that matter for the claim the analogy is usually wheeled out to support: that culture evolves by something like natural selection, that we can study it with the same conceptual toolkit, and that this explains why some ideas spread and others don't. Which of the breaks are just "analogies are imperfect, move on" and which ones actually threaten the Darwinian picture of culture? And if the analogy collapses under its own weight at some point, is there a better one — or is the whole project of borrowing evolutionary biology to explain culture just a category mistake dressed up in clever vocabulary?
