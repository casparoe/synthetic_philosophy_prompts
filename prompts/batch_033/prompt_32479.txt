I cover the science-and-ideas beat for a general-audience magazine, so I end up at a lot of panels where people use Big Quotes to settle arguments. Last week it was a panel on free will and neuroscience, and one of the panelists — a physicist, actually, not the neuroscientist — kept reaching for Einstein's "God does not play dice" every time someone brought up quantum indeterminacy as a possible opening for free will. His line was that randomness at the quantum level can't be what grounds human freedom, because randomness is just as incompatible with responsibility as determinism is, and Einstein supposedly saw that coming. Fair enough, but the way he used the quote it was doing about four different jobs at once: sometimes it sounded like a point about the completeness of quantum mechanics, sometimes like a point about what God must be like, sometimes like a flat metaphysical claim that the universe is deterministic underneath. I've heard the line all my life and realized I've never actually sorted out what it means, or what it *could* mean.

So: can you lay out the different precise claims "God does not play dice" might be expressing, from the narrow technical readings to the broad theological and metaphysical ones? For each reading I'd want to know (a) whether it's plausible or just wrong, (b) whether it's something Einstein actually would have signed onto, and (c) whether it does any real work toward the panelist's conclusion that quantum indeterminacy can't underwrite free will. Give me a menu, not a single exegisis — I want to see which readings are doing real work and which are just rhetoric. And finish with a one-sentence bottom line, formatted as \answer{...}.
