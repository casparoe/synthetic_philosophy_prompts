I've been in an epistemology reading group for a couple years now, and every time we get to the closure debate it plays out exactly the same way. The anti-closure side starts from Nozick's tracking idea: knowledge requires that your belief is sensitive to the truth — roughly, if p had been false, you wouldn't have believed p. That immediately breaks closure under known entailment. You can know you have hands, because in the nearby worlds where you're handless (you lost them in an accident, say) you wouldn't still believe you had hands; but you can't know you're not a handless brain in a vat, because in the world where you are one, you'd still believe you weren't. So the tracking theorist is stuck with the infamous conjunction — I know I have hands, but I don't know I'm not a handless brain in a vat — and they mostly bite the bullet and say the bullet tastes better than the skeptical alternative. The closure side says that conjunction is just absurd, that any view licensing it has stopped talking about knowledge as the rest of us use the word, and that the right response to skepticism has to be found without giving up closure. Then the safety camp shows up claiming to keep the modal spirit of tracking — your belief couldn't easily have been false — while restoring closure, and the argument moves to weather safety really does preserve closure, whether it handles lottery cases and necessary truths, and whether it's secretly just sensitivity in a hat.

I've started to suspect the reason nobody moves is that the two camps are using the word 'knowledge' to do different jobs. One side treats knowledge as a success term for a particular belief: did that belief's connection to the truth hold up under the relevant counterfactual? The other side treats knowledge as something you can reason with: if you know p, and you know p entails q, then q is yours, end of story. If that's right, the impasse might be less about the facts and more about which job the concept has to do first — and maybe the concept just isn't built to do both at once.

I want a diagnosis, not a verdict. Is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one about what 'knowledge' means, or two research programs answering different questions under one heading? Are there hidden formal or empirical questions doing the real work — for instance, whether there's a single modal condition that both preserves closure and doesn't over-ascribe knowledge in lottery-style cases — such that settling one of them would actually move somebody? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a theorem, a distinction, a new kind of case, or just a reframing of the question?
