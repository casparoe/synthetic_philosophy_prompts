Having spent my career in the physical sciences, I am relatively new to philosophy, and I have been asked to moderate a recorded conversation with a philosopher who defends a looping-effects account of psychiatric classification, in the tradition of Ian Hacking. I understand the broad shape of the position: classifying people can change the people classified, and those changes feed back into the classifications themselves, so that the human kinds studied by psychiatry are not fixed targets in the way that many of the kinds I worked with as a scientist are. What I lack is a sense of where the view is under the most serious pressure, and where its defenders have had to make interesting concessions or refinements.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond asking the guest to restate the view? I would like at least one question on each of the following, and for each a follow-up keyed to the reply the guest is most likely to give:

- whether the distinction between interactive kinds and indifferent kinds is sharp or only a matter of degree, and whether there are borderline cases that cause trouble for the account;
- whether looping effects threaten the stability that psychiatric research presupposes, for instance in longitudinal studies, randomized controlled trials, or the search for biological markers;
- what looping effects imply for the validity and reliability of diagnostic categories in contemporary psychiatric manuals;
- whether the account is best read as anti-realist or constructivist about mental disorder, or whether it can be combined with a modest realism;
- whether looping effects also arise in somatic medicine, and what the answer does to the claim that psychiatry is distinctive in this respect;
- how norms and values enter into classification, including whether a classification can become self-fulfilling or self-defeating in a way that undercuts its clinical usefulness.

For each cluster, please anticipate the defender's likely move — for example, the reply that looping effects are not a threat to research but a phenomenon to be studied, or that psychiatric kinds can be real without being natural kinds — and write the follow-up so that it presses the next step rather than allowing the reply to close the issue. I would also like a final question about what kind of evidence, if any, could count against the looping-effects account rather than merely complicating its application.
