I lead a technical AI safety team, and we are split between two positions on what kind of guarantee an alignment-relevant property should have before it can count as a safety result.

The worst-case camp holds that a property is a safety result only if it is stated over an explicitly chosen adversary or threat model and holds for all inputs in that set; average-case or distributional statements are not safety results, because deployment of powerful systems includes adversarial optimization (including by the system itself and by other actors), and because the relevant failures are asymmetric enough that averaging over them is exactly the wrong operation. The average-case camp holds that worst-case guarantees for general-purpose learned systems are either unattainable, vacuous, or silently conditional on a threat model the designer merely stipulated; the only honest and useful target is a distributional or expected-risk statement, with worst-case reasoning kept as a heuristic for choosing and stress-testing the distribution.

Draft the document that a good-faith adversarial collaboration between these two camps should produce. Write it as follows.

1. Agreed claims. A numbered list of substantive claims both camps can sign, including claims about the inadequacy of IID validation, about distribution shift, about asymmetric costs, about the limits of guarantees against unbounded adversaries, and about what a guarantee must specify in order to be checkable at all. Keep these non-vacuous; avoid wording that only one side would sign.

2. Disagreement table. For each residual disagreement, give (a) the precise claim the worst-case camp affirms and the average-case camp denies, phrased so the affirming side would accept the wording; (b) the precise claim the average-case camp affirms and the worst-case camp denies, phrased likewise; and (c) your judgment of whether the disagreement is substantive or partly verbal, with reasons.

3. Cruxes. Identify the sub-questions such that a settled answer would move at least one camp. For each crux, specify a concrete case, experiment, or argument that both camps would accept as bearing on it, and record in advance what each camp predicts that test would show. Candidate cruxes include, but are not limited to: whether the input distribution for a capable general system is better modeled as a distribution one can specify or as an adversary one must bound; whether the threat model in a worst-case guarantee is any less stipulated than the distribution in an average-case guarantee; whether asymmetric catastrophic costs can be absorbed into an expected-utility framing or instead force a minimax criterion; and whether worst-case methods that work for narrow input spaces (adversarial examples, verified small programs) can scale to general reasoning. Do not simply list these; draft them precisely and commit to the formulations.

4. Verbal dispute report. State which parts of the debate you believe are disagreements about words or about the honorific use of "safety," and which parts are substantive disagreements about how to reason under uncertainty about a system that may be optimizing against you. Commit to the diagnosis.

Assume the document will be read by strong ML engineers who have not picked a side. Use the technical vocabulary of statistical learning theory, robust optimization, formal verification, and decision theory freely, but define any term whose meaning the two camps might themselves contest.

Be committed throughout. Do not lay out options and leave them to me: make the drafting choices, phrase each camp in its strongest acceptable wording, and defend the contested formulations. Where you think one camp is smuggling an assumption into a shared term such as "guarantee," "distribution," or "adversary," say so outright.
