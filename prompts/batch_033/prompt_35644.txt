I have been working through Amartya Sen's 'The Impossibility of a Paretian Liberal' and, while I can follow the formal proof without difficulty, I find myself uncertain about what the result actually establishes concerning the concept of a right. I would be grateful for a careful conceptual unpacking.

The standard presentation runs as follows: if we require both the Pareto principle (if everyone prefers x to y, society should prefer x to y) and a condition of minimal liberalism (each individual is to be decisive over at least one pair of social states falling within their private sphere), we can construct a preference profile that yields a cyclical social ordering, so that no best element exists. But I want to understand what this tells us about rights, as opposed to what it tells us about the machinery of social choice.

In particular, I would like an explanation that addresses the following three questions:

(1) Why should we think of a right as a decisive power over a pair of social alternatives, rather than as a constraint on what others may do, or as a side-constraint that removes certain options from the feasible set before social evaluation begins? If rights are constraints rather than elements incorporated into the social welfare function alongside preferences, does the paradox dissolve, or does it reappear in a different guise?

(2) Is the real lesson that the Pareto principle and liberalism conflict only if we insist on a social welfare function that yields a complete and transitive ordering — and that giving up that insistence is less costly than it first appears? Or is the demand for acyclic (rather than fully transitive) social preferences sufficient to preserve the tension?

(3) Some have argued that the paradox reveals a deep tension within liberalism itself, not merely a technical incompatibility between two formal conditions. Is that claim warranted, or does it rest on reading more into the formal result than it can bear?

I am less interested in a survey of the technical fixes (rights as constraints, conditional Pareto, and so on) than in getting clear on what the paradox conceptually establishes — and what it does not. Please write your answer in British English.
