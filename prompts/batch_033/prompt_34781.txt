I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I'm working on a novel set in London in the early 1960s. One of the central scenes is a long argument between two old friends — a high court judge and a law professor — about whether the law should punish private immorality, specifically a case involving two consenting adult men. I want the scene to be a real intellectual fight, not a debate-club exercise where one side lands all the points. The judge holds something like Lord Devlin's position: a society's shared morality is a kind of glue, and if the law stops enforcing it the whole thing comes apart, so the law may punish conduct that ordinary people find genuinely intolerable, disgusting, and worthy of indignation. The professor holds something like H.L.A. Hart's position: the law isn't there to enforce morality as such, only to prevent harm to others, and the 'disintegration thesis' is an empirical claim nobody has actually shown.

Could you write me the dialogue? I want real back-and-forth, where each of them responds to the other's actual points rather than giving speeches. Get into the weeds: the Wolfenden Report's line that private morality isn't the law's business, Devlin's jury-box test and whether it's just majoritarian prejudice dressed up, Hart's distinction between positive and critical morality, the question of whether the judge can produce any actual evidence that unpunished private vice dissolves a society, and the professor's problem of explaining what counts as 'harm' without smugling in a moral theory of his own. Neither should be a strawman; both should be sharp, and I want the reader to feel the pull of both positions, not to know who won. It would also help if each of them concedes at least one real point. I keep finding my drafts turn into one character lecturing and the other waiting for thier turn, so push them to actually tangle with each other's examples. Don't worry about period-accurate citations or footnotes — I just need the arguments to feel alive and precise.
