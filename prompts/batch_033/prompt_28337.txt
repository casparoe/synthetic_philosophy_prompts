I have been trying to understand the doctrine of double effect, not as an abstract principle but as it actually shows up in practice. Across several otherwise quite different areas of practice, people draw a sharp line between harm one brings about as a means or an aim and harm one brings about as a side effect, even when the harm is equally certain in both cases. A physician who gives a dying patient escalating doses of morphine to relieve pain is generally treated as having provided palliative care, not as having killed the patient, even though she knows the morphine will depress breathing and may bring death a few hours sooner; a physician who gives a dose with the aim of ending the patient's life is treated as a killer, even if the dose is smaller and the physiological mechanism identical. Military manuals and the law of armed conflict draw a parallel distinction between bombing a munitions plant in order to destroy a military target, foreseeing that civilian workers will die, and bombing the same plant in order to kill or terrorize those workers, where the same number of deaths is expected either way. A moral-theological tradition makes the distinction in an especially vivid form: it has held that removing a pregnant woman's cancerous uterus is permissible even though the fetus will die as a side effect of the surgery, while performing a craniotomy to save the mother's life is impermissible because the fetus's death is the very means by which the mother is saved.

I would like to know what theory of human action and intention this whole pattern presupposes. Reconstruct the implicit view: what it means, exactly, for a bad effect to be intended rather than merely foreseen; what work the distinction between an effect's being a means and its being a side effect is supposed to be doing; and whether that distinction can be drawn in a principled way or is only a device for marking moral conclusions already reached on other grounds. Then consider an apparent inconsistency in the surrounding practices. Some of the same legal systems that treat the first physician as having done nothing criminal instruct juries that a person may be taken to intend the natural and probable consequences of his acts, and in tort law foreseeability alone is frequently enough for liability. Does the legal distinction in the medical and military cases presuppose one theory of intention while the criminal and tort rules presuppose another, or can the two be reconciled?

Finally, say where the practice is genuinely underdetermined — which rival theories of intention and responsibility would license the same verdicts — and tell me what would have to change in at least one of the practices I described if the underlying view were replaced by a purely causal or probabilistic account, on which responsibility tracks the harms one foreseeably brings about rather than the harms one intends.
