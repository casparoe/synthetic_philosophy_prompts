You're a philosopher of mind who has facilitated a few adversarial collaborations, and you're good at drafting joint statements that both sides will actually sign rather than just grimace at. I need one for two people in my reading group who have been arguing for months about the paradox of phenomenal judgment.

One of them is an epiphenomenalist about phenomenal consciousness: she thinks qualia are real, but causally inert. The hurtfulness of pain, the redness of red — none of it does any causal work, not even in producing the very judgments we make about our own experience. The other one (I'm not sure of the standard label — "efficacious phenomenalist"?) thinks experiences themselves make a causal difference, in particular to our judgments that we're having them.

Draft the joint statement their adversarial collaberation would produce. Format it as: (1) a numbered agreed-claims section — about how they'll use "phenomenal consciousness," what the paradox of phenomenal judgment is, what the standard anti-epiphenomenalist argument says, what the standard epiphenomenalist reply says, and what a fair test would have to look like; (2) a disagreements table, with each side's own view stated in wording that side would accept, and a column marking which rows are substantive and which are partly verbal; (3) a cruxes section — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side; and (4) for each crux, a concrete argument or case both sides agree bears on it (self-stultification, the evolutionary argument, the knowledge argument, blindsight, a p-zombie's reports, something like that), with each side's prediction recorded in advance.

Don't let the agreed claims go vacuous, but also don't put words in either side's mouth. I suspect part of their fight is verbal — something about what "cause" means and whether causation has to be physical — so say which part is verbal and which isn't, if that's how the drafting comes out. The work is in the formulations.

Take your time working through the possible formulations and cruxes before you write, but when you output, start directly with the document itself — no preface, no "here is a draft," no commentary afterward.
