I keep coming back to Pascal's fragments on justice and force: his claim that a just claim with no force behind it cannot make itself obeyed, that force with no justice behind it is mere tyranny, and that, being unable to give force to the just, societies instead make whatever is strong count as just. I want to run that framework against a problem that postdates him by more than three centuries: the doctrine of humanitarian military intervention, and in particular the responsibility-to-protect norm, under which sovereignty is conditional on a state's willingness to shield its own population from mass atrocity and outside powers may use force when a state fails that duty.

If Pascal were alive today, what would his position be on humanitarian intervention? Would he see it as the rare case in which justice and force are finally joined in the right direction, or as one more instance of force legitimating itself after the fact, now in the vocabulary of universal rights? I'm especially interested in whether his skepticism that there is a justice all peoples recognize — his sense that what passes for justice is mostly custom and geography — would make him suspicious of a self-appointed international community claiming a universal standard while enforcing it selectively. Part of me suspects the label humanitarian intervention does more legitimating work than descriptive work, and I want to know whether his framework cuts for or against that suspicion.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
