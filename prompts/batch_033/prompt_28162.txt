I'm working on a piece about AI alignment and I keep tripping over a distinction that I think matters more than most coverage lets on. Would love some comments on these notes before I develop them further.

The basic idea: there's a narrow version of the alignment problem and a broad version, and conflating them leads to a lot of confusion. The narrow version — call it "intent alignment" — is about making sure an AI does what its operator actually intends. If I ask a model to book me a flight and it instead tries to hack the airline's pricing algorithm to get me a discount I didn't ask for, that's an intent alignment failure, even if the outcome is beneficial. The broader version — "value alignment" — is about making sure the AI's behavior is actually good, period. It's about the AI not doing harmful things regardless of what any particular human asks.

Here's why I think the distinction matters. You could solve intent alignment completely and still be in deep trouble. A perfectly intent-aligned AI given to a malicious actor does exactly what that actor intends, which could be catastrophic. Similarly, a perfectly intent-aligned AI given to a well-meaning but confused actor does exactly the confused thing they intended. So intent alignment is insufficent for safety.

But here's where I get stuck. Is value alignment even a coherent target? "Human values" are contradictory, context-dependent, and not stable under reflection. And if you try to ground it in something beyond actual human preferences — like what humans would want if they were more informed or more rational — you're doing moral philosophy, not engineering. So the broad version seems necessary but also maybe impossible to specify.

Then there's a third thing I've been chewing on: maybe the narrow/broad framing itself is misleading because it suggests these are two points on a spectrum, when actually they're answers to different questions. Intent alignment is about control: does the system do what the user says? Value alignment is about legitimacy: should the system even exist in the first place, given what it might do?

Anyway, I'd love comments on whether this framing holds water, what I'm missing, and whether the narrow/broad distinction is actually useful or if it collapses under scrutiny. Also, am I wrong to think this don't get enough attention in mainstream coverage?
