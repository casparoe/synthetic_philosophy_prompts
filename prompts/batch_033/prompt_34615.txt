Two colleagues in my department got into a long argument at a reception last week, and I have been turning it over ever since. The dispute was about whether a person can ever rationally hold a belief while also believing that their own cognitive processes are, in that very instance, unreliable.

The case they kept returning to was this: A mathematician works through a complicated proof late at night, checks it several times, and becomes convinced it is correct. The next morning she learns that, without her knowledge, she had been given a drug the previous evening that significantly impairs mathematical reasoning for several hours — right when she was working on the proof. She has no independent way to verify the proof right now (the manuscript is locked in her office, and she is traveling). She still feels the proof is correct, and when she mentally replays the steps, they still seem valid to her. But she also knows that the drug makes people confidently endorse faulty reasoning without being able to detect the faults.

The question: Is it rationally permissible for her to continue believing the proof is correct?

Here is roughly how the exchange went:

(1) Abrams said no: she has undefeated higher-order evidence that her reasoning is unreliable, so she should suspend judgment. The fact that the steps still seem right to her is exactly what you would expect if the drug were working as described — it would make faulty steps seem right. So her seeming-based evidence is neutralized.

(2) Chen pushed back: if she cannot actually identify any error in the reasoning, and the steps genuinely appear valid to her on careful reflection, then she is responding to her first-order evidence. Withholding belief from something that seems true to you, solely because of a statistical fact about people in your situation, amounts to epistemic self-sabotage. You would be failing to believe what your evidence supports.

(3) Abrams replied that this conflates two levels. The first-order evidence is what the proof steps actually say; the higher-order evidence is about her capacity to evaluate those steps. If the higher-order evidence indicates her evaluation is likely corrupted, it is not self-sabotage to defer to that — it is recognizing that your seeming is an unreliable instrument. Otherwise, you would have to say that a drunk person who insists his reasoning is flawless should trust his own judgment too.

(4) Chen answered that the drunk-person case is different because the drunk person can typically be made to see his errors once sober. The mathematician, by contrast, is reflecting carefully now, knows about the drug, and still cannot find a flaw. At some point, respecting your own epistemic agency requires trusting the deliverances of your own careful reflection, even when you have statistical evidence that people in your position often err. Otherwise, Chen said, you collapse into a regress: why trust your higher-order reasoning about the drug's effects any more than your first-order reasoning about the proof?

(5) Abrams came back: the asymmetry is that the higher-order evidence does not rely on the compromised faculty. The mathematician's reasoning about the drug's effects and about what she should believe is general statistical reasoning, not the specialized mathematical reasoning the drug impairs. So there is no symmetry, and no regress.

(6) Chen was not satisfied: the drug impairs mathematical reasoning, but the judgment that "this particular step follows from that one" is itself a mathematical judgment. You cannot quarantine the higher-order reasoning cleanly. If the drug makes you overconfident about entailments, it might also make you overconfident about what counts as "general statistical reasoning" versus "specialized mathematical reasoning." The distinction Abrams needs is itself subject to the very impairment it is meant to manage.

They went back and forth a while longer, but that is the core of it. I would like you to adjudicate this move by move: for each of the six moves above, say whether it is clearly correct, clearly mistaken, or somewhere in between, and briefly justify each rating. Then give an overall assessment: who has the stronger position on this particular case, and what — if anything — are both parties missing?
