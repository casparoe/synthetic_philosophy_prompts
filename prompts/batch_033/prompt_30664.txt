I cover science and ideas for a living, and I have come to realize that my instincts about when to defer to another person's judgment are not something I can state as a rule. Before I write another story that turns on a dispute among experts, I want to know what principle, if anything, I am actually applying.

Here are my verdicts on cases, reported honestly.

Case 1. Two climate scientists I regard as equally able, working from the same published data, give sharply different ranges for the equilibrium climate sensitivity — how much warming a doubling of carbon dioxide would produce. I cannot read their models. I want to report a wide range that roughly splits the difference between them, and this feels like the right response.

Case 2. A friend and I disagree about how to split a restaurant bill. I have just done the arithmetic twice and checked it against the receipt. I feel no pressure to move toward her number, though I will recheck once as a courtesy. Splitting the difference would be absurd.

Case 3. Two political scientists, both well published, disagree about whether a particular voting reform increased turnout. I am not a social scientist, but I have read both papers, and one's identification strategy strikes me as plainly more credible than the other's. I feel entitled to rely on that study more heavily, even though its author is no more eminent than the other.

Case 4. A colleague I consider my intellectual equal and I disagree about whether a novel we both read recently is good. I feel no inclination to moderate my judgment. It would be strange to change an aesthetic view merely because she disagrees.

Case 5. A fellow reporter whose instincts I respect and I watch the same interview of a source. I think the source is lying; she thinks he is telling the truth. We are equally good at this kind of thing. I keep my view, though I cannot articulate what it rests on.

Case 6. A friend who is roughly as intelligent and well informed as I am disagrees with me about a moral question — whether a particular immigration policy is just. I feel no pressure to conciliate, but I also cannot say why this case differs from Case 1, where I do conciliate. That asymmetry is the one that bothers me most.

I know the literature divides roughly between conciliationists, who say one should move substantially toward the view of an epistemic peer, and steadfasters, who say one may largely retain one's view. My verdicts do not place me cleanly in either camp, and I would rather have a principle than a label.

Is there a single principle — a rule about when, and how much, to revise in the face of disagreement — that captures all six verdicts, or nearly all? If no clean principle gets all of them, tell me which verdicts it forces me to give up and why those are the right ones to abandon. Then apply the principle you arrive at to a case I have not listed: two equally capable film critics disagree about whether a movie is worth seeing, and I have to decide whether to go tonight.
