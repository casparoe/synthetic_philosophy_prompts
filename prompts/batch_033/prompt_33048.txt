You're a professional epistemologist who works on the epistemology of disagreement, and you've been asked to help two people who have spent the past year arguing past eachother — a committed conciliationist and a committed steadfast person (a "steadfaster," if you like). They've agreed to do a proper adversarial collaboration, and I want you to draft the joint statement they could both sign at the end of it. Don't summarise the debate; produce the document itself.

Think carefully about the drafting. Start by brainstorming the possible ways of stating the disagreement, and for each candidate formulation say why it would or wouldn't be acceptable to one side — then pick the best. The statement should have three seperate sections.

First, a numbered list of claims both sides can sign. These should be non-vacuous: what counts as a peer, which cases are central, what the phenomenon to be explained is (for example, that learning a peer disagrees often rationally lowers confidence), and what both sides agree is off the table or settled. Don't pad this with truisms.

Second, the precise residual disagreement. This is the hard bit. Formulate each position so its own proponent would accept the wording, and avoid slanted or vacuous formulations. In particular, sort out whether the real disagreement concerns the independence principle, how much weight higher-order evidence can have, whether conciliationism is self-undermining when applied to itself, the uniqueness thesis, or something more basic. If part of the dispute is verbal, say which part and why. Also address what each side thinks about a case where the other party is someone you reasonably regard as your epistemic superior or inferior, not just a peer, and whether the view is meant to cover full belief, credences, or both.

Third, a crux table. For each genuine crux, give a concrete case or argument both sides agree bears on it, and write down in advance what each side predicts the case shows. Try to go beyond the familiar restaurant-check-style examples: use cases about disagreement over the disagreement itself, cases where the disputed proposition concerns your own reliability, and cases where one party's evidence includes the fact that the other is conciliatory.

Constraints: no bibliography, no citations, no reading list — the document should stand on its own arguments. Write in British English throughout (analyse, not analyze; organise, not organize). And write the statement itself, not a commentary on how you wrote it.
