I've been teaching the Death in Damascus puzzle in my grad seminar and I keep thinking there's a genuinely good piece of fiction hiding inside it. You know the setup — merchant sees Death in the Damascus market, Death gives him a startled look, merchant panics and rides hard to Aleppo, and that evening Death finds him there and says I was surprised to see you in Damascus this morning, since I had an appointment with you here in Aleppo tonight. Philosophers treat it as a tidy little puzzle about causal vs evidential decision theory: does fleeing *cause* your death or just *reveal* where it'll happen? But I want something that captures what it would actually be like to live through that. Write a short story where the protagonist knows the tale — he's heard it, he recognizes he's in a Death-in-Damascus situation — and now he has to decide what to do. I want the phenomenology of it: what does deliberation feel like when you suspect your own reasoning is just discovering a fact about where you'll die rather than changing anything? Don't just dramatize the philosophical argument; make me feel the vertigo. And if you can work the structure of the paradox into the narrative form itself — like, the story turns out to have been inevitable in a way the reader only sees at the end — so much the better.
