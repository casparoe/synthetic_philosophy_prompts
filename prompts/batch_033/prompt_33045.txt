I sit on the Safety Review Board of an independent AI research institute. Until now the institute has confined itself to systems everyone on the board agrees are tools: retrieval-augmented question answering, code completion, fixed-pipeline document analysis, and the like — systems that perform bounded tasks, hold no persistent objectives, and take no actions beyond producing an output. A team has now submitted a proposal to build what it calls a lab agent: a system that takes high-level instructions (such as "survey recent work on X and identify the open problems"), decomposes them into subtasks, plans over multi-day horizons, writes and executes code, queries internal and external resources, maintains and revises its own task list, and takes actions with side effects such as sending messages, opening issues, and requesting compute. The team also asks that the system be allowed to briefly defer a shutdown command when it judges that stopping immediately would corrupt a long-running job, finishing to a safe point first.

The team's core argument is that the system would still be a tool, because its top-level objective is set by the operator and it has no preferences of its own; the capability gains, they say, come from moving the operator's goal into a planner, not from giving the system its own goals. The board is split. Some members see the proposal as crossing a genuine line from tools to agents and want it rejected or heavily conditioned; others reply that the tool/agent distinction is a vague continuum, that every interactive system has some goal-directedness, and that blocking this while funding the institute's current systems would be arbitrary.

I need to circulate a written opinion before Thursday's meeting. Draft the memo. It should recommend a concrete decision — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with a time frame and review triggers. It should offer a working characterization of the tool/agent distinction that actually does work for this decision rather than falling back on intuitions about autonomy or "having goals," and it should identify which features of the proposed system are doing the risk-bearing work. It should also give an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side, and it should be written so that a board member with no philosophy background could apply it.
