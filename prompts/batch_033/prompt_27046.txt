I'm in this practical ethics course right now and we've been stuck on the inefficacy objection for two weeks: the argument that since my individual action (not flying, skipping meat, showing up to vote) wont be the thing that tips the scale, I can't be obligated to do it, because the harm happens either way. I understand the argument, but I keep flip-flopping on what actually follows from it. In seminar some people say it only applies to consumer purchases, others say it would also let a doctor off the hook for joining a practice that harms patients one patient at a time, and someone else argued that making no difference isn't the same as making no contribution — that my action can still be part of the set of actions that together cause the harm even if no single action changes the outcome. Then there's the expected-utility reply, that a tiny chance of being the tipping point can still justify the action, and the worry that the whole framing treats collective harms as if they were just very large individual harms.

Rather than a balanced survey, I want a definite view. Please give me the five most important implications of taking the inefficacy objection seriously — for climate, meat, voting, or individual responsibility generally. Be committed: say where you think the objection succeeds, where it fails, and why, and don't just lay out the options.
