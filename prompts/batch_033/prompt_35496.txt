I'm a novelist — working on a novel about a small intentional community, actually — and I keep tripping over this pattern that I don't have a word for. I'm hoping there's a name for it somewhere in philosophy or political theory or game theory or wherever.

So here's the situation I keep writing myself into, and then I notice it in real life too. A group of people share some resource or project — could be a communal house, could be a literary magazine's slush pile, could be a group chat with a no-kick policy. Any single person in the group can take an action that affects everyone. Maybe it's inviting a new person to move in, or rejecting a submission everyone else wanted to discuss, or adding someone to the chat. And here's the thing: if even one person thinks the action is a good idea, they can just go ahead and do it. Nobody can stop them, because the system gives everyone equal unilateral power. The people who think it's a bad idea might not even find out until it's done.

What's strange is how this plays out as the group gets bigger. In a group of three, you need that one enthusiastic person to actually exist. In a group of thirty, the odds that *someone* is overconfident, or having a bad day, or just has terrible judgement about this particular thing — those odds go way up. So the bigger the group, the more the group's effective decision-making tilts toward its most reckless or overoptimistic member. The median view doesn't matter. The majority view doesn't matter. What matters is the most extreme view in favor of acting.

I started calling this "the enthusiast's trigger" in my notebook, but I'm sure someone smarter than me has analyzed it properly. I'm not just looking for a term to drop at parties though. I want to understand what exactly the boundaries of this thing are. Is it the same as a "tyranny of the minority," or is it different? How does it relate to the idea that large groups are vulnerable to tail risk because of their most extreme members? And if there isn't an established name, I'd love a defintion that really carves it at the joints, plus maybe a couple of edge cases that test whether something counts or not.

Oh, and — could you tell me how confident you are in the answer, and point out the one thing you're least sure about? I'd rather hear "this is my best guess but I'm shaky on X" than have you pretend everything's settled.
