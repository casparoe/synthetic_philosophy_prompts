I chair my school council's Rewards and Incentives subcommittee. Each term we receive a handful of proposals from staff, pupils and parent representatives for schemes meant to improve things like reading, attendance, recycling or participation: for example, awarding house points for every book a pupil logs, or giving a prize to the form class with the cleanest room at the end of each week. Our job is to decide which proposals to recommend to the headteacher. We have about an hour per proposal, none of us has formal training in economics or statistics, and we have no budget for data collection or pilot studies, so the screening has to be done on paper. The stakes are modest but real: a poor scheme wastes a term, encourages pupils to game the system, and damages the council's credibility.

I have read that Goodhart's law describes a recurring failure of exactly this kind of scheme: roughly, once a measure is turned into a target, people find ways to move the measure without producing the outcome it was meant to track. The wider phenomenon, sometimes called specification gaming, is that people tend to satisfy the letter of a scheme while missing its spirit. I would like you to turn that principle into a practical screening procedure the subcommittee can actually run. It should be a checklist or decision flow telling us, in order, what questions to ask about a proposed incentive; what counts as a sufficiently good answer to each; where we should stop and reject or revise; and where we should hand the decision to a person because it turns on values rather than facts. It must be usable by non-specialists in under an hour and should be honest about which steps are really judgement calls dressed up as procedure. It should also explain how to treat hard cases, such as a metric that only partially tracks the goal, a case where gaming is difficult to distinguish from genuine improvement, or a case where the only alternative to a gameable metric is having no scheme at all.

Please apply the procedure to three proposals we might actually see: (a) house points for every twenty minutes of independent reading a pupil records in an app; (b) a weekly prize for the form class that reports the least litter in and around its form room; and (c) certificates and a trip for the pupils in the form that raises the most money during the school's annual charity drive. Make sure at least one of the worked examples reaches a conclusion we would find unwelcome, and finish by stating clearly what the procedure cannot catch. Please write the answer in British English.
