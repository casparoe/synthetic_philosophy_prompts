I just spent two hours arguing with a friend who kept insisting that "every truth is knowable" — that for any fact about the world, it must be possible, at least in principle, for someone to know it. He used this to dismiss my claim that there might be truths that are simply beyond the reach of any possible investigation, saying my position was incoherent. I pushed back by mentioning Fitch's paradox, but he waved it off as a technical trick that doesn't affect the spirit of the slogan.

I want to go back to him with a clearer breakdown. What are the distinct precise claims that "every truth is knowable" could be making? I can see that it might mean something trivial — like "if a truth is known, then obviously it was knowable" — and something much stronger — like "for any true proposition, there is some possible epistemic agent in some possible situation who could come to know it." But I'd like a systematic menu of readings, from weakest to strongest, with an assessment of which are plausible, which are false, and which would actually support his dismissal of unknowable truths. And I'd especially like to understand: which reading does Fitch's paradox actually target, and does the paradox refute that reading or only a strawman?
