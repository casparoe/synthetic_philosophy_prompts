Here is an argument I've seen in the NHST-versus-Bayes debates, offered to show that the replication crisis is a story about scientists' incentives rather than about significance testing, and that switching to Bayesian methods would not fix it. Each premise looks right to me and the conclusion feels wrong, which is exactly the situation where I don't trust my own diagnosis.

(1) Null-hypothesis significance testing, carried out correctly, controls the false-positive rate at 5%: if the null hypothesis is true, the test rejects it at most 5% of the time.
(2) So a field that carried out its tests correctly would have at most 5% false positives among its published positive findings.
(3) The replication crisis shows far more than 5% of published positive findings failing to replicate.
(4) Therefore the crisis must be caused by misuse — p-hacking, HARKing, optional stopping, publication bias — rather than by the method itself.
(5) A method is not to blame for its misuse.
(6) So the replication crisis gives no reason to prefer Bayesian methods; whatever replaced NHST would be gamed in its own way.

I can't accept (6), because by the same reasoning no inferential method could ever be criticized for the way its properties interact with ordinary scientific incentives, and that seems to prove too much. If I have to choose, I'd rather keep (1) than (5), but I want to know what I'm paying. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I want a definite diagnosis, not a menu of options.
