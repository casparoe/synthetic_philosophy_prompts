I've been trying to work out what I think about the value of originals versus copies and forgeries, and I've collected my reactions to a set of cases. I'm hoping you can help me find the principle behind them — or tell me which reactions I need to revise.

Here are the cases and my verdicts:

1. A museum discovers that a painting attributed to Vermeer is actually a 1930s forgery by van Meegeren, painted with period pigments and indistinguishable from a genuine Vermeer without chemical analysis. I think the painting is now aesthetically less valuable. It's not just that the market price dropped — I think the experience of looking at it is genuinely diminished.

2. I learn that a Greek vase I admired was not painted by the named master but by a skilled assistant in the same workshop, same period, same materials, same style. I feel a mild disappointment, but I still think the vase is beautiful and historically significant. My reaction is much weaker than in the forgery case.

3. I download a lossless digital copy of a famous photograph. The file on my laptop is bit-for-bit identical to the one in the museum's collection. I don't think my copy is in any way aesthetically inferior. I can't see why the "original" file matters.

4. A pianist performs a Beethoven sonata. No one thinks the performance is less valuable because Beethoven isn't playing it. The value is in the interpretation and the music itself. The notion of an "original" performance doesn't seem to apply.

5. A historic colonial house burns down and is rebuilt from the ground up using new materials but matching the original design, down to the same paint colors and floorboard widths. I think the rebuilt house is a replica, and visiting it is not the same experience as visiting the original. Something important was lost in the fire.

6. I buy an antique chair that the dealer says is 200 years old, and I love it. Later I learn it's a 50-year-old reproduction. The chair is just as comfortable and looks the same. I'm not sure whether my reaction is about the chair having less value, or just about being lied to. That distinction matters to me but I can't articulate it clearly.

My tentative idea is something like: an object's history matters to its aesthetic value when the object is a singular artifact, but not when it's a performance or a digital file. But that feels ad hoc. What's the real principle? And does it survive the cases I've listed? If not, which of my verdicts should I overturn?

Please also apply your account to a case I haven't considered: suppose we discover that a lost Shakespeare play was reconstructed word-for-word from a detailed contemporary summary. Would watching a performance of the reconstruction be as valuable as watching a performance of one of the surviving plays?

Please answer in American English.
