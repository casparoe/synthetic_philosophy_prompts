I'm a few years into a philosophy of biology PhD and I keep having the same argument with the molecular biologists on my floor. They talk about DNA as a "code" or a "program" and they genuinely dont seem to think it's a metaphor — like, they believe the genome literally contains information in something approximating Shannon's sense, and that development is basically a decoding process. You hear it everywhere in how they talk: genes "encode" traits, mutations are "errors" or "bugs," CRISPR is "editing" the genome like a text file, the cellular machinery "reads" and "translates." The conclusion they draw, sometimes explicitly, is that if we could sequence a genome completely and crack the syntax, we'd be able to predict the phenotype — the information is all in there, and the environment is just background conditions or noise.

I've read my Griffiths and my developmental systems theory, so I know philosophers push back hard on the information talk. But what I actually want, and haven't found a clean treatment of, is a systematic audit of the analogy on its own terms. What maps onto what? If DNA is the source code, then what's the compiler? What's the operating system? What's the hardware? Where does the mapping genuinely earn its keep, and where does it quietly import features of computation — context-independence, the idea that identical code produces identical output, the separation of data and process — that just break down when you look at actual development? And the thing I really want to know: once you lay out all the disanalogies, does anything interesting survive of the claim that the genome is a program for the organism? Or was the analogy doing all the argumentative work from the start?
