My colleague on the ethics board has been pretty consistent about autonomous systems cases. Here's her track record:

- Surgical robot with a software bug that caused a wrong incision: the manufacturer is liable.
- ML diagnostic tool that missed a tumor because it was trained on mostly white patients and the patient was Black: the hospital that deployed it is liable for not validating across demographics.
- A drone that used online reinforcement learning in a disaster zone and flew into a power line in a way the designers couldn't have anticipated: genuine responsibility gap, no one's at fault.
- A self-driving car that encountered a novel road situation and killed a pedestrian, where the system's behavior was emergent from its training rather than any specific bug: she also called this a responsibility gap.
- An autonomous trading bot that lost millions because it interacted with another bot in an unpredictable way: the bank that deployed it is liable for not having kill switches.

Now we have a new proposal: a hospital wants to deploy an LLM-based triage system that gives preliminary diagnoses and urgency ratings. It's been fine-tuned on the hospital's own patient records. The hospital has followed all standard testing protocols. The worry is that the model might give catastrophically wrong advice with high confidence in a way that a human triage nurse would catch, but the system presents it so smoothly that the patient doesn't question it.

What would she say — is this a responsibility gap or does someone bear responsibility? What's the rule she's implicitly running, and what other rules fit her verdicts just as well? And if I wanted to test which rule she's really using, what's the one case I should ask her about next?
