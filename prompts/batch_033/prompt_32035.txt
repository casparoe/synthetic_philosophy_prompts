The debate over the normative status of the Kelly criterion has been running for decades, and despite contributions from mathematicians, economists, and professional gamblers, it feels as though the same moves recur without resolution. On one side, the Kelly proponent argues that a gambler facing a sequence of positive-expectation bets should maximize the expected logarithm of wealth, because this strategy avoids eventual ruin and dominates all others in asymptotic growth rate. On the other side, the critic replies that this simply is expected utility theory with a logarithmic utility function, that there is nothing uniquely rational about log utility, and that Kelly betting is often far too aggressive for an agent with a finite horizon or a non-logarithmic risk tolerance. Both sides appear to accept the same mathematics; the impasse seems to be about what the mathematics implies.

Diagnose this disagreement. Is it a verbal dispute about whether the word "optimal" means maximizing expected wealth, expected log wealth, or something else? Is it a substantive disagreement about which risk preferences are rationally required, with one side smuggling in an implicit norm under the guise of a mathematical result? Are the two sides answering different questions — one about the asymptotic properties of repeated bets under a fixed strategy, the other about the preferences of a finite agent making a single decision — and talking past each other under a shared vocabulary? Or is there a hidden empirical disagreement about what kinds of betting situations real agents actually face? Give your diagnosis with close attention to the characteristic moves each side makes in the literature, and say what, if anything, would count as progress — a formal result, a conceptual distinction, or a change of question — given your diagnosis.
