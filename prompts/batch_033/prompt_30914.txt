I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in decision theory, eighteen students, one eighty-minute meeting per week. The material has recently become personal for me: over the past year I have made a series of small financial and professional choices that, taken together, have the Allais shape — I took a certain payment over a gamble with a higher expected value, and separately chose a long shot with a larger upside over a safer moderate option. Expected utility theory, as I have been teaching it, says no single utility function can represent both of those choices. I do not think I was being incoherent, and I want my students to feel the force of that problem rather than hear about it.

Design an eighty-minute classroom activity for that seminar that makes students live through the following arc. First, through a series of small-stakes choices, they should produce the Allais pattern themselves — the sure thing in one pair, the larger prize in the other — without being told what pattern is being elicited. Second, they should collide with the impossibility result: working in small groups, they should try to assign utilities to the outcomes so that both of their choices maximize expected utility, and fail. Third, the activity should introduce risk-weighted expected utility as the machinery that vindicates their choices: a utility function over outcomes, a continuous strictly increasing risk function r on probabilities with r(0)=0 and r(1)=1, with risk aversion corresponding to r(p) at most p for interior p, and rank-dependent weighting of outcomes from worst to best. The payoff should be that both choices from the first stage turn out to maximize a single risk-weighted expected utility.

Give me concrete rules and materials, a point-based payoff scheme that requires no real money but keeps the choices meaningful, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the formal components above — including the failure of the independence axiom and why rank-dependent weighting repairs it without giving up a single ordering of gambles. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (students who recognize the Allais paradox and game their answers, groups that stall on the algebra, stakes too small to produce the pattern) and how to handle each failure. Be frank about which feature of risk-weighted expected utility the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in the debrief.
