The setup is this: a driver is on a motorway with two identical-looking exits. He's absent-minded, meaning that when he reaches an exit, he can't remember whether it's the first or the second one. His preferences are: if he exits at the first exit, he gets a payoff of 0; if he exits at the second, he gets 4; if he stays on the motorway and misses both exits, he gets 1. Before setting off, he can plan: he chooses a probability p of exiting at any given exit. His expected payoff from the planning perspective is 0·p + 4·(1-p)p + 1·(1-p)², which simplifies to 1 + 2p - 3p². Maximising that gives p = 1/3, with an expected payoff of 4/3. So far so good. But now suppose he's actually at an exit, trying to decide what to do. He knows he's at some exit, but he doesn't know which one. He reasons that the probability he's at the first exit is 1/(1+p) and the probability he's at the second is p/(1+p). Using these self-locating probabilities, he calculates the expected payoff of continuing versus exiting, and finds that the optimal p is now 1/2, not 1/3. The authors claim this is a paradox: the planning stage and the action stage give different answers, and it's not obvious which one should govern his behaviour.

I get the maths, sort of, but I'm struggling with the philosophical upshot. Is the point that there's a genuine inconsistency in how we should think about rational choice when memory is imperfect, or is one of the two calculations just wrong?
