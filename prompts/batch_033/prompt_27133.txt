Write a dialogue between two philosophers of science who disagree about whether the patterns identified by Dennett's three stances—the physical stance, the design stance, and the intentional stance—are all real in the same sense, or whether only the physical stance tracks what is fundamentally real.

One philosopher, a realist about higher-level patterns, holds that the intentional stance picks out genuine, objective regularities in the world. On this view, the fact that you can predict a chess player's moves by treating them as a rational agent with beliefs and desires is not a convenient fiction but reflects a real pattern—one that is no less real for being invisible from the physical stance, just as thermodynamic regularities are no less real for being invisible from the stance of fundamental particle physics. The three stances are not in competition; they reveal different layers of real structure, and the patterns at each layer are equally real on their own terms.

The other philosopher, a reductionist, holds that the intentional stance is a predictive instrument whose success tracks our cognitive and computational limitations, not the world's own joints. As neuroscience matures, the design and intentional stances will be progressively displaced by the physical stance, and the patterns they identify will be revealed as provisional heuristics—useful now, but not permanent features of reality. The fact that we cannot currently do without the intentional stance is a fact about us, not about the world.

The dialogue should press on several questions: What criteria distinguish a real pattern from a merely projected one? Does the in-practice indispensability of the intentional stance constitute evidence for the reality of its patterns, or is indispensability a pragmatic notion that carries no ontological weight? Do the three stances form a genuine hierarchy of fundamentality, or are they irreducibly distinct ways of engaging with the world? And is the design stance a stable middle ground between the physical and intentional stances, or does it ultimately collapse into one of the other two? Both characters should be equally competent, respond to each other's strongest arguments rather than talking past each other, and push each other to clarify what they mean by "real" and what would settle the disagreement.
