I've been trying to work out what I actually think emotions are, so I've been collecting my own verdicts on cases rather than just going with whichever theory sounds best. Here's where I've landed:

- A loud noise goes off behind me and I jump; my heart's racing and for a second I feel jangled. But I don't think I was afraid of anything. Not an emotion — just a startle.
- I'm scared of flying. I know, and fully accept, that it's safer than driving, and I don't judge that the plane will crash. But the fear is real. Definitely an emotion.
- A friend cancels plans at the last minute and I feel a quick flash of anger — even though I immediately think they had a good reason and I'd have done the same in their shoes. Emotion, yes.
- I snap at my partner because I haven't eaten all day and I'm 'hangry'. That doesn't feel like an emotion to me — more like a bodily state pushing me around. Not an emotion.
- After my mother died, the grief wasn't really about any one thought or any one bodily feeling. It came in waves, I'd sometimes forget and then remember, and it coloured everything for months. But it'd be mad to say that wasn't an emotion.
- There's a low-grade anxious hum I sometimes have for days without being able to say what I'm anxious about. I want to call that a mood, not an emotion — but I can't say exactly why.
- I feel a little schadenfreude when a colleague I envy makes a small mistake in a talk. I think it's petty and I wish I didn't, but the feeling is there. Emotion.
- Pride when my daughter graduated: that felt like an emotion, even though it seems tied up with a judgement that her achievement reflects on me somehow.

Is there a single account of what emotions are that gets all of these, or nearly all? I'm torn between the idea that emotions are evaluative judgements, that they're perceptions of bodily changes, and that they're irreducible feelings. My working rule is something like: an emotion is an appraisal of something as mattering to my wellbeing, one that I can't simply decide away. But that seems to wrongly rule in the startle (my body appraised the noise as a threat, and I couldn't decide it away), and its strained by the objectless anxiety case. Can you state the best version of each of the three views, say which of my verdicts each one forces me to drop, and then say what principle — if any — saves the most of them? And please tell me what it predicts about a case I keep going back and forth on: the mild disgust I feel when I see a plate of food that I know is fine but that reminds me of something rotten.
