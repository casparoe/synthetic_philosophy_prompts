I am taking an upper-level course on the metaphysics of modality, and I have noticed a pattern in the readings that I do not have a name for. The pattern appears in arguments about whether a particular object—a person, a statue, a table—could have had a property different from its actual one. In one part of the argument, the author reasons by considering another possible world in which that very object exists and has the different property; the claim seems to get its force from the object's being the same. But when an objection is raised about how one and the same thing could have incompatible properties, the author retreats to saying that the object in the other world need not be strictly the same thing after all, only a suitably similar stand-in for it. A few pages later the strict language returns, and the conclusion is stated as being about the original object again. I have seen the same pattern in a discussion of whether a person could have had different parents and in a discussion of whether a statue could have been made from a different lump of clay: in both cases the strict reading makes the conclusion interesting, while the loose reading makes the premises defensible.

Is there a name for this pattern, or for the underlying choice it exploits? I am not looking for a bare label. I want to know how to tell when an author is working consistently within one view—perhaps a view on which modal claims are always about suitable stand-ins—and when the author is sliding between two views as argumentative convenience requires. Is the slide simply an equivocation, or is it a recognized dialectical move with its own logic and conditions of legitimacy? If there is no established name, what would you call it, and what would be its boundary cases?
