I'm a novelist, and I've been wrestling with a phenomenon in my dialogue writing that I can't find a satisfactory name for. Here is what happens. I will have a character say something perfectly natural in context — something that, given what that character knows and intends, means one thing. But the reader, who has access to information the character lacks (backstory, other characters' secrets, the shape of the narrative so far), will pick up a second, richer meaning from the same words — a meaning the character did not and could not have intended. This is not dramatic irony, because in dramatic irony the audience knows the character is mistaken or that their words will recoil on them in a way they cannot foresee; the second meaning undercuts or ironizes the first. What I am describing is different: the character's intended meaning stands perfectly intact, and the second meaning sits alongside it, not cancelling it but adding a layer that feels, to the reader, as though it was "meant" — just not by the character. If anyone meant it, I did, as the author. But I did not signal it with any of the usual devices: no wink to the audience, no break in tone, no narrator comment. The words simply happen to carry this extra weight because of the larger narrative structure they are embedded in.

A concrete example from something I am drafting: a woman tells her husband, over breakfast, "I think we should invite your brother this weekend." She means it as a peace offering after a family argument; she is trying to be generous. The reader, however, knows that the brother has been having an affair with the husband's business partner, and that this information is about to surface in a way that will destroy the husband's professional life. The wife knows none of this. Her words, read in that light, carry a second meaning — something like "the weekend you are about to have will be far worse than you imagine, and your brother will be at the centre of it." The reader feels this second meaning as deliberate, as though the sentence was constructed to deliver it, but the character is entirely innocent of it. It is not foreshadowing in the usual sense, because nothing about the line itself points forward; it is only the reader's collateral knowledge that makes it resonate.

I have been calling this "retroactive double meaning" in my notes, but I suspect there must be an established term, or at least a discussion, somewhere in the philosophy of language or literary theory. It seems adjacent to the idea that speakers can convey more than they literally say — implicature, I think it is called — but here the "speaker" who conveys the extra meaning is not the character; it is the text, or the author, or the narrative situation itself. Is there a name for this specific configuration? If there are several near-miss concepts, I would like to know what they are, how each differs from what I am describing, and which comes closest. And if there really is no term, I would be grateful for a precise definition and a suggested name that distinguishes it from dramatic irony, from ordinary implicature, and from the kind of authorial intrusion where the narrator simply tells the reader something the character does not know.
