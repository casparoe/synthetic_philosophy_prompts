I am a novelist. For years I have been trying to articulate something about the relationship between a plotted story and the freedom of its characters, and a friend with philosophical training recently told me I was circling a problem that has a name: the problem of future contingents, Aristotle's sea battle. I have read enough to grasp the basic argument — that if it is now true that there will be a sea battle tomorrow, then the battle is unavoidable, which seems to rule out real contingency, and that the main replies are to deny that future-tense statements have truth values, to insist they are true but in a way that does not entail necessity, or to accept the conclusion and give up on an open future. But I want to understand this from inside my own craft, not as a visitor to philosophy.

Restate the problem of future contingents — the argument, its logical structure, and the principal philosophical responses to it — in the vocabulary of narrative craft. I do not want a loose analogy between determinism and plot. I want a systematic translation: take the logical structure of the argument and express it using the concepts a working novelist lives with — the fixed text versus the reading experience, narrative time versus story time, foreshadowing and prolepsis, the implied author, dramatic irony, the sense in which a character's decision can feel real and significant within a story whose ending is already written, the difference between what the reader knows and what the character knows at a given moment, and whatever else you need. Be faithful: do not smuggle assumptions from narrative theory into the translation and then claim the philosophers were saying that.

Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the philosophical debate translate cleanly into the novelist's vocabulary? Which parts can be paraphrased only with strain or distortion? Which parts have no counterpart at all in how we think about narrative? For each piece of residue, tell me whether it points to something the philosophers are confused about — a pseudo-problem that dissolves once you think in narrative terms — or whether it points to something my vocabulary cannot capture, a genuine feature of time and truth that narrative theory was never built to handle.

Do not use headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables. Continuous prose only, the way you would write a letter.
