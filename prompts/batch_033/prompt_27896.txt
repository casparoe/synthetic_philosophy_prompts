I'm teaching an upper-division philosophy of biology seminar — about fourteen students, one eighty-minute session — and the next reading is Gould and Lewontin's spandrels critique. I want the students to have felt the central charge before they read it: that the adaptationist programme is methodologically suspect precisely because it can generate a plausible adaptive story for nearly any trait whatever, and that this fluency is a weakness rather than a strength. Please design an in-class game that engineers that experience. Small groups will be shown a series of real organismal traits — I'm considering the giant panda's "thumb" (a modified sesamoid bone, not a true digit), the human chin, and the female spotted hyena's pseudo-penis, all cases where adaptationist and non-adaptationist accounts have been seriously defended — and will compete to produce the most fluent, specific, and plausible adaptive explanation for each. Scoring should reward speed, detail, and narrative plausibility rather than truth, so that the winning group has likely produced a string of just-so stories. Midway through, the twist: the facilitator reveals that for each trait there is a serious alternative account the winning stories failed to exclude — a byproduct or spandrel, a developmental or architectural constraint, a nearly neutral variant, or an adaptation so constrained by available parts that it looks nothing like an optimal solution — and the class must decide whether anything in their stories would have ruled those alternatives out.

The game should close with a debrief mapping what happened onto the specific components of the critique: the just-so story, the failure to consider alternatives, unfalsifiability, and the conflation of current utility with evolutionary origin. Give me concrete rules, materials, an eighty-minute timeline, a point-based payoff scheme that does not use real money, and a debrief script. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I suspect students will object that their stories could still be true, or that the twist stacked the deck — and how to handle those reactions without simply asserting the critique. Finally, be explicit about which feature of the adaptationism debate the activity cannot reproduce and that I will need to supply myself in discussion.
