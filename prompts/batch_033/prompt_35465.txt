I'm TAing for an intro epistemology course and next week I have to teach the dogmatism paradox — you know, the one about what you should do when you find out that someone just as smart and well-informed as you disagrees with you. If you stick to your guns you're being dogmatic, but if you always budge then you can't hold any belief that smart people disagree about, which is basically every interesting belief. The prof gave me a fifty-minute section and told me not to just lecture at them but to walk them through it so they feel the force of the paradox themselves.

I don't need you to explain the paradox to me — I get it. What I need is a plan for the session. I want to know the dependency structure: what idea absolutely has to land before the next one can make sense, and why. I want the one concrete example that carries the whole thing, and I want to know why that example and not the obvious alternative (like the restaurant-bill-splitting one, which I'm worried collapses into a different problem). I want the misconceptions first-years will predictably form — I'm guessing at least one of them will think the paradox just shows relativism is true, and another will try to dodge it by saying "well I'd just look at the evidence again" — and I want to know exactly where in the fifty minutes I should head each one off. And I want one question I can ask in the last three minutes that'll tell me whether they actually understood the paradox as a paradox, rather than just nodded along. Oh, and tell me what I should openly refuse to resolve at this level — I don't want to pretend the paradox has a neat solution when it doesn't, but I also don't want to leave them thinking epistemology is a dead end.
