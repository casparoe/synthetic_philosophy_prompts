I keep going in circles on the platonism/nominalism debate because of an argument a friend walked me through, and now I'm worried it proves too much. It goes like this:

(1) You can only know about the kinds of things you can be causally connected to.
(2) Numbers, as the platonist conceives them, are abstract objects, and abstract objects are causally inert.
(3) So you can't be causally connected to numbers, and therefore you can't know anything about them.
(4) But you do know things about numbers — e.g., that there's no largest prime.
(5) So platonism is false; numbers can't be the kind of thing the platonist says they are.

So far so good. But the same engine seems to eat the nominalist too. A nominalist who wants to say that 2+2=4 is true-in-mathematics but not about abstract objects still needs to talk about sentence types, meanings, truth, validity, or at least resemblance classes of concrete tokens. All of those are just as abstract and causally inert as numbers. By premise (1), we can't know that two ink marks are tokens of the same sentence, or what a proof says, or whether an inference is valid. The obvious dodge is that the nominalist doesn't need real sentence types, and talk of being the same sentence is just a useful fiction. But then you owe an account of what makes the fiction useful and what it is for two marks to say the same thing, and every account I know of quietly quantifies over abstracta. So the causal premise doesn't just kill platonism; it kills the nominalist's ability to state nominalism. An objection that refutes both sides of a debate usually has a bug in it.

I can see a few exits. Reject (1): maybe knowledge doesn't require causal connection to the thing itself, just a reliable route to the truth about it. But then doesn't that hand the platonist everything they need? Reject (4): swallow full-blown skepticism about math, which seems insane and also self-undermining. Or accept that the causal premise really does force us to give up sentence types and meanings too, and try to be a hardcore nominalist about linguistic abstracta — which sounds like it costs way more then anyone in this debate wants to pay. I'd much rather keep (4) than (1), but I don't trust my own preference here. Which step actually goes wrong, and what does rejecting it commit you to?
