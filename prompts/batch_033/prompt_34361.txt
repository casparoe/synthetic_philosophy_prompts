I run the policy shop at a small nonprofit that builds short-term coalitions between groups that normally won't be in the same room — an animal-welfare group and a hunters' group, a religious-traditionalist group and a secular civil-liberties group, that kind of pairing. We get maybe one of these a month, and the stakes are high: coalitions fall apart, board members resign, the local paper writes about us. My staff are organizers, not philosophers, and the executive director wants a written procedure she can sign off on, not a seminar.

Lately I've been reading about this idea called moral trade — the thought that two groups with very different moral views can each come out ahead by their own lights if they trade concessions: each gives ground on something it sees as relatively unimportant in exchange for the other side giving ground on something it sees as much more important. Neither side is compromising its core values; they're just trading on the basis of different priorities. I find the idea compelling but I have no idea how to actually run it.

Could you turn moral trade into a decision procedure I can use? I want a checklist my team can run on a proposal in a few hours: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the step is a value judgment rather than a fact. Please deal with the hard cases: one side says an issue is an absolute and not up for trade; the two sides cant agree on any common scale; a trade helps both sides but harms a third party; one side's views are bad enough that dealing with them at all feels like legitimizing them; and both sides claim to care more about the same thing so there's no obvious swap. Then run the procedure on two or three worked examples, and make at least one of them a case where the procedure tells me not to do a trade I'd otherwise be tempted to make.
