Take the Milgram obedience experiments and analyze them through the lens of the situationist critique of virtue ethics. I know the basic setup — participants told to shock a "learner" for wrong answers, experimenter in a lab coat insisting they keep going, 65% going all the way to 450 volts — and I know the situationist argument: that behavior is driven by situational features (the incremental escalation, the authority figure, the institutional setting, the difficulty of extricating yourself) rather than by stable character traits like compassion or cruelty, so virtue ethics is built on something that doesn't exist. What I want is a careful mapping, in both directions.

First, theory → episode. What does the situationist say the experiment actually shows? Which virtues were supposedly in play, what were the specific situational factors, and is the right reading that most people lack virtue, or that virtue is too fragile to do the work virtue ethics needs, or that the whole trait-attribution framework is the problem? What can the virtue ethicist say back — and in particular, does the variation across conditions (proximity, the two-peer-rebel condition) help them, or does it just give the situationist more grist?

Second, episode → theory. What does Milgram actually show about the situationist critique? Is it a clean confirmation, an anomaly, or a case the critique was never really designed to handle? If the situationist points to the 65% as evidence that character doesn't explain behavior, does the virtue ethicist get to point to the 35% — and the rebels in the peer condition — as evidence that it does? Or is that move question-begging?

I want the analysis to be disciplined about the mapping: who the players are in the virtue-ethical picture, what counts as evidence for each side, and where each side should be silent rather than forcing the data to speak. And be honest about whether the episode is a knockout for either position, or wether it mostly reveals that they're talking past each other.
