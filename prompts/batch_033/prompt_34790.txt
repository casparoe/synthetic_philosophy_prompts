Two members of my reading group, Dana and Priya, spent most of an evening arguing about a decision facing a third member, Leo. Leo must choose between two offers: an associate position at a corporate law firm and a poorly paid job in a chamber orchestra. He is an accomplished cellist who also did well in law school, and he has been unable to decide for weeks. I took notes on their exchange and would like a point-by-point adjudication.

(1) Dana argued that Leo's protracted indecision is itself evidence that the two careers cannot be weighed on a single scale. If one option were simply better overall, Leo would have recognized it and chosen it. Since he keeps reversing himself, the goods at stake—security and status on one side, music and autonomy on the other—must be incommensurable: there is no common currency in which they can be compared.

(2) Priya replied that this moves too fast. Indecision can reflect ordinary uncertainty about the facts: which job would actually prove tolerable, how much Leo would miss performing, how much money would matter to him in five years. Or the two options could be exactly equal in overall value, so that Leo keeps hunting for a difference that is not there. Neither uncertainty nor equality forces us to posit incommensurable values.

(3) Dana answered that equality is the wrong diagnosis. If the options were equal, it would not matter which one Leo chose, and a small improvement to one side—say, the firm adds a hundred dollars to the salary—would make that option better and settle the matter. In hard choices, small improvements of that kind typically settle nothing; Leo would still hesitate. That is the signature of the options' being on a par rather than equal. And if they are on a par, the reasons run out before the choice does, so Leo is not rationally required to pick either one; the choice is instead an occasion for commitment. By committing to one path, Leo can create reasons that did not exist before—reasons of the form "this is my project now"—and that commitment can make the chosen path genuinely better for him going forward.

(4) Priya said the small-improvement argument is suggestive but not decisive. The values at stake may be lumpy, so that a hundred dollars is too small a change to register against a life-sized difference even though a large enough change would tip the balance. Or Leo's preferences may simply be incomplete, with no determinate ranking for a small improvement to reveal. As for commitment, either it is backed by reasons Leo already had—in which case the choice was not underdetermined after all—or it is not, in which case "creating reasons" is a flattering name for arbitrariness. If a choice can make itself correct after the fact, the difference between a good choice and a rationalized one disappears.

I could not tell how much of this was substantive and how much was terminological, and neither would concede a step. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the four moves, say whether it is clearly mistaken, defensible but incomplete, or largely right, and briefly justify each rating. Then say what, if anything, both sides are missing, and who is more right overall. Keep the verdict keyed to what each person actually said.
