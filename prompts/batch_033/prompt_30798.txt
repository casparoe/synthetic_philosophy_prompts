I design type systems for a living — subtyping, refinement types, parametric polymorphism, the usual apparatus — and lately I have been reading early Chinese philosophy in the evenings, mostly to get out of my own head. The text I keep returning to is the White Horse Dialogue, attributed to Gongsun Long of the School of Names. Its thesis is that a white horse is not a horse. As I understand the argument, the moves are these. Horse names shape; white names color; and what names color is not what names shape, so the compound term white horse differs from the term horse. An objector in the dialogue pushes back that if there is a white horse, one cannot say there is no horse, so a white horse surely is a horse. The advocate replies with a seeking test: ask for a horse and a yellow or black horse will do; ask for a white horse and a yellow or black horse will not do. If white horse were just horse, the two requests would be answered by the same things; they are not, so white horse is not horse.

I want you to restate this argument in the vocabulary I actually work in: types and their inhabitants, subtyping, type equality, intersection and refinement types, and nominal versus structural typing. Go through the moves one by one and give each the most faithful type-theoretic rendering you can, including, where it helps, what a type checker or a function signature would say. I am fluent in this vocabulary, so do not explain the basics; use it precisely.

Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the argument map cleanly, which parts have to be distorted to fit, and which parts have no counterpart in type theory at all? I suspect the cleanest mapping treats the thesis as a claim about type identity rather than inhabitance — that white horse and horse are distinct types even though every inhabitant of the former is an inhabitant of the latter — but I want you to test that rather than assume it. In particular, does the type-theoretic rendering quietly import an extensional, set-of-values picture that the School of Names may not have shared? Does the seeking test come out as a fact about extensions or about something else? Say what the translation buys me and what it silently loses, and tell me whether the residue points to a blind spot in my vocabulary or to a looseness in the original argument.

One more request: be honest about the exercise itself. If you think the request rests on a confused premise — for example, if it presupposes that the dialogue is doing something like formal semantics when it may be doing something else, or if the ambiguity of the word fei (非), which can express either non-identity or exclusion from a category, already decides the interpretation — say so plainly and do the nearest defensible thing instead of producing a tidy mapping. Where the text is disputed or you are choosing one interpretation among several, flag it rather than presenting it as settled.
