In my philosophy club we read this dialogue about an ant colony — the one with Aunt Hillary, the ant who argues that her colony has a kind of mind even though no individual ant has one. A kid in the club built this argument out of it, and now I'm stuck because I can't except the conclusion.

(1) An ant colony does things that look smart — it keeps the nest at the right temperature, builds bridges, moves the whole colony when conditions turn bad — but no individual ant understands or intends any of it.
(2) So if a system's behavior is coordinated and goal-directed even though none of its parts has the goal, the system itself has a mind. The colony is like that, so the colony has a mind.
(3) But that principle isn't special to ants: a market sets prices no trader intended, a crowd at a stadium does the wave without anyone planning it, and Wikipedia produces articles no single editor could have written.
(4) So by the same principle, the market, the crowd, and Wikipedia have minds too.
(5) But that seems wrong: a market is just a bunch of trades, a crowd doing the wave is just a bunch of people, and Wikipedia is just a bunch of edits.
(6) So the principle in (2) proves too much and must be false. And if (2) goes, the ant colony doesn't have a mind either — Aunt Hillary was wrong and the whole dialogue is just a cute metaphor.

I'd rather keep (2), because that felt like the whole point of the reading, but then I'm stuck with markets and crowds having minds, which feels like it makes having a mind way too easy. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
