I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm working on a feature about a definitional fault line that keeps appearing in AI safety conversations: how narrowly the alignment problem should be defined. On one side are researchers who think the problem is best stated narrowly, as getting a system to reliably do what its operators intend, with "intend" understood in terms of the task the operators actually set, their considered instructions, or the preferences they would endorse on reflection. On the other side are researchers who think this concedes the crucial point: the target should be human values themselves, including values the operators have not articulated, do not know they hold, hold inconsistently, or would revise under moral uncertainty, and that "do what the operator intends" is a lossy proxy that can be satisfied while the system does something disastrous, or that quietly bakes in the operator's current errors. I have interviewed people in both camps and have watched them talk past each other, so I want to write the document they would produce if they actually had to collaborate rather than argue.

Act as a professional philosopher working at the intersection of AI alignment and metaethics. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming several candidate formulations for each disputed point before settling on the one you will put forward. Draft the joint statement that a committed intent-alignment theorist and a committed value-alignment theorist could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Structure it as follows: (1) a numbered "Agreed claims" section, containing only claims both sides can sign and that are neither vacuous nor slanted; (2) a disagreements table whose rows state, for each residual disagreement, the precise formulation each side would accept as a fair statement of its own view, plus a neutral formulation of what is at stake; and (3) a cruxes section. For each crux — a sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side — state a concrete case, test, or argument both sides would accept as bearing on it, and record in advance what each side predicts about that case. Where a disagreement is merely verbal, say so explicitly and say which term is doing the work.

The statement should engage the hard cases rather than rest at slogans: the assistant that faithfully executes an instruction whose predictable consequences the operator would disown; the operator whose stated preferences conflict with their revealed or reflected preferences; the system that asks permission before acting on a dubious instruction; the system that fulfills the literal instruction while defeating its evident point; and the problem of value drift, lock-in, and moral uncertainty over long horizons. I am not asking you to adjudicate the dispute or to assign victory; I am asking for a constructive document both camps could put their names to, with the residual disagreement made exact.
