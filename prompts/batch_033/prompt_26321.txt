I'm putting together an application for a one-year dissertation fellowship, and I need a two-page research proposal. My area is philosophy of mind, specifically the nature of pain and its badness. Here's the half-formed idea I want developed.

Pain asymbolia — typically associated with lesions to the posterior insula or its connections — is a condition in which patients can identify noxious stimuli as painful (they correctly report the intensity, location, and quality of the sensation) but deny that the experience is unpleasant or distressing. They do not withdraw from the stimulus, do not wince, and express no desire for it to stop. It hurts, but they do not mind that it hurts. Philosophers have made heavy use of this case. Representationalists about pain say it shows that pain's phenomenal character is exhausted by its sensory content, with the badness supplied by a distinct evaluative response. Imperativists say it reveals pain's command-like structure: the sensory component is preserved but the imperative to disengage is missing. Evaluativists say it demonstrates that the sensory core is evaluatively neutral, with normal pain's badness supplied by a separate affective evaluation. What all three camps share is the claim that asymbolia cleanly dissociates the sensation of pain from its unpleasantness, and therefore that the badness of normal pain cannot be intrinsic to the sensation itself.

But all three camps treat asymbolia as straightforward evidence for their view while drawing incompatible conclusions from it. That suggests the case is doing too much argumentative work on the basis of too little clinical detail. My suspicion is that the clinical literature does not actually license the clean dissociation philosophers assume: patients' reports are messier than the standard summaries suggest, the lesion sites are more varied, and the very idea of "the same sensation, minus the badness" may be a philosophical projection rather than a clinical finding. If that is right, then the philosophical debate has been built on a somewhat idealized picture of the evidence, and correcting for that might change which theories are viable.

I want the proposal to do the following:

1. State the question precisely. Something like: does pain asymbolia establish that the unpleasantness of pain is a component separable from its sensory core, such that any adequate theory of pain's badness must locate it outside the sensation proper? Or does it merely show that the sensory and affective dimensions of pain have partially dissociable neural realizers — a fact that is consistent with the badness being intrinsic to the unified experience in the healthy case, and which forces no particular conclusion about where the badness resides?

2. Explain why the existing positions all point to asymbolia as support but cannot all be right, and why this impasse cannot be resolved simply by appealing to the clinical facts as they are currently marshalled in the philosophical literature.

3. Lay out the method. The empirical side is a systematic review of the clinical case literature on pain asymbolia and related dissociations (congenital insensitivity to pain with intact tactile discrimination, cases of suffering without clear sensory localization, and the various post-stroke and post-surgical syndromes that selectively impair pain affect). I want to attend to the exact phrasing of patient reports, the neurological variability across cases, the tasks used to elicit reports, and — crucially — the interpretive decisions already built into the clinical descriptions that philosophers then take over as raw data. The philosophical side is to identify which interpretive moves are forced by the evidence and which are optional, and to draw out the resulting constraints for the three main theories. The idea is not to do armchair neurology but to show that the philosophical arguments have been trading on an underdescribed empirical case, and that a more careful description shifts the argumentative terrain.

4. Say what a result looks like. A new adequacy condition on theories of pain's badness: something like, a theory must explain how pain can be a phenomenologically unified experience whose badness is not merely tacked on to a sensation that could in principle occur without it, while also allowing that pathology can degrade the experience in ways that selectively spare the sensory identification of noxious stimuli. And an argument that this condition eliminates at least some versions of at least one of the three main views.

5. Say what counts as failure. The risk is discovering that the clinical evidence is genuinely too sparse or too ambiguous to decide between the philosophical interpretations. In that case, the contribution would be a demonstration that the philosophical debate has been overreading a small and variable set of clinical cases — which is itself a useful result, since it would shift the burden to other kinds of argument.

6. Scope the project for one year of full-time work, with a rough timeline that distinguishes the clinical review phase from the philosophical argument phase.
