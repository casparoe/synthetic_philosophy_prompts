I'm a PhD student in philosophical logic working on the semantics of conditionals, and I've been struggling to understand the dialectical force of Lewis's triviality results. I'm hoping you can help me see what I'm missing.

The standard narrative I encounter in the literature is that Lewis's triviality results refute truth-conditional approaches to indicative conditionals and motivate the turn toward non-truth-conditional accounts — the suppositional theory, expressivism, dynamic semantics, and so on. But when I work through the proofs, I don't see why the results bear asymmetrically on the two approaches.

Here's my reconstruction. Lewis's first triviality result shows that if the probability of a conditional 'If A, then C' equals P(C|A) for every probability function over the language, the language trivializes. His later results relax this: they show that even for a single probability function, maintaining the identity while allowing conditionals to embed under conjunction forces triviality. Now, a truth-conditional semanticist can respond by simply denying the identity — by saying that the probability of a conditional is not, in general, the conditional probability, and that the empirical data supporting Adams's Thesis reflect an approximation or a pragmatic phenomenon. That's a cost, but it's a clear option. A non-truth-conditional theorist, on the other hand, denies that conditionals express propositions, but then owes an account of why conditionals appear to embed under negation, in the antecedents of other conditionals, and under quantifiers — the very embedding behavior that Lewis's proofs exploit. That's also a cost.

So both approaches face a burden: truth-conditionalists must explain away Adams's Thesis, and non-truth-conditionalists must explain away embedding. Given this apparent symmetry, why is Lewis's result so often presented as a decisive argument against truth-conditionality rather than as a puzzle that both sides must address? What breaks the symmetry?
