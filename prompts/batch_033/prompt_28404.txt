I've been trying to work out what I think about moral worth — when a right action is done for the right reasons, such that the agent deserves credit. I've collected my verdicts on a set of cases, but I can't find a principle that gets all of them, and I suspect at least one of my verdicts has to go. I'd like you to help me extract the principle, test it, and tell me where I need to revise.

Here are the cases and my verdicts.

1. **The reluctant son.** A man visits his mother in the hospital every day. He finds it draining, gets no satisfaction from it, and resents the disruption to his life. But he goes anyway because he believes it is his duty. He would not go if he didn't believe it was his duty. Verdict: his action has moral worth.

2. **The impulsive rescuer.** A woman sees a child struggling in a pond and immediately jumps in to save her. She doesn't deliberate; she later says she just saw a child in danger and acted. Verdict: her action has moral worth.

3. **The tax-dodge donor.** A man writes a large check to a homeless shelter solely because he wants the tax deduction. He would not have donated otherwise. The shelter does excellent work. Verdict: his action lacks moral worth.

4. **Huck Finn.** Huck helps the enslaved Jim escape. Huck has been raised to believe that helping a slave escape is theft and therefore morally wrong. He believes, sincerely, that he is doing something wrong. He does it anyway because he cares about Jim. Verdict: his action has moral worth.

5. **The overdetermined volunteer.** A woman volunteers at a food bank both because she genuinely enjoys helping and because she believes she has a duty to help. If either motive were absent, she would still volunteer. Verdict: her action has moral worth.

6. **The principled disaster.** A man believes, on sincere Kantian grounds, that one must never lie. A murderer comes to his door asking where his friend is hiding. The man tells the truth, and his friend is killed. He acted from a genuine commitment to moral principle. Verdict: his action lacks moral worth.

7. **The lucky egoist.** A business owner treats his employees generously — good wages, reasonable hours, genuine respect — solely because he believes happy employees are more productive. He has no concern for their welfare as such. Verdict: his actions lack moral worth.

8. **The Kantian saint.** Someone who has, over time, lost all natural sympathy for other people and finds human company burdensome. Nevertheless, he acts beneficently toward others solely from the motive of duty. Verdict: I am genuinely torn. Part of me says this must have moral worth — he's doing the right thing from duty, and what more could we ask? But another part finds something missing, something colder than what I want to credit.

The tensions I can already see: Cases 4 and 6 seem to pull in opposite directions. In both, the agent acts from a sincere moral psychology, but in one the action is right and in the other it's wrong — so maybe the verdicts just track objective rightness plus the right kind of motive? But then Case 1 and Case 8 should be parallel, and I'm torn on one but not the other. And Case 2, the impulsive rescuer, seems to have moral worth without any thought about duty at all, which sits uneasily with Case 1.

Please structure your response as follows.

- **The pattern** (~250 words). State the best candidate principle you can find that captures as many of my verdicts as possible. Give the principle precisely enough that it yields determinate verdicts on new cases.

- **Fit with the cases** (~400 words). Walk through each case and say whether the principle gets it right, strains to accommodate it, or flatly contradicts it. Flag the cases where the fit is worst.

- **Verdicts to reconsider** (~200 words). Tell me which of my verdicts the principle forces me to drop or revise, and why those are the ones to give up rather than others. If you think a different principle saves more verdicts at an acceptable cost in complexity, say so.

- **New cases** (~200 words). Give me two or three cases I haven't considered and say what the principle predicts for them. Pick at least one that you think will genuinely test my commitment to the principle.

- **What's really at stake** (~150 words). Step back and say what deeper disagreement about moral worth my pattern of verdicts reveals — what I seem to care about, and what I might be getting wrong about it.
