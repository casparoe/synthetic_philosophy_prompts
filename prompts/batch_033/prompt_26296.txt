Someone in my investing group summarized the no-trade theorem for the rest of us like this: "Back in the 80s, a couple economists proved the 'no-trade theorem': if everyone has the same prior and is rational, and everyone knows everyone is rational, then nobody should ever trade a financial asset. The argument is that if I offer to sell you a stock, you should ask why I'd sell if I thought it was worth holding — and conclude I must know something bad. So you'd only buy at a lower price. But I'd anticipate that and only sell at a price that already reflects my bad news. Unraveling this, the only trades that can happen are at prices where neither of us expects to gain, so rational people simply don't trade. The fact that the NYSE does billions of shares a day is therefore pretty direct evidence that real markets aren't rational."

I'm inclined to beleive the part about sellers knowing something, because that's basically how I think about bid-ask spreads, but the leap to "markets are irrational" feels too strong to me and I don't know enough to say where the argument goes wrong. Can you go through it sentence by sentence and tell me what an economist who actually works on this stuff would accept, what they'd wince at, and what's just wrong — and whether the conclusion about real-world trading volume being evidence of irrationality survives once the accurate parts are cleaned up? I'm trying to figure out how much of this I should actually rely on when I think about my own trades. Please write your answer in American English.
