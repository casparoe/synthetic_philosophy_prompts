Analyze Robert Moses's low-hanging overpasses on the Long Island parkways through the lens of the claim that artifacts can have politics. I'm a software engineer, and I think about this question in the context of systems I build — recommendation pipelines, access controls, default settings — but I want to test the thesis against a clearer, non-digital case. The standard story, as I understand it: Moses designed the overpasses with clearances too low for buses, which meant that people who relied on public transit — disproportionately poor and Black — could not reach Jones Beach, while car-owning suburbanites could. I want a careful mapping of this case onto the thesis that technological artifacts can embody political arrangements in their physical design, not merely in how they happen to be used. Specifically: if the overpasses are political, where exactly does the politics reside — in the physical structure itself, in Moses's intentions, in the institutional authority that let him make that design choice, or in the broader transportation system that made bus access the relevant variable in the first place? Does the case support a strong reading of the thesis (the artifact itself is political, independent of anyone's awareness of it) or only a weaker one (powerful people can use artifacts to enact political goals they already have)? And what does the case reveal about the limits of the thesis — are there ways in which treating the overpasses as "having politics" obscures as much as it illuminates?
