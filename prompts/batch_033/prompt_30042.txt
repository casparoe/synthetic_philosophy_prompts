I'm trying to settle, for myself, when, if ever, I may form a moral belief on someone else's say-so rather than by working the matter through myself, and I keep finding that the debate is conducted through single cases — a friend whose moral judgment you trust, a stranger, the moral consensus of a community, a self-described expert — while the criteria of adequacy are left implicit. Before I read any more of it, I want the specification. What must a theory of moral testimony get right, whatever verdict it finally defends, if it is to be adequate for deciding when deference is epistemically and practically legitimate?

Write the desiderata: the cases it must handle (including, at least, the trusted friend, the stranger, community consensus, and the expert who has worked through the arguments); the distinctions it cannot collapse — for example, between acquiring a moral belief from testimony and acquiring moral understanding, between testimonial knowledge and the moral worth or autonomy of acting on it, and between moral and non-moral testimony if you think that asymmetry is real; and the constraints it must respect from epistemology and moral psychology, with the consideration behind each. Then mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are merely the property of one camp; flag any pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied; and say which single requirement does the most to exclude the standard views.

Close with a short scorecard of the pure optimist, the pure pessimist, and the understanding-based view against the list — but keep the weight on the criteria, not the scoring. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence (the one requirement that does the most work, or the compressed list), then give the reasoning; no preamble, no restating of the question.
