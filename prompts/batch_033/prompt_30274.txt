I have been following the literature on the norm of assertion for a while now, and I keep having the same experience: the main positions—that assertion is governed by knowledge, by truth, by belief, by justified or reasonable belief, or by certainty—each have canonical arguments, and each has a stock reply to the others' objections. The knowledge account explains why conjunctions of the form "p, but I don't know that p" sound defective, but it seems to overreach in cases where speakers reasonably assert justified falsehoods. The truth account explains why false assertions are defective, but it struggles with the fact that a speaker can blamelessly assert a justified falsehood. The belief account handles insincere assertion, but it seems too weak: one can believe something on terrible grounds and still strike people as having violated a norm by asserting it. The reasonable-belief and certainty accounts each capture some of these cases while losing others. Nobody denies the central data; everyone agrees that assertion is somehow answerable to epistemic standards; and yet the debate appears to have reached a fixed point where the same moves are exchanged and nobody shifts.

My working hypothesis is that this is not really a single disagreement about which rule is the rule of assertion. It looks to me as though the different camps are answering different questions: some are asking what condition is constitutive of the speech act of assertion, others are asking what epistemic state a speaker must be in for an assertion to be permissible, and still others are asking what an assertion commits or entitles the speaker to. But I am not confident, and I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this a substantive disagreement about one shared target, a verbal disagreement about the word "norm," or a case of several well-posed questions being run together under the heading "the norm of assertion"? Are the sides disagreeing about the data, about which cases count as data—for instance, whether lottery propositions are assertable, or whether so-called selfless assertions are permissible—about the explanatory target, or about bedrock intuitions concerning what assertion is for?

Given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress here? A sharper distinction among types of norms (constitutive, regulative, evaluative, commitment-generating), a formal or empirical result, a taxonomy of assertion-like speech acts, or a change of question? Or should we expect the debate to remain at this fixed point, and if so, what explains that? I am less interested in a survey of the positions than in an account of the structure of the disagreement itself.
