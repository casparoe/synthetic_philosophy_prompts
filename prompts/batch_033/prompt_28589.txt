Here are my verdicts on a number of cases about existence, non-existence, and absent goods and bads. I would like you to work out what principle, if any, captures the pattern among them, and to tell me what that principle commits me to.

Case 1. A friend was invited to a wedding but could not travel. I think it was bad for her that she missed it: she was deprived of a pleasure she would otherwise have had.

Case 2. A couple consider having a second child and are reasonably confident the child would have had a very happy life, but they decide against it. I do not think they have done anything bad to the child; there is no child who was deprived of anything.

Case 3. A couple know that if they conceive now, their child will be born with a condition that causes constant, severe pain until an early death. I think conceiving would be bad. I also think that if they decide not to conceive, the fact that this suffering never occurs is good — even though there is no person who is spared it.

Case 4. Consider a merely possible person who would have had a life of pure, unalloyed pleasure. The fact that this person was never born does not seem to me to be bad in the way my friend's missed wedding was bad; I am not sure it is bad at all.

Case 5. A parent has a child and then keeps the child confined to a room, so that the child is prevented from enjoying any of the pleasures of life. I think this is very bad for the child. So the absence of pleasure is bad when there is an existing person who is deprived of it, but apparently not bad when there was never a person at all.

Case 6. A couple decide never to have children. I do not think they have wronged or harmed anyone by this decision, no matter how happy their possible children would have been.

My tentative principle is something like the asymmetry associated with Benatar: the absence of pain is good even when there is no one who experiences being spared, whereas the absence of pleasure is not bad unless there is an existing person who is deprived of it. I am uneasy about this principle, though. In Case 3 I cannot say for whom the absent pain is good, and yet I still want to say it is good; in Case 4 I cannot say for whom the absent pleasure would be bad, and that seems to settle the matter. I cannot see what justifies treating the two cases differently.

The difficulty is this. If the absence of pain is always good and the absence of pleasure is never bad when no one exists, then non-existence appears to come out ahead of existence for any life that contains even a little pain, and I am pushed towards the conclusion that coming into existence is always a harm. I reject that conclusion: I think many lives are worth starting, including lives with the ordinary mix of joy and suffering.

So my question is this. Is there one principle — or a small set of candidate principles — that captures my verdicts in the six cases, stated precisely? Does that principle, together with the rest of what I have said, commit me to antinatalism, or does that conclusion require an additional premise I have not endorsed? And if my verdicts cannot all be kept together with my rejection of antinatalism, which of them would a coherent principle force me to give up, and what would that principle say about a further case, for example a life that contains a great deal of pleasure but also some severe pain near the end?

Please answer in British English.
