I'm taking a seminar on the philosophy of cosmology this semester, and we've spent the last few weeks on the Boltzmann brain problem. I've found myself increasingly convinced of a position that I'm not sure my classmates or even my professor take seriously enough, and I'd like to pressure-test it.

Here's the view I've settled into: the Boltzmann brain problem is not just a quirky theoretical puzzle but a genuine and unresolved skeptical threat to cosmological reasoning. Specifically, I think that any cosmological model that predicts a future eternity with a positive cosmological constant — which includes the ΛCDM model we actually seem to live in — generates a situation where, over the vast timescales involved, random thermal fluctuations in the de Sitter vacuum should produce vastly more Boltzmann brains (disembodied minds that fluctuate into existence with false memories of an orderly past) than ordinary observers embedded in the earlier, structure-forming phase of the universe. If we then apply anything like a principle of typicality — that we should treat ourselves as randomly selected from the set of observer-moments — we are forced to conclude that we are overwhelmingly likely to be Boltzmann brains, which would undermine all our empirical beliefs, including the very cosmological reasoning that led us to this conclusion. The standard responses strike me as inadequate. The move of saying that we should simply reject any theory that predicts we are atypical observers seems to beg the question: it amounts to ruling out theories on the basis that they conflict with our pre-theoretic conviction that we are not Boltzmann brains, but that conviction is precisely what the argument calls into doubt. Appeals to the measure problem — the idea that we lack a principled way to count observer-moments in an infinite universe and that the right measure might assign negligible weight to Boltzmann brains — feel like a promissory note rather than a solution, since we don't have an agreed-upon measure and the choice of measure itself seems to require the very kind of empirical input the argument threatens. And the suggestion that the universe might decay before Boltzmann brains come to dominate strikes me as a cosmological bet we have no independent reason to make.

I want to know what would count against this view. I'm not asking for the standard replies I've already read in the literature; I want to know which observations, theoretical developments, or philosophical arguments would genuinely undermine my position, sorted by how decisive they would be. What would a future cosmologist have to discover about the decay rate of the false vacuum, or about the measure problem, or about the nature of observers, for the Boltzmann brain problem to recede from a skeptical threat to a mere curiosity? Are there defeaters that are already available but underappreciated? And I want an honest assessment: has my position been qualified into unfalsifiability — is it the kind of view that simply absorbs any counterargument by reinterpreting it, and if so, is that a problem for a view of this kind?
