Across a striking range of institutions, a single number produced by a timed, decontextualized cognitive test is treated as a stable fact about a person rather than as a sample of performance on one occasion. School systems use one composite cutoff on such a test to decide which children enter gifted programs and which do not. Military organizations summarize a recruit's performance across a battery of abstract subtests — vocabulary, block design, arithmetic reasoning, working-memory span — into a single qualifying score and use that score to assign people to occupational specialties. Disability adjudication systems treat a composite IQ score as central evidence about whether an adult has an intellectual disability, even when the same file contains detailed evidence of the person's actual functioning at work, at home, and in the community.

Yet these same institutions also act on assumptions that sit uneasily with that practice. They acknowledge that the same person's performance varies across the very subtests that get averaged together, and that the same composite score can be reached by very different subtest profiles — one child strong in verbal tasks and weak in spatial ones, another the reverse — while the cutoff treats the two as interchangeable. They acknowledge that coaching, practice, motivation, sleep, and test anxiety can move the number by several points. They periodically re-norm the instruments, so that the same raw performance yields a different score depending on the decade in which the person was born, and they norm scores against age groups, so that a seven-year-old and a seventy-year-old with the same raw performance receive different numbers.

Reconstruct the theory of intelligence that this gatekeeping practice presupposes, if any. Does the practice strictly presuppose that intelligence is a single, general, stable, context-independent capacity that these tests measure more or less directly, or is it compatible with a weaker view — for example, that the composite score is merely a useful predictor of certain later outcomes and carries no commitment about what intelligence is? Identify which features of the practice do the real presupposing (the averaging of heterogeneous subtests, the use of a hard cutoff, the age-norming, the discounting of everyday functioning, the periodic re-norming) and which features are merely incidental or historically contingent. Then assess whether the practice is internally coherent given the rival assumptions these same institutions also accept, and explain what would have to change in the practice if the presupposed view were replaced by a thoroughgoing alternative — for instance, a multiple-intelligences view, a dynamic view on which intelligence is a developing expertise rather than a fixed trait, or a contextual view on which intelligent behavior is relative to a domain and a setting.
