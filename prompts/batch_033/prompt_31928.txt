I am a novelist at work on a book whose protagonist exists, in recognizably different forms, across several mutually incompatible timelines, and I have found that the language of possible worlds is doing real work in my planning rather than merely decorating it. I want to understand the metaphysics well enough that the book's internal logic is coherent, and I would be grateful for an exact, in-the-weeds explanation of the difference between transworld identity and counterpart theory.

Please address the following, in order:

1. State precisely what each view claims. What does a transworld-identity theorist mean by saying that one and the same individual exists in many possible worlds, and what does the counterpart theorist mean by saying that individuals are world-bound and are represented at other worlds by counterparts? I want the semantics of de re modal statements made explicit: how does each view analyze a sentence such as 'in another timeline she is a surgeon' or 'she could have chosen otherwise'?

2. Lay out the main arguments that motivate counterpart theory over transworld identity, especially the problem of accidental intrinsics and whatever pressure it puts on the idea that a single object can bear incompatible intrinsic properties in different worlds. Explain how the transworld-identity theorist can resist these arguments, and what the resistance costs.

3. Lay out the main objections to counterpart theory, in particular the complaint that when we say a particular person might have won an election we are talking about that very person and not about some distinct individual who merely resembles her. Explain the standard counterpart-theoretic replies, and say plainly whether those replies preserve or quietly revise the intuitive meaning of 'could have been'.

4. Identify the places where the two views genuinely diverge: the status of essential and accidental properties, the question whether the relation that pairs an individual with its otherworldly representatives is an equivalence relation, the role of context and similarity in selecting counterparts, and whether identity across worlds is something stipulated or something discovered.

5. Distinguish counterpart theory itself from realism about possible worlds. I would like to know whether counterpart theory can be accepted by someone who takes worlds to be abstract representations, or whether it only makes sense alongside a fully realist picture of worlds.

6. Close with an assessment aimed at my actual problem: which of these two pictures, if either, better fits the way a novelist treats a character as one and the same individual across retellings, divergent drafts, and parallel-universe plots, and what would a novelist have to surrender to use either picture with rigor rather than as metaphor?
