I am preparing the final draft of an article on population axiology for a volume on the foundations of consequentialism, and I would be grateful for a rigorous consistency audit of the position I have arrived at before I submit the manuscript. The article is meant to stake out a unified view, so it matters a great deal whether the pieces actually cohere. Here is where I stand, stated as a set of seven commitments:

(1) The Repugnant Conclusion is genuinely repugnant: a world containing an enormous number of lives barely worth living is, all else equal, worse than a world containing a smaller number of lives of very high quality. I treat this as a fixed point that an adequate axiology must avoid.

(2) Total utilitarianism straightforwardly entails the Repugnant Conclusion; therefore total utilitarianism cannot be correct as stated, and some modification or alternative is required.

(3) I am drawn to the person-affecting intuition: roughly, that an act can be wrong only if it makes things worse for some particular person or persons. This explains, among other things, why bringing a miserable person into existence is wrong (it harms that person) while there is no symmetric obligation to bring a happy person into existence (there is no existing person who is benefited by being brought into being).

(4) I endorse the Asymmetry: we have strong moral reasons not to bring miserable lives into existence, but no corresponding moral reason to bring happy lives into existence. This strikes me as a basic and non-negotiable feature of common-sense morality.

(5) I also hold that we have substantial obligations to future generations — for instance, to mitigate climate change and to avoid depleting resources they will need — and I want my view to underwrite these obligations rather than explain them away or dismiss them as merely prudential.

(6) I find critical-level views, on which a life must exceed some positive threshold of well-being to count positively toward the value of an outcome, unsatisfying. The choice of threshold seems ineliminably arbitrary, and the view feels ad hoc — a gerrymandered fix designed solely to block the Repugnant Conclusion rather than something independently motivated.

(7) I have the intuition that merely adding happy people to an already well-off population does not make the world better, even if the added lives are well worth living. (This is sometimes called the neutrality intuition for additions to a population that is already large and flourishing.)

Please go through these commitments and identify which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to be in tension, and what the least costly revision would be to restore consistency. I would like the tensions ranked by severity rather than listed as equally problematic, and I would appreciate specificity about which commitment would have to give way in each case and what the philosophical cost of that revision would be.
