So I've been thinking about this strategic situation and I wonder if it has a name. Two players face a pot of money that starts small and grows each time someone passes. Player 1 goes first: they can take the pot or pass it to Player 2, which doubles it. Then Player 2 has the same choice — take or pass, doubling again — and this goes back and forth for, say, 100 rounds. If both keep passing, the pot gets huge and they split it at the end. Here's the puzzle: on the very last round, Player 2 has no reason to pass; they'd just grab it. But knowing that, on the second-to-last round Player 1 should grab it first, because Player 2 is going to grab it anyway next turn. And you can unwind this logic all the way back to round 1: Player 1 should take the tiny initial pot immediately. That conclusion feels absurd — both players would be way better off if they just passed a few times. And in real experiments, people do pass for many rounds and end up with much larger payouts. So there's a real tension between what the step-by-step logic recommends and what reasonable people actually do. Is there a name for this specific paradox? More importantly, I want to know what you think is actually going on — is the backward induction reasoning flawed, or is it correct and people are just being irrational, or is the game somehow mis-specified? Don't just give me a label; I want a committed take on where the mistake is, if there is one.
