My moral philosophy professor has spent several weeks on the question of why promises bind, and she has given us a series of case verdicts rather than a stated theory. So far she has held all of the following:

- If I promise to return a borrowed book on Tuesday, and the owner will be away, will not need it, and will be no worse off if I do not return it, I am still obligated to return it on Tuesday.
- If, before Tuesday, the owner tells me "never mind — keep it as long as you like," the obligation is dissolved.
- A promise extracted by a credible threat is not binding.
- A promise to help someone tamper with evidence is not binding.
- A deathbed promise to look after the promisee's child remains binding after the promisee dies.
- A promise remains binding even when the promisee has entirely forgotten it, has made no plans around it, and would feel no disappointment either way.

Next week's case is this. A visitor from a community with no institution of promising — no shared practice of creating obligations by saying "I promise" — is staying with us. I tell the visitor, "I promise to pick you up at the station on Friday." The visitor does not recognize this as a conventional act; she simply takes me to have given her a firm assurance about my future conduct, and she accepts it as such, though she has no plans that depend on my coming. Am I bound exactly as I would be by a promise to someone raised inside our practice?

I am less interested in the bare yes or no than in the rule she seems to be running. Which accounts of promissory obligation — reliance, expectation or assurance, social practice, transfer of a right to the promisee, fidelity to one's word, or something else — are still consistent with all six verdicts, and which do the six already rule out? What does each surviving account predict about the visitor case, and what is your best guess about her verdict? Finally, what further case would best discriminate between the surviving accounts?
