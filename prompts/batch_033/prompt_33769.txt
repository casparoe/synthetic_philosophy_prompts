I'm a grad student TA-ing for an undergrad decision theory course, and we're about to spend two weeks on the money pump argument. You know the classic setup: someone has intransitive preferences (A > B, B > C, C > A), and a clever trader can extract money from them by offering a series of trades that each look individually good but cycle them back to where they started minus some cash. The standard lesson is that rationality requires transitive preferences, otherwise you're exploitable.

But I've been reading around and there are actually a bunch of differnt ways people push back on this, and I want to make a handout that helps the undergrads map out the logical space instead of just memorizing a list of -isms. So I'm looking for a decision tree or flowchart: you start by asking whether the money pump argument succeeds in showing that intransitive preferences are irrational, and at each branch you get a yes/no question, and at the leaves you land on a named position. The positions I definitely want on the tree are: (1) the standard money-pump defender who says yes, intransitive preferences are irrational because they make you exploitable; (2) the sophisticated chooser who says you can have intransitive preferences but avoid being pumped by looking ahead and refusing the first trade; (3) the resolute chooser who says you can bind yourself to a plan of action and stick to it even when tempted to deviate mid-stream; (4) the just-noticeable-differences defender who says intransitive preferences are perfectly fine when the differences between options are small enough that you can't reliably discriminate them; (5) the incommensurability defender who says some values just can't be placed on a single scale, so intransitivity is sometimes the right call; and (6) the pragmatic skeptic who says money pumps are too contrived and unrealistic to tell us anything deep about rationality.

If there are positions in the logical space that nobody actually defends, flag them and give a sentence on why — is the view obviously bad, or just underexplored? Also, if any two of the leaves I listed can't actually be separated by your branching questions, be honest about that rather than papering it over. The questions themselves should be things an undergrad could answer without already knowing the labels. So dont ask "are you a sophisticated chooser?" — instead ask something like "If you knew in advance that someone might offer you a series of trades that would eventually cycle back to your starting point minus some money, could you rationally just refuse the first trade?" I want them to see where their intuitions actually land them.
