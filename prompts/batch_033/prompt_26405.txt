I'm writing a paper for my ethics seminar on supererogation, and before I dive into evaluating the major views, I want to get clear on what a theory of supererogation is even supposed to deliver. So: what are the desiderata? What cases must a theory get right — the soldier on the grenade, the stranger who donates a kidney, the person who forgives an unrepentant wrongdoer, and so on? What distinctions must it respect — between the supererogatory and the merely permissible, between supererogation and imperfect duty, between what is praiseworthly to do and what is blameworthly to omit? What puzzles must it dissolve — in particular, the central one: if a supererogatory act is morally better than a merely permissible alternative, why isn't performing it obligatory? Flag which requirements are non-negotiable constraints on any adequate view and which are optional commitments tied to particular moral frameworks (consequentialist, deontological, virtue-ethical). If any pair of desiderata turn out to be in tension — for instance, if making sense of the "beyond duty" character of supererogation ends up undermining the claim that such acts are genuinely morally better — I especially want that identified. I'll apply the list to specific theories myself; I just need the specification done rigorously.
