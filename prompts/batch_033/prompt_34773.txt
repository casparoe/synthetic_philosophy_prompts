In a discussion about paternalistic public health policies — mandatory calorie labeling, soda taxes, smoking bans — someone cited Dostoevsky's Grand Inquisitor to support the claim that most people, if given the choice, would trade their freedom for happiness and security, and that this is not a defect in them but a realistic assessment of the burdens of liberty. The phrase they kept returning to was something like "the Grand Inquisitor was right: freedom is a burden most people are better off without."

I've read the chapter, and I'm not convinced the argument is this simple. But rather than fight about what Dostoevsky "really meant," I want to get clear on what the slogan itself could be asserting. It seems to me that "freedom is a burden most people are better off without" could express anything from a psychological claim about what people actually prefer, to an ethical claim about what is good for them, to a political claim about the justification of authoritarian governance — and the move from Dostoevsky's literary argument to a policy position about soda taxes depends on which reading you adopt and whether that reading can carry the weight.

Please lay out the distinct precise claims this slogan could be taken to express, ranging from the psychological to the ethical to the political. For each, say whether it is (a) trivially true, (b) substantive and plausible, (c) substantive and dubious, or (d) too vague to evaluate. Then identify which reading or readings would actually support the use the slogan was put to in the conversation — namely, justifying paternalistic policies that override or constrain individual choice — and say whether the slogan, on those readings, holds up under scrutiny.
