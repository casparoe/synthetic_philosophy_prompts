I hold the view that consequentialist cluelessness — the fact that we cannot reliably trace the long-run, ramifying causal consequences of ordinary actions — poses a problem for consequentialism that is more serious than the standard replies acknowledge. By "more serious" I mean: it is not just an epistemic inconvenience but a structural feature of the theory that severs the connection between its criterion of rightness and any usable decision procedure, and that attempts to close the gap either collapse into something no longer recognizably consequentialist or rely on epistemic norms that consequentialism itself cannot ground.

I want to know what would count against this view. That is: what evidence, arguments, formal results, or philosophical developments should move me away from it, or at least substantially weaken it? I am not asking for the standard replies — I already know the move to expected value with a bounded probability distribution over outcomes, the appeal to sophisticated consequentialism and two-level theories, the suggestion that cluelessness is just ordinary uncertainty and no special problem, and the response that any moral theory faces analogous epistemic limits. I want to know what would count as a genuine defeater, sorted by how decisive it would be.

In particular: (1) What would a successful consequentialist reply have to achieve that existing replies do not? (2) Are there results from decision theory, formal epistemology, or elsewhere that could, if established, dissolve the problem rather than merely manage it? (3) Which of my commitments — about causation, about the structure of moral reasons, about the relationship between criteria of rightness and decision procedures — is the load-bearing one, and what would it take for that commitment to fail? (4) Are there developments in the consequentialist literature since Lenman's original paper that I may be underestimating, and what would they have to show to count as a solution rather than a restatement of the problem? (5) Is there a version of this view that is unfalsifiable or insulated against all possible counterargument, and if so, is that a problem for a view of this kind, or is it appropriate for a claim about the conceptual structure of a moral theory?

I want an honest map of the view's exposure: what it risks, what it would take for it to be wrong, and whether its defenders have reason to be worried.
