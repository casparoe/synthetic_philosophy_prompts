I've been teaching the statue and the clay puzzle in my metaphysics seminar for going on fifteen years now, and I've started to wonder whether the whole debate about material constitution is just stuck in a way that's worth diagnosing rather than trying to solve. Here's the setup, in case you somehow haven't heard it: you take a lump of clay, you shape it into a statue. Before the shaping, you had the clay. After, you've got a statue. Are the statue and the lump of clay one object or two? They're in the same place, made of the same stuff, but they seem to have different properties — the clay was there on Monday, the statue wasn't (it came into existence on Tuesday when the sculptor finished). The statue is essentially statue-shaped; the clay isn't. Squash the statue and the statue is destroyed but the clay survives.

Every position on this has been on the table for decades. Monists say there's just one object and we're getting confused by sortal predicates — the statue just *is* the clay, described differently. Pluralists say there are two coincident objects and we need to get comfortable with that, which then forces them into the grounding problem (what makes them different if they're made of exactly the same matter?). Nihilists say there are no statues at all, just clay arranged statue-wise, and if you're willing to bite that bullet the puzzle dissolves. Constitution theorists say the clay constitutes the statue without being identical to it, which sounds nice but then you have to explain what "constitution" is without it collapsing into identity. All the moves are known. The arguments are known. Nobody's budging.

So here's my question: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? I'm starting to suspect it's not a substantive metaphysical dispute at all, but a clash of bedrock intuitions about what it even means to be an object — and if that's all it is, then maybe the whole thing is just a verbal dispute about how to use the word "object" dressed up in technical language. But I'm not sure. Diagnose this for me. Is there a factual question hidden in here somewhere? Are the sides just working with different criteria for individuation that they can't argue for because they're basic? Or is it like the disagreement is really about what we want from a metaphysical theory — elegance, fidelity to ordinary language, some other theoretical virtue? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress, or should I just tell my students the debate is a dead end and move on to something else?
