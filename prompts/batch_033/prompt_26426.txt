I've been following political debates for years, and I keep noticing a dialectical pattern that I don't have a name for. It goes like this. One side makes a moral claim: "This policy is unjust." The other side responds: "And what are you going to do about it? You don't have the power to change anything." This move treats the moral claim as incomplete — even irrelevant — without force behind it. But then, when that same second side exercises power, the first side responds: "You may have the power, but you have no right to use it." This move treats force as illegitimate without justice behind it.

What interests me is that these two moves are not just symmetric — they seem to feed on each other. The more you insist that justice needs force to matter, the more you invite the reply that force needs justice to be legitimate. And the more you insist on that, the more you invite the first move again. Each side's argument undermines the other's, but neither can do without what the other supplies. The result is that political argument oscillates between appeals to justice that are powerless and exercises of power that are unjustified, with each pole constantly pointing to the deficiency of the other.

I'm trying to find out whether this specific pattern — the mutual dependence and mutual destabilization of justice and force — has a name in political philosophy. I know it's somewhere in the neighborhood of discussions about legitimacy, the enforcement problem for justice, and the relation between morality and power. But I'm after the specific dialectical structure: the way each concept needs the other to be effective, yet each also undermines the other's claim to stand alone. Is there an established term for this, and if so, who has analyzed it most carefully? And if not, what would you call it, and how would you distinguish it from related ideas such as the distinction between normative and descriptive legitimacy, or the circularity worry about the rule of law?
