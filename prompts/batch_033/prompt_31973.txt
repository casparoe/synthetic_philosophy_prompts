I retired a few years ago from a career in molecular biology — spent most of it on ribosome structure, actually — and I've been using the free time to read philosophy and adjacent stuff I never got around to. A friend recommended Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach and I've been working through it slowly, taking notes and everything. There's a distinction he draws that I find genuinely interesting but I'm not sure I've fully internalized it: the one about frame messages, outer messages, and inner messages. I get the basic idea as he lays it out. The frame message is the information that tells you how to interpret what follows — it says something like "this is a message, and here's the decoding scheme." The inner message is what you get once you apply that decoding scheme: the content proper. And the outer message is something conveyed by the fact that the inner message was sent at all, in the particular way it was sent, by the particular sender, in the particular context. So far so good.

But when I try to apply this triad to concrete cases, the boundaries start to blur in ways that bother me. Take a DNA sequence in a cell. The frame message would be the genetic code itself — the codon-to-amino-acid mapping — plus the ribosome and the whole translation apparatus that implements it. The inner message would be the primary structure of the protein. But what exactly is the outer message? Is it something about the evolutionary history that produced that sequence — the selection pressures, the historical contingencies? Or is it the fact that this particular sequence was conserved across lineages, which tells you something about its functional importance? And then I start wondering about a subtler case: suppose a mutation introduces a premature stop codon. The inner message is a truncated protein. But the outer message might be something like "this organism is under mutational stress" or "this gene is dispensible enough that the cell hasn't bothered to fix it." Except now the outer message seems to depend on my background knowledge about mutation rates and selection — it's not really carried by the DNA molecule in the way the inner message is. So the outer message feels less like it's located in the signal and more like it's located in the relationship between the signal and my model of the world.

The same worry comes up in human communication. If a friend texts me "I'm fine," the frame message is English grammar and lexicon, the inner message is roughly the proposition that their state is satisfactory, and the outer message might be "I want you to stop asking" or even "I'm not fine but I don't want to talk about it." But to pick up that outer message I already need to have decoded the inner message and then done a bunch of extra inference about my friend's psychology and our relationship. So the outer message seems to be a second-order interpretation layered on top of the inner message, not something that sits alongside it at the same level. And then I think: couldn't I just as well say that the "I'm not fine" reading is actually the inner message, and the words "I'm fine" are just the vehicle for it, with irony or social convention doing the decoding work that English grammar normally does? In which case the frame message would have to include all that pragmatic knowledge too, and the whole triad becomes context-dependent in a way that makes me wonder whether it carves nature at the joints at all.

I guess my question is whether there's a principled way to draw these three categories so they don't collapse into each other under pressure, or whether the triad is better understood as a heuristic for noticing different aspects of how meaning gets located rather than a strict ontology. And if you think the distinction holds up, I'd love a walkthrough of a couple of cases — maybe one from molecular biology and one from everyday communication — where you can point to exactly what belongs in each category and why a given piece of information can't be reassigned to a different one. Please write the answer in plain continuous prose, the way you'd write an email or a letter — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables. And American English, please.
