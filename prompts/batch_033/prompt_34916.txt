Ok so I'm in this philosophy of probability seminar and I've gotten myself tangled up in an argument that seems to show that none of the three big interpretations of probability actually works, and I'm pretty sure I've made a mistake somewhere but I can't find it. The conclusion feels way too strong — like, probability theory is clearly doing *something* right, both in science and in everyday reasoning — so the argument has to have a flaw. But every time I try to locate it I end up thinking the premises are all individually plausible. Here's the argument:

(1) If a probability statement like "P(rain tomorrow) = 0.3" is meaningful, it must be interpreted either objectively (as a fact about the world) or subjectively (as a fact about someone's mind).

(2) The only live objective interpretations are frequentism and propensity. (Classical probability à la Laplace is dead because of the principle of indifference problems.)

(3) Frequentism says P(rain tomorrow) = 0.3 means that in a suitable reference class of "days like tomorrow," rain occurs 30% of the time. But there are infinitely many ways to carve up the reference class (same city and month? same pressure reading? same season across all years on record?), and different carvings give different frequencies. So frequentism either makes probability arbitrary (pick your reference class) or undefined (there's no priviledged carving).

(4) Propensity says the chance setup — the atmospheric conditions, etc. — has a physical disposition of strength 0.3 to produce rain. But what *is* a disposition of strength 0.3, exactly? If we cash it out as "would produce rain in 30% of trials under the same conditions," we're back to frequentism and its reference class problem. If we don't cash it out, we've just renamed the mystery.

(5) So objective probability is either arbitrary or mysterious.

(6) Subjectivism says P(rain tomorrow) = 0.3 is a degree of belief, constrained only by the probability axioms (coherence). But coherence alone doesn't pin down any particular number: assigning 0.3, 0.7, or 0.99 to rain tomorrow can all be coherent, provided you adjust your other beliefs accordingly.

(7) If coherence is the only constraint, then there's no sense in which a subjective probability can be *wrong* about the world — only incoherent. But we do criticize probability assignments as wrong: if you assign 0.99 to rain tomorrow in the Sahara in July, you're not just incoherent, you've made a mistake about how the world works.

(8) The subjectivist can reply that your degree of belief should be calibrated to the objective chance, when one exists. But this just pushes the problem back: it presupposes we already have a working notion of objective chance, which is exactly what (3)–(5) called into question.

(9) So subjective probability is either unconstrained opinion, or it leans on an objective notion we can't supply.

(10) Therefore, no interpretation of probability works: probability statements are either arbitrary, mysterious, or mere opinion.

(11) But probability statements are indispensable in science (statistical mechanics, quantum theory, climate modeling) and in rational decision-making.

(12) So an indispensable part of science and rationality rests on philosophical foundations that don't hold up.
