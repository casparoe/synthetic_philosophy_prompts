I'm taking an aesthetics seminar this term, and last week we read a pair of essays that seem to generate an argument I can't find my way out of. I've reconstructed it below, and I'd like you to tell me where it goes wrong — or, if it doesn't go wrong in the strict sense, which premise you think is least costly to abandon and why.

(1) Aesthetic judgments — "this sonnet is beautiful," "that building is ugly" — are expressions of sentiment. When I call a poem beautiful, I am reporting my own feeling of pleasure in response to it, not attributing a mind-independent property to the poem the way I would if I said it has fourteen lines.

(2) Sentiments are not truth-apt. If you find the same poem dull and I find it moving, neither of us has made a factual error. We simply respond differently, and there is no further fact about the poem that could settle which response is the correct one.

(3) Therefore, aesthetic judgments are not the sort of thing that can be correct or incorrect in the way factual claims are. No aesthetic judgment is, strictly speaking, truer than its negation.

(4) If no aesthetic judgment is truer than its negation, then the judgment that the St. Matthew Passion is a greater artistic achievement than a thirty-second advertising jingle is no more correct than the judgment that the jingle is the greater achievement. The two judgments are, at bottom, on a par — just different sentiments.

(5) But that conclusion is impossible to live with. We do not in practice treat all aesthetic judgments as equal. We argue about art, we revise our tastes, we defer to certain critics and not others, we speak of some people as having better taste or a more refined ear, and we think someone who genuinely cannot perceive any difference in merit between Bach and a jingle is missing something — not merely different from us, but worse at engaging with music.

(6) So the argument must contain a mistake. But where? I find (1) and (2) individually plausible, and (3) seems to follow from them. The step from (3) to (4) looks straightforward. And yet (5) seems undeniable. So either one of the premises is false after all, or one of the inferences is invalid, or the conclusion doesn't actually conflict with (5) in the way I think it does.

I'd like you to diagnose the argument. Identify the first step you think fails, explain why it looks plausible, and spell out what abandoning it would commit us to. If you think the argument is valid and the real work is choosing which premise to reject, then walk me through the costs of each option.
