I'm putting together a handout for a metaphysics reading group that's about to work through van Inwagen's Material Beings, and I want a decision tree that takes someone from their answers about a handful of concrete cases to a named position on the special composition question. The leaves I care about are: mereological nihilism, universalism, van Inwagen's organicism (the 'life' answer), Markosian's brutalism, and at least one of the old moderate answers like contact or fastening. If composition-as-identity can be worked in, do it, but if it cuts across the tree instead of sitting on a branch, say so.

The branch nodes should be things a person can answer without knowing any of the labels — cases, not doctrine. So: a hammer's handle and head — one object or two? The handle and a random pebble on Mars — do they compose something? A living cat and the same particles just after it dies — is there still a cat-shaped composite there? Whether it's acceptable for there to be simply no statable principle about when composition happens. Whether borderline cases of composition are possible (a heap losing grains, that sort of thing), because that's where the restricted answers get squeezed.

I'd like you to build the tree from the logical space first — the questions and the yes/no combinations they allow — and then locate the actual named positions inside it. If a combination is empty, tell me that and hazard a guess at why: incoherent, just unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any two leaves the tree can't genuinely seperate and say what further question would split them. Keep it to something that would fit on one or two pages of a handout.

Also keep apart what's settled and what's still contested among people working on this. That the contact answer is basically a punching bag is pretty settled; how badly the vagueness argument damages every restricted answer is not. Don't present a contested take as the consensus.
