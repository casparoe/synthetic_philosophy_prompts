I have been working through L.A. Paul's argument about transformative experience and have reconstructed it as follows:

(1) A decision is rational only if the agent can assign subjective values to the possible outcomes and select the option with the highest expected value.
(2) To assign a subjective value to an outcome, one must be able to project what it would be like to live in that outcome — to imaginatively grasp the phenomenology and evaluative standpoint of one's future self in that outcome.
(3) An experience is epistemically transformative if it teaches you something you could not have learned without undergoing it, and personally transformative if it changes your core preferences or values in a way you cannot fully anticipate.
(4) For an experience that is both epistemically and personally transformative, you cannot project what it would be like from the perspective of your future self: the epistemic novelty means you lack the relevant concepts or imaginative access, and the personal transformation means the evaluative standpoint you would need to occupy is not yet yours.
(5) Therefore, by (2) and (4), you cannot assign subjective values to the outcomes of such an experience.
(6) Therefore, by (1) and (5), you cannot make a rational decision about whether to undergo it.
(7) Having a child is widely taken to be both epistemically and personally transformative in the relevant sense.
(8) Therefore, by (6) and (7), the decision to have a child cannot be rational.
(9) Yet we routinely treat such decisions as paradigmatic exercises of practical reason: we deliberate, weigh considerations, seek advice, and hold one another accountable for how we decide.
(10) So either the argument is unsound, or our ordinary practices of practical reasoning are systematically deluded when it comes to life's most consequential choices.
