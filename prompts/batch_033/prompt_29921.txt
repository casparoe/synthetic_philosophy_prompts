I got into an argument at a workshop about population ethics, and I've realized I can state my view much more easily than I can defend it against a determined critic. The view is the procreation asymmetry, roughly: if a child would have a life not worth living, that fact is a strong reason not to bring them into existence; but if a child would have a life well worth living, the fact that they would be happy is not by itself a reason to bring them into existence. The absence of a possible happy person doesn't strike me as a loss in the same way the presence of a possible miserable person strikes me as a harm. I don't accept the stronger claim that coming into existence is always a net harm, and I think the asymmetry can be held without it, but I do treat the asymmetry as a fixed point that any theory of procreative ethics has to accommodate.

I want to know how exposed this actually is. What would have to be true for the asymmetry to be false, or for me to be rationally required to give it up? I'm after the defeaters sorted by how decisive they are: the cases where it gives the wrong verdict, the arguments that it commits me to something I should reject, the nearby asymmetries it might force me into accepting, and the places where it might collapse into a person-affecting view or into totalism without me noticing. Which objections should a honest defender of the asymmetry concede as real costs, and which are just smuggling in the conclusion? Also, is the view as I've stated it precise enough to be falsifiable at all, and if it's not, is that a genuine problem for a principle like this or just the normal condition of a normative commitment? I'd rather have the unflattering version than a list of ways to keep it no matter what.
