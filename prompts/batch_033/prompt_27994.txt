I run a small philosophy-and-film discussion group, and next month we're watching Gattaca. I want to frame the discussion around procreative beneficence and liberal eugenics, not the usual discrimination-is-bad reading. Here's what's been nagging at me: the film depicts a world where nearly everyone is genetically selected, and the one natural-born protagonist has to fake his way in by borrowing a valid person's identity. Julian Savulescu's procreative beneficence principle says parents choosing among possible children have a moral reason to pick the one expected to have the best life. If you take that seriously, the parents in Gattaca are mostly doing the right thing at the individual level, and yet the society they produce is a nightmare. I can't tell wether the film is attacking that principle, attacking the social system built on top of it, or just attacking a strawman where genes are destiny. The film also leans hard on the idea that Vincent's grit beats his brother's genetic advantages, but that seems like a point about determinism, not about whether selecting for better expected prospects is right.

Read the film through that lens for me. Where does it genuinely put pressure on procreative beneficence and liberal eugenics, and where does it trade on a confusion (say, between old state eugenics and parental choice, or between selection itself and a genetic caste system)? What would a defender of Savulescu or Agar say back to the film, and which scenes should I point the group to for that back-and-forth? Give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no introduction or conclusion.
