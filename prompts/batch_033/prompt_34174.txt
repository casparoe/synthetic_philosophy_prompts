I've been working through the accuracy-first program in epistemology, and I've hit an argument that seems to show the framework either collapses into toothlessness or forces a principle everyone rejects. It's too strong a conclusion to accept, but I can't find the exit. I'll lay it out and hope you can tell me which step to reject and what the damage is.

The accuracy-first program evaluates credences by their proximity to the truth. Its central move is to rule out credence functions that are outclassed in accuracy terms. Two flagship results anchor the program:

- Probabilism: any credence function violating the probability axioms is strictly accuracy-dominated — some alternative is more accurate in every possible world. (Joyce)
- Conditionalization: any plan for updating one's credences that diverges from conditionalizing on the evidence has lower expected accuracy than some alternative plan, where expectation is computed relative to the agent's own current credences. (Greaves and Wallace)

Now here is the argument that seems to prove too much.

(1) If expected-accuracy dominance relative to a privileged probability function is enough to establish irrationality — which the conditionalization argument assumes — then we need a story about which function earns the privilege and why.

(2) In the conditionalization argument, the privileged function is simply the agent's current credence function, Cr_now. But why?

(3) The natural answer: Cr_now encodes the agent's total evidence and her best rational response to it. So it is the right baseline for computing expected accuracy. Call this the Current-Lights View.

(4) Now consider an agent who knows, at noon, that at 1 p.m. she will be given a drug that temporarily impairs her reasoning without supplying any new evidence. She knows the drug will cause her to assign credence 0.95 to some proposition P, while her current total evidence — by her own careful assessment — supports only 0.5.

(5) Should the agent, at noon, defer to her future drugged self and adopt 0.95? Intuitively no. She knows the drug distorts reasoning; she should hold at 0.5.

(6) But what accuracy-based test does Cr_drug fail? If we evaluate it by expected accuracy relative to Cr_now, it indeed fares poorly — but that only tells us Cr_drug is irrational by current lights. It does not tell us Cr_now should refuse to defer to Cr_drug. To get that verdict, we would need to say that the privileged probability function is not Cr_now but rather the function that best responds to the agent's total evidence — where total evidence includes the fact that she will soon take the drug.

(7) But now the trouble spreads. If the privileged function is whatever function best responds to total evidence, then the fact about her future credence — that at 1 p.m. she will assign 0.95 to P — is itself part of her total evidence at noon. Should that fact push her current credence toward 0.95? That sounds very much like the Reflection Principle: roughly, that one's current credence should equal one's expected future credence.

(8) The Reflection Principle has well-known counterexamples — forgetfulness cases, cognitive impairment, anticipated loss of evidence — and the drug case is squarely among them. It is generally agreed that Reflection is false as a universal requirement.

(9) So we face a dilemma. Horn one: stick with the Current-Lights View, on which Cr_now is always privileged. Then the framework never criticizes an agent with stubbornly irrational priors, because those priors serve as their own baseline for expected-accuracy calculations. The framework becomes too weak — it cannot say why an agent who assigns credence 0.99 to a patent falsehood is doing anything wrong, so long as her credences are formally coherent and she conditionalizes. Horn two: adopt a more objective privileged function — the one that best responds to total evidence. But then the framework appears to force Reflection, which is false.

(10) Either way, accuracy-first epistemology looks untenable: it is either too permissive or it overgenerates a discredited norm.

I am most suspicious of step (3) — the claim that the Current-Lights View is the right story about why Cr_now gets to set the baseline for expected accuracy — but I cannot see how to modify it without pulling down the conditionalization argument along with it. Where exactly does this argument go wrong, and what does a satisfactory repair cost the broader accuracy-first program?
