I'm a graduate student in philosophy, and I've been working through some debates about rational choice and preference structure. Recently I picked up a book aimed at a general audience — it's one of those "philosophy for the interested layperson" volumes that covers a dozen topics in breezy chapters — and I came across a passage on money pump arguments that I've been turning over in my head. The author is trying to explain why transitivity of preferences is a requirement of rationality, and they offer the following summary:

> Suppose you have intransitive preferences: you prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A. A clever bookie can then offer you a series of trades. Since you prefer C to A, you'll pay a small amount to swap A for C. Then since you prefer B to C, you'll pay to swap C for B. Then since you prefer A to B, you'll pay to swap B for A. You're now back where you started, holding A, but you've paid three times. The bookie can repeat this indefinitely, draining your wallet. This proves that intransitive preferences are irrational because they license a Dutch book against you — you'd be willing to accept a set of bets that guarantees you a loss no matter what happens. What's more, since anyone with intransitive preferences can be exploited in this way, we can see that preferences must be transitive as a matter of logic, not just prudence.

Something about this bothers me, but I can't quite pin it down. I've read enough to know that Dutch book arguments and money pump arguments are usually treated as distinct things — one involves synchronic bets on states of the world, the other involves diachronic trades — and that this matters for what kind of rationality constraint each is supposed to vindicate. I also vaguely recall that the money pump doesn't straightforwardly "prove" intransitive preferences are irrational, because a sophisticated agent who foresees the whole sequence might simply refuse the first trade, and that this raises a deeper question about whether the argument targets preferences themselves or only a certain kind of myopic choice procedure. But I'm not confident about any of this, and the book's author presumably knows more than I do.

Could you go through the summary sentence by sentence and tell me what's accurate, what's subtly misleading, and what a careful defender of money pump arguments would actually claim? I'd especially like to know whether the author's slide from "this is a money pump" to "this is a Dutch book" to "this is a matter of logic" holds up, and whether the conclusion that intransitive preferences are irrational follows from the accurate parts of the story.
