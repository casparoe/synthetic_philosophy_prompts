Suppose you're advising a small philosophy of mind research group—perhaps four or five people, funded for five years—that wants to make the most progress on the paradox of phenomenal judgment for epiphenomenalism. The paradox, briefly: if phenomenal consciousness is causally inert, then our judgments about our own conscious experiences (including the judgment that we are having them) cannot be caused by those experiences. But if our phenomenal judgments are not caused by the experiences they are about, it is unclear why we should trust them—which seems to undercut the very introspective evidence that motivates realism about consciousness, and a fortiori epiphenomenalism, in the first place.

The group could pursue any of several broad strategies. Among them: (a) developing a theory of phenomenal judgment that explains how physically-caused judgments can nonetheless reliably track phenomenal facts, without phenomenal causation; (b) arguing that the paradox rests on a mistaken epistemology of introspection, and that we do not need a causal connection to know our own experiences; (c) abandoning epiphenomenalism for a view that grants consciousness causal powers while preserving whatever arguments made epiphenomenalism attractive in the first place; (d) rejecting the framing of the problem wholesale—for instance, by adopting a metaphysics on which the physical–phenomenal distinction itself is misguided, so the paradox never gets off the ground; or (e) some other line of work you regard as more promising.

Which of these strategies (or others you would propose) should the group prioritize, and in what order? For each, I want to know three things. First, what would count as real progress on it within five years—not a full resolution necessarily, but a concrete advance that would change how the rest of the field engages with the paradox. Second, how much of the broader debate about consciousness depends on getting this piece right; that is, how upstream is it? Third, what is the biggest internal obstacle—the thing most likely to cause the line of work to stall, whether a technical impasse, a reliance on contested background commitments, or a risk of terminological deadlock. I am less interested in a survey of existing positions than in a defended research agenda: where would five years of focused work actually shift the dialectic, and why there rather than elsewhere?
