I'm a journalist who covers philosophy and social science, and a few years ago I wrote a piece on Samuel Scheffler's argument about the collective afterlife — the idea, roughly, that the badness of human extinction cannot be reduced to the sum of the individual deaths it would involve, because many of our values and projects depend for their coherence on the background assumption that humanity will continue after we are gone. I found the argument deeply persuasive, and I have defended it in conversation ever since. But I've started to worry that I have never really subjected it to a serious stress test. I want to know what would count against it.

The core of the position, as I understand it, is this: if we were told that humanity would go extinct painlessly thirty days after our own death, we would not react with mere sadness at the loss of lives we will not share — we would experience a kind of evaluative collapse. The projects that give our lives meaning would seem pointless; the value we place on things like curing cancer or advancing knowledge would be undermined. This reveals that our values presuppose an ongoing human future in a way that standard ethical theories miss. The badness of extinction is not just the aggregated badness of the deaths; it is the destruction of the conditions that make our values intelligible in the first place.

I want a map of this position's exposure. What observations, arguments, intuitions, or thought experiments would count against it? Which would be fatal, and which merely awkward? What would defenders of the view themselves concede as counting against it, and what would they dismiss as misunderstanding the claim? I am especially interested in whether the position has been insulated — whether the move from "most people would feel this way" to "this reveals something about the structure of value" can be challenged, and whether the doomsday and infertility scenarios are doing genuine argumentative work or simply dramatizing a weaker claim that everyone already accepts. I would also like to know whether there is empirical work on how people actually respond to these scenarios that either supports or undercuts the argument, and whether the view has been qualified in ways that make it unfalsifiable — and if so, whether that is a problem for a view of this kind. Please write the answer in continuous prose, the way you would write a letter or an essay: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
