I've been rereading Howards End this month after a close friendship of mine kind of fell apart, and it keeps hitting me that the novel is full of the exact two things G. E. Moore says are the greatest goods: the pleasures of personal affection and the enjoyment of beautiful things. That's a view I'd always nodded along with, but now it feels less obvious. I'd like a careful reading of the novel through Moore's views on beauty and friendship. Does Howards End vindicate the idea that these two are the highest goods, complicate it, or quietly turn against it? Specifically: where does the novel treat beauty and affection as genuinely good in themselves, and where does it make them look like luxuries the Schlegels can afford because people like Leonard Bast can't? I keep thinking about the concert scene and about how the sisters' whole life of art and talk runs on inherited money, and about the fact that the person who most wants into that life gets destroyed by it. And the ending, when everyone is settled at Howards End — is that Moore's ideal realized, or a compromise the book is half-ashamed of? I'm not looking for a 'Forster was influenced by Moore' trivia hunt, more a reading that takes both Moore's theory and the novel seriously, and says what the novel adds to the philosophy — maybe something about justice, or about who gets to have beauty and friendship at all. Dont worry about settling it neatly; I want the places where the novel resists Moore as much as the places it fits.
