I'm a graduate student in economics, but I've been reading some philosophy of probability on the side, and I keep tripping over the same issue. In my coursework, we treat probabilities as point values — you run a regression, you get a confidence interval, but the underlying framework assumes there's a single probability distribution governing everything. In practice, though, I often find myself in situations where I can't honestly settle on one distribution. When I'm trying to forecast something — say, the likelihood that a particular policy will be enacted, or the chance that a new technology will reach a certain adoption threshold by 2030 — I don't have a single number. I have something more like a range, and within that range I can't defensibly pick one value over another. It's not that I'm completely ignorant; I can rule out extremes. But my uncertainty has a kind of thickness to it that a single probability seems to misrepresent.

I've tried describing this to colleagues, and they usually say something like "just take your best guess" or "use a uniform prior over the range." But both of those feel like they're smuggling in information I don't have. A uniform prior over [0.2, 0.6] is a very specific claim about the world, and I can't justify it any more than I can justify 0.4. What I want to say is something like: my belief state is the whole set of probability distributions that are consistent with what I know, and I shouldn't have to artificially pick one.

I've come across a few terms that seem adjacent — "Knightian uncertainty," "ambiguity aversion," "robust Bayesianism" — but each time I look into them, they seem to be about something slightly different. Knightian uncertainty, as I understand it, is about the distinction between situations where you know the probabilities and situations where you don't even have that. But I'm talking about cases where I can assign probabilities, I just can't assign them uniquely. And ambiguity aversion is about preferences — about how people behave when faced with unclear probabilities — whereas I'm asking about the prior epistemological question: what is the right way to represent a state of uncertainty that doesn't resolve to a single distribution? So my question is: is there an established name for the position that rational belief should be represented by sets of probability distributions rather than by a single distribution? And if there is, I'd like to understand more than just the label — I want to know whether this view has the resources to explain why "just pick one" is a mistake, and whether it runs into its own version of the arbitrariness problem when you have to specify the boundaries of the set. If there isn't an established name, I'd be curious to hear how you would characterize the view and what its main challenges are.
