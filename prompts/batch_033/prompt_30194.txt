I've been trying to get clear on the line between manipulation and legitimate persuasion, and an argument I built from a standard account now seems to prove far too much.

(1) An influence is manipulative just in case it moves someone to a decision by bypassing or subverting their capacity for rational deliberation, rather than by engaging it with reasons.
(2) Framing a choice — for instance, telling a patient that a surgery has a 90% survival rate rather than a 10% mortality rate — predictably changes what people choose, and it does so through a known cognitive bias, not by adding any new consideration.
(3) So a doctor who gets a patient's consent by choosing the survival frame manipulates the patient.
(4) But the mechanism at work in framing — shifting salience, emphasis, ordering, and emotional coloring — is also at work in a great deal of ordinary, unremarkable communication: a friend who stresses one side of a decision, a teacher who makes a point vivid with an example, a colleague who chooses which item to mention first in a list of options.
(5) If the doctor's framing is manipulation, then so are the friend's emphasis, the teacher's example, and the colleague's ordering.
(6) That conclusion seems absurd: we want "manipulation" to pick out a fairly specific kind of wrongful influence, not to indict most everyday persuasion.
(7) So the account in (1) must be false. But I'm not sure where the argument goes wrong, because each step looked fine on its own.

I'd like to keep the verdict that the doctor's framing and the friend's emphasis are not manipulative, and I'd like to keep manipulation as a real and relatively narrow category. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd also like to know how the account you end up with handles a harder case in the same neighborhood: a website that lets you subscribe in one click but requires several confusing screens and a phone call to cancel.
