I have long held the view that there are free-floating rationales — normative reasons for action that do not require a reasoner. By this I mean that certain considerations count in favor of certain actions, and against others, quite independently of whether they connect to the aims, desires, or concerns of any particular agent. The fact that a child is drowning gives anyone in a position to help a reason to do so, and this reason does not depend on whether saving the child happens to serve that person's projects or satisfy their preferences. In the terminology of the literature, I am committed to the existence of agent-neutral reasons, and I take this commitment to rule out reductive desire-based accounts of normativity, as well as constitutivist views that tie all reasons to the structure of agency.

I am now trying to be honest with myself about what would count against this view. I am not asking for a survey of objections — I am familiar with the standard moves. Instead, I want a map of the view's exposure. What would have to be true, or what would have to be shown, for the view to be false or substantially weakened? I am interested in the full range of defeaters: arguments that would be decisive if they succeeded, empirical findings or psychological results that would create serious trouble, cases the view cannot describe, theoretical costs it might be forced to absorb, and ways it might collapse into a rival position. I want to know, for each, whether defenders of free-floating reasons would themselves concede that it counts against them, or whether it is only pressed by critics from outside the view. I also want to know what the view predicts or explains that its rivals do not — what does it get right, and would losing those explanatory advantages itself count as a serious defeater? And I want an honest assessment of whether the view has been insulated by qualifications, and if so, whether that insulation is a problem — does it reflect a healthy refinement of the view, or a slide into unfalsifiability?

Finally, rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. I should add that I find the argument from the metaphysics of reasons particularly troubling: the charge that free-floating reasons are ontologically mysterious, that they demand a kind of normative realism that is hard to square with a naturalistic picture of the world. If that argument can be made precise, I suspect it would be the one that moves me most. But I am open to being told that something else should worry me more.
