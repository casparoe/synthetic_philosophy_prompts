I recently finished writing a nonfiction book. I checked every factual claim against the best evidence I could find, and I would defend any single one of them if pressed. I genuinely believe each claim in the book. And yet I also know, from long and humbling experience, that any book-length work of this kind contains at least one mistake somewhere. I do not know which claim is wrong, but I would be surprised if they were all right. So I seem to hold that P1 is true, P2 is true, and so on for every claim in the book, while also holding that the conjunction of P1 through Pn is false. This feels awkward but not obviously irrational; I cannot find which belief I should give up, and giving up either the belief in each individual claim or the belief that the book contains at least one error would strike me as dishonest.

Is there an established name for this configuration, and if so, are there important distinctions between different versions of it that I should know about? I am not satisfied with a bare label; I want to understand whether I am actually being irrational, what principle of rational belief would have to go to make room for this, and whether the puzzle changes depending on whether the claims are about independent matters of fact rather than, say, the outcomes of a structured chance setup. If there is no name, what would you call it and how would you define it so as to capture exactly this predicament and not a different one?

I would prefer the response in continuous prose, the way one would write a letter or an essay — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, please.
