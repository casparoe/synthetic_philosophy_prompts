The dispute between Mencius and Xunzi over human nature (xing) has run for over two thousand years and still organizes much contemporary work in Chinese and comparative philosophy, yet the two positions seem no closer to resolution than when Xunzi first set himself against Mencius. Mencius holds that human nature is good: we possess incipient moral dispositions — compassion, shame, deference, and a sense of right and wrong — that develop into full virtue when properly nurtured, as a sprout tends toward a mature plant. Xunzi holds that human nature is bad: our innate desires and partiality, left to themselves, generate contention and disorder, and everything good in a person is acquired through deliberate effort — ritual, teaching, and social convention — which reshapes the raw material rather than unfolding it. Both positions agree that ordinary people can become sages and that cultivation is indispensable; the disagreement is over whether the material on which cultivation works is already oriented toward the good or must be redirected from its natural course.

I have been trying to work out whether the contemporary revival of this debate is worth joining directly, and my working hypothesis is that the dispute persists because it is not one well-formed disagreement. Mencius's xing looks like a teleological notion — the nature of a thing is its characteristic pattern of development when it flourishes — while Xunzi's xing looks closer to an initial, unregulated endowment of desires and capacities, so that the claim that human nature is good and the claim that human nature is bad may be answers to two different questions sharing one word. But I suspect that diagnosis is too deflationary, and that underneath it lies a substantive disagreement about human moral psychology that cannot be dissolved by a distinction. Diagnose this debate: what kind of disagreement is it — verbal or substantive, a difference over the meaning of xing, a difference over which state of a person reveals their nature, a hidden empirical question about the untaught child, or something else? Engage what each side actually maintains rather than reducing them to slogans, and then say what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a conceptual distinction, an empirical finding, a reformulation of the question, or an honest conclusion that the question is ill-posed and should be retired. I want a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of the positions.
