The setup: a guy leaves a bar, pretty out of it. He's driving home and there are two exits on the way. First exit dumps him in a bad part of town (payoff zero). If he stays on the road he reaches a second exit, which is his actual route home (payoff 4). If he blows past both he ends up way out on the highway (payoff 1). The weird part is he's so absent-minded that whenever he's at an intersection, he has no idea which one it is — first or second. So before he starts driving he has to pick a probability p of exiting at whatever intersection he finds himself at, and that's his whole strategy.

First guy (Dan) said this is straightforward: you just maximize expected value from the starting point. The math gives p = 1/3 with an expected payoff of 4/3, end of story. The driver commits to this and executes.

Second guy (Mike) said: hold on. Imagine you're actually at an intersection mid-drive. You don't know which one it is, but you can compute the probability you're at the first vs. the second given your own strategy. And if you do that, and then ask what maximizes expected payoff from right here, you might get a different answer than what your original plan says. He said this means the ex ante plan isn't stable — the driver would have reason to deviate once he's actually at an intersection, and that's a problem because the whole setup assumes he can't distinguish them, so he can't condition his behavior on which one he's at.

Dan said that's confused: the driver knew from the start he'd face this exact situation, he already took it into account when he picked p = 1/3, and there's no new information at the intersection that should change his mind. He said Mike was effectively trying to have the driver re-optimize at each intersection while also treating his own future choices as given, which double-counts something.

Mike said no, the point is deeper — the puzzle exposes a tension in how decision theory handles cases where your current self and your future self are in epistemically indistinguishable situations but your actions at one point affect the probabilities of reaching the other. He said there's a whole literature on whether the standard solution is right or whether it papers over a genuine inconsistency, and that reasonable people still disagree.
