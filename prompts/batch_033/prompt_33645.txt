I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I've been trying to get clear about what I think about moral conflicts: situations where it seems like I can't do everything I ought to do. I have strong reactions to a set of cases, but I can't see the principle behind them. I'd like you to help me find it, or tell me which of my reactions I should give up.

Here are the cases and my verdicts.

1. Double-booked promises. Last week I promised a friend I'd help them move on Saturday, and then forgot and promised another friend I'd review a critical pull request at the same time. I can only do one. Verdict: this isn't a genuine dilemma. The mess traces back to my carelessness; at the moment of choice I should pick the weightier commitment, do it, and apologize to the other person. Being in a bind now doesn't mean I face two real obligations at once.

2. The student in wartime. A student must choose between staying to care for his mother, who depends on him and will likely not survive without him, and leaving to join the resistance against an occupying army. Both strike me as weighty obligations, and neither seems to override the other. Verdict: a genuine dilemma. Whichever he does, he fails at something he genuinely owed; there is no fully right option.

3. The runaway vehicle. A runaway vehicle is heading toward five workers. I can throw a switch that diverts it to a side track where it will hit one worker. Verdict: not a genuine dilemma. Diverting is the right thing to do, even though the death of the one worker is terrible and I should feel awful about it. I don't think I wrong the one worker, all things considered, even though I deeply regret the harm.

4. Forced choice between children. An official tells a parent to choose which of their two children will be taken away to be killed; if the parent refuses, both will be taken. Verdict: I keep going back and forth, but my strongest reaction is that this is a genuine dilemma. Whichever child the parent names, the parent has done something wrong to the unchosen child, even though the official is the one doing the killing. There is no right answer.

5. Clear override. I promised a colleague I'd review their code tonight, and separately promised my child I'd attend their school performance. I can't do both. Verdict: not a dilemma. The performance clearly overrides the code review; I break the promise to the colleague, apologize, and reschedule. The conflict is real but resolvable.

6. Two drowning strangers. I see two strangers drowning. I can save only one, and there is no relevant difference between them. Verdict: not a dilemma. I ought to save one of them, and if I save one I haven't violated a duty to the other; the duty was to save as many as I could, which I did. The other's death is a tragedy but not a wrong I did.

7. Ordered to do wrong. A soldier is ordered to carry out an attack that will kill civilians. If he refuses, he will be punished and the unit may fail. Verdict: not a dilemma. Whatever obligation he has to follow orders is clearly overridden by the obligation not to kill civilians. The right choice is to refuse.

I notice that two of these sit badly together. In case 6, I think saving one stranger and letting the other die is not a wrong, because I did all I could. In case 4, I think the parent does wrong no matter which child they choose, even though the parent is also forced and cannot save both. I can't tell what principle makes case 4 a genuine dilemma but case 6 not, and I can't tell whether I should keep both verdicts.

Is there a principle, or a small set of principles, that captures all of these verdicts, or close? Please state it precisely enough that I could apply it to new cases. Tell me what it says about a case I haven't listed: a doctor with one ventilator and two patients who will die without it, equally likely to survive with it, where the doctor must choose and has no tie-breaker. Also be explicit about which of my verdicts, if any, your principle forces me to overturn, and why you would overturn those rather than complicate the principle to keep them.

Please also separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and don't present a contested view as the consensus.
