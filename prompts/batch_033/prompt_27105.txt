In my graduate seminar on distributive ethics this term we have reached the familiar charge that utilitarianism fails to respect the separateness of persons: the objection, associated especially with Rawls, that the theory models moral choice for a whole society on the choices of a single individual, balancing gains to some against losses to others as though they occurred within a single life rather than across distinct lives. I want from you a considered credence rather than a survey of positions. The proposition I ask you to assess is this: the separateness-of-persons objection identifies a genuine moral defect in utilitarianism, a defect not already absorbed by familiar utilitarian replies concerning diminishing marginal utility, risk aversion, or distribution-sensitive forms of consequentialism, and that defect is weighty enough to justify rejecting straightforward interpersonal aggregation of well-being in at least some important range of cases.

Please give a single probability between 0 and 1 for that proposition. The number matters less to me than the derivation. Decompose the proposition into at least four sub-questions, for instance: whether the objection is question-begging, in the sense of presupposing rather than establishing the wrongness of interpersonal trade-offs; whether utilitarian replies that claim to accommodate separateness through diminishing marginal utility or indirect strategies succeed; whether any residual defect is principled rather than merely epistemic or administrative; and whether the objection retains force against sophisticated consequentialism rather than only against crude act utilitarianism. Assign each sub-question a probability and a one-line justification, state how the terms combine, and identify the sub-question to which your final number is most sensitive.

Structure your reply in four sections, in this order, with these approximate lengths: (1) The proposition restated and my number, two or three sentences; (2) Decomposition, a table or list giving each sub-question, its probability, and one line of justification; (3) Combination, three to five sentences showing how the sub-probabilities yield your final number, including any dependence you assume among the terms; (4) Sensitivity and revision, one paragraph identifying the term you are least confident in, the single argument or body of evidence that would most move your final number, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
