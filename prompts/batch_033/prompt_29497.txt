I keep running into two different ways of figuring out what caused what, and I can't tell if their the same idea wearing different clothes or two genuinely rival theories, so I'd like you to sort it out.

The first is tort law. To hold someone liable for an injury, the plaintiff usually has to prove factual causation, and the standard test is but-for: would the harm have happened without the defendant's conduct? If it would have happened anyway, there's no causation. Courts mostly stick with this, but they patch it for cases where it gives silly answers, like two fires that merge and either one alone would have destroyed the house, or two shooters firing at the same moment. So the law's basic picture is counterfactual: causation is about a world with the cause removed. I know there's also a proximate cause doctrine; I'm setting that aside and focusing on the factual causation step.

The second is medicine and drug regulation. The FDA generally won't approve a drug for an efficacy claim based only on observational evidence, no matter how carefully it's statistically adjusted. It wants randomized controlled trials, where you actively intervene to change the treatment and watch whether outcomes change. The gold standard is basically change the variable and see what happens. That sounds like a different picture: causation is about manipulation, not about subtracting the cause from the world.

These also seem aimed at different targets. The law wants a yes-or-no answer about whether this defendant's conduct caused this plaintiff's injury, while a trial gives you a population-level average effect. I don't know whether that difference matters for the underlying theory, but I suspect it does.

So here's what I want. Reconstruct the implicit theory of causation behind each practice: what does the but-for test strictly presuppose about what causation is, and what does the RCT standard strictly presuppose? Say where each practice is genuinely committed to that theory and where it's underdetermined, so that more than one theory would license it. Then say whether the two implicit theories are compatible, in tension, or just answers to different questions. Finally, tell me what would have to change in tort law, in drug regulation, or in both if we unified them under a single theory of causation.
