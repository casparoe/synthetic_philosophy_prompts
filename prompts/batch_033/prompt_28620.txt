I'm a cognitive psychology postdoc, and a methods-curious grad student in our lab has been poking at my statistics views, and I've realized I've never actually checked weather they form a coherent package. I'd like an audit rather than a lecture. Here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) I'm a practicing frequentist: I report p-values and confidence intervals, and I think null hypothesis significance testing is a reasonable error-control procedure.
(2) I think the replication crisis is mostly a p-hacking and publication-bias problem, not a failure of the logic of frequentist inference itself.
(3) I think optional stopping is a real problem — peeking at the data and stopping when you hit significance inflates the false positive rate — and that preregistration is the main fix the field needs.
(4) I'm suspicious of Bayesian methods because the prior feels like a free parameter: with enough freedom in the prior you can get whatever posterior you want.
(5) But I find Bayes factors and likelihood ratios appealing, because they seem to measure strength of evidence directly in a way a p-value doesn't.
(6) I also think a lot of replication failures in my area are just low power: the true effects are real but small, and the original studies were too underpowered to find them reliably.
(7) I think it's a serious error to conflate the probability of the data under the null with the probability of the null given the data, and my field does this constantly.
(8) And I think the .05 cutoff is arbitrary; there's no meaningful difference between p = .049 and p = .051.

Does this set of views hang together? I want to know which pairs are actually in tension and which only look that way, ranked by how severe the tension is, and for each real tension what the cheapest fix would be and what it costs. Also, please seperate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among statisticians and methodologists; if the field is split, say so rather than presenting one side as the consensus.
