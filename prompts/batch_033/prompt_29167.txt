I edit a literary magazine and we are preparing a feature on how readers should think about the relationship between an author's intentions and the meaning of a text. I would like to include a decision tree that takes a reader from their response to a concrete interpretive case to a named position in the debate over authorial intention. The tree should open with a case rather than a definition: for instance, whether the meaning of a disputed passage changes when the author later states in an interview that she meant something other than what most careful readers took her to mean. Each subsequent branch node should be another concrete scenario — a posthumously published draft that resolves an ambiguity differently from the published version, a private letter in which the author contradicts the plain sense of her own text, a line whose significance shifts when the reader learns it was inserted by an editor without the author's knowledge, a passage that supports a reading the author has explicitly disavowed. Each leaf should carry the name of the position (actual intentionalism, hypothetical intentionalism, anti-intentionalism of the New Critical sort, reader-response theory, and any others the tree can distinguish) together with a one-sentence statement of its most awkward commitment — the consequence its defenders are stuck with but rarely advertise.

Build the tree from the logical space first: identify the questions on which these views genuinely come apart, map the combinations of answers those questions allow, and then locate the actual positions within that space. If some combinations are unoccupied, note them and briefly say why — is the view incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored? Flag any pair of positions the tree cannot separate, rather than inventing a distinction that does no work.

The audience is general readers of literary fiction, not philosophers, so the cases should feel like real reading experiences and the language should avoid technical terms where ordinary ones will do. The tree should sort people by what they actually say about cases, not by which label they prefer, so no node should simply ask whether they think the author's intention determines meaning.
