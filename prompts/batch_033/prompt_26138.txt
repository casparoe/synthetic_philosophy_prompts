I'm a high school junior taking a semester-long elective on Indian philosophy. We've spent the last few weeks on Nāgārjuna's Mūlamadhyamakakārikā, and I keep coming back to the tetralemma — the catuṣkoṭi — where for any given proposition, Nāgārjuna rejects the affirmation, the negation, the conjunction, and the disjunction. My teacher described it as a kind of exhaustion: you run thought through every logical posture available to it, and none of them can hold. What I find unsettling, and also fascinating, is that the fourth corner — "neither P nor not-P" — looks like it should be the escape hatch, the place where you finally stop grasping, but Nāgārjuna treats it as just another position that covertly reaches for something, and lets it go too.

I want to write a poem that does this structurally rather than just describing it. Here's my idea: a poem in four stanzas, each corresponding to one corner of the tetralemma. The first stanza asserts something — maybe about the self, or about whether things have essences — and the second stanza negates it, the third tries to hold both assertion and negation together, and the fourth tries to settle into neither. But the constraint I want is that each stanza should contain the seed of its own undoing, so the reader feels the collapse happening inside the stanza rather than waiting for the next one to deliver the refutation. And the poem as a whole shouldn't end with "neither" as a stable resting place — the fourth stanza should undermine itself too, so that by the end there's genuinely nowhere to stand, not even in the rejection of standing.

I'd prefer no Sanskrit terms or explicit Buddhist vocabulary — I want someone who knows Madhyamaka to recognize the tetralemma in the movement of the poem, and someone who doesn't to feel the conceptual vertigo without needing footnotes. Four stanzas, maybe sixteen to twenty-four lines total. No fixed meter, but the rhythm and line breaks should do some of the work of building up and dissolving whatever each stanza tries to say.
