I'm teaching a second-year undergraduate course called "Problems in Philosophical Logic," and in two weeks I'm giving a lecture on Lewis Carroll's "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles" — the infinite regress argument about the justification of deduction. The students have already done a unit on validity and soundness, and they've seen modus ponens and modus tollens in their introductory logic course, but they haven't encountered any skeptical challenges to deduction before. I have fifty minutes, a whiteboard, and about forty students, most of whom are philosophy majors but a handful are from mathematics or computer science.

I don't want you to explain the tortoise problem to me — I know it. I want you to plan how I should teach it. Specifically: (a) What is the right order of ideas? What absolutely has to be in place before I can even state the tortoise's challenge, and what depends on what? (b) What single example or case should carry the explanation — should I use Carroll's own dialogue, and if so, how should I present it so that students don't treat it as a cute story and miss the philosophical structure? (c) What misconceptions will the students predictably form or bring with them? I'm already worried about: "the tortoise is just being irrational," "this is the same as Hume's problem of induction," and "we solve it by adding the rule as an axiom." But there are probably others I'm not thinking of. For each misconception, at what point in the sequence should I head it off — before it arises, or let it surface and then undermine it? (d) What should I leave out at this level and explicitly say I'm leaving out? (e) What is one question I can ask at the end — not a reading-comprehension check, but something that would tell me whether they've genuinely grasped the force of the problem rather than just memorized the regress structure?

Please think through the dependency structure of the ideas carefully before you answer, and don't just list the misconceptions — map each one onto the stage of the explanation where it is most likely to surface. Approach this as a philosopher of logic who has taught this material multiple times and has seen where students reliably get stuck.
