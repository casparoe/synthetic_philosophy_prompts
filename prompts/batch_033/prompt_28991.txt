[Essay begins]

The conceivability argument for dualism runs roughly as follows: I can conceive of a world physically identical to ours but lacking consciousness—the zombie world. If I can conceive of it, it is metaphysically possible. If it is metaphysically possible, then consciousness is not identical to or logically supervenient on the physical. Therefore, physicalism is false.

A standard physicalist reply grants the inference from conceivability to possibility but denies the antecedent: the physicalist claims we cannot genuinely conceive of zombies—we only think we can because we are ignorant of the relevant psychophysical identities. On this view, the conceivability intuition is an illusion produced by our incomplete knowledge, much as someone ignorant of chemistry might think they can conceive of water that isn't H2O.

But here is the tension I want to explore. The physicalist who takes this line is committed to the following claim: whether a scenario is genuinely conceivable can depend on empirical discoveries about the actual world. Before the discovery that water is H2O, the scenario "water is not H2O" seemed conceivable; after the discovery, we recognize it was never genuinely conceivable—we were merely under an illusion of conceivability. The same, the physicalist says, will happen with zombies once we discover the psychophysical identities.

But notice what this does to the epistemology of conceivability. If empirical discoveries can retroactively reveal that what seemed conceivable was not in fact conceivable, then we can never be confident, at any given time, that our current conceivability judgments are reliable. For all we know, future empirical discoveries will reveal that what now seems conceivable—including, perhaps, the zombie scenario—is not genuinely conceivable after all. The conceivability-possibility link becomes epistemically inert: we can never use it to learn anything about what is possible, because we can never be sure we are genuinely conceiving rather than merely seeming to conceive.

The physicalist might embrace this result: conceivability is indeed not a reliable guide to possibility, and we should not use it to draw metaphysical conclusions. But this seems too quick. We do, in many cases, take conceivability as evidence of possibility—in science, in everyday reasoning, and in philosophy outside the dualism debate. The challenge is to articulate a version of the conceivability-possibility link that is strong enough to do useful work but not so strong that it falls prey to the physicalist's retroactive-illusion argument.

My proposed resolution distinguishes two kinds of conceivability. "Thin" conceivability is the mere absence of a detected contradiction: a scenario is thinly conceivable if one can entertain it without noticing any logical inconsistency. "Thick" conceivability requires a positive, detailed modal imagination of the scenario—one must be able to fill in the scenario's details in a coherent way, not merely fail to find a contradiction. The physicalist's retroactive-illusion argument, I suggest, works against thin conceivability but not against thick conceivability. When we thickly conceive of zombies—imagining in detail a world physically identical to ours, with all the same causal processes unfolding, but no consciousness—we are not merely failing to detect a contradiction. We are positively imagining a coherent scenario. The discovery of psychophysical identities, if it happens, would not reveal that our thick conceiving was illusory; it would at most reveal that the scenario we thickly conceived is not actual, which is irrelevant to its possibility.
