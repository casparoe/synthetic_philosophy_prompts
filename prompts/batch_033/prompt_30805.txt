Assignment question:

The MIU system has the alphabet M, I, U, the single axiom MI, and the following rules: (1) xI → xIU; (2) Mx → Mxx; (3) xIIIy → xUy; (4) xUUy → xy. Can MU be derived in this system? Either give a derivation or give a rigorous argument that no derivation exists. Then explain what your answer shows about what Hofstadter calls jumping out of the system.

Student's answer:

The string MU cannot be derived. Define the I-count of a string as its number of I symbols. The axiom MI has I-count 1. Rule (1) leaves the I-count unchanged, since it only appends a U to a string that ends in I. Rule (2) doubles the I-count, since it duplicates everything after the initial M. Rule (3) lowers the I-count by exactly 3, since it replaces three I's with a single U. Rule (4) leaves the I-count unchanged, since it only deletes U's. The I-count modulo 3 is therefore invariant: it starts at 1, and although rule (2) doubles the count, doubling a number not divisible by 3 always yields another number not divisible by 3. So no reachable string has an I-count divisible by 3. But MU has I-count 0, which is divisible by 3. Hence MU is not a theorem of the system.

This is a miniature version of what Gödel made precise. The MIU system is incomplete, since MU is a well-formed string that the system can neither prove nor refute. The argument works by jumping out of the system: instead of mechanically applying the rules from the inside, we step outside and notice an invariant of the entire rule set, something no internal derivation could have produced. Jumping out of the system, in the end, means adopting a stronger system — for instance, adding a rule that deletes all I symbols — from whose standpoint MU is derivable after all; what is underivable in one system is always derivable in some larger one. That is why the MU-puzzle is often read as a small model of Gödelian incompleteness.
