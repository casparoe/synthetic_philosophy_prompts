I've been thinking about promising — specifically, about what makes a promise binding and when that bindingness lapses or is defeated. I find that my gut reactions to particular cases don't line up neatly with any single theory I know, and I'd like help working out what principle, if any, they add up to.

Here are the cases and my verdicts:

1. I promise a colleague I'll read their draft by Friday. Thursday night, a family member is hospitalized, and I spend the evening at the hospital. I don't read the draft. Verdict: the promise doesn't bind me in those circumstances — the obligation is defeated, not merely outweighed.

2. I promise a friend I'll keep a secret they confide. Later I learn the secret concerns ongoing financial fraud at our workplace. Verdict: the promise doesn't bind me; I'm free to report it.

3. A stranger at a party, slightly drunk, promises to donate a kidney to me after hearing I'm on a waiting list. The next morning, sober, they say they didn't mean it. Verdict: the promise doesn't bind them.

4. My elderly neighbor promises to leave me her antique desk in her will. She forgets to update the will and dies; the desk goes to her nephew. Verdict: the promise bound her, and her estate has a moral debt to me, even though I have no legal claim.

5. I promise my young daughter I'll take her to the aquarium on Saturday. Saturday morning I wake up with a bad migraine. I cancel. Verdict: the promise still has force — I owe her a rescheduled trip and an apology — but I wasn't wrong to cancel.

6. A contractor promises to finish a renovation by June 1. They're delayed by a supply-chain disruption entirely outside their control. Verdict: the promise still binds, but the breach isn't culpable; they owe completion as soon as feasible, not damages.

7. I promise a friend I'll attend their wedding. Two days before, my spouse has a serious medical episode and I can't leave town. Verdict: same as case 1 — the obligation is defeated, not just overridden.

8. Someone promises under credible threat of violence to hand over their wallet. Verdict: no binding force whatsoever.

9. I tell a distant acquaintance "let's get lunch sometime" meaning it as a genuine promise, but they treat it as mere politeness and never follow up. Verdict: the promise binds me weakly — I should make a good-faith effort to schedule something, but if they never respond, I'm released.

10. I promise to donate to a charity after a friend's fundraising pitch, but I later discover the charity has a terrible record of actually delivering aid. Verdict: the promise doesn't bind me; the basis on which I made it was false.

What I can't figure out is whether there's a unified principle here. Cases 1 and 7 feel like they should be explained the same way — something about impossibility or catastrophic cost — but is the distinction between an obligation's being "defeated" and its being "outweighed" doing real work, or am I just labeling an intuition? Case 3 and case 8 both involve compromised consent, yet I feel they're different in an important way — drunkenness and coercion aren't the same kind of defect — but I end up with the same verdict for both, which makes me wonder whether I'm missing a distinction that should matter. Case 4 and case 9 both involve promises that are in some sense inert or unclaimed, yet I judge them very differently: the desk promise binds fully, the lunch promise barely binds. And case 10 seems to involve a mistake about the object of the promise, but not all mistakes release us — if I promise to buy you a book I think is in print but it's out of print, I still owe you something.

Is there a principle, or a small set of principles, that captures these verdicts? Which of my verdicts would I have to revise to get a simpler account, and what does the resulting account say about a case I haven't considered: a promise made by someone suffering from early-stage dementia who understood the promise when they made it but no longer remembers having made it?
