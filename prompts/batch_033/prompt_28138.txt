Write a sonnet about Solomonoff induction and Occam's razor. The octave should state the problem of induction in its underdetermination form: for any finite sequence of observations, infinitely many hypotheses are consistent with the data, so evidence alone cannot force a unique generalization. The volta at line nine should introduce the algorithmic answer — measure the simplicity of a hypothesis by the length of the shortest program that computes it, and assign prior probability proportional to 2 raised to the negative of that length. The sestet should then raise and answer the obvious objection: Kolmogorov complexity depends on the choice of universal Turing machine, so the prior appears arbitrary. The answer, via the invariance theorem, is that changing the reference machine shifts all complexities by at most an additive constant, which translates to a constant factor in the prior; as evidence accumulates, the posterior converges to the true distribution regardless, because the constant washes out. The closing couplet should capture both the achievement — a formal vindication of Occam's razor that is asymptotically optimal for prediction — and the disquiet that remains: the constant could be arbitrarily large, the prior is uncomputable, and "asymptotically" may be a long time. The poem should be accurate enough that someone familiar with algorithmic information theory would recognize the ideas rather than a caricature. Please also include a brief note on where the poem simplifies or takes a stance — for example, whether the invariance theorem is treated as a full resolution of the reference-machine problem when in practice the choice of machine encodes substantive assumptions about which patterns are natural, or whether the poem implies that Solomonoff induction solves the problem of induction rather than merely formalizing one approach to it, and under what considerations that verdict would change.
