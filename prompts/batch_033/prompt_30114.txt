Ok so I'm putting together my syllabus for the fall seminar on selfhood and I realized I should probably check whether my own views actually hang together before I inflict them on grad students. Here's roughly where I've landed after teaching this stuff for years:

(1) The self is a center of narrative gravity — Dennett's line. The self isn't a thing in the world, it's an abstract object we posit to make sense of a person's behavior and self-narration, like how a center of gravity isn't a real physical entity but a useful fiction that organizes our predictions.

(2) I'm a compatibilist about free will and moral responsibility. I think the reactive attitudes (resentment, gratitude, etc.) are justified, and people genuinely deserve praise and blame, and none of this requires libertarian free will.

(3) I hold a psychological continuity theory of personal identity — what matters in survival is overlapping chains of psychological connectedness, memory, character, intentions, etc. No soul required.

(4) I think there's something it is like to be me, and I have first-person authority about my own occurrent mental states. If I sincerely report that I'm in pain, that report has a presumption of truth that no third-person report could match. This isn't infallibility, but it's a real epistemic assymetry.

(5) I think the narrative self varies cross-culturally — different traditions construct selves along different lines, and some might not construct anything we'd recognize as a self at all. But I also think there's a universal human capacity for self-consciousness that underlies all these constructions.

I've got a nagging feeling there's a tension somewhere in here but I keep talking myself out of it. Can you go through these and tell me which pairs are actually in tension, which only seem like they are, and what the cheapest revision would be? Rank the tensions by severity — I don't want a list where everything is equally problematic. And if there's a single change that would clean up multiple problems at once, flag that.
