I serve on the Committee for Named Lectureships and Prizes at my university. We are currently reviewing a petition, signed by a substantial number of graduate students and several faculty members, requesting that the 'Frege Prize in Philosophical Logic'—an annual undergraduate essay prize endowed in 1982—be renamed. The petitioners cite the contents of Frege's political diary from 1924, published posthumously only in 1996, in which Frege expressed virulent anti-Semitic views, called for the expulsion of Jews from Germany and the stripping of their political rights, and voiced enthusiastic support for the völkisch movement. The petitioners argue that continuing to honour Frege by name is incompatible with the university's stated commitment to an inclusive intellectual community, and that the belated publication of the diary means that the original naming decision was made in ignorance of facts we now cannot responsibly ignore.

The counterargument, advanced by several senior colleagues in logic and philosophy of language, is that Frege's contributions to logic, the philosophy of mathematics, and the philosophy of language are foundational to the discipline and entirely separable from his late political views. They note that the diary was written in 1924, decades after his major philosophical works, and that nothing in *Begriffsschrift*, *Grundlagen*, or 'On Sense and Reference' so much as gestures toward the political commitments of the diary. They also warn of a slippery slope: if we rename this prize, consistency will demand we revisit every named honour in the university, many of which attach to figures whose political or personal lives would not survive contemporary scrutiny. Some add that the very idea that a thinker's political views are relevant to the assessment of their technical work in logic is a category mistake—one that threatens the autonomy of intellectual inquiry.

As the committee member tasked with preparing a draft opinion for our next meeting, I need a short memo—no more than a thousand words—that recommends a concrete course of action: retain the name, rename the prize, or some third option (e.g., retain the name but with a contextualising statement, or rename the prize while preserving its thematic connection to Frege's field). The memo must engage seriously with the strongest arguments on both sides, and it must address what I take to be the central philosophical question underlying the dispute: whether and in what sense Frege's political views bear on his philosophical legacy. Specifically, does the fact that Frege's philosophy appears to be 'pure'—technical, ahistorical, concerned with timeless logical relations—itself constitute a reason to treat his political views as irrelevant, or does it rather invite the suspicion that the appearance of purity is itself a kind of evasion, one that insulates certain kinds of work from moral scrutiny precisely by denying that moral scrutiny could apply? The committee includes non-philosophers, so the memo should be accessible to an academic audience outside the discipline. Please write it in British English.
