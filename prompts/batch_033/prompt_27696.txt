I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, mostly philosophy and the social sciences, and this term I got talked into helping grade an undergraduate political philosophy course. We just finished a unit on luck egalitarianism, and there's one exam answer I keep going back and forth on. The question we set was:

"Explain Dworkin's distinction between option luck and brute luck and the role it plays in his equality of resources. What is the envy test supposed to ensure, and how is the hypothetical insurance market supposed to deal with unchosen differences in talent and handicap? Then discuss one objection — either that the view is too harsh on people who make bad choices, or that it cannot draw a stable line between chosen and unchosen features of a person's situation. Does the objection succeed?"

Here is what the student wrote:

"Luck egalitarianism says that no one should be worse off through brute luck — the stuff that just happens to you, like being born with a disability or losing your house to a tornado — while inequalities that come from option luck, the risks you knowingly took, are fine because you could have chosen otherwise. Dworkin's equality of resources is the classic version. He starts with an auction where everyone gets equal tokens and bids for the resources they want; the outcome is fair when it satisfies the envy test, meaning no one prefers anyone else's bundle. But the auction can't fix the brute luck of being born with low talent or a handicap. So Dworkin introduces a hypothetical insurance market: we ask what insurance people would have bought behind a veil of ignorance about their talents, and we use the premiums they would have paid to compensate the unlucky. This effectively turns brute luck into option luck, because the person who would have insured against a handicap gets a payout, while someone who gambles and loses is stuck with the loss since they could have insured. I think the view can answer the objection that it's too harsh on bad choosers. If everyone had a fair chance to insure, then refusing to bail out someone who chose not to isn't harsh; it's just respecting their choice. There's also a version of the stable-line objection worth mentioning: Arneson's equal access to advantage view says that if you didn't choose your expensive tastes, you shouldn't have to bear their costs, which suggests Dworkin draws the line between choice and circumstance in the wrong place."
